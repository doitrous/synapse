## label
A restrictive pattern is a low FVC with a preserved or high FEV1/FVC ratio; an obstructive pattern is a low FEV1/FVC ratio

## canonical_key
pft.pattern.restrictive-obstructive

## aliases
Restrictive versus obstructive lung disease pattern
Spirometric pattern recognition

## arabic_label
النمط التقييدي مقابل النمط الانسدادي في وظائف الرئة

## arabic_aliases
النمط التقييدي والانسدادي

## definition
Pulmonary function testing separates disease into two basic spirometric patterns. A restrictive pattern (e.g. silicosis, interstitial lung fibrosis, late pregnancy) shows a reduced total lung capacity and vital capacity from stiff lungs or a restricted chest wall, while the FEV1/FVC ratio is preserved or even increased because both numerator and denominator fall together. An obstructive pattern (e.g. bronchial asthma, emphysema, COPD) shows a reduced FEV1/FVC ratio because airway narrowing slows expiratory flow disproportionately, while the FVC itself may be near-normal or only mildly reduced. The single number that separates the two is the FEV1/FVC ratio, not FVC alone.

## explicit_objective
Given spirometry data (or a flow-volume/volume-time curve), classify the pattern as restrictive or obstructive using the FEV1/FVC ratio, and name a disease example of each.

## pitfalls
Classifying by FVC or TLC alone. A restrictive disease also lowers FVC, so a low FVC on its own does not distinguish the two patterns — the FEV1/FVC ratio (preserved/high in restriction, low in obstruction) is the discriminating number.

## label
RV/TLC% is normal or decreased in restrictive disease and increased in obstructive disease

## canonical_key
pft.rv-tlc-ratio.pattern-direction

## aliases
RV/TLC ratio by disease pattern
Air trapping and the RV/TLC ratio

## arabic_label
نسبة الحجم المتبقي إلى السعة الرئوية الكلية حسب النمط

## arabic_aliases

## definition
The RV/TLC% (residual volume as a fraction of total lung capacity) moves in opposite directions in the two PFT patterns. In restrictive disease the whole lung shrinks roughly proportionally, so RV/TLC% stays normal (or can fall slightly) even though both RV and TLC are reduced in absolute terms. In obstructive disease, airway narrowing causes air trapping: RV rises (sometimes markedly) while TLC rises less or stays similar, so RV/TLC% increases — this is the physiological basis of "hyperinflation" on a chest film in emphysema or asthma.

## explicit_objective
State the direction of change of RV/TLC% in restrictive versus obstructive lung disease and explain air trapping as the mechanism behind the obstructive rise.

## pitfalls
Assuming RV/TLC% always falls when lung volumes shrink. In restriction the ratio is preserved because RV and TLC fall together; it is air trapping (RV rising faster than TLC), not shrinkage, that changes the ratio in obstruction.

## label
Bohr's method uses expired CO2 to measure physiological dead space

## canonical_key
deadspace.bohr-method.co2

## aliases
Bohr method for dead space
CO2-based dead space measurement

## arabic_label
طريقة بور لقياس الحيز الميت الفسيولوجي

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Bohr's method measures physiological dead space (VD) from the CO2 dilution principle: because only alveolar gas exchanges CO2 with pulmonary capillary blood, the CO2 concentration of mixed expired air is a weighted average of alveolar CO2 (from ventilated, perfused alveoli) and dead-space CO2 (essentially inspired-air CO2, near zero). Comparing mixed-expired CO2 against arterial (or end-tidal alveolar) CO2 in the Bohr equation (VD/VT = (PaCO2 − PECO2)/PaCO2) yields the fraction of each breath that is dead space. Because it uses CO2 rather than N2, Bohr's method captures physiological dead space — anatomical dead space plus any alveolar dead space from underperfused but ventilated alveoli — unlike Fowler's N2-washout method, which captures anatomical dead space only.

## explicit_objective
State what Bohr's method measures (physiological dead space) and the gas/principle it relies on (CO2 dilution), and contrast it with what Fowler's method measures.

## pitfalls
Confusing Bohr's method (CO2, physiological dead space) with Fowler's method (N2 washout, anatomical dead space only). The two methods use different gases and answer different questions: Bohr's includes any alveolar dead space from V/Q mismatch, Fowler's does not.

## label
Fowler's method uses single-breath nitrogen washout to measure anatomical dead space

## canonical_key
deadspace.fowler-method.n2-washout

## aliases
Fowler method for anatomical dead space
N2 washout curve

## arabic_label
طريقة فاولر لقياس الحيز الميت التشريحي

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Fowler's method measures anatomical dead space using a single-breath nitrogen-washout curve. The subject inspires 100% O2, then expires while a rapid N2 analyzer plots expired N2 concentration against expired volume: N2 concentration is near zero at first (pure dead-space gas, which never mixed with the alveolar 100% O2), rises sharply during a mixed transition phase, then plateaus at the alveolar N2 concentration. Anatomical dead space is read off the curve as the volume at the mid-point of the rapidly-rising transition phase — the volume expired before the "50% N2" point is taken as air that came from the conducting airways alone.

## explicit_objective
Describe how the single-breath N2-washout curve is generated and how anatomical dead space is read from it, and state what it measures compared with Bohr's method.

## pitfalls
Reading the plateau phase, rather than the mid-point of the rising transition phase, as the dead-space boundary. The plateau represents pure alveolar gas; the anatomical dead-space volume is defined at the transition's midpoint by convention, not at either pure extreme.

## label
FEV1/FVC ratio and the flow-volume/volume-time curve shape distinguish normal, obstructive and restrictive breathing

## canonical_key
pft.fev1-fvc-ratio.pattern-interpretation

## aliases
FEV1% interpretation
Flow-volume loop and volume-time curve reading

## arabic_label
تفسير منحنى الحجم-الزمن ونسبة FEV1/FVC

## arabic_aliases

## definition
FEV1% (FEV1/FVC × 100) is read directly off a forced expiration volume-time curve: FEV1 is the volume expired in the first second, FVC is the total volume expired. A normal FEV1% is roughly 75-80% or higher; a value clearly below 70% signals obstruction (the curve rises slowly and plateaus late), while a normal or supranormal FEV1% with a reduced absolute FVC signals restriction (the curve is small but has the normal steep early shape). On a flow-volume loop, obstructive disease shows a "scooped-out" concave expiratory limb from airway collapse, while restriction shows a narrow loop of reduced volume but normal or increased peak flow relative to its size.

## explicit_objective
Calculate FEV1% from a volume-time curve or given FEV1/FVC values, and use the value together with the curve's shape to classify the pattern as normal, obstructive or restrictive.

## pitfalls
Treating any reduced FEV1 alone as diagnostic. FEV1 falls in both patterns; it is the FEV1/FVC *ratio*, together with the curve's own shape (scooped concave versus small-but-normal-shaped), that separates obstruction from restriction.

## label

## canonical_key
ecg.q-wave.septal-depolarisation

## aliases
Q wave mechanism
Septal depolarisation on ECG

## arabic_label
تمثل موجة Q إزالة استقطاب الحاجز البطيني

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Ventricular depolarisation begins in the interventricular septum, activated from the left bundle branch before the free ventricular walls depolarise. This early, small, leftward-to-rightward septal depolarisation is recorded as the small initial negative deflection (Q wave) in leads that view the septum from the left (e.g. leads I, aVL, V5-V6), before the much larger R wave from free-wall depolarisation follows. A physiological "septal Q wave" is small and narrow; a deep or wide pathological Q wave instead signals transmural myocardial infarction, where dead tissue generates no depolarisation current and the electrode instead "sees through" to the opposite wall.

## explicit_objective
State what the normal Q wave represents (septal depolarisation) and distinguish a normal small septal Q wave from a pathological Q wave of infarction by depth/width.

## definition
A fully occluded coronary artery causes transmural (full-thickness) injury: the injured zone cannot repolarise normally, producing ST-segment elevation in the leads overlying it (the acute-phase sign) and a subendocardial injury current in the opposite leads (reciprocal ST depression). Treating ST elevation and ST depression as interchangeable "abnormal ST segment" findings. ST elevation over the infarct territory signals transmural injury in evolution; ST depression (often reciprocal, in the opposite leads) or ST depression alone signals ischaemia without full-thickness injury — different mechanisms with different urgency.

## arabic_label

## definition
Correct auscultatory blood-pressure technique requires: the manometer/device at the same vertical level as the heart (a device held too high or low introduces a hydrostatic error), the cuff bladder centred over the brachial artery with its lower edge about 2.5 cm proximal to the cubital fossa (leaving room for the stethoscope diaphragm), and the diaphragm placed under (not over) the cuff edge. ## explicit_objective
List the technique errors that distort an auscultatory blood-pressure reading (device height, cuff position, diaphragm placement) and explain why a palpatory estimate is done first.

## pitfalls
Treating the palpatory step as optional or merely a backup method. Its specific purpose is to set the correct starting inflation pressure so the auscultatory gap is not mistaken for the true systolic pressure.

## arabic_label

## definition
For a continuous flow (the same total volume per minute passing through every level of the circulation), velocity at any point is inversely proportional to the total cross-sectional area available at that point (velocity = flow/area). The aorta has the smallest total cross-sectional area of the circulation, so blood moves fastest there (about 40-50 cm/s in large arteries); despite each individual capillary being tiny, the sheer number of capillaries in parallel gives them the largest total cross-sectional area of any level, so velocity there falls to roughly 0.03-0.05 cm/s — the "traffic jam" that gives time for exchange to occur. Velocity then rises again through the venules and veins as their combined cross-sectional area narrows back down toward the heart.

## explicit_objective
State that blood flow velocity is inversely proportional to total cross-sectional area, and rank velocity across the aorta, arterioles, capillaries and veins accordingly.

## pitfalls
Assuming capillaries are slow because they are narrow. An individual capillary's small diameter is not what slows flow — it is the enormous total cross-sectional area created by billions of capillaries running in parallel that slows velocity, exactly the same principle (inverse to total area) that makes the aorta fast.

## definition
In atrial fibrillation, ventricular filling time varies beat to beat because the irregular rhythm gives some beats too little diastolic filling time; a beat that follows very closely on the one before generates too weak a stroke volume to produce a palpable peripheral pulse wave at all, even though it is heard as a heartbeat at the chest. Counting the radial pulse therefore gives a lower rate than simultaneously auscultating the heart directly — this difference (auscultated rate minus palpated pulse rate) is the pulsus deficit, and it is a classic bedside sign of atrial fibrillation (and other irregular rhythms) precisely because a regular rhythm gives every beat the same, adequate filling time.

## explicit_objective
Define pulsus deficit and explain the mechanism (variable diastolic filling time in an irregular rhythm) that produces it, naming atrial fibrillation as the classic association.

## pitfalls
Assuming pulsus deficit means the heart rate itself is abnormally low. It reflects a *discrepancy* between two measurements of the same beats (auscultated versus palpated), not a true bradycardia — the heart rate by auscultation can be normal or even fast while a pulsus deficit is present.

Minute respiratory volume (total minute ventilation) is the total volume of air moved in and out of the lungs in one minute, calculated as tidal volume (VT) multiplied by respiratory rate (RR).
