## label
Erythropoiesis speeds up after haemorrhage or haemolysis, and the reticulocyte count is the marker of it

## id
CON-HEM-F8EE5FA3992E35

## canonical_key
reticulocyte.count.rises-with-accelerated-erythropoiesis

## definition
When red cells are lost faster than usual — by bleeding or by haemolysis — erythropoietin drives the bone marrow to release red cells before they are fully mature. These early cells still carry ribosomal RNA and are counted as reticulocytes, so a rising reticulocyte count is the laboratory sign that the marrow is answering a real demand for red cells.

## explicit_objective
State that the reticulocyte count rises after haemorrhage or haemolysis, and explain why: it is the marrow's compensatory response, not a separate disease process.

## pitfalls
Reading a raised reticulocyte count as itself abnormal, rather than as the marrow correctly responding to a real loss of red cells. A low reticulocyte count in the same setting is the worrying finding, because it means the marrow cannot respond.

# Item

## label
Polycythaemia is primary when the marrow itself overproduces red cells, and secondary when erythropoietin drives it from outside

## id
CON-HEM-5F0CEC52166316

## canonical_key
polycythemia.classification.primary-vs-secondary-epo-driven

## definition
Polycythaemia is a red cell count, haemoglobin, and haematocrit above normal. Primary polycythaemia (polycythaemia vera) is a bone-marrow disorder that overproduces red cells independent of erythropoietin. Secondary polycythaemia is a normal marrow responding to a raised erythropoietin drive — from chronic hypoxia (high altitude, chronic lung or cardiac disease) or, less commonly, from a tumour that secretes erythropoietin itself.

## explicit_objective
Distinguish primary polycythaemia (marrow disorder) from secondary polycythaemia (erythropoietin-driven), and name the drivers of each.

## pitfalls
Assuming any raised red cell count must be erythropoietin-driven. Primary polycythaemia (polycythaemia vera) raises the count with an erythropoietin level that is often low, because the marrow is not responding to the hormone at all — it has stopped needing to.

# Item

## label
Oxygen occupies the sixth coordination bond of haem iron; deoxyhaemoglobin leaves it empty

## id
CON-RES-361A5D87C12875

## canonical_key
hemoglobin.oxygen-binding.heme-iron-coordination-bonds

## definition
The iron atom at the centre of each haem group forms six coordination bonds. Four go to the nitrogens of its own porphyrin ring, and the fifth binds the nitrogen of the proximal histidine of the globin chain. The sixth is the oxygen-binding site: it holds oxygen in oxyhaemoglobin and is unoccupied in deoxyhaemoglobin.

## explicit_objective
State what occupies the fifth and sixth coordination bonds of haem iron in deoxyhaemoglobin and in oxyhaemoglobin.

## pitfalls
Treating the fifth and sixth coordination bonds as interchangeable. The fifth is permanently occupied by the proximal histidine whether or not oxygen is bound; only the sixth site changes with oxygenation.

## label
ABO and Rh compatibility is checked by mixing serum against antigens; a person with neither A nor B antigen is the universal red-cell donor

## id
CON-HEM-A6BFFFDC813515

## canonical_key
bloodgroup.abo-rh.compatibility-and-universal-donor

## definition
ABO and Rh blood groups are named for the antigens on the red-cell surface. A person's plasma carries antibodies against whatever ABO antigen they lack, so mixing a sample against known antisera and watching for agglutination identifies the group. Because O-group red cells carry neither A nor B antigen, they trigger no ABO antibody reaction in a recipient of any ABO group, which is why group O, Rh-negative blood is the universal red-cell donor — the recipient's own antibodies have nothing on the donor cell to attack.

## explicit_objective
Explain, from the antigen–antibody logic of the ABO system, why group O blood is the universal red-cell donor, and state how ABO/Rh compatibility is tested.

## pitfalls
Treating "universal donor" as meaning O blood is compatible with everyone's plasma too, rather than specifically that O red cells carry no ABO antigen for a recipient's antibodies to attack. Plasma compatibility runs the other way (AB is the universal plasma donor), and confusing the two direction is a common exam trap.

## definition

## definition
Haematocrit (packed cell volume) is the percentage of a whole-blood sample's volume occupied by red cells, measured by centrifuging an anticoagulated capillary sample and reading the packed column against a ruler scale. The normal value is about 40–54% in men and 40–50% in women. It rises in polycythaemia or dehydration (more cells, or less plasma to dilute them) and falls in anaemia or overhydration (fewer cells, or more plasma diluting them) — a saline infusion lowers it by diluting the plasma without changing the red-cell mass, which is why haematocrit alone cannot distinguish a true anaemia from a diluted sample.

## explicit_objective
State the technique and normal value of haematocrit, and predict which direction it moves with polycythaemia, anaemia, dehydration, and dilution by intravenous fluid.

## pitfalls
Treating haematocrit as a reliable stand-alone diagnosis of anaemia or polycythaemia. It moves with plasma volume as much as with red-cell mass, so a patient can have a normal haematocrit with a genuinely low red-cell count if they are also dehydrated, or vice versa.

MCV, MCH and MCHC are calculated red-cell indices, each derived from haematocrit, red-cell count and haemoglobin

## id
CON-HEM-488777E982E1DB

## canonical_key
redcellindices.mcv-mch-mchc.definitions-and-calculation

## definition
Mean corpuscular volume (MCV) is the average volume of a single red cell, calculated as haematocrit × 10 ÷ red-cell count in millions/mm³ (normal 81–93 cubic microns). Mean corpuscular haemoglobin (MCH) is the average amount of haemoglobin per red cell, calculated as haemoglobin (g/dl) × 10 ÷ red-cell count in millions/mm³ (normal 27–33 pg). Mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration (MCHC) is the amount of haemoglobin in 100 ml of red cells, calculated as haemoglobin (g/dl) × 100 ÷ haematocrit (normal about 34 g/dl). Together they classify an anaemia as microcytic, normocytic or macrocytic, and as hypochromic or normochromic.

## explicit_objective
Define MCV, MCH and MCHC, give their calculation from haematocrit/red-cell count/haemoglobin, and use them to classify an anaemia.

## pitfalls
Confusing MCH (an amount of haemoglobin per cell) with MCHC (a concentration of haemoglobin per volume of packed cells) — the two use different denominators and a cell can be macrocytic with a normal MCHC, which only makes sense once the two calculations are kept separate.

## definition

## pitfalls

## label
ESR is the rate red cells settle in an anticoagulated column over one hour, raised physiologically in menses and pregnancy

## id
CON-HEM-A708323F4E072A

## canonical_key
esr.test.technique-normal-value-and-physiological-causes

## definition
Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) is measured by timing how far red cells settle down a Westergren (Wessermann) tube of anticoagulated blood over one and two hours, read as the height of clear plasma left above them. Normal values are about 3–5 mm in the first hour for men and 5–7 mm for women (roughly doubling by the second hour). ESR rises physiologically in menses and pregnancy, and pathologically wherever plasma fibrinogen and globulins rise — inflammation, infection, malignancy, trauma — because that raises red-cell stickiness and rouleaux formation. It is a sensitive but non-specific marker, useful for following a disease course rather than naming a diagnosis. A decreased ESR is rare and occurs in congenital afibrinogenaemia, where the sedimentation-promoting protein itself is absent.

## explicit_objective
State how ESR is measured and its normal values, and predict the direction it moves with menses, pregnancy, inflammation, and congenital afibrinogenaemia.

## pitfalls
Treating a raised ESR as diagnostic of a specific disease. It is a sensitive marker of tissue damage or inflammation in general, not a specific one — it is used alongside other tests and to follow a known disease's course, not to name a new one from itself.

## label
Vitamin K enables the liver's synthesis of clotting factors II, VII, IX and X

## id
CON-HEM-A513A64850C745

## canonical_key
vitamink.coagulation.enables-clotting-factor-synthesis

## definition
Vitamin K is a cofactor for the hepatic enzyme that carboxylates glutamic acid residues on clotting factors II (prothrombin), VII, IX and X, a modification the factors need to bind calcium and function. Vitamin K deficiency or its pharmacological antagonism (warfarin) therefore leaves the liver making these factors in an inactive form, prolonging coagulation time and, in the laboratory, the prothrombin time (PT/INR).

## explicit_objective
State that vitamin K is required for the liver's synthesis of functional clotting factors II, VII, IX and X, and predict the laboratory consequence of its deficiency.

## pitfalls
Treating vitamin K deficiency as though the liver simply stops making the factors. It still makes them — without vitamin K they are secreted in a functionally inactive, uncarboxylated form that cannot bind calcium, which is why the defect is reversible by giving vitamin K rather than requiring factor replacement.
