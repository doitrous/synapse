## label
The ventral portion of the fourth pharyngeal arch cartilage gives rise to the thyroid cartilage

## canonical_key
embryology.pharyngeal-arch.fourth-arch-thyroid-cartilage

## definition
The laryngeal cartilages develop from the cartilage bars of the fourth and sixth pharyngeal arches, not from the third or fifth. The ventral (paired) portions of the fourth arch cartilage fuse in the midline to form the thyroid cartilage, while the sixth arch cartilage forms the cricoid, arytenoid and corniculate cartilages. There is no fifth pharyngeal arch cartilage bar in humans — the fifth arch is transient and leaves no persistent skeletal derivative — and the stapes and styloid process derive from the second (hyoid) arch cartilage, not the third.

## explicit_objective
State that the fourth pharyngeal arch cartilage's ventral portion forms the thyroid cartilage, and that the fifth arch has no persisting cartilage derivative.

## label
The second pharyngeal arch gives rise to the stylohyoid muscle

## canonical_key
embryology.pharyngeal-arch.second-arch-stylohyoid-muscle

## definition
The second (hyoid) pharyngeal arch is innervated by the facial nerve (CN VII) and gives rise to the muscles of facial expression, stapedius, stylohyoid, and the posterior belly of digastric. The anterior belly of digastric and mylohyoid are first-arch (trigeminal, V3) derivatives, stylopharyngeus is a third-arch (glossopharyngeal, IX) derivative, and geniohyoid is not a pharyngeal-arch muscle at all — it is a hypoglossal-innervated derivative of the occipital myotomes.

Identify the stylohyoid muscle as a second pharyngeal arch (facial nerve) derivative, distinct from first-, third-arch and non-arch muscles of the neck.

## label
The connective tissue layer is the vascular layer of the scalp

## canonical_key
anatomy.scalp.connective-tissue-vascular-layer

## definition
The scalp has five layers, classically remembered by the mnemonic SCALP: Skin, dense subcutaneous Connective tissue, epicranial Aponeurosis (galea aponeurotica), Loose areolar tissue, and Pericranium. The second layer — the dense subcutaneous connective tissue — is the vascular and nerve-bearing layer: the scalp's arteries and cutaneous nerves run within it, tethered to the skin above and the aponeurosis below by fibrous septa, which is also why scalp wounds in this layer bleed profusely and why vessels do not retract.

## explicit_objective
Identify the dense subcutaneous connective tissue layer as the vascular layer of the scalp, within the five-layer SCALP scheme.

The greater petrosal nerve carries preganglionic parasympathetic fibers to the lacrimal gland

## canonical_key
neuroanatomy.cranial-nerve.greater-petrosal-lacrimal-pathway

## definition
Preganglionic parasympathetic fibers to the lacrimal gland originate in the superior salivatory nucleus of the facial nerve (CN VII) and leave the facial nerve as the greater petrosal nerve, which joins the deep petrosal (sympathetic) nerve to form the nerve of the pterygoid canal; this reaches the pterygopalatine ganglion, where the parasympathetic fibers synapse, and postganglionic fibers travel via the zygomatic and lacrimal nerves to the lacrimal gland. The chorda tympani, by contrast, is the facial nerve branch supplying the submandibular and sublingual glands, not the lacrimal gland, and the zygomaticotemporal and lesser petrosal nerves belong to different pathways (the lesser petrosal carries parotid parasympathetics from the glossopharyngeal nerve).

## label
The body of the caudate nucleus is a suprathalamic structure

## canonical_key
neuroanatomy.basal-ganglia.caudate-body-suprathalamic

## definition
The caudate nucleus is a C-shaped structure with a head, body and tail that arches over the thalamus. Its body runs along the floor of the body of the lateral ventricle, directly superior to the thalamus, which is why the body of the caudate is classed as a suprathalamic structure, separated from the thalamus by the stria terminalis and thalamostriate vein. The head of the caudate lies anterior and lateral to the anterior horn of the lateral ventricle (not directly above the thalamus), the tail curves inferiorly into the temporal horn alongside the amygdala, and the lentiform nucleus (putamen + globus pallidus) sits lateral to the thalamus rather than above it.

## explicit_objective
Identify the body of the caudate nucleus as the suprathalamic part of the caudate, distinguishing it from the head, tail, and lentiform nucleus.

The vagus nerve runs in the carotid sheath between the internal jugular vein and the common carotid artery

## canonical_key
neuroanatomy.cranial-nerve.vagus-carotid-sheath-course

Within the carotid sheath in the neck, the vagus nerve (CN X) descends in the posterior groove between the internal jugular vein (laterally) and the internal/common carotid artery (medially), giving off the pharyngeal and superior/recurrent laryngeal branches along its course before entering the thorax.
