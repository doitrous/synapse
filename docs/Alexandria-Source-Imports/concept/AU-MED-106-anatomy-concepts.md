## definition
The subclavian artery is conventionally divided into three parts by scalenus anterior, the muscle crossing its front. The first part, medial to the muscle, gives three branches: the vertebral artery, which ascends through the transverse foramina of the upper six cervical vertebrae toward the brain; the internal thoracic artery, which descends behind the costal cartilages to supply the anterior chest wall; and the thyrocervical trunk, a short trunk dividing into the inferior thyroid, transverse cervical and suprascapular arteries. The second part, behind scalenus anterior, gives only the costocervical trunk. The third part, lateral to the muscle, usually gives no named branch and continues at the outer border of the first rib as the axillary artery, the standard anatomical landmark for the subclavian-to-axillary naming change.

## explicit_objective
State the three branches of the first part of the subclavian artery, name the one branch its second part gives, and state where the artery is renamed the axillary artery.

## pitfalls
Assigning the costocervical trunk to the first part instead of the second — a classic mix-up, since both trunks contain "cervical" in the name. A student who cannot say which part gives which trunk will mis-answer any "branch of the first part" spot question, and confusing the subclavian-axillary boundary with the axillary-brachial boundary (which is the lower border of teres major, not the first rib) is a second common error.

## definition
Two separate arterial trees supply most of the neck. The thyrocervical trunk, a first-part branch of the subclavian artery, divides almost immediately into the inferior thyroid artery (which crosses behind the carotid sheath to reach the thyroid gland and lies close to the recurrent laryngeal nerve, the surgical hazard in thyroidectomy), the transverse cervical artery and the suprascapular artery (both crossing the posterior triangle to reach the scapular region). The common carotid artery, on each side, ascends within the carotid sheath alongside the internal jugular vein and vagus nerve without giving a branch in the neck, and bifurcates at the upper border of the thyroid cartilage (roughly the C3/C4 vertebral level) into the internal carotid artery (which gives no branch in the neck) and the external carotid artery, which gives eight named branches, most relevantly here the superior thyroid, lingual, facial and maxillary arteries; the facial artery grooves the submandibular gland, crosses the mandible at the anterior border of masseter, and runs a tortuous course to the medial angle of the eye. The internal jugular vein descends lateral to the common and internal carotid arteries within the same sheath and drains into the brachiocephalic vein behind the sternoclavicular joint.

## explicit_objective
State the branches of the thyrocervical trunk and the external carotid artery, and the level and contents of the carotid bifurcation and carotid sheath.

## pitfalls
Confusing the inferior thyroid artery (from the thyrocervical trunk, subclavian territory) with the superior thyroid artery (from the external carotid, carotid territory) — they are the two ends of the thyroid's dual blood supply, not interchangeable branches of one vessel. A second error is placing a named branch on the internal carotid artery in the neck; it gives none until it enters the cranium.

The posterior cricoarytenoid is the only abductor of the vocal folds; the lateral cricoarytenoid and interarytenoid adduct, and cricothyroid tenses (lengthens) the vocal folds

## canonical_key
larynx.intrinsic-muscle-actions

## aliases
Intrinsic muscles of the larynx

## arabic_label
عضلات الحنجرة الداخلية وأفعالها

## arabic_aliases
العضلة الحلقية الطرجهالية الخلفية
العضلة الحلقية الدرقية

## definition
Five pairs of intrinsic laryngeal muscles (plus the unpaired transverse arytenoid) move the vocal folds and adjust the size of the rima glottidis. The posterior cricoarytenoid is the only abductor — it rotates the arytenoid cartilage to swing the vocal process laterally, opening the airway, and its bilateral paralysis is a surgical emergency because nothing else can open the airway. The lateral cricoarytenoid and the transverse/oblique arytenoids adduct the folds (closing the airway for phonation and to protect it during swallowing). The cricothyroid, the only intrinsic muscle supplied by the external laryngeal nerve rather than the recurrent laryngeal nerve, tilts the cricoid on the thyroid cartilage to lengthen and tense the vocal folds, raising pitch; the thyroarytenoid (vocalis) shortens and relaxes them instead. All other intrinsic muscles are supplied by the recurrent laryngeal nerve.

## explicit_objective
State which intrinsic laryngeal muscle abducts the vocal folds, which one tenses them, and which nerve supplies each.

## pitfalls
Assuming any adductor could substitute for the posterior cricoarytenoid's abductor role — abduction has exactly one muscle, which is why its bilateral nerve injury is uniquely dangerous. A second error is supplying the cricothyroid with the recurrent laryngeal nerve like every other intrinsic muscle; it alone answers to the external laryngeal nerve, which is why a superior laryngeal nerve injury selectively weakens pitch control rather than airway patency.

## canonical_key

## arabic_label

## definition
The laryngeal skeleton is built from the thyroid cartilage (the largest, forming the laryngeal prominence), the cricoid cartilage (the only complete cartilaginous ring in the airway, signet-ring shaped with its broad lamina posteriorly), the paired arytenoid cartilages (pyramidal, sitting on the cricoid lamina and giving attachment to the vocal folds via their vocal processes), and the epiglottis (a leaf-shaped elastic cartilage attached to the back of the thyroid cartilage). The laryngeal inlet, the opening from the pharynx into the larynx, is bounded in front by the epiglottis, on each side by the aryepiglottic fold, and behind by the interarytenoid fold. Inside, the laryngeal cavity is divided by two pairs of folds: the vestibular (false) folds above and the vocal (true) folds below, with the rima glottidis between the true folds and the rima vestibuli between the false ones. Between the two pairs of folds on each side lies the laryngeal ventricle, whose anterior recess extends upward as the saccule; the region above the vestibular folds is the vestibule, and below the vocal folds is the infraglottic (subglottic) cavity, continuous with the trachea.

## explicit_objective
Name the cartilages and folds that bound the laryngeal inlet, and list the three named parts of the laryngeal cavity in order from above down.

## pitfalls
Confusing the aryepiglottic fold (a side boundary of the inlet, running from the epiglottis to the arytenoid) with the vestibular or vocal folds (internal cavity dividers, not inlet boundaries) — they are different structures at different levels. Confusing the laryngeal saccule (a small anterior recess of the ventricle) with the ventricle itself, or with the vestibule (the whole space above the false folds), is a second common error.

## label
A typical rib articulates with the vertebral column at three points: its head with two vertebral demifacets and the intervertebral disc between them, and its tubercle with the transverse process of the lower vertebra, stabilised by the costotransverse ligament

## canonical_key
rib.vertebral-articulations

## aliases

## arabic_label
مفاصل الضلع مع الفقرات

## arabic_aliases
المفصل الضلعي الفقري
المفصل الضلعي المستعرضي

## definition
A typical rib (ribs 2 to 9) makes two synovial joints with the vertebral column. The costovertebral joint, at the rib's head, is a double joint: the head carries two demifacets separated by a crest, articulating with the demifacet of its own numbered vertebra above and the demifacet of the vertebra above that, with the intra-articular ligament of the head anchoring the crest to the intervertebral disc between them. The costotransverse joint, more laterally, is between the rib's tubercle (specifically its articular part) and the facet on the transverse process of the vertebra of the same number, stabilised by three costotransverse ligaments (lateral, superior and the costotransverse ligament proper running from the rib neck to the transverse process). Ribs 1, 10, 11 and 12 are atypical and articulate with only one vertebra each, since their heads carry a single facet rather than two demifacets.

## explicit_objective
State the two joints a typical rib makes with the vertebral column, which vertebral levels each involves, and which ligament stabilises the costotransverse joint.

## pitfalls
Assuming every rib's head meets only "its own" vertebra — a typical rib's head spans two vertebral levels via its two demifacets and the disc between them, which is exactly why a typical rib's number does not correspond to a single vertebral level the way the costotransverse joint does.

## definition
Each brachiocephalic (innominate) vein forms behind its own sternoclavicular joint by the union of the internal jugular vein (draining the head and neck) and the subclavian vein (draining the upper limb) — the left internal jugular plus left subclavian vein for the left brachiocephalic vein, and the corresponding right-sided veins for the right. The left brachiocephalic vein is the longer of the two (about 6 cm, against 2.5 cm on the right) because it must cross obliquely from left to right behind the manubrium, in front of the three great branches of the aortic arch, before joining the right brachiocephalic vein behind the first right costal cartilage to form the superior vena cava. Its tributaries along this course include the left inferior thyroid vein, the left superior intercostal vein, and sometimes the thymic and pericardiacophrenic veins.

## explicit_objective
State which two veins unite to form the left brachiocephalic vein, and where it terminates.

## pitfalls
Selecting the internal thoracic vein, or a same-side jugular-plus-jugular combination, as the pair forming the brachiocephalic vein — the union is always one internal jugular plus one subclavian vein on the same side, never two veins of the same name. A second error is placing the union behind the right sternoclavicular joint for the left vein; each brachiocephalic vein forms on its own side and only meets its partner in the midline, behind the first right costal cartilage.

## definition
The nasal septum divides the nasal cavity into right and left halves and is built from three parts: the perpendicular plate of the ethmoid bone superiorly and posteriorly, the vomer inferiorly and posteriorly, and the septal (quadrangular) cartilage anteriorly, filling the gap between the two bony parts and reaching the nasal bones and anterior nasal spine. ## definition
Each paranasal sinus drains into a specific part of the nasal cavity, and the pattern is a fixed anatomical fact rather than a gravity-dependent one (which is why sinusitis of the maxillary sinus, whose ostium sits high on its medial wall, drains poorly when upright). The frontal sinus drains via the frontonasal duct into the ethmoidal infundibulum and then the hiatus semilunaris, a curved groove on the lateral wall under the middle concha, in the middle meatus; the anterior and middle ethmoidal air cells also open into the middle meatus, the anterior ones via the hiatus semilunaris and the middle ones onto or near the ethmoidal bulla, a rounded bulge above the hiatus; the maxillary sinus opens into the middle meatus as well, through the hiatus semilunaris. The posterior ethmoidal air cells open into the superior meatus. The sphenoidal sinus, sitting in the body of the sphenoid bone, opens not into a meatus at all but into the sphenoethmoidal recess, above and behind the superior concha.

## explicit_objective

## pitfalls
Assuming the maxillary sinus drains into the inferior meatus because it sits low in the face — its actual ostium is high on its medial wall, opening into the middle meatus via the hiatus semilunaris, which is precisely why gravity does not help clear an infected maxillary sinus. Confusing the ethmoidal bulla (a bulge, middle-ethmoidal drainage landmark) with the hiatus semilunaris (a groove, frontal/maxillary/anterior-ethmoidal drainage landmark) is a second common error.

## definition
The bony nasal cavity is bounded on six sides. ## definition
The first rib is short, flat and sharply curved, with a head that articulates with only the body of T1 (it is an atypical rib, with a single facet rather than a demifacet). Its upper surface carries the scalene tubercle, the insertion of scalenus anterior, which divides the surface into two grooves: the subclavian vein groove in front of the tubercle and the subclavian artery groove (shared with the lowest trunk of the brachial plexus) behind it — the artery and the nerve trunk lie together, separated from the vein by the muscle belly, which is the anatomical basis for thoracic outlet syndrome compressing the artery and nerve but sparing the vein when scalenus anterior is the culprit. At the rib's neck, immediately posterior, lies the stellate (cervicothoracic) ganglion, the fused inferior cervical and first thoracic sympathetic ganglia, closely related to the superior intercostal artery. The rib's shaft gives origin to scalenus medius posteriorly and serratus anterior's first digitation, and attaches to Sibson's fascia (suprapleural membrane) at its inner border.

## explicit_objective

## pitfalls
Placing the subclavian vein behind the scalene tubercle with the artery and nerve — the vein crosses scalenus anterior's anterior surface and is the one structure of the three NOT sharing the artery's groove, which is the key discriminator in any first-rib labelling question. ## definition
A typical thoracic vertebra (roughly T2 to T8) has a body that is heart-shaped in cross-section and carries superior and inferior costal demifacets on each side for the corresponding rib heads. Projecting backward from the body, a pair of short, thick pedicles (notched above and below to help form the intervertebral foramina) connect to a pair of laminae, which meet in the midline to complete the vertebral arch and give rise to a long, sharply downward-sloping spinous process — the reason thoracic spinous processes overlap like roof tiles and make palpating an individual thoracic level harder than a lumbar one. At the junction of pedicle and lamina, a transverse process projects laterally and carries, near its tip, a costal facet that articulates with the tubercle of the numerically corresponding rib (the costotransverse joint). Superior and inferior articular processes complete the arch, forming synovial facet joints with the vertebrae above and below.

## explicit_objective

## pitfalls
Placing the rib-tubercle facet on the lamina or the spinous process rather than the transverse process — only the transverse process carries a costal facet, and it is the sole vertebral contribution to the costotransverse joint (the costovertebral joint at the rib head uses the body's demifacets instead). A second error is expecting the pedicle to carry a costal facet; the pedicle's role is structural (forming the intervertebral foramen), not articular.

## definition
A typical rib is a long, flat, twisted bone with a head, neck, tubercle, angle, shaft, and superior and inferior borders. Along the inner aspect of its lower border runs the costal groove, which shelters the main intercostal neurovascular bundle running between the internal and innermost intercostal muscle layers; from above down within the groove the order is Vein, Artery, Nerve (VAN). Because the bundle tucks up under the rib's lower border, a needle or trocar inserted into the intercostal space is placed just above the lower rib's upper border (i.e. in the lower part of the space, away from the groove of the rib above) to avoid it — the anatomical basis for the safe triangle used in thoracocentesis and chest-drain insertion. A collateral (smaller) branch of the neurovascular bundle runs along the upper border of the rib below, which is why even the "safe" lower part of the space is not entirely free of vessels and nerve.

## explicit_objective
State where the costal groove lies on a typical rib, and the order of vein, artery and nerve within it.

## pitfalls
Placing the neurovascular bundle along the rib's UPPER border instead of its lower border — the groove and its main bundle belong to the lower border, which is exactly why a clinician aims a needle at the upper border of the rib below rather than the lower border of the rib above.

## definition
The heart's surface outline on the anterior chest wall is traced by four points: the upper right point at the upper border of the third right costal cartilage, about 1 cm from the sternal edge; the upper left point at the lower border of the second left costal cartilage, about the same distance out; the lower right point at the lower border of the sixth right costal cartilage; and the apex, in the left fifth intercostal space at (or just medial to) the midclavicular line, where the apex beat — the left ventricle's own impulse, the only chamber that reaches the chest wall directly — is normally felt and heard. ## definition
The inferior vena cava (IVC) forms at the level of the fifth lumbar vertebra (L5), behind and to the right of the bifurcation of the aorta, by the union of the two common iliac veins — a union that occurs below and to the right of the arterial bifurcation, so the IVC lies to the right of the aorta throughout the abdomen. It ascends retroperitoneally along the right side of the vertebral column, grooving the posterior surface of the liver, and receives the lumbar veins, right gonadal vein, renal veins, right suprarenal vein and hepatic veins along its course (the left gonadal and left suprarenal veins drain into the left renal vein instead, an asymmetry with the corresponding right-sided vessels draining directly into the IVC). It pierces the central tendon of the diaphragm through the caval opening at the level of T8, the highest and most anterior of the diaphragm's three named openings, before entering the postero-inferior aspect of the right atrium.
