## definition
The femoral sheath is a funnel-shaped downward prolongation of the abdominal fascial layers (fascia transversalis anteriorly, fascia iliaca posteriorly) around the femoral vessels for about 4 cm below the inguinal ligament. It is divided by two vertical septa into three compartments: a lateral compartment for the femoral artery, an intermediate compartment for the femoral vein, and a medial compartment — the femoral canal — which contains only fat and lymphatics (including the node of Cloquet). The femoral canal's upper opening, the femoral ring, is bounded anteriorly by the inguinal ligament, posteriorly by the pectineal ligament, medially by the lacunar ligament, and laterally by the femoral vein's connective-tissue sheath. Because the canal is otherwise empty, it is the weak point through which a loop of bowel or omentum can herniate — a femoral hernia — which appears below and lateral to the pubic tubercle, distinguishing it from an inguinal hernia.

## explicit_objective
State the three compartments of the femoral sheath and what each contains, name the four boundaries of the femoral ring, and explain why the femoral canal (not the artery or vein compartment) is the site of a femoral hernia.

## pitfalls
Treating the femoral sheath and the femoral canal as the same structure. The sheath is the whole three-compartment tube; the canal is only its medial, empty compartment. A student who conflates them will misplace the femoral hernia route, and will also forget that the femoral artery and vein have their own separate compartments and do not herniate.

## definition
The saphenous opening is an oval deficiency in the deep fascia (fascia lata) of the thigh, about 4 cm below and lateral to the pubic tubercle. It is bridged by a sieve-like layer, the cribriform fascia, perforated by the great saphenous vein and by lymphatics draining into the superficial inguinal nodes. Its margins are the falciform margin — a sharp lateral edge continuous with the iliotibial tract — and a rounded medial margin. The great saphenous vein crosses this opening to pierce the cribriform fascia and drain into the femoral vein, the saphenofemoral junction, the point ligated in varicose vein surgery.

## explicit_objective
Locate the saphenous opening relative to the pubic tubercle, name what floors it, and state which vein passes through it to reach the femoral vein.

## pitfalls
Confusing the saphenous opening (a gap in the deep fascia through which a vein passes) with the femoral ring (a gap one layer deeper, through which bowel can herniate). The two sit close together in the same region but are different apertures in different fascial layers, and only the femoral ring is the route of a femoral hernia.

## canonical_key

## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
The femoral nerve arises from the posterior divisions of L2–L4, enters the thigh deep to the inguinal ligament lateral to the femoral sheath, and almost immediately breaks up into anterior and posterior divisions. Its muscular branches supply the quadriceps femoris (rectus femoris, vastus medialis, lateralis and intermedius — knee extensors), sartorius and pectineus. Its cutaneous branches are the anterior cutaneous nerves of the thigh (front of the thigh) and the saphenous nerve, which follows the femoral artery through the adductor canal and continues down the medial side of the leg and foot as the only branch of the lumbar plexus to reach the foot. Quadriceps contraction, tested clinically as the knee (patellar) reflex, is lost with a femoral nerve lesion, along with the ability to extend the knee against resistance.

## explicit_objective
List the muscles the femoral nerve supplies and the movement they produce, name its two cutaneous branches, and state the skin territory each covers.

## pitfalls
Assigning the saphenous nerve's territory (medial leg and foot) to the femoral nerve's muscular distribution, and forgetting that the saphenous nerve is purely cutaneous — it carries no motor fibres, so a femoral nerve injury weakens the quadriceps but any residual sensory loss below the knee is from the saphenous nerve specifically, not "the femoral nerve" as a motor label.

The obturator nerve leaves the pelvis through the obturator foramen, divides into anterior and posterior branches supplying the adductor muscles, and carries sensation from a small patch of the medial thigh

## canonical_key
hip.obturator-nerve-course-distribution

## aliases
Obturator nerve

Posterior division of obturator nerve

## arabic_label
العصب السدادي

## arabic_aliases
الثقبة السدادية

## definition
The obturator nerve arises from the anterior divisions of L2–L4, runs along the lateral pelvic wall and leaves the pelvis through the upper part of the obturator foramen. It divides into an anterior branch (running in front of adductor brevis, supplying gracilis, adductor longus and adductor brevis, and giving a small cutaneous branch to the medial thigh) and a posterior branch (running behind adductor brevis, supplying obturator externus and the adductor part of adductor magnus). Its hamstring part is supplied instead by the tibial division of the sciatic nerve. Sensory loss from an obturator nerve lesion is confined to a small area of skin on the medial thigh; the dominant clinical sign is weak adduction of the hip.

## explicit_objective
Trace the obturator nerve from the pelvis to the medial thigh, name the muscles each of its two divisions supplies, and state its sensory territory.

## pitfalls
Assigning the whole of adductor magnus to the obturator nerve. Adductor magnus has a dual nerve supply: its adductor part follows the obturator nerve (posterior division), but its hamstring part is supplied by the tibial division of the sciatic nerve — the same dual pattern biceps femoris shows in reverse. Missing this means mis-predicting which part of adductor magnus survives an obturator nerve injury.

## definition
The greater sciatic foramen is bounded by the sacrotuberous and sacrospinous ligaments, the greater sciatic notch of the hip bone, and the sacrum, and is subdivided by piriformis (which passes through it) into a compartment above piriformis (superior gluteal vessels and nerve) and a compartment below it (sciatic nerve, inferior gluteal vessels and nerve, pudendal nerve and vessels, nerve to obturator internus, nerve to quadratus femoris, and posterior cutaneous nerve of the thigh). The lesser sciatic foramen, below the ischial spine and bounded by the sacrospinous and sacrotuberous ligaments and the lesser sciatic notch, re-admits the pudendal nerve and internal pudendal vessels into the perineum, and also transmits the tendon of obturator internus and the nerve to it. A structure that leaves the pelvis through the greater foramen and re-enters through the lesser one — the pudendal nerve is the classic example — is easy to place wrongly on a single pass.

## explicit_objective
Name the boundaries of the greater and lesser sciatic foramina, list what passes through each (above and below piriformis for the greater foramen), and identify the one structure common to both.

## pitfalls
Forgetting that the pudendal nerve leaves through the greater sciatic foramen and re-enters the perineum through the lesser one — a student who lists it only once, or only for one foramen, misses the "in through the big door, out through the small one" pattern the bank tests directly.

## label
Gluteus maximus extends and laterally rotates the hip, is supplied by the inferior gluteal nerve, and inserts mostly into the iliotibial tract with only a minority of fibres reaching the gluteal tuberosity

## canonical_key
hip.gluteus-maximus-action-nerve

## aliases

## arabic_label
العضلة الألوية الكبرى

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Gluteus maximus is the largest and most superficial gluteal muscle, arising from the posterior gluteal line of the ilium, the sacrum, coccyx and sacrotuberous ligament, and supplied by the inferior gluteal nerve (L5, S1, S2). It is the chief extensor of the flexed hip (rising from sitting, climbing stairs) and a lateral rotator; it also stabilises the extended knee through its iliotibial tract insertion. Most of its fibres insert into the iliotibial tract; only the deeper fibres reach the gluteal tuberosity of the femur directly — a detail the department's own bank flags with a "take care" note because it is the point most often mis-stated.

## explicit_objective

## definition

## definition

## explicit_objective
Name the three extracapsular ligaments of the hip joint and the direction each limits, state what the ligamentum teres carries, and give the usual direction of a traumatic hip dislocation.

## definition
The hip is a ball-and-socket synovial joint reinforced by three capsular ligaments, all of which spiral so that they wind tighter — and so stabilise the joint — in extension: the iliofemoral ligament (Y-shaped, anteriorly, the strongest ligament in the body, checking hyperextension), the pubofemoral ligament (anteroinferiorly, limiting excessive abduction and extension), and the ischiofemoral ligament (posteriorly, limiting internal rotation and adduction). Inside the joint, the ligamentum teres runs from the fovea of the femoral head to the acetabular notch, carrying a small artery (a branch of obturator artery) that contributes to the femoral head's blood supply, mainly in childhood. Because the bony and ligamentous fit of the hip is so much deeper and stronger than the shoulder's, dislocation is uncommon and requires major trauma; when it happens, it is posterior in roughly 90% of cases (as in a "dashboard injury", the femur driven backward with the hip flexed), because the joint capsule and ligaments are relatively weaker posteroinferiorly than anteriorly.

## pitfalls
Assuming the hip dislocates as easily, and in as many directions, as the shoulder. The hip's deep socket and strong spiral ligaments make dislocation rare and overwhelmingly posterior — a student answering "anterior" by analogy with the (much more mobile, much less stable) shoulder will be caught by this question.

## arabic_label

## definition
As the knee reaches full extension, the medial femoral condyle's larger articular surface makes the femur rotate medially on the tibia (or, in a non-weight-bearing limb, the tibia rotate laterally on the femur) — the "screw-home mechanism" — which tightens the collateral and cruciate ligaments and locks the knee into a stable, low-effort standing position. To begin flexion again, this lock must first be released: popliteus, arising from the lateral femoral condyle and inserting on the posterior tibia, contracts to laterally rotate the femur on the tibia (unlocking it), after which the hamstrings can flex the joint. A popliteus that cannot act therefore leaves a knee that cannot begin to bend from full extension, regardless of hamstring strength.

## explicit_objective
Describe the screw-home locking mechanism of the knee in extension, and explain how popliteus reverses it to permit flexion.

## pitfalls
Assuming any weakness in bending the knee from full extension must be a hamstring problem. If the knee cannot be unlocked in the first place, the hamstrings — however strong — have nothing to flex, because popliteus performs the necessary first step. The bank tests this by asking what fails when a patient cannot "unlock" the knee, not what fails when they cannot flex it further once already bent.

## definition

A sciatic nerve injury spares the medial leg and the medial-malleolus region because that territory is saphenous (femoral-derived), not sciatic.

## definition
Within the femoral triangle, the femoral vein lies medial to the femoral artery at the triangle's base (the reverse of their relation lower down, where the vein comes to lie posterior to the artery in the adductor canal), and receives the great saphenous vein plus several deep tributaries — profunda femoris vein, the medial and lateral circumflex femoral veins, and the deep external pudendal vein. The small (short) saphenous vein is not one of them: it drains into the popliteal vein instead, behind the knee. Profunda femoris, the femoral artery's largest branch, arises just below the inguinal ligament and gives the medial and lateral circumflex femoral arteries (which, though textbooks occasionally show either arising directly from the femoral artery, usually branch from profunda femoris in this bank's convention) before continuing as three or four perforating arteries. As profunda femoris runs posteriorly to reach the back of the thigh, adductor longus crosses in front of it, so the belly of adductor longus is the muscle that separates the femoral artery (which continues anteriorly into the adductor canal) from profunda femoris (which passes deep to adductor longus toward the perforating branches).

## explicit_objective
State which vein the femoral vein's deep tributaries are (and that the small saphenous vein is not one of them), name the usual origin of the medial circumflex femoral artery, and name the muscle that separates the femoral artery from profunda femoris.

## pitfalls
Assuming the small saphenous vein drains into the femoral vein by analogy with the great saphenous vein — it drains into the popliteal vein instead. Assuming the femoral artery and profunda femoris run parallel and separate for their whole course without a specific muscle marking the split — adductor longus is the answer this bank wants. Forgetting that the femoral vein's medial position relative to the artery is specific to the base of the femoral triangle, not a fixed relation throughout the thigh.
