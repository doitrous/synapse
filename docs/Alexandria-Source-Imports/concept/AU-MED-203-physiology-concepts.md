## label
Muscle spindles sit within a gelatinous fluid-filled periaxial space inside their capsule

## canonical_key
physiology.muscle-spindle.periaxial-fluid-filled-capsule

## definition
Each muscle spindle is a fusiform, encapsulated sensory receptor lying parallel to the extrafusal (working) muscle fibers, containing a small number of specialised intrafusal fibers (typically about two nuclear bag fibers and several nuclear chain fibers, not six to eight bag fibers) that carry both sensory (Ia and II afferent) and motor (gamma efferent) innervation — so it is not purely sensory. The intrafusal fibers, together with their sensory and motor endings, sit within a periaxial space filled with a gelatinous fluid inside the spindle's connective-tissue capsule, and the organ signals mainly muscle length and the rate of change of length, not muscle tension (which is the Golgi tendon organ's role).

## explicit_objective
Identify that the muscle spindle's intrafusal fibers lie within a gelatinous fluid-filled periaxial space, distinguishing this from its role (length, not tension) and its dual sensory-plus-motor innervation.

## label
Parkinson disease is associated with dopamine deficiency in the nigrostriatal pathway

## canonical_key
physiology.basal-ganglia.parkinson-dopamine-deficiency

## definition
Parkinson disease results from progressive degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta, which normally project to the striatum via the nigrostriatal pathway. The resulting dopamine deficiency in the basal ganglia circuit underlies the cardinal motor features of Parkinsonism — bradykinesia, rigidity, resting tremor and postural instability — and is the rationale for dopamine-replacement therapy (levodopa). Norepinephrine, serotonin and GABA are also affected in various basal ganglia and brainstem circuits, but the defining, name-giving deficiency of Parkinson disease specifically is dopaminergic.

## explicit_objective

## label
The corticobulbar tract innervates most cranial nerve motor nuclei bilaterally

## canonical_key
physiology.descending-tracts.corticobulbar-bilateral-innervation

## definition
The corticobulbar tract descends from the motor cortex to synapse on brainstem cranial nerve motor nuclei, and — as a teaching generalisation tested against the alternative statements here — is described as bilaterally innervating the cranial nerve nuclei, meaning each nucleus normally receives corticobulbar input from both cerebral hemispheres so a unilateral upper motor neuron lesion spares most cranial-nerve-supplied muscles (the lower face and tongue genioglossus are the clinically important, largely-unilateral exceptions). This is contrasted with the corticospinal tract, where fine, discrete movements (e.g. of the digits) are chiefly mediated by the crossed lateral corticospinal tract, not the small, mostly axial-muscle ventral corticospinal tract, and where pyramidal-tract conduction is fast and direct rather than delayed.

## explicit_objective
State the general rule that corticobulbar fibers supply cranial nerve motor nuclei bilaterally, as the basis for upper motor neuron lesions largely sparing cranial musculature.

## label
The crossed extensor reflex pairs ipsilateral withdrawal with contralateral limb extension

## canonical_key
physiology.spinal-reflexes.crossed-extensor-reflex

## definition
The crossed extensor reflex is a polysynaptic spinal reflex triggered by a painful (nociceptive) stimulus to a limb: on the stimulated side, flexor muscles contract to withdraw the limb (the flexor withdrawal reflex), while simultaneously, via interneurons crossing the spinal cord, the contralateral limb's extensor muscles contract and its flexors relax, extending that limb to support the body's weight as the stimulated limb lifts away. This is distinct from simple reciprocal inhibition (antagonist relaxation on the same side of one joint) and from autogenic inhibition (Golgi tendon organ-mediated relaxation of the same muscle under excess tension).

## explicit_objective
Describe the crossed extensor reflex as contralateral limb extension accompanying ipsilateral withdrawal from a painful stimulus.

The superior olivary nucleus computes the direction a sound originates from

## canonical_key
physiology.auditory-system.superior-olivary-nucleus-sound-localization

## definition
The superior olivary complex, in the caudal pons, is the first site in the ascending auditory pathway where inputs from both cochlear nuclei (both ears) converge on the same neurons, allowing it to compute interaural time differences and interaural intensity differences and so determine the horizontal direction a sound is coming from. The cochlear nucleus and inferior colliculus process and relay auditory information but do not themselves perform this first binaural comparison, and the inferior olivary nucleus is an unrelated medullary structure that projects climbing fibers to the cerebellum, not an auditory relay at all.

Retinal ganglion cells are the retinal neurons that generate action potentials

## canonical_key
physiology.retina.ganglion-cells-generate-action-potentials

## definition
Photoreceptors, bipolar cells and horizontal cells in the retina communicate almost entirely by graded (analogue) potentials, releasing neurotransmitter in proportion to membrane potential rather than firing all-or-none spikes, because the distances involved are short. Retinal ganglion cells are the exception: they integrate this graded input and generate true action potentials, which then propagate the length of the optic nerve axon to the brain — the first true spiking neurons in the visual pathway.

Barbiturates potentiate GABA-mediated inhibitory neurotransmission

## canonical_key
pharmacology.cns-depressants.barbiturates-potentiate-gaba

## definition
GABA is the principal inhibitory neurotransmitter of the brain, acting on GABA-A receptors to open chloride channels and hyperpolarise neurons. Barbiturates bind an allosteric site on the GABA-A receptor and potentiate its action by prolonging the duration that the chloride channel stays open in the presence of GABA, enhancing inhibitory neurotransmission; at higher concentrations barbiturates can also directly open the channel independent of GABA, which underlies both their sedative-hypnotic/anticonvulsant efficacy and their narrow therapeutic index (risk of fatal respiratory depression in overdose).
