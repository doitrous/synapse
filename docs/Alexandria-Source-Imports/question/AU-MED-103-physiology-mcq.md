## title
What is the role of vitamin K?

## question
What is the role of vitamin K?

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Import queue

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Synthesis of some coagulation factors

## explanation_a
Correct. Vitamin K is a cofactor for the hepatic enzyme that carboxylates glutamic acid residues on clotting factors II, VII, IX and X — a modification these factors need to bind calcium and function in the cascade. Without it, the liver still makes the factors but secretes them in an inactive, uncarboxylated form, which is why a deficiency (or its pharmacological antagonism by warfarin) prolongs coagulation time and the PT/INR. The point to remember: vitamin K is a synthesis cofactor, not a factor itself, and its effect is reversible by repletion.

## answer_b
Activate platelets aggregation

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Platelet aggregation is driven by mediators such as thromboxane A2 and platelet-activating factor, released from the platelets themselves once activated by exposed collagen or vWF — not by vitamin K.

## answer_c
Participate in methylation of homocysteine to methionine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This describes vitamin B12's role in one-carbon metabolism, not vitamin K's role in coagulation.

## answer_d
Vasoconstriction of the damaged vessel

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Vasoconstriction is an immediate, local smooth-muscle response to vessel injury (via serotonin and thromboxane A2 released from platelets), not a function of vitamin K.

## question
Which immunoglobulin shares in hypersensitivity reactions?

## subject

## status

## vignette

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. IgM is the first antibody produced in a primary immune response and a potent complement activator, but it is not the class bound to mast cells and basophils that mediates immediate hypersensitivity.

## answer_b
IgG

## explanation_b
Incorrect. IgG is the dominant serum immunoglobulin and crosses the placenta, but immediate hypersensitivity is not mediated through IgG.

## answer_c
IgE

## explanation_c
Correct. IgE binds high-affinity receptors on mast cells and basophils; when an allergen cross-links IgE already bound to these cells, it triggers degranulation and the release of histamine and other mediators within minutes — the mechanism of immediate (type I) hypersensitivity such as urticaria, allergic rhinitis and anaphylaxis. IgE circulates at the lowest concentration of any immunoglobulin class, which is easy to mistake for unimportance, but its role is defined by where it is bound (mast cells, basophils), not by how much of it is in the serum.

## answer_d
IgA

## explanation_d
Incorrect. IgA is the class that predominates in external secretions (saliva, tears, mucosal surfaces), defending the point of entry — a different role from mediating an allergic reaction.

## question
What causes anaemia in renal failure?

## subject

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Folic acid deficiency causes a macrocytic anaemia through impaired DNA synthesis, unrelated to renal failure's own mechanism.

## answer_b
Vit B12 deficiency

## explanation_b
Incorrect. B12 deficiency also causes macrocytic anaemia, but through impaired absorption (intrinsic factor, ileal disease), not through renal failure.

## answer_c

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Iron deficiency causes microcytic hypochromic anaemia through impaired hemoglobin synthesis, not through loss of renal function.

This is the anaemia of chronic kidney disease, and it is why erythropoietin-stimulating agents (not iron or vitamins alone) are the specific treatment.

## title
Rh-negative mother, Rh-positive husband, history of abortion — what may happen to the current pregnancy?

## question
A 25-year old woman, pregnant in her 7th month, is Rh negative and her husband is Rh positive. She had an abortion in an earlier pregnancy. What do you expect may happen to this baby?

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Import queue

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
May suffer from haemolytic anaemia of the newborn

## explanation_a
Correct. An Rh-negative mother carrying an Rh-positive fetus can become sensitised against the Rh(D) antigen when fetal cells cross into her circulation — commonly at a prior delivery or, as here, a prior abortion. In a subsequent pregnancy with another Rh-positive fetus, her anti-D antibodies cross the placenta and destroy fetal red cells, producing haemolytic disease of the newborn (erythroblastosis fetalis). This is exactly the scenario a missed anti-D dose after the earlier abortion would have prevented.

## answer_b
May be born safely if that mother had a blood transfusion of O negative blood

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A blood transfusion of O-negative blood to the mother does nothing to prevent or reverse Rh sensitisation — the relevant intervention is anti-D immunoglobulin, not a red-cell transfusion.

## answer_c
May be born safely if the mother has taken D antigen after abortion

## explanation_c
Incorrect, and a dangerous reversal of the correct logic. Giving the Rh(D) antigen itself would sensitise the mother, not protect her — the correct prophylaxis is the anti-D antibody, given to clear fetal cells before they can trigger sensitisation.

## answer_d
This pregnancy carries no additional risk beyond a normal pregnancy

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A prior sensitising event (the earlier abortion, in an Rh-negative mother with an Rh-positive partner) is exactly what raises the risk of this pregnancy above baseline — the scenario describes the classic risk factors for Rh isoimmunisation, not a risk-free pregnancy.

## question
What is responsible for blood fluidity?

## subject
haem

## vignette

## answer_a
Heparin and antithrombin 3

## explanation_a
Correct. Heparin, a naturally occurring anticoagulant made by mast cells and basophils, facilitates antithrombin III's inactivation of activated clotting factors (IXa, Xa, XIa, XIIa). Absence of plasminogen

Slow blood flow

## explanation_c
Incorrect, and backwards. Slow or stagnant blood flow is a risk factor for thrombosis (one arm of Virchow's triad), not a mechanism that keeps blood fluid.

## answer_d
Rough vascular system

## explanation_d
Incorrect, and backwards. A rough (damaged) vessel surface exposes collagen and tissue factor, promoting clot formation — an intact, smooth endothelium is what supports fluidity.
