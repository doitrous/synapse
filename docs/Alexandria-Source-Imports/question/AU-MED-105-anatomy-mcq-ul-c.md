## title
The most commonly fractured carpal bone

## question
The most carpal bone to be fractured is:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Pisiform.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The pisiform is a sesamoid bone within flexor carpi ulnaris, not the carpal bone fractured most often.

## answer_b
Hamate.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The hamate can fracture (classically its hook, in racquet-sport injuries) but far less often than the scaphoid.

## answer_c
Triquetral.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The triquetral is an uncommon fracture site compared with the scaphoid.

## answer_d
Scaphoid.

## explanation_d
Correct. The scaphoid bridges the proximal and distal carpal rows and is fractured far more often than any other carpal bone, classically by a fall onto an outstretched, dorsiflexed hand that loads it against the radius. Because its blood supply enters mainly at its distal pole and runs retrograde, a fracture through the waist or proximal third can starve the proximal fragment and cause avascular necrosis and non-union. This is why snuff-box tenderness after a fall is treated as a probable scaphoid fracture and re-imaged even when the first film looks normal.

## title
The carpal sesamoid bone

## question
Which of the following bone is considered a sesamoid bone (embedded within a muscle)?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Pisiform.

## explanation_a
Correct. The pisiform is the wrist's one sesamoid bone: rather than ossifying as part of the true carpal row, it develops within the tendon of flexor carpi ulnaris, which is what a sesamoid bone is by definition — a bone embedded in a tendon or muscle. It sits on the anterior surface of the triquetral and forms the medial attachment of the flexor retinaculum. Because it is a sesamoid rather than a true carpal bone, it is the exception when the eight carpal bones are counted as forming two rows of true bones.

## answer_b
Hamate.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The hamate is a true carpal bone of the distal row, not a sesamoid.

## answer_c
Triquetral.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The triquetral is a true carpal bone of the proximal row, not a sesamoid.

## answer_d
Scaphoid.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The scaphoid is a true carpal bone of the proximal row, not a sesamoid.

## title
Nerve supply to the lateral half of flexor digitorum profundus

## question
lateral part of flexor digitorum profundus muscle is supplied by:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
ulnar nerve

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The ulnar nerve supplies the medial half of flexor digitorum profundus, not the lateral half.

## answer_b
radial nerve

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The radial nerve supplies the extensor, not the flexor, compartment of the forearm.

## answer_c
posterior interosseous nerve

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The posterior interosseous nerve is the radial nerve's deep branch to the extensor compartment, not the flexors.

## answer_d
anterior interosseous nerve

## explanation_d
Correct. Flexor digitorum profundus is unusual among the forearm flexors in having a split nerve supply: its lateral (radial) half, which flexes the index and middle fingers, is supplied by the anterior interosseous nerve, while its medial (ulnar) half, to the ring and little fingers, is supplied by the ulnar nerve. This split explains why an isolated anterior interosseous nerve lesion weakens flexion at the index finger's distal interphalangeal joint but spares the little finger. It is the same lateral/medial split rule that governs pronator teres's own exception in the anterior compartment.

## title

## question
The following muscles are supplied by the anterior interosseous nerve EXCEPT:

msk

## status

## title
A correct statement about pronator teres

## question
Regarding pronator teres which of the following statements is correct?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
It forms the lateral border of the cubital fossa

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Brachioradialis, not pronator teres, forms the lateral border of the cubital fossa; pronator teres forms the medial border.

## answer_b
It is supplied by median nerve

## explanation_b
Correct. Pronator teres is supplied by the median nerve, which passes between its two heads — a larger humeral head from the medial epicondyle and a smaller ulnar head from the coronoid process — to enter the forearm, making pronator teres itself a classic median-nerve entrapment site (pronator syndrome). It forms the medial, not lateral, border of the cubital fossa (brachioradialis forms the lateral border), and it belongs to the superficial, not deep, layer of the anterior compartment. The median nerve passes between the two heads rather than deep to both of them.

## answer_c
The Median nerve passes deep to both heads

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The median nerve passes between pronator teres's two heads, not deep to both of them.

## answer_d
It is one of the deep flexors of the forearm

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Pronator teres is one of the superficial flexors of the forearm, not a deep flexor.

## title
Scaphoid fracture: the artery at risk

## question
A 14-year-old boy falls on his hand and has a fracture of the scaphoid bone. which of the following arteries may be affected?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Brachial artery

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The brachial artery lies in the arm and cubital fossa, far proximal to the scaphoid, and is not at risk from a wrist-level fracture.

## answer_b
Ulnar artery

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The ulnar artery runs medially through Guyon's canal, away from the scaphoid, which lies on the lateral (radial) side of the carpus.

## answer_c
Deep palmar arterial arch

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The deep palmar arch lies in the palm, distal to the scaphoid, and is not directly related to the bone's fracture site.

## answer_d
Radial artery

## explanation_d
Correct. The radial artery crosses directly over the scaphoid as it runs through the floor of the anatomical snuff box, so a scaphoid fracture — the classic fall-on-outstretched-hand injury — can also injure this segment of the artery, on top of the bone's own well-known risk of avascular necrosis from disruption of its distally-entering blood supply. This dual risk (arterial injury and bone necrosis) is why scaphoid fractures are managed cautiously even when they look undisplaced.

## answer_e
Princeps pollicis artery

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Princeps pollicis is a small terminal branch to the thumb, distal to and not directly related to the scaphoid.

## title
Anterior interosseous nerve lesion: muscles paralyzed

## question
Examination indicates a lesion of the anterior interosseous nerve. Which of the following muscles are paralyzed?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Flexor pollicis longus and brevis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Flexor pollicis brevis is a thenar muscle supplied by the median nerve's recurrent branch, not the anterior interosseous nerve.

## answer_b
Flexor pollicis longus and opponens pollicis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Opponens pollicis is a thenar muscle supplied by the median nerve's recurrent branch, not the anterior interosseous nerve.

## answer_c
Flexor digitorum profundus and pronator quadratus

## explanation_c
Correct. The anterior interosseous nerve supplies three muscles: flexor pollicis longus, the lateral (radial) half of flexor digitorum profundus, and pronator quadratus, so a lesion paralyses the lateral half of flexor digitorum profundus and pronator quadratus together with flexor pollicis longus. This is tested by the "OK sign": asked to pinch thumb to index fingertip, the patient cannot flex either interphalangeal joint fully and instead makes a triangular pinch, with no accompanying sensory loss since the nerve is purely motor.

## answer_d
Flexor digitorum profundus and superficialis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Flexor digitorum superficialis is supplied by the median nerve proper, not the anterior interosseous nerve; only flexor digitorum profundus (its lateral half) is anterior-interosseous territory.
