# Item

## id
QST-AUMED106PHYS-RESP-RESP-Q01

## title
PFT pattern in silicosis

## question
A 67-year-old, 76 kg man is evaluated for pulmonary complications of silicosis. Spirometry shows a reduced total lung capacity and vital capacity, while his FEV1/FVC ratio is preserved (even slightly increased). Which spirometric pattern does this represent?

## subject
resp

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Obstructive lung disease

## explanation_a
Incorrect. An obstructive pattern (e.g. bronchial asthma, emphysema, COPD) is defined by a LOW FEV1/FVC ratio because airway narrowing slows expiratory flow disproportionately to the volume expired. This patient's ratio is preserved, which is the opposite of the obstructive signature. Silicosis is a classic restrictive, not obstructive, occupational lung disease.

## answer_b
Restrictive lung disease

## explanation_b
Correct. Silicosis stiffens the lung parenchyma, so total lung capacity and vital capacity both fall — the restrictive pattern. Because both FEV1 and FVC fall together in restriction, the FEV1/FVC ratio stays preserved or is even slightly increased, which is exactly the finding described. The single number that separates restriction from obstruction is the FEV1/FVC ratio, not the absolute volumes, and here it points squarely to restriction.

## answer_c
Mixed obstructive-restrictive disease

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A mixed pattern would require evidence of both a reduced FEV1/FVC ratio (the obstructive component) and reduced lung volumes (the restrictive component) occurring together. This case shows only reduced volumes with a preserved ratio, which is a pure restrictive picture with no obstructive signature at all.

## answer_d
Normal spirometry for his age

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Reduced TLC and VC are, by definition, abnormal spirometric findings, not a normal-for-age variant. Age-related lung changes do occur but are far smaller in magnitude than the changes described here, and the clinical context (silicosis, an occupational fibrotic lung disease) makes a pathological restrictive process the expected explanation.

## title
RV/TLC% direction in restrictive disease

## question
In the same silicosis patient (restrictive pattern, reduced TLC and VC with a preserved FEV1/FVC ratio), what happens to his RV/TLC%?

## subject
resp

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
It stays normal

## explanation_a
Correct. In restrictive disease the whole lung shrinks roughly proportionally — both residual volume and total lung capacity fall together — so the RV/TLC% ratio is preserved even though both individual volumes are reduced in absolute terms. This is the mechanistic reason RV/TLC% cannot be used on its own to diagnose restriction; the ratio looks unremarkable even in significant disease.

## answer_b
It is markedly decreased

## explanation_b
Incorrect. RV/TLC% falling would require residual volume to drop faster than total lung capacity. In restriction the two fall together proportionally, so the ratio is preserved rather than decreased; a marked fall is not the pattern silicosis produces.

## answer_c
It is markedly increased

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A rise in RV/TLC% is the obstructive signature, caused by air trapping — airway collapse lets residual volume rise disproportionately to total lung capacity. Silicosis is a restrictive, not obstructive, process, so this direction of change does not apply here.

## answer_d
It cannot move independently of TLC

## explanation_d
Incorrect. RV and TLC are related but not rigidly locked together; it is precisely their relationship (proportional fall in restriction, disproportionate RV rise in obstruction from air trapping) that makes RV/TLC% a useful discriminating measurement between the two disease patterns, not an automatically fixed number.

## title
Bohr's method and CO2

## question
Which of the following dead-space measurement methods uses expired CO2 as its measuring principle?

## subject
resp

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Closed-circuit (helium-dilution) method

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The closed-circuit helium-dilution method measures functional residual capacity, not dead space, and uses helium (an inert, non-absorbed gas) rather than CO2 as its tracer. It answers a different physiological question from the dead-space methods.

## answer_b
Fowler's method

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Fowler's method measures anatomical dead space using a single-breath nitrogen-washout curve, not CO2. The subject inspires 100% oxygen and the analyser tracks expired nitrogen, which is the opposite gas principle from Bohr's CO2-based method.

## answer_c
Auscultatory blood pressure measurement

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Auscultatory blood pressure measurement uses Korotkoff sounds heard through a stethoscope as a cuff deflates; it has no relationship to expired gas analysis or dead-space measurement at all, and is listed here only as a distractor unrelated to respiratory physiology methods.

## answer_d
Bohr's method

## explanation_d
Correct. Bohr's method measures physiological dead space using the CO2-dilution principle: only alveolar gas exchanges CO2 with pulmonary capillary blood, so comparing mixed-expired CO2 against arterial or end-tidal CO2 yields the fraction of each breath that is dead space. Because it uses CO2, Bohr's method captures physiological dead space (anatomical plus any alveolar dead space from underperfused alveoli), unlike Fowler's N2-based method.

## title
Fowler's method and anatomical dead space

## question
A subject inspires a single breath of 100% oxygen, then exhales while a rapid nitrogen analyser plots expired N2 concentration against expired volume. This single-breath nitrogen-washout curve is used to measure:

## subject
resp

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Physiologic dead space

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Physiological dead space (anatomical plus any alveolar dead space from ventilated-but-underperfused alveoli) is what Bohr's CO2-based method measures, not Fowler's N2-washout curve. Fowler's method cannot detect alveolar dead space at all, so it does not capture the full physiological dead space.

## answer_b
Alveolar dead space

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Alveolar dead space specifically (ventilated alveoli that are poorly perfused) requires comparing CO2 in arterial blood against expired gas, which is Bohr's method's domain. The single-breath N2-washout curve has no way to distinguish perfused from underperfused alveoli, so it cannot isolate alveolar dead space.

## answer_c
Residual volume

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Residual volume is the air remaining in the lungs after a maximal expiration and is measured by helium dilution, nitrogen washout to equilibrium, or body plethysmography — a different measurement entirely from reading a single-breath transition curve for dead space.

## answer_d
Anatomical dead space

## explanation_d
Correct. Fowler's method measures anatomical dead space: because the first gas expired comes purely from the conducting airways (which never mixed with the inspired 100% O2), expired N2 concentration is near zero at first, rises sharply through a mixing transition, then plateaus at the alveolar level. Anatomical dead space is read as the volume expired up to the midpoint of that rapidly-rising transition phase.

## title
Inspiratory capacity definition

## question
Which lung capacity is defined as the maximal volume that can be inspired starting from the resting expiratory level (the end of a normal, quiet expiration)?

## subject
resp

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Vital capacity

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Vital capacity is the maximal volume expired after a maximal inspiration — the sum of inspiratory reserve volume, tidal volume and expiratory reserve volume. It is measured from the top of a full inspiration downward, not from the resting expiratory level upward, so it does not match this definition.

## answer_b
Inspiratory capacity

## explanation_b
Correct. Inspiratory capacity is the maximal volume that can be inspired starting from the resting expiratory level, and equals tidal volume plus inspiratory reserve volume (IC = TV + IRV). It represents how much further a person can breathe in from a normal quiet breath's end-point, which is exactly the definition given.

## answer_c
Expiratory reserve volume

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Expiratory reserve volume is the volume that can still be expired after a normal, quiet expiration — the mirror-image measurement taken in the opposite direction (further exhalation, not inhalation) from the same resting expiratory level.

## answer_d
Tidal volume

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Tidal volume is simply the volume moved in a single normal breath at rest; it is a component that sums into inspiratory capacity (along with inspiratory reserve volume) but is not itself the maximal inspiratory volume being described.

## title
Determinants of anatomical dead space

## question
Which of the following increases anatomical dead space?

## subject
resp

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Bronchoconstrictor drugs

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Bronchoconstrictor drugs narrow the airways, the opposite direction from what widens anatomical dead space. Narrowing the conducting airways does not add volume to the fixed anatomical dead space; if anything, severe bronchoconstriction changes dynamic air trapping by a different mechanism entirely, not dead space itself.

## answer_b
Bronchodilator drugs

## explanation_b
Correct. Bronchodilator drugs widen the conducting airways, so more of each breath's volume sits in wider tubes before reaching the alveoli — this directly enlarges anatomical dead space. Anything that widens or lengthens the conducting airways (bronchodilation, larger body size, increasing age) increases anatomical dead space by the same underlying mechanism.

## answer_c
A smaller body/trunk size

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A larger, not smaller, body or trunk size increases anatomical dead space, because a bigger person has longer conducting airways carrying a proportionally larger fixed volume. A smaller body size would tend to reduce anatomical dead space, the opposite of what the question asks.

## answer_d
Younger age

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Anatomical dead space increases with advancing age, not with younger age — from progressive loss of elastic recoil and mild airway dilation over the lifespan. A younger person typically has a smaller, not larger, anatomical dead space relative to an older adult.

## title
Breathing reserve as a fitness indicator

## question
Which of the following pulmonary function measures is the best indicator of physical fitness?

## subject
resp

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
FEV1

## explanation_a
Incorrect. FEV1 is a static, single-breath measurement of expiratory flow and does not directly compare ventilatory capacity against what is actually used during maximal exercise. It says nothing about how much ventilatory reserve remains during exertion, which is what a fitness indicator needs to capture.

## answer_b
Breathing reserve

## explanation_b
Correct. Breathing reserve is the difference between maximal voluntary ventilation and the minute ventilation actually achieved during maximal exercise, expressed as a percentage of MVV. A large reserve at maximal exertion means the cardiovascular system, not ventilatory capacity, is the limiting factor — the normal, fit pattern — making breathing reserve a direct functional indicator of fitness.

## answer_c
Vital capacity

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Vital capacity is a static resting lung volume; it reflects how much air can be moved in a single maximal breath but does not by itself predict how much ventilatory capacity is left in reserve during exercise, since a normal resting VC does not guarantee a large breathing reserve.

## answer_d
Minute respiratory volume

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Minute respiratory volume (tidal volume times respiratory rate) describes ventilation at a given moment, whether at rest or during exercise, but on its own does not compare that ventilation against the person's maximal capacity — the comparison that breathing reserve specifically provides.

## title
Identifying an MVV tracing

## question
A volume-time tracing shows rapid, large-amplitude breathing sustained throughout the entire recorded interval — visually distinct from normal tidal breathing or a single forced-expiration sweep. This curve represents:

## subject
resp

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Minute respiratory volume (MRV)

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Minute respiratory volume is a single calculated number (tidal volume times respiratory rate) describing normal resting ventilation; it is not itself a distinctive sustained-oscillation curve shape, and a tracing of quiet tidal breathing looks nothing like the rapid, large-amplitude pattern described.

## answer_b
Maximal voluntary ventilation (MVV)

## explanation_b
Correct. Maximal voluntary ventilation is measured by having the subject breathe as fast and as deeply as possible for a short timed interval (typically 12-15 seconds). On a volume-time tracing this produces exactly the distinctive shape described: rapid, large-amplitude oscillations sustained across the whole recorded interval, unlike a single forced-expiration sweep or normal tidal breathing.

## answer_c
Forced expiratory volume (FEV)

## explanation_c
Incorrect. FEV (forced expiratory volume) is read from a single forced-expiration curve — one large downward sweep followed by a plateau — not a sustained oscillating pattern. A single-breath curve and a sustained multi-breath tracing are visually unmistakable from one another once the shapes are named.

## answer_d
Forced vital capacity (FVC)

## explanation_d
Incorrect. FVC, like FEV1, comes from a single maximal inspiration followed by one forced expiration, producing one downward sweep rather than repeated rapid oscillations. This is the same single-breath curve shape that distinguishes it from the MVV tracing described in the stem.

## title
Worked minute respiratory volume calculation

## question
A patient has a tidal volume of 2.8 L and a respiratory rate of 23 breaths per minute. What is the minute respiratory volume (MRV)?

## subject
resp

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
6.4 L/min

## explanation_a
Incorrect. 6.4 L/min would be a plausible normal resting minute ventilation, but it does not match the arithmetic of the values given. Minute respiratory volume is calculated as tidal volume multiplied by respiratory rate, not divided or otherwise combined, so this figure does not follow from 2.8 L x 23/min.

## answer_b
25.8 L/min

## explanation_b
Incorrect. 25.8 L/min does not equal 2.8 x 23; it looks like it might result from adding related quantities rather than multiplying them, which is not how minute respiratory volume is calculated. The correct operation is a straightforward multiplication of tidal volume by respiratory rate.

## answer_c
64.4 L/min

## explanation_c
Correct. Minute respiratory volume equals tidal volume multiplied by respiratory rate: MRV = VT x RR = 2.8 L x 23/min = 64.4 L/min. This worked example's tidal volume is unusually large for a typical resting adult (normal resting VT is closer to 0.5 L), but the arithmetic being tested — applying the MRV = VT x RR formula correctly — is unaffected by whether the example numbers are themselves typical.

## answer_d
80.4 L/min

## explanation_d
Incorrect. 80.4 L/min overshoots the correct product of 2.8 x 23. Applying the MRV formula precisely (2.8 multiplied by 23) gives 64.4 L/min, not this larger figure, which appears to result from a rounding or multiplication error rather than the correct calculation.

## title
Closed-circuit helium-dilution FRC formula

## question
The closed-circuit helium-dilution method calculates functional residual capacity (FRC) from an initial helium volume (V1) and the helium concentrations before (C1) and after (C2) equilibration. Which formula does this bank's answer key give?

## subject
resp

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
FRC = V1 x (C1 - C2) / C2

## explanation_a
Correct. The bank's answer key gives the closed-circuit formula as FRC = V1 x (C1 - C2) / C2, which follows from conservation of helium: the same total amount of helium (V1 x C1) is now diluted into a larger volume (the spirometer's known volume plus the patient's unknown FRC) at the lower equilibrated concentration C2. Rearranging that mass-balance relationship isolates the unknown FRC term and yields exactly this expression. This same formula and worked figure are also what the concept record's own p8/p20 source citations use to identify the closed-circuit method by name.

## answer_b
FRC = V1 x (C1 + C2) / C2

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Adding C1 and C2 rather than subtracting them does not follow from a mass-conservation argument for a diluted, non-reacting tracer gas like helium; the correct relationship depends on how much the concentration fell (C1 minus C2), which reflects how much extra volume (the patient's FRC) diluted it.

## answer_c
FRC = V1 x C1 / C2

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This simplified form omits the drop in concentration entirely and would not correctly back out the added (FRC) volume from the known starting volume and concentration; the dilution mathematics specifically requires the difference C1 - C2, not C1 alone, in the numerator.

## answer_d
FRC = V1 - (C1 - C2) / C2

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Subtracting a ratio from V1, rather than multiplying V1 by the ratio, does not match the dimensional or conservation logic of gas dilution — the FRC term must scale with V1 (a larger known starting volume implies a larger unknown FRC for a given concentration drop), which only the multiplicative form captures correctly.
