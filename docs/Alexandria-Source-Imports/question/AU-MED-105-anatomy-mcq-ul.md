## title
True statement about the clavicle

## question
Regarding the clavicle, which is true?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Trapezius muscle is attached to its medial 2/3

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Trapezius attaches to the posterior border of the clavicle's lateral one-third, not its medial two-thirds.

## answer_b
Pectoralis major is attached to its medial half

## explanation_b
Correct. Pectoralis major's clavicular head attaches along the anterior border and superior surface of roughly the medial half of the clavicle. This is the mirror image of trapezius's lateral-third attachment on the posterior border. It is one of the two large muscles that anchor onto the shaft itself rather than an end or a ligament.

## answer_c
When fractured, its lateral third is elevated by sternomastoid muscle

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Sternomastoid attaches to the medial fragment, which it pulls upward — the lateral fragment is the one displaced, and it moves downward under the limb's weight, not upward.

## answer_d
It articulates with the coracoid process of the scapula

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The clavicle articulates only with the manubrium sterni and the acromion; the coracoid process is tethered to it only indirectly by the coracoclavicular ligament, not a true joint.

## title
The earliest bone to ossify

## question
The earliest bone to ossify is the:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Radius

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The radius begins ossifying later, in the diaphysis around the eighth embryonic week.

## answer_b
Ulna

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The ulna's diaphysis begins ossifying at a similar, later stage than the clavicle.

## answer_c
Clavicle

## explanation_c
Correct. The clavicle is the first bone in the body to begin ossifying, intramembranously, in the sixth embryonic week — even though, paradoxically, its medial epiphysis is the last of any bone to fuse, not completing until the mid-20s. This makes the clavicle a useful marker in forensic and developmental age estimation at both ends of life. Its early, intramembranous start (rather than the cartilage-model route most long bones use) is the specific fact this item tests.

## answer_d
Humerus

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The humerus begins ossifying later than the clavicle and by the more typical cartilage-model (endochondral) route.

Displacement of the lateral fragment in a clavicle fracture

## question
A 37-year-old female patient has a fracture of the clavicle at the junction between its medial 2/3 and lateral 1/3. The lateral portion of the fractured clavicle is displaced downward by which of the following?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Weight of the arm

## explanation_a
Correct. The lateral fragment carries the weight of the entire upper limb hanging from it, and this weight is what pulls it downward, forward and medially; pectoralis major assists by pulling it medially, but the dominant force named in this option — the limb's own weight — is the one the bank's key credits. Trapezius remains attached to the lateral fragment but is too weak against that dead weight to hold it up, which is exactly why the shoulder drops rather than staying level.

## answer_b

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Deltoid arises from the lateral fragment itself and does not act to displace it; it is pectoralis major, not deltoid, that contributes an additional medializing pull.

## answer_c
Pectoralis minor muscle and gravity

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Pectoralis minor attaches to the coracoid process of the scapula, not to the clavicle, and plays no part in clavicular fragment displacement.

## answer_d
Trapezius and pectoralis minor muscles

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Trapezius is attached to the lateral fragment but is too weak to prevent — let alone cause — its downward drop; pectoralis minor is not attached to the clavicle at all.

## title
Artery injured with a bleeding surgical-neck fracture

## question
A 10-year-old boy falls off his bike and has difficulty moving his shoulder. His radiograph and angiograph reveal a fracture of the surgical neck of the humerus and bleeding from the point of the fracture. This accident most likely leads to damage to which artery?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Axillary artery

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The axillary artery lies well proximal to the surgical neck, ending at the lower border of teres major before the arm even begins.

## answer_b
Deep (profunda) brachial artery

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The profunda brachii artery runs in the spiral groove of the humeral shaft with the radial nerve, a more distal and posterior relation than the surgical neck.

## answer_c
Posterior circumflex humeral artery

## explanation_c
Correct. The posterior circumflex humeral artery winds around the surgical neck of the humerus together with the axillary nerve, passing through the quadrangular space, which is exactly why a surgical-neck fracture at this level threatens both structures together. The anterior circumflex humeral artery also crosses the surgical neck anteriorly, but the angiographic bleeding point described here, at the point of fracture, is classically attributed to the posterior vessel travelling with the nerve. This pairing of the axillary nerve and posterior circumflex humeral artery at the surgical neck is a frequently tested clinical correlation.

## answer_d
Superior ulnar collateral artery

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The superior ulnar collateral artery runs with the ulnar nerve much further distally, around the elbow, not at the surgical neck of the humerus.
