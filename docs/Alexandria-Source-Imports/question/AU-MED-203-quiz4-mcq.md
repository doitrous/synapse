## title
The exception among neural crest derivatives

## question
Which of the following isn't derived from the neural crest?

## subject

## status

A

## answer_a
Oligodendrocytes

## explanation_a
Correct. Oligodendrocytes, the CNS myelinating glia, arise from the neuroepithelium of the neural tube, not the neural crest. The exception among these four options.

Pia mater

Mesenchyme of pharyngeal arches

## explanation_d
This IS a neural-crest derivative, so it is not the answer: pharyngeal-arch ectomesenchyme (cartilage, bone, connective tissue of the head and neck) derives substantially from neural crest cells.

## title
The true statement about primary motor and sensory cortex

## question
Regarding the cortical areas of the cerebral hemisphere, which of the following is true?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
The premotor area is present in the postcentral gyrus

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The premotor area lies anterior to the precentral (primary motor) gyrus, in the frontal lobe — not in the postcentral (primary sensory) gyrus.

## answer_b
Astereognosis occurs due to injury of the primary auditory area

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Astereognosis results from a primary SOMATOSENSORY area (S1, postcentral gyrus) lesion, not the primary auditory area, which instead relates to hearing.

## answer_c
Broca's area is the sensory speech area

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Broca's area (inferior frontal gyrus) is the EXPRESSIVE (motor) speech area; Wernicke's area (superior temporal gyrus) is the sensory (receptive) speech area.

## answer_d
In both the primary motor and primary sensory areas the body is represented upside-down

## explanation_d
Correct. In both the precentral (primary motor) and postcentral (primary sensory) gyri, the somatotopic map is inverted: the leg and foot are represented superiorly (extending onto the medial surface), the trunk and arm at the mid-convexity, and the face/hand inferiorly and laterally near the Sylvian fissure. A shared inversion of BOTH primary areas.

Nerve first affected in internal carotid artery aneurysm

## question
Which of the following nerves is firstly affected in case of internal carotid artery aneurysm?

## subject
neuro

## status

Trochlear

## title
Occlusion in neuronal pool integration

## question
Which of the following is related to occlusion?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Due to stimulation of two afferent neurons away from each other

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Two afferents stimulated far away from each other, with no overlapping neuronal pool, would produce simple additive summation, not the convergence occlusion requires.

## answer_b
Occurs when two afferent neurons shared a common discharge zone

## explanation_b
Correct. Occlusion is a spinal integrative phenomenon in which two afferent inputs converge on an overlapping (shared) pool of postsynaptic neurons, so their combined response is LESS than the arithmetic sum of each input's individual response, because some of the same neurons are already being fired by both inputs. This is why a clinician cannot simply add up the expected effect of two separate reflex-triggering stimuli when they are known to recruit an overlapping spinal neuronal pool.

## answer_c
Results in a greater response than if each afferent neuron is stimulated alone

## explanation_c
Incorrect — reversed. Occlusion produces a LESS-than-additive (not greater) combined response; a greater-than-additive response instead describes facilitation.

## answer_d
Occurs when two afferent neurons shared a common facilitated zone

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A shared subliminal FACILITATED zone (neurons brought near but not to threshold by either input alone) describes facilitation, the opposite integrative phenomenon from occlusion's shared, already-discharging zone.

## title
Definition of a ganglion

## question
Which of the following defines a ganglion?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Group of nerve fibers with same origin, end & function

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A group of nerve FIBERS sharing origin, course and function (inside or outside the CNS) is a tract or nerve, not a ganglion, which is defined by cell bodies, not fibers.

## answer_b
Group of close nerve cells with same function inside CNS

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A group of nerve CELL BODIES sharing function INSIDE the CNS is a nucleus, not a ganglion, which is specifically an extra-CNS structure.

## answer_c
Group of nerve fibers with different origin, end & function

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This does not describe a coherent anatomical grouping at all (fibers of different origin/end/function are not organised as a named structure), and fibers regardless are not what defines a ganglion.

## answer_d
Group of close nerve cells with same function outside CNS

## explanation_d
Correct. By definition, a ganglion is a cluster of neuron CELL BODIES (not fibers) located OUTSIDE the CNS — for example dorsal root ganglia (sensory) or autonomic ganglia. Distinguishing it from an intra-CNS nucleus (cell bodies) or a tract/nerve (fibers).

## title
Mechanism of enkephalin's block of pain transmission

## question
Enkephalin blocks pain transmission by:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Blocking the response of pain receptors to painful stimuli

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Enkephalin acts centrally at the dorsal horn synapse, not peripherally on the pain receptor's own response to a painful stimulus.

## answer_b
Slowing down transmission of pain impulses through synapses in the pain pathway

## explanation_b
Incorrect as the specific mechanism — enkephalin does more than generically 'slow' transmission; it acts via a specific presynaptic Ca2+-channel-blocking mechanism (option D).

## answer_c
Inhibiting the response of the cerebral cortical somatic sensory area to pain signals

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Enkephalin acts at the spinal (dorsal horn) level, not at the cerebral cortex.

## answer_d
Blocking Ca2+ channels in the central terminals of pain sensory fibers

## explanation_d
Correct. Enkephalin, an endogenous opioid released by inhibitory interneurons in the dorsal horn, binds presynaptic opioid receptors on the central terminals of primary nociceptive afferents, closing voltage-gated Ca2+ channels there, reducing Ca2+ influx and hence neurotransmitter release onto the second-order (transmission) neuron. A presynaptic inhibitory mechanism.

## title
Most lateral nucleus in the cerebellar white matter

## question
Which of the following is considered the most lateral nucleus in the white matter of the cerebellum?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Fastigial

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The fastigial nucleus is the most MEDIAL of the four deep cerebellar nuclei, not the most lateral.

## answer_b
Emboliform

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The emboliform nucleus lies between the globose and dentate nuclei, not at the most lateral position.

## answer_c
Globose

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The globose nucleus, together with the emboliform, forms the intermediate ('interposed') nuclear group, not the most lateral nucleus.

## answer_d
Dentate

## explanation_d
Correct. The dentate nucleus is the largest and most lateral of the four deep cerebellar nuclei (fastigial, globose, emboliform, dentate, medial to lateral), embedded in the cerebellar medulla (white matter). Its efferent fibers project chiefly via the superior cerebellar peduncle to the contralateral red nucleus and thalamus, supporting the planning of skilled voluntary movement.

## title
Primary cutaneous hyperalgesia

## question
Primary cutaneous hyperalgesia:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Develops in the normal skin region around the area of flare

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Hyperalgesia developing in the normal skin surrounding the area of flare describes SECONDARY, not primary, hyperalgesia.

## answer_b
Is an abnormal condition in the skin in which painful stimuli become more severe

## explanation_b
Incorrect as the best-characterising statement — this vague description does not identify the specific PERIPHERAL mechanism (receptor threshold change) that defines primary hyperalgesia, which option C names directly.

## answer_c
Is due to changes in threshold of pain receptors

## explanation_c
Correct. Primary hyperalgesia occurs directly within the injured tissue, where local inflammatory mediators sensitize nociceptors, lowering their activation threshold so normally mild stimuli become painful. Secondary hyperalgesia in the surrounding normal skin, by contrast, results from central (dorsal horn) sensitization rather than a change in the peripheral receptors themselves.

## answer_d
Is associated with a throbbing type of pain

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A throbbing pain quality is not the specific defining feature of primary hyperalgesia, which is instead defined by its peripheral receptor-threshold mechanism and its location within the injured tissue itself.

## title
Location of the substantia nigra

## question
The substantia nigra is present in which of the following sites?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Upper pons and lower midbrain

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The substantia nigra does not span into the pons; it is confined to the midbrain.

## answer_b
Medulla oblongata

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The medulla oblongata is well below the substantia nigra's actual (midbrain) location.

## answer_c
Superior midbrain

## explanation_c
Incorrect as the single best answer — the substantia nigra is not confined only to the SUPERIOR midbrain; it extends throughout the midbrain's length, making the broader 'midbrain' answer the more complete and accurate choice.

## answer_d
Midbrain

## explanation_d
Correct. The substantia nigra, a dopaminergic nucleus of the basal ganglia system, is located throughout the midbrain (mesencephalon), extending from its upper to lower limits. Its degeneration underlies Parkinson disease.

## title
Cause of decreased CSF sugar

## question
Which of the following causes a decrease in CSF sugar?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Traumatic tap

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A traumatic tap introduces blood into the CSF sample but does not itself lower true CSF glucose.

## answer_b
Meningitis

## explanation_b
Incorrect per this source's own printed key, though bacterial meningitis is the condition most standardly taught to cause LOW CSF glucose (hypoglycorrhachia), via glucose consumption by bacteria and infiltrating leukocytes.

## answer_c
Encephalitis

## explanation_c
Correct per this source's own printed answer key. Note: standard clinical teaching instead attributes low CSF glucose specifically to bacterial (pyogenic) meningitis (option B), with viral meningitis and encephalitis classically showing a NORMAL CSF glucose — a distinction used clinically to differentiate bacterial from viral CNS infection. This source's own answer table nonetheless marks C; see this concept's field_notes for the discrepancy.

## answer_d
Jaundice

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Jaundice (raised bilirubin) does not lower CSF glucose.
