## title
What occupies the 6th coordination bond of haem iron in oxyhaemoglobin?

## question
What occupies the 6th coordination bond of haem iron in oxyhaemoglobin?

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette


## answer_a
Oxygen

## explanation_a
Correct. The 6th coordination bond is the one that is empty in deoxyhaemoglobin and occupied by oxygen once the molecule is oxygenated; the 5th bond, by contrast, is permanently occupied by the proximal histidine's imidazole nitrogen and never carries oxygen. Distinguishing which bond is fixed and which is the one that actually does the carrying is the whole point of this fact.

## answer_b
The imidazole nitrogen of the proximal histidine

## explanation_b
Incorrect. That nitrogen occupies the 5th coordination bond, which anchors haem to the globin chain permanently — it is not the bond oxygen binds.

## answer_c
The imidazole nitrogen of the distal histidine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The distal histidine sits near the 6th bond in the pocket but does not itself occupy a coordination bond of the iron; confusing 'distal histidine' with 'the 6th bond's ligand' is the specific trap this option sets.

## answer_d
Carbon dioxide

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Haemoglobin carries most CO2 bound to the globin chain's terminal amino groups (as carbamino compounds), not at the haem iron's coordination bonds.

A

## explanation_a
Incorrect. All four chains are not identical — two are alpha (141 residues) and two are beta (146 residues); this option collapses that distinction.

Two 141-residue alpha chains and two 146-residue beta chains, one haem per chain

Two 146-residue alpha chains and two 141-residue beta chains

A single chain carrying four haem groups

## explanation_d
Incorrect. That description fits neither haemoglobin nor myoglobin; haemoglobin is four separate chains each with one haem, and myoglobin is one chain with one haem.

## title
Why does fetal haemoglobin (HbF) have a higher oxygen affinity than adult HbA?

## question
Why does fetal haemoglobin (HbF) have a higher oxygen affinity than adult HbA?

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette


## answer_a
Its haem group is chemically different from HbA's haem

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Haem itself (iron protoporphyrin IX) is identical in HbF and HbA; the difference lies entirely in the globin chain, not the haem.

## answer_b
It contains four alpha chains instead of two

## explanation_b
Incorrect. HbF is α2γ2, exactly like HbA is α2β2 — it still has two alpha chains, just paired with gamma instead of beta.

## answer_c
Its gamma chain binds 2,3-BPG far less tightly than the beta chain does

## explanation_c
Correct. 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate is what lowers haemoglobin's oxygen affinity, and HbF's gamma chain does not bind it as tightly as HbA's beta chain does — so HbF ends up with the higher affinity, exactly what lets the fetus draw oxygen across the placenta from the mother's lower-affinity HbA.

## answer_d
It has a 6th coordination bond that binds oxygen irreversibly

## explanation_d
Incorrect. No form of haemoglobin binds oxygen irreversibly at the 6th coordination bond — reversibility is what makes it a carrier rather than a trap, in HbF exactly as in HbA.

C

Glutamic acid at position 6 is replaced by serine

Glutamic acid at position 6 is replaced by valine

A whole beta chain is deleted

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A deleted or absent chain describes thalassaemia's quantity defect, not sickle cell disease's single-residue structural defect.

A single amino-acid substitution in the beta chain, as in sickle cell disease

## explanation_a
Incorrect. That describes sickle cell disease's structural defect. Thalassaemia's chains are structurally normal — the fault is in how much of a chain is made, not its sequence.

## answer_b
Oxidation of hameoglobin iron from Fe2+ to Fe3+

A patient's congenital methaemoglobinaemia, due to haemoglobin M, fails to improve after treatment with a reducing agent. What is the best explanation?

## question
A patient's congenital methaemoglobinaemia, due to haemoglobin M, fails to improve after treatment with a reducing agent. What is the best explanation?

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette


## answer_a
The dose of reducing agent given was too low

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This distractor tempts a student to assume a dosing problem rather than recognising that haemoglobin M is mechanistically unresponsive to reducing agents at any dose.

## answer_b
Haemoglobin M patients also have a deficiency of NADH-cytochrome b5 reductase

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Haemoglobin M and NADH-cytochrome b5 reductase deficiency are two separate causes of congenital methaemoglobinaemia; a patient with the HbM mutation does not necessarily also have the reductase deficiency, and the two are not the same condition.

## answer_c
The tyrosine substitution in HbM stabilises the ferric (Fe3+) state through a tight ionic bond, which a reducing agent cannot reverse

## explanation_c
Correct. In haemoglobin M, histidine is replaced by tyrosine, and the resulting phenolate-iron ionic bond stabilises the ferric state so tightly that reducing agents such as methylene blue cannot break it. This is why treatment for HbM is conservative (transfusion), unlike NADH-cytochrome b5 reductase deficiency, which responds well to reducing agents because the enzyme system is simply missing rather than chemically blocked.

## answer_d
Reducing agents only work on acquired, not congenital, methaemoglobinaemia in general

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This overgeneralises — congenital NADH-cytochrome b5 reductase deficiency, also a congenital cause, does respond well to reducing agents; the non-response is specific to the HbM mutation, not to congenital causes as a class.

C
