## title
Incomplete maxillary-mandibular fusion and macrostomia

## question
Incomplete fusion between the maxillary process and the mandibular process gives rise to:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette


## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Macrostomia

## explanation_a
Correct. The mouth's normal width depends on partial fusion of the maxillary and mandibular processes at the angle of the mouth; failure of this fusion leaves an abnormally wide oral fissure extending toward the ear, called macrostomia. It is the direct consequence of incomplete maxillary-mandibular fusion, as opposed to a defect of the medial nasal processes or of excessive (rather than deficient) fusion. In short: identify macrostomia as the abnormally wide mouth that follows failed maxillary-mandibular process fusion.

## answer_b
Median cleft upper lip

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Median cleft upper lip results from failure of fusion of the two medial nasal processes in the midline, not from a maxillary-mandibular fusion defect.

## answer_c
Microstomia

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Microstomia (an abnormally small mouth) results from excessive fusion of the maxillary and mandibular processes, the opposite defect to the incomplete fusion described here.

## answer_d
Cleft lower lip

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A cleft lower lip is a distinct, rare midline mandibular-prominence fusion defect, not the consequence of failed maxillary-mandibular process fusion at the angle of the mouth.

Maxillary processes and the definitive palate

## question
The maxillary processes are involved in formation of:

## subject
neuro

## status

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The primitive (primary) nasal septum forms from the frontonasal prominence/medial nasal processes, not the maxillary processes.

## answer_b
Definitive nasal septum

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The definitive nasal septum is a midline structure formed from the frontonasal prominence derivatives and later cartilage/bone, not the paired maxillary processes.

## answer_c
Primitive palate

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The primitive palate forms from the intermaxillary segment, itself a product of the two medial nasal processes fusing, not from the maxillary processes.

## explanation_d
Correct. The maxillary processes grow paired lateral palatine shelves that become horizontal and fuse in the midline (and with the primitive palate and nasal septum), forming the definitive (secondary) palate — the major portion of the adult palate. This is distinct from the small anterior primitive palate, which instead comes from the medial-nasal-process-derived intermaxillary segment. In short: identify the maxillary processes as the source of the definitive (secondary) palate.

Bifid tongue mechanism

## question
A bifid tongue is a result of:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner

## vignette


## correct_answer

## answer_a
Persistence of the ventral end of the second arch mesoderm

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Persistence of second-arch mesoderm is not the mechanism of bifid tongue; the tongue's anterior two-thirds musculature is a first-arch-region and occipital-myotome matter, not a second-arch persistence defect.

## answer_b
Persistence of the ventral end of the third arch mesoderm

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Third-arch mesoderm contributes to the posterior tongue and pharyngeal structures, not to a bifid-tongue defect of the anterior two-thirds.

## answer_c
Failure of the fusion of lateral lingual swellings

## explanation_c
Correct. The anterior two-thirds of the tongue develops from two lateral lingual swellings and a small midline tuberculum impar; normally the lateral swellings overgrow and fuse across the midline, burying the tuberculum impar. ## answer_d
Duplication tuberculum impar

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A duplicated tuberculum impar is not the standard mechanism taught for bifid tongue; the accepted mechanism is failure of the lateral lingual swellings to fuse over the (single) tuberculum impar.
