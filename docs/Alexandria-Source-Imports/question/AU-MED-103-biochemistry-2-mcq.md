## title
How much iron does the body normally contain, and what happens to iron once it exceeds ferritin's storage capacity?

## question
How much iron does the body normally contain, and what happens to iron once it exceeds ferritin's storage capacity?

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
About 5 g, mostly in haemoglobin; excess beyond ferritin's capacity accumulates as haemosiderin

## explanation_a
Correct. Total body iron is about 5 g, roughly two-thirds of it locked up in circulating haemoglobin rather than in storage. Ferritin is the cell's normal, soluble storage form, but once iron intake outpaces what ferritin can hold, the excess is deposited as haemosiderin, a partially degraded, insoluble aggregate of ferritin and denatured protein that is far less readily mobilised. This is exactly the distinction a question about iron overload states (such as haemochromatosis) is testing: ferritin is the physiological reserve, haemosiderin is the overflow.

## answer_b
About 50 g, mostly in the liver; excess beyond ferritin's capacity is excreted in bile

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Total body iron is about 5 g, not 50 g, and while the liver does store a large share of it, most iron overall is bound up in circulating haemoglobin, not the liver; the body has no meaningful biliary excretion route for iron.

## answer_c
About 5 g, mostly in myoglobin; excess beyond ferritin's capacity is excreted in urine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Myoglobin holds only a small fraction of body iron compared with haemoglobin, and the kidney is not a meaningful route for iron excretion — the body has almost no regulated way to excrete excess iron, which is precisely why iron overload is dangerous.

## answer_d
About 0.5 g, mostly in transferrin; excess beyond ferritin's capacity accumulates as haemosiderin

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Total body iron is about 5 g, not 0.5 g, and transferrin is the small circulating transport pool, not where most body iron sits.

## title
Once absorbed, in which oxidation state does iron travel bound to plasma transferrin, and where do most transferrin receptors sit?

## question
Once absorbed, in which oxidation state does iron travel bound to plasma transferrin, and where do most transferrin receptors sit?

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Ferrous (Fe2+); mostly on hepatocytes

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Transferrin binds iron in the ferric, not ferrous, state, and hepatocytes are not where most transferrin receptors are concentrated.

## answer_b
Ferric (Fe3+); mostly on erythropoietic (red cell precursor) cells

## explanation_b
Correct. After crossing the enterocyte's apical membrane as Fe2+, absorbed iron is oxidised back to the ferric (Fe3+) state before it is loaded onto plasma transferrin, which specifically binds Fe3+. Most transferrin receptors sit on erythropoietic (red cell precursor) cells in the bone marrow, since these cells have by far the largest ongoing demand for iron to build new haemoglobin — a student who only remembers 'iron is absorbed as Fe2+' can easily and wrongly assume the transport form is also Fe2+, missing this oxidation step.

## answer_c
Ferrous (Fe2+); mostly on erythropoietic (red cell precursor) cells

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This gets the receptor location right but the oxidation state wrong — transferrin-bound iron is ferric (Fe3+), the product of re-oxidising the ferrous iron that crossed the enterocyte.

## answer_d
Ferric (Fe3+); mostly on mature circulating red cells

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Mature circulating red cells have already finished haemoglobin synthesis and carry comparatively few transferrin receptors compared with their still-maturing erythropoietic precursors in the marrow.

## title
Beyond haemoglobin and myoglobin, which class of proteins also depends on iron, and why does this matter for cellular respiration?

## question
Beyond haemoglobin and myoglobin, which class of proteins also depends on iron, and why does this matter for cellular respiration?

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Cytochromes of the respiratory chain, which use iron to shuttle electrons during oxidative phosphorylation

## explanation_a
Correct. The cytochromes of the mitochondrial electron transport chain each carry a haem group whose iron cycles between the ferrous and ferric states as it accepts and donates electrons, exactly the property that lets the respiratory chain pass electrons stepwise down to oxygen. This is a distinct iron-dependent role from oxygen carriage or storage: it makes iron essential not just for haemoglobin's oxygen transport but for the basic machinery of aerobic ATP production in every cell.

## answer_b
Immunoglobulins, which use iron to stabilise their disulphide bonds

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Immunoglobulin disulphide bonds are formed and stabilised by disulphide-bond chemistry between cysteine residues, with no iron involvement.

## answer_c
Glycogen synthase, which uses iron as an allosteric activator

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Glycogen synthase's activity is controlled by covalent phosphorylation/dephosphorylation and by glucose-6-phosphate, not by iron.

## answer_d
Albumin, which uses iron to maintain plasma oncotic pressure

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Albumin's contribution to plasma oncotic pressure comes from its abundance and molecular size, not from any iron content.

## title
Which structural feature makes IgM the most efficient immunoglobulin class at agglutination and complement fixation?

## question
Which structural feature makes IgM the most efficient immunoglobulin class at agglutination and complement fixation?

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
It is a pentamer, giving it up to ten antigen-binding sites

## explanation_a
Correct. IgM circulates as a pentamer of five basic Y-shaped units joined at their tails, giving it up to ten antigen-binding sites per molecule (versus two for a single IgG monomer). That multivalency makes IgM extremely efficient at cross-linking antigens into large lattices (agglutination) and at fixing complement, even though any single binding site's affinity is often modest — which is exactly why IgM dominates the early, low-affinity phase of a primary immune response before higher-affinity IgG takes over.

## answer_b
It is the smallest immunoglobulin, so it diffuses fastest into tissue

## explanation_b
Incorrect. IgM is the largest circulating immunoglobulin because it is a pentamer, not the smallest, and its size actually confines it mostly to the bloodstream rather than diffusing readily into tissue.

## answer_c
It lacks a heavy chain entirely

## explanation_c
Incorrect. IgM has its own distinct heavy chain (the mu chain); every immunoglobulin class is defined by which heavy chain it carries.

## answer_d
It is produced only after class-switching

## explanation_d
Incorrect. IgM is in fact the class made before class-switching occurs; class-switching converts a B cell from making IgM to making IgG, IgA or IgE instead.

## title
An immunoglobulin's antigen-binding specificity is carried mainly by which chain pairing, and how many domains does one heavy chain typically contribute?

## question
An immunoglobulin's antigen-binding specificity is carried mainly by which chain pairing, and how many domains does one heavy chain typically contribute?

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Two heavy chains only, each contributing two domains

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Antigen-binding specificity requires both a heavy and a light chain paired together at each arm of the Y, not heavy chains alone.

## answer_b
One heavy and one light chain per antigen-binding arm, the heavy chain typically contributing four domains (one variable, three constant)

## explanation_b
Correct. Each immunoglobulin monomer is built from two identical heavy chains and two identical light chains (either kappa or lambda), and each antigen-binding arm pairs one heavy with one light chain. A typical heavy chain folds into four immunoglobulin domains — one variable (VH) domain, which together with the light chain's variable domain forms the antigen-binding site, plus three constant (CH) domains that make up the rest of the heavy chain and determine the class's effector functions.

## answer_c
Two light chains only, each contributing five domains

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Light chains alone cannot bind antigen; a light chain contributes only two domains (one variable, one constant), not five, and specificity always requires heavy-light chain pairing.

## answer_d
One heavy and one light chain per antigen-binding arm, the heavy chain typically contributing one domain

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A single constant domain would not account for the heavy chain's actual structure; most classes carry three constant domains per heavy chain (IgM and IgE carry four, having an extra constant domain instead of a hinge).

Activated by AMP and fructose 2,6-bisphosphate; inhibited by ATP and citrate

## explanation_a
Correct. A fall in energy charge, sensed as a rise in AMP, switches PFK-1 on because AMP rises steeply as ATP starts to fall (via the adenylate kinase reaction), making it a sensitive low-energy alarm. Fructose 2,6-bisphosphate, made by a separate hormonally-controlled enzyme, is PFK-1's single strongest activator and is what lets insulin push glycolysis forward in the fed liver. On the inhibitory side, a high ATP level binds a distinct, lower-affinity allosteric site once energy charge is already high, and citrate, which accumulates when the citric acid cycle is already well supplied, adds a second brake that ties glycolysis's pace to the cycle's own downstream capacity.

## answer_b
Activated by ATP and citrate; inhibited by AMP and fructose 2,6-bisphosphate

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This reverses every pairing -- ATP and citrate are PFK-1's inhibitors (signalling that energy and citric-acid-cycle capacity are already adequate), while AMP and fructose 2,6-bisphosphate are its activators.

## answer_c
Activated by ADP alone; inhibited by fructose 2,6-bisphosphate alone

## explanation_c
Incorrect. ADP is not PFK-1's key activating signal (AMP is the more sensitive one), and fructose 2,6-bisphosphate is an activator, not an inhibitor.

## answer_d
Activated by fructose-6-phosphate, its own substrate, with no other regulator

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Fructose-6-phosphate is PFK-1's own substrate, not an allosteric regulator; PFK-1's actual regulators (AMP, fructose 2,6-bisphosphate, ATP, citrate) are distinct molecules from its substrate.

Why is dietary haem iron (from meat) absorbed more efficiently than non-haem iron (from plant sources)?

## question
Why is dietary haem iron (from meat) absorbed more efficiently than non-haem iron (from plant sources)?

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Haem iron enters the enterocyte as an intact iron-porphyrin complex via its own transporter, bypassing the reduction and chelation problems that limit non-haem iron uptake

## explanation_a
Correct. Haem iron is taken up by the enterocyte as an intact iron-porphyrin ring via a dedicated haem transporter, so it avoids the problems that limit non-haem iron: the need to be reduced from ferric to ferrous first, and the risk of being chelated by dietary phytates, tannins or phosphates before it ever reaches the mucosal surface. This is why meat-derived iron is absorbed at a substantially higher percentage than plant-derived iron, even at the same elemental iron dose.

## answer_b
Haem iron is absorbed in the stomach, while non-haem iron is absorbed only in the colon

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Neither form of dietary iron is absorbed in the stomach or colon; both haem and non-haem iron are absorbed mainly in the duodenum, just by different mechanisms.

## answer_c
Haem iron requires vitamin C for absorption, while non-haem iron does not

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Vitamin C helps non-haem iron absorption, by reducing ferric to ferrous iron and by chelating it away from inhibitors; haem iron, entering as an intact complex, does not need this reduction step at all.

## answer_d
There is no real difference in absorption efficiency between haem and non-haem iron

## explanation_d
Incorrect. There is a well-established, clinically significant difference: haem iron's bioavailability is substantially higher than that of non-haem iron, which is part of why iron-deficiency risk differs between diets containing meat and strictly plant-based diets.
