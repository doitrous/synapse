## title
Which membrane component lets fat-soluble substances cross by simple diffusion

## question
Which structural feature of the cell membrane allows fat-soluble substances to pass through it directly?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Alexandria content lane (W1-102-HIST author1)

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The phospholipid bilayer

## explanation_a
Correct. The cell membrane's lipid component is phospholipid arranged as a bilayer, hydrophilic heads facing the aqueous solutions on either side and hydrophobic fatty-acid tails packed in the middle. A fat-soluble (lipid-soluble) substance dissolves directly in that hydrophobic core and diffuses straight across it without needing any protein channel or carrier. This is why lipid solubility, not molecular size alone, is the main predictor of how easily a substance crosses a plasma membrane by simple diffusion.

## answer_b
Integral channel proteins

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Integral channel proteins span the bilayer to move ions and water, and carrier proteins move small polar molecules such as glucose; both exist because those hydrophilic substances cannot cross the hydrophobic lipid core unassisted. A fat-soluble substance needs no such assistance, so this route is not the one being tested here.

## answer_c
The surface glycocalyx

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The glycocalyx is the carbohydrate coat on the membrane's outer face, built for recognition and adhesion, not for transport. It sits on top of the bilayer rather than providing any pathway through it.

## answer_d
Peripheral cytoskeletal proteins

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Peripheral proteins sit on the cytoplasmic face of the membrane and form the cytoskeleton that gives the cell its shape and elasticity. They play no part in letting any substance, fat-soluble or otherwise, cross the membrane.

## title
The cell coat's function

## question
What is the main function of the cell coat (glycocalyx) on the outer surface of the plasma membrane?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Alexandria content lane (W1-102-HIST author1)

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Anchoring the cytoskeleton to the inner membrane surface

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Anchoring the cytoskeleton is the job of peripheral membrane proteins on the cytoplasmic (inner) face; the cell coat sits on the opposite, outer face and has no cytoskeletal role.

## answer_b
Generating the membrane potential

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The membrane potential is set by ion gradients and channel/pump proteins, not by the carbohydrate coat. The glycocalyx has no electrical role.

## answer_c
Providing structural rigidity to the lipid bilayer

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Rigidity is not the glycocalyx's function; it is a fuzzy, flexible surface layer of glycoprotein and glycolipid, not a structural scaffold for the bilayer.

## answer_d
Cell-to-cell recognition and adhesion

## explanation_d
Correct. The cell coat, or glycocalyx, is the carbohydrate-rich layer on the external face of the plasma membrane only, made of glycoprotein and glycolipid. It carries the cell's surface receptors and antigens, which is what lets one cell recognise, adhere to and communicate with another and lets the immune system tell self from non-self. This recognition-and-adhesion role, not any structural or electrical one, is the function tested here.

## title
What pinocytosis takes into the cell

## question
Pinocytosis is the process by which a cell takes in:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Alexandria content lane (W1-102-HIST author1)

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Large particulate matter such as bacteria

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Taking in large particulate matter such as bacteria or cell debris is phagocytosis, a distinct, larger-scale form of endocytosis carried out chiefly by macrophages and neutrophils, not pinocytosis.

## answer_b
A specific ligand bound to a surface receptor

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Uptake of one specific ligand already bound to its own surface receptor is receptor-mediated endocytosis, which concentrates a particular molecule via coated pits rather than sampling fluid indiscriminately.

## answer_c
Membrane-bound secretory vesicles from the Golgi

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Secretory vesicles budding from the Golgi apparatus move material out of the cell (exocytosis) or between organelles; they are not what pinocytosis brings in.

## answer_d
Extracellular fluid together with its dissolved ions

## explanation_d
Correct. Pinocytosis ("cell drinking") is the non-selective uptake of extracellular fluid and whatever small solutes and ions are dissolved in it, taken in by small vesicles pinching off the plasma membrane. It is one of the membrane's three endocytic pathways alongside phagocytosis and receptor-mediated endocytosis, and it is distinguished from the other two by not being size-selective or receptor-specific. Because it samples the surrounding fluid indiscriminately, pinocytosis is the route by which a cell also drinks in whatever ions happen to be dissolved in that fluid.

## title
Cytoskeletal element forming the centriole

## question
Which cytoskeletal element is arranged as nine triplets to form a centriole?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Alexandria content lane (W1-102-HIST author1)

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Intermediate filaments

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Intermediate filaments are the supportive, tissue-specific cytoskeletal proteins (cytokeratin, vimentin, desmin, and so on); they form no part of the centriole's wall.

## answer_b
Microtubules

## explanation_b
Correct. A centriole is a short cylindrical structure whose wall is built of nine bundles of three microtubules each - twenty-seven microtubules in total - with no membrane and no central pair. A pair of centrioles, surrounded by a tubulin matrix, forms the centrosome, the cell's microtubule-organising centre, which duplicates and moves to opposite poles to organise the mitotic spindle. Centrioles also template the basal bodies of cilia and flagella, which share the same nine-triplet microtubule structure.

## answer_c
Actin microfilaments

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Actin microfilaments are the thinnest cytoskeletal element, involved in cell motility and the terminal web beneath microvilli; they are not the building block of the centriole.

## answer_d
Myosin thick filaments

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Myosin thick filaments are a muscle-contraction protein, not a general cytoskeletal element, and play no part in centriole structure.

## title
Mutated protein impairing flagellar function

## question
Mutation of which protein would most directly impair flagellar function in sperm?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Alexandria content lane (W1-102-HIST author1)

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Tubulin

## explanation_a
Correct. The flagellum's motile core (axoneme) is built of microtubules, themselves polymers of the protein tubulin, arranged in the classic 9+2 pattern with dynein arms that slide the microtubule doublets past each other to produce the beat. A mutation impairing tubulin's ability to polymerise or interact with dynein would directly cripple this microtubule-based motile machinery. The centriole that templates the flagellum's basal body is itself built of tubulin-based microtubule triplets, underscoring how central this one protein is to ciliary and flagellar structure.

## answer_b
Actin

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Actin is the core protein of microvilli and of the cell's contractile/motility machinery elsewhere, but it is not the structural protein of the flagellar axoneme, which is microtubule- (tubulin-) based.

## answer_c
Keratin

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Keratin is an intermediate filament protein providing structural support in epithelial cells; it plays no part in flagellar motility.

## answer_d
Collagen

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Collagen is an extracellular connective-tissue fibre protein, entirely unrelated to the intracellular microtubule machinery that powers a flagellum.

## question
A brain tumour of neuroglial (astrocytic) origin is expected to stain positive on immunohistochemistry for which intermediate filament protein?

## subject

## explanation_b

Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP)

Incorrect. Vimentin is the intermediate filament of connective tissue and muscle; it marks a mesenchymal, not a neuroglial, tumour.

Face of the Golgi apparatus that buds transport vesicles

## question
Which face of the Golgi apparatus buds off the vesicles that carry its finished products onward?

## vignette

D

## answer_a
The cis (entry) face

## title
Classification of the Na+-K+ pump as a transport mechanism

## question
The Na+-K+ ATPase pump, which moves three sodium ions out of the cell for every two potassium ions moved in, is classified as:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Alexandria content lane (W1-102-HIST author1)

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Facilitated diffusion

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Facilitated diffusion moves a substance down its concentration gradient via a carrier protein without using ATP directly; the Na+-K+ pump moves both ions against their gradients and consumes ATP directly, which facilitated diffusion does not.

## answer_b
Secondary active transport

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Secondary active transport uses the electrochemical gradient built by a primary active transporter (such as the Na+-K+ pump itself) to move a different solute, often via a cotransporter; it does not directly hydrolyse ATP the way the pump itself does.

## answer_c
Primary active transport

## explanation_c
Correct. The Na+-K+ pump is ATP-driven primary active transport, directly hydrolysing ATP to move three sodium ions out of the cell for every two potassium ions moved in, against both ions' concentration gradients. Because it moves unequal charge (3 positive charges out for 2 in), the pump is also electrogenic, contributing directly to the resting membrane potential in addition to maintaining the ionic gradients themselves. This direct ATP-hydrolysis, against-the-gradient, electrogenic transport is what defines it as primary, not secondary or facilitated, active transport.

## answer_d
Simple diffusion

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Simple diffusion is passive, unassisted movement down a concentration gradient with no protein and no energy expenditure; the Na+-K+ pump is the opposite - active, protein-mediated, and moving both ions against their gradients.

## title
Why an anticholinesterase relieves myasthenia gravis

## question
Myasthenia gravis is caused by autoantibodies directed against the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor at the neuromuscular junction. Why does an anticholinesterase drug relieve its muscle weakness?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Alexandria content lane (W1-102-HIST author1)

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
It regenerates destroyed acetylcholine receptors

## explanation_a
Incorrect. An anticholinesterase does not repair or regenerate receptors that autoantibodies have destroyed or blocked; the receptor loss itself is not reversed by this drug class.

## answer_b
It blocks the autoantibodies directly

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Anticholinesterases do not act on the autoantibodies at all; they act purely on the enzyme that normally breaks down acetylcholine, leaving the autoimmune process itself untouched.

## answer_c
It prolongs acetylcholine's action at the remaining receptors by slowing its breakdown

## explanation_c
Correct. Myasthenia gravis is caused by autoantibodies against the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor, reducing the number of functional receptors available at the neuromuscular junction and so weakening neuromuscular transmission. An anticholinesterase drug inhibits acetylcholinesterase, the enzyme that normally breaks down acetylcholine in the synaptic cleft, which prolongs acetylcholine's presence and action there. With more time to act on the fewer remaining receptors, this compensates - functionally, not by fixing the underlying autoimmune receptor loss - for the reduced receptor number, which is exactly why an anticholinesterase relieves the disease's muscle weakness.

## answer_d
It replaces acetylcholine with a longer-acting analogue

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Anticholinesterases do not supply any acetylcholine analogue; they act only by slowing the breakdown of the acetylcholine already being released.
