## title
'Scope' in the word 'microscope' — what part of the word is it?

## question
'Scope' in the word 'microscope' — what part of the word is it?

## vignette


## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## answer_a
Suffix

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A suffix sits at the end of a word and modifies the root into a noun or adjective — here "scope" is the core meaning, not a modifier attached to something else.

## answer_b
Combining vowel

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The combining vowel is the single letter — usually "o" — inserted only to join two consonants smoothly; "scope" carries a full meaning of its own, which a combining vowel never does.

## answer_c
Prefix

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A prefix sits in front of a root and narrows its meaning; "scope" is not attached in front of another root here, it is the root.

## answer_d
Word root

## explanation_d
Correct. Every medical term is built around a word root that carries its core meaning, with an optional prefix and suffix attached around it and a combining vowel inserted only where two consonants would otherwise collide. In "microscope", "scope" is that root — an instrument or act of viewing — while "micro-" is the prefix narrowing it to "small", so the whole word reads as "instrument for viewing small things". The thing worth holding is that a root can stand as the core of many different words (microscope, endoscope, stethoscope), which is exactly why learning the root pays off far beyond the one word it was first met in.

D

# Item

## id
QST-FND-PLURALISATION-001

## title
What is the plural of 'thorax'?

## question
What is the plural of 'thorax'?

## vignette


## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## answer_a
Thoraces

## explanation_a
Correct. A medical noun borrowed from Latin or Greek keeps that language's own plural ending rather than taking an English "-s", and a noun ending in "-x" pluralises to "-ces" — the same pattern that turns "phalanx" into "phalanges" and "appendix" into "appendices". "Thorax" follows it exactly: thorax becomes thoraces. The pattern to hold is the ending, not the whole word — once "-x becomes -ces" is fixed, any new Latin noun with that ending can be pluralised correctly without having met it before.

## answer_b
Thoracis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This is not a recognised plural form for "thorax"; it resembles the adjectival stem ("thoracic") rather than the plural.

## answer_c
Thoasis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This drops letters from the root rather than applying the -x → -ces pattern, and is not a real plural of "thorax".

## answer_d
Thorases

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This is the trap: adding an English-style "-s" (via "-ses") to a Latin noun instead of changing its ending to "-ces".

QST-OBS-GRAVIDA-PARA-001

## title
A woman has had four pregnancies and has two living children. How is this recorded on her chart?

## question
A woman has had four pregnancies and has two living children. How is this recorded on her chart?

## vignette
A 32-year-old woman attends her first antenatal visit. She reports four pregnancies in total; two reached term and resulted in the two children she has today, and two ended before viability.

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## answer_a
Gravida 2, para 4

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This reverses the two counts — gravida is the total number of pregnancies (four), and para is the number reaching viability (two), not the other way round.

## answer_b
Gravida 4, para 2

## explanation_b
Correct. Gravida is the total number of times a woman has been pregnant, counting every pregnancy whether or not it reached viability — here that is four. Para is only the pregnancies that reached a viable gestation, regardless of the outcome of the delivery — here two, the pair that produced her living children, while the other two ended before viability and never become a para. The number to hold onto is that gravida can never be smaller than para, because every para was first a gravida, but a gravida is not always a para.

## answer_c
Gravida 4, para 4

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This treats every pregnancy as though it reached viability, ignoring that two of the four did not.

## answer_d
Gravida 4, para 0

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This is the mistake of reading "para" as though it meant live children still living today, or of assuming none of the four pregnancies counts unless a full term birth is currently ongoing; two of the four pregnancies did reach viability and so do count toward para.

B

# Item

## id
QST-FND-DESCRIPTIVE-EPONYM-001

## title
Which best describes the term "fallopian tube"?

## question
Which best describes the term "fallopian tube"?

## vignette


## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## answer_a
A descriptive term, built from roots that state its location and function

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A descriptive term is built from roots, prefixes and suffixes that state what the structure is or does — "fallopian" carries no such internal meaning to decode.

## answer_b
An eponym, naming the person credited with first describing it

## explanation_b
Correct. Most medical terms are descriptive, built from roots, prefixes and suffixes that state what a structure is or does, but some are eponyms instead, naming the person credited with first describing them. "Fallopian" names Gabriele Falloppio, the anatomist credited with describing the tube — the word itself has no root meaning "carries an egg" or similar to decode, unlike a genuinely descriptive term. The thing to remember is that an eponym cannot be worked out from its parts the way "cardiomegaly" or "nephroptosis" can; it has to be learned as a whole word tied to a name.

## answer_c
Both a descriptive term and an eponym at once

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The two naming systems are distinct: a term is either built to be decoded from its parts or it names a person, not both — "fallopian tube" is only the latter.

## answer_d
Neither — it is a purely modern coined term

## explanation_d
Incorrect. "Fallopian" is not a coinage without a source; it is an eponym, tracing directly to Gabriele Falloppio.
