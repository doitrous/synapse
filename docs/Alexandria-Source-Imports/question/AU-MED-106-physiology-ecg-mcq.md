# Item

## id
QST-AUMED106PHYS-ECG-ECG-Q01

## title
PR interval and first-degree AV block

## question
A prolonged PR interval on ECG is the best indicator of which of the following?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Ventricular repolarisation delay

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Ventricular repolarisation is reflected in the ST segment and T wave (and its overall duration in the QT interval), not the PR interval. The PR interval instead spans from the start of atrial depolarisation to the start of ventricular depolarisation, so it says nothing directly about repolarisation.

## answer_b
First-degree AV block (AV-nodal conduction delay)

## explanation_b
Correct. The PR interval reflects the time taken for atrial depolarisation plus the delay imposed by the AV node before ventricular depolarisation begins. A PR interval prolonged beyond the normal 0.12-0.20 second range signals slowed AV-nodal conduction, the definition of first-degree AV block, making it the best available indicator of AV-node function among ECG intervals.

## answer_c
Bundle branch block

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Bundle branch block is reflected in a widened QRS complex (beyond 0.12 sec), not a prolonged PR interval. QRS duration and PR interval measure different parts of the conduction system — ventricular conduction versus AV-nodal conduction — and are not interchangeable signs.

## answer_d
Ischaemia in the affected territory

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Ischaemia is signalled by ST-segment depression and T-wave inversion, changes in the repolarisation portion of the tracing, not by the PR interval. A prolonged PR interval on its own does not indicate active ischaemia in any territory.

# Item

## id
QST-AUMED106PHYS-ECG-ECG-Q02

## title
ECG signs of ischaemia

## question
On an ECG, an inverted T wave is accompanied by which additional finding as a sign of myocardial ischaemia?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Elevated ST segment

## explanation_a
Incorrect. ST elevation signals a fully occluded coronary vessel progressing toward transmural infarction, not reversible ischaemia. Ischaemia and infarction are different mechanisms — subendocardial hypoperfusion versus a completed occlusion — and ST elevation belongs to the infarction side of that distinction.

## answer_b
Prolonged PR interval

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A prolonged PR interval reflects AV-nodal conduction delay, an entirely separate mechanism from ischaemia. There is no established mechanistic link between AV-nodal conduction time and the repolarisation abnormalities that ischaemia produces.

## answer_c
Deep Q wave

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A deep, wide Q wave signals infarcted, electrically silent myocardial tissue — a sign of completed infarction, not the reversible ischaemic process. A small Q wave from normal septal depolarisation is a separate, unrelated normal finding.

## answer_d
Depressed ST segment

## explanation_d
Correct. ST-segment depression, together with T-wave inversion, is the classic reversible sign of subendocardial ischaemia. Ischaemia alters repolarisation (the ST-T segment) before it alters depolarisation (the QRS complex), because the subendocardial layer is most vulnerable to reduced perfusion and repolarises abnormally early relative to the outer myocardial layer.

## title
Lead aVR's normal appearance

## question
Which standard limb/augmented ECG lead normally shows a fully inverted (negative) P-QRS-T complex?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Lead II

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Lead II views the heart from a position roughly aligned with the mean depolarisation vector (down and to the left, toward the apex), so it normally shows an upright, positive QRS complex — the opposite of what this question asks about.

## answer_b
Lead V3

## explanation_b
Incorrect. V3 is a precordial (chest) lead positioned over the anterior chest wall; it is not one of the standard limb/augmented leads and does not show a normally inverted complex — precordial leads typically transition from negative to positive complexes moving from V1 to V6.

## answer_c
Lead aVR

## explanation_c
Correct. Lead aVR views the heart from the right shoulder, roughly opposite the mean direction of ventricular depolarisation, which travels down and to the left toward the apex. Because the depolarisation wave moves away from aVR's exploring electrode, its P wave, QRS complex and T wave are all normally inverted — the one standard lead where full inversion is the expected, normal appearance rather than a sign of pathology.

## answer_d
Lead V2

## explanation_d
Incorrect. V2, like V3, is a precordial lead over the anterior chest and is not one of the six limb/augmented leads; it does not show the normally-inverted appearance that characterises aVR specifically.

## title
Limb-lead electrode colour convention

## question
In the standard limb-electrode colour convention, which colour is assigned to the left-arm electrode?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Red

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Red is conventionally the right-arm electrode colour, not the left arm. Placing a right-arm-coded electrode on the left arm (or vice versa) is exactly the kind of swap this colour convention exists to prevent, since it inverts lead I and distorts the whole limb-lead set.

## answer_b
Green

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Green is conventionally assigned to the left-leg electrode, not the left arm. Because the limb leads are defined by specific electrode pairings, mixing up the leg and arm colours would produce a lead configuration inconsistent with the standard 12-lead layout.

## answer_c
Yellow

## explanation_c
Correct. In the standard (AHA-derived) limb-electrode colour convention, the left-arm electrode is coded yellow. Colour coding exists purely to prevent misplacement, since swapping electrodes between limbs inverts the resulting leads in a way that is easy to overlook without a clear, consistent colour system.

## answer_d
Black

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Black is conventionally the right-leg (earth/ground) electrode in many coding schemes, not the left arm. The ground electrode does not contribute to any of the recorded leads, unlike the left-arm electrode, which forms part of leads I, II, and aVL.

QST-AUMED106PHYS-ECG-ECG-Q06

## title
QRS duration and bundle branch block

## question
A QRS complex measuring more than 0.12 seconds indicates which of the following?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Rapid heart rate

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Heart rate is calculated from the interval between successive R waves (300 divided by large squares between them), not from QRS duration. A rapid heart rate and a wide QRS complex reflect entirely different measurements and can occur independently of each other.

## answer_b
First-degree heart block

## explanation_b
Incorrect. First-degree heart block is defined by a prolonged PR interval, reflecting delayed AV-nodal conduction, not a widened QRS complex. PR interval and QRS duration measure different segments of the conduction pathway.

## answer_c
Myocardial infarction

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Myocardial infarction is signalled by ST elevation evolving to a deep, wide Q wave, not primarily by QRS widening. A widened QRS complex can occur in structural heart disease generally, but a bundle branch block specifically, not an evolving infarct, is the standard interpretation of duration beyond 0.12 seconds.

## answer_d
Bundle branch block

## explanation_d
Correct. Normal ventricular depolarisation completes within 0.06-0.10 seconds because the His-Purkinje system activates both ventricles almost simultaneously. A bundle branch block interrupts conduction down one bundle branch, forcing that ventricle to be activated slowly, cell-to-cell, from the other side — this widens the QRS beyond 0.12 seconds, the standard cutoff for a complete bundle branch block.

## title
Heart rate from an ECG strip

## question
On a regular-rhythm ECG strip, there are 6 large squares between two successive R waves. Using the standard 300-divided-by-large-squares rule, what is the heart rate?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
85 beats/min

## explanation_a
Incorrect. 85 beats/min does not follow from dividing 300 by 6; it would require roughly 3.5 large squares between R waves, not 6, so this figure does not match the strip described in the stem.

## answer_b
75 beats/min

## explanation_b
Incorrect. 75 beats/min corresponds to 4 large squares between R waves (300/4=75), not the 6 large squares stated in this question. Reading the wrong number of squares off a strip is a common source of this kind of error.

## answer_c
60 beats/min

## explanation_c
Incorrect. 60 beats/min corresponds to 5 large squares between R waves (300/5=60), one fewer than the 6 large squares specified here, so it does not match the given strip measurement.

## answer_d
50 beats/min

## explanation_d
Correct. Standard ECG paper runs at 25 mm/s, giving 300 large squares per minute. For a regular rhythm, heart rate equals 300 divided by the number of large squares between two successive R waves: 300/6 = 50 beats/min. This method only applies to a regular rhythm; an irregular rhythm requires counting R waves over a longer strip instead.

## title
ECG calibration standard

## question
At standard ECG paper calibration (25 mm/s, 10 mm/mV), one large square corresponds to which time and voltage values?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
0.04 sec / 0.1 mV

## explanation_a
Incorrect. 0.04 sec / 0.1 mV is the value of one SMALL square, not one large square. Each large square contains five small squares, so its time and voltage values are five times larger than the small square's.

## answer_b
0.2 sec / 0.5 mV

## explanation_b
Correct. At standard calibration (25 mm/s paper speed, 10 mm/mV gain), each small square (1 mm) represents 0.04 seconds and 0.1 mV, and each large square (5 mm, made of five small squares) therefore represents 0.2 seconds and 0.5 mV. This calibration is what lets duration and amplitude be read directly off the ECG grid.

## answer_c
1 sec / 1 mV

## explanation_c
Incorrect. 1 sec / 1 mV would correspond to five large squares' worth of time and two large squares' worth of voltage at standard calibration, not a single large square; this option overstates both values considerably.

## answer_d
0.1 sec / 0.2 mV

## explanation_d
Incorrect. 0.1 sec / 0.2 mV does not match the standard calibration for either a small or a large square — it appears to mix values from the two square sizes rather than describing either one consistently.

## title
Q wave and septal depolarisation

## question
Interventricular septal depolarisation is represented on the ECG by which wave?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
P wave

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The P wave represents atrial depolarisation, an entirely separate event from septal depolarisation, which occurs later, as the first part of the ventricular (QRS) complex.

## answer_b
R wave

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The R wave represents the bulk of free-ventricular-wall depolarisation, which follows septal depolarisation. It is the larger, later deflection in the QRS complex, not the small initial one associated with the septum.

Q wave

## explanation_d
Correct. Ventricular depolarisation begins in the interventricular septum, activated from the left bundle branch before the free ventricular walls depolarise. This early, small, septal depolarisation is recorded as the small initial negative deflection — the Q wave — in leads that view the septum from the left.

## explanation_b
Correct. The P wave represents atrial depolarisation, and its amplitude and duration reflect the mass and size of the depolarising atrial tissue. Bundle branch block

## explanation_c

## answer_d
First-degree AV block

## explanation_d
Incorrect. First-degree AV block prolongs the PR interval (the timing between the P wave and the QRS complex), not the P wave's own amplitude or shape. PR-interval prolongation and P-wave enlargement are distinct findings reflecting different mechanisms.

## explanation_a

## answer_b
ST-segment depression alone

## explanation_b
Incorrect. ST-segment depression alone likewise signals reversible subendocardial ischaemia, not a completed or evolving infarct. Ischaemia and infarction are different mechanisms — the former is reversible hypoperfusion, the latter is established tissue death.

ST-segment elevation evolving to a deep, wide pathological Q wave

A shortened QT interval

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A shortened QT interval is the ECG change seen in hypercalcaemia, an electrolyte disturbance affecting the ventricular action potential's plateau phase — it has no established relationship to acute myocardial infarction or ischaemia.

## title
Hypercalcaemia and the QT interval

## question
What ECG change results from hypercalcaemia?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
QT interval is shortened

## explanation_a
Correct. The QT interval spans ventricular depolarisation through the end of repolarisation, and its duration is set mainly by how long the plateau (phase 2) of the ventricular action potential lasts. Raised extracellular calcium shortens this calcium-dependent plateau, completing it faster, which shortens the QT interval on the surface ECG — the opposite effect to hypocalcaemia, which prolongs the plateau and lengthens QT.

## answer_b
QT interval is prolonged

## explanation_b
Incorrect. QT-interval prolongation is the effect of hypocalcaemia (which prolongs the action potential's plateau), not hypercalcaemia. Hypercalcaemia moves the QT interval in the opposite direction — shortening, not lengthening, it.

## answer_c
PR interval is prolonged

## explanation_c
Incorrect. PR-interval changes reflect AV-nodal conduction timing, which is not the mechanism by which calcium disturbances affect the ECG. Calcium's principal ECG effect is on ventricular repolarisation duration (QT interval), not AV-nodal conduction.

## answer_d
QRS complex is widened

## explanation_d
Incorrect. QRS widening reflects a ventricular conduction problem such as bundle branch block or severe hyperkalaemia, not a calcium disturbance. Hypercalcaemia's characteristic ECG effect is specifically on the QT interval, not QRS duration.
