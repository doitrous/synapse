# Item

## id
QST-AU106ANAT-ADD-ADD-Q01

## title
First rib relations to the subclavian vessels and stellate ganglion

## question
Regarding the anatomical relations of the first rib, which of the following is correct?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The subclavian artery grooves the rib anterior to the scalenus anterior insertion, and the subclavian vein posterior to it

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This reverses the actual arrangement — the subclavian vein grooves the rib anterior to the scalenus anterior insertion (the scalene tubercle), while the artery and the lower trunk of the brachial plexus lie posterior to it.

## answer_b
The scalene tubercle separates a groove for the subclavian vein (anteriorly) from one for the subclavian artery and the lower trunk of the brachial plexus (posteriorly), and the stellate ganglion crosses the neck of the rib

## explanation_b
Correct. The scalene tubercle, the insertion of scalenus anterior on the inner border of the first rib, separates a shallow groove anteriorly for the subclavian vein from a deeper groove posteriorly for the subclavian artery and the lower trunk of the brachial plexus; the sympathetic stellate (cervicothoracic) ganglion lies just behind the neck of the rib, at the same level. This vein-anterior, artery-posterior arrangement around the scalene tubercle is the anatomical basis for how the two vessels are distinguished during first-rib resection or on a cervical-rib radiograph. Recognising the tubercle as the landmark separating the two grooves, and the stellate ganglion's relation to the rib's neck, is the discrimination this item tests.

## answer_c
The first rib gives no attachment to scalenus anterior; only scalenus medius attaches to it

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Scalenus anterior inserts squarely on the first rib's scalene tubercle; it is scalenus medius that inserts further back, behind the groove for the subclavian artery.

## answer_d
The phrenic nerve, not the stellate ganglion, is the structure that crosses the neck of the first rib

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The stellate ganglion, not the phrenic nerve, crosses the neck of the first rib; the phrenic nerve instead runs down over the front of scalenus anterior, more anteriorly.

## title
Costotransverse articular facet on the thoracic vertebra

## question
On a typical thoracic vertebra, which structure carries the costal facet that articulates with the tubercle of the corresponding rib?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The lamina

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The lamina is part of the vertebral arch that completes it in the midline; it carries no costal facet.

## answer_b
The transverse process

## explanation_b
Correct. Near the tip of the transverse process lies a costal facet that articulates with the tubercle of the numerically corresponding rib, forming the costotransverse joint — the vertebra's sole contribution to that joint (the rib head instead articulates with the body's demifacets at the costovertebral joint). Only the transverse process, not the lamina or spinous process, carries this articular facet. Distinguishing the transverse process from the pedicle and lamina by its lateral projection and articular tip is the objective this item tests.

## answer_c
The pedicle

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The pedicle is a short, thick strut connecting the vertebral body to the lamina; its role is structural, forming part of the intervertebral foramen, not articular.

## answer_d
The spinous process

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The long, steeply downward-sloping spinous process, formed where the two laminae meet in the midline, carries no costal facet.

# Item

## id
QST-AU106ANAT-ADD-ADD-Q04

## title
VAN order in the costal groove

## question
Within the costal groove of a typical rib, what is the order of the vein, artery and nerve from above downward?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Nerve, Artery, Vein

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This reverses the order entirely; the nerve lies lowest within the groove, not highest.

## answer_b
Artery, Vein, Nerve

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The vein lies uppermost within the groove, not the artery.

## answer_c
Vein, Artery, Nerve

## explanation_c
Correct. The costal groove runs along the inner aspect of a typical rib's lower border and shelters the main intercostal neurovascular bundle between the internal and innermost intercostal muscles; from above downward the order is Vein, Artery, Nerve, the mnemonic VAN. Because the bundle tucks up under the lower border of the rib above, a needle or chest-drain trocar is inserted just above the upper border of the rib below (the lower part of the intercostal space) to avoid it, the clinical basis of the "safe triangle" for thoracocentesis. Getting the VAN order right also explains why the nerve, lying lowest, is the structure most often spared by a needle correctly placed just above the lower rib.

## answer_d
Vein, Nerve, Artery

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The artery lies in the middle of the three, between the vein above and the nerve below, not at the bottom.

## id
QST-AU106ANAT-ADD-ADD-Q06

## title
Surface location of the cardiac apex beat

## question
Where is the cardiac apex beat normally palpated?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Left 4th intercostal space, midclavicular line

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The fourth intercostal space is one space too high; the normal apex beat sits at the fifth space.

## answer_b
Left 5th intercostal space, midclavicular line

## explanation_b
Correct. The apex of the heart, formed by the left ventricle — the only chamber that reaches the chest wall directly — normally lies in the left fifth intercostal space at or just medial to the midclavicular line, and this is where its impulse, the apex beat, is normally felt and heard. This point also happens to be the mitral auscultation area, though for a different reason (it is where the mitral valve's closure sound radiates loudest, not because the valve itself sits there). Locating the apex beat at the fifth left interspace, midclavicular line, is a basic clinical landmark for estimating heart size and rhythm.

## answer_c
Right 5th intercostal space, midclavicular line

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The apex beat is a left-sided finding, formed by the left ventricle; the right side of the chest is not where it is normally palpated.

## answer_d
Left 5th intercostal space, anterior axillary line

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The midclavicular line, not the more lateral anterior axillary line, is the normal landmark; a beat displaced to the anterior axillary line suggests cardiomegaly, not the normal position.

## title
Internal jugular vein position within the carotid sheath

## question
Within the carotid sheath, how does the internal jugular vein lie in relation to the common and internal carotid arteries?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Medial to both arteries

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Medial placement is not the internal jugular vein's position; the vein lies lateral to the carotid arteries, not medial.

## answer_b
Lateral to both arteries

## explanation_b
Correct. The internal jugular vein descends within the carotid sheath lateral to the common carotid artery below and the internal carotid artery above, alongside the vagus nerve, before draining into the corresponding brachiocephalic vein behind the sternoclavicular joint. This lateral relationship is why the internal jugular vein is the vessel most readily palpated and cannulated just lateral to the carotid pulse in the neck. Knowing the vein lies lateral, not medial or anterior, to the arterial pair within the sheath is the discrimination this item tests.

## answer_c
Anterior to both arteries

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The vein is not simply anterior to the arteries; all three structures (vein, arteries, vagus nerve) run together within the sheath with the vein lateral, not stacked front-to-back.

## answer_d
Posterior to both arteries

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Posterior placement does not describe the vein's position either; it lies lateral to the arterial pair, not behind them.

# Item

## id
QST-AU106ANAT-ADD-ADD-Q10

## title
Origin of the right common carotid artery

## question
Where does the right common carotid artery typically arise from?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Directly from the arch of the aorta

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Only the LEFT common carotid artery arises directly from the arch of the aorta. The Telegram addendum bank's own printed answer for this item states the right common carotid arises "from the arch of the aorta", but this is a genuine printed-key error, recorded here rather than taught as fact (see CON-CVS-CA12C007E2BEA2 conflicts) — the right vessel in fact arises one step downstream, from the brachiocephalic trunk.

## answer_b
The brachiocephalic (innominate) trunk

## explanation_b
Correct. The brachiocephalic (innominate) trunk, the first and largest branch of the aortic arch, ascends behind the right sternoclavicular joint and there divides into the right common carotid and right subclavian arteries; the LEFT common carotid, by contrast, arises directly from the arch as its second branch. Each common carotid artery then ascends within the carotid sheath without branching until it reaches the upper border of the thyroid cartilage (roughly C3/C4), where it bifurcates into the internal and external carotid arteries. Distinguishing the right common carotid's indirect origin, via the brachiocephalic trunk, from the left's direct origin from the arch is the discrimination this item tests — and the exact point the addendum bank's own printed key states incorrectly.

## answer_c
The right subclavian artery

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The right subclavian artery is the brachiocephalic trunk's other terminal branch, a sibling vessel to the right common carotid, not its parent.

## answer_d
The right internal thoracic artery

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The right internal thoracic artery is a first-part branch of the right subclavian artery, two steps removed from the aortic arch, not the origin of the common carotid.

QST-AU106ANAT-ADD-ADD-Q13

## title
Left brachiocephalic vein tributaries

## question
Which of the following is a named tributary of the left brachiocephalic vein, in addition to the left internal jugular and left subclavian veins that form it?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The left superior intercostal vein

## explanation_a
Correct. Along its oblique course behind the manubrium, in front of the aortic arch's three branches, the left brachiocephalic vein receives the left inferior thyroid vein and the left superior intercostal vein as named tributaries, together with the left internal thoracic vein and, at times, the thymic and pericardiacophrenic veins. This is why the left superior intercostal vein (sometimes visible on a chest radiograph as the "aortic nipple") drains into the left brachiocephalic vein rather than directly into the azygos system, unlike the lower posterior intercostal veins on the left, which drain via the hemiazygos system instead. Recognising this vein as one of the left brachiocephalic vein's own tributaries, distinct from the azygos system proper, is the fact this item tests.

## answer_b
The azygos vein

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The azygos vein is the superior vena cava's own sole named tributary, entering it directly at the second right costal cartilage — it is not a tributary of the left brachiocephalic vein.

## answer_c
The right superior intercostal vein

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The right superior intercostal vein drains into the right brachiocephalic vein (or directly into the azygos system), the mirror-image vessel on the opposite side, not into the left brachiocephalic vein.

## answer_d
The thoracic duct

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The thoracic duct empties at the left venous angle, the junction where the left internal jugular and left subclavian veins meet to form the left brachiocephalic vein, rather than as a tributary draining into the vein's body further along its course.

## title
External carotid versus thyrocervical trunk branches

## question
Which of the following is a branch of the external carotid artery, not the thyrocervical trunk?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Inferior thyroid artery

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The inferior thyroid artery is a thyrocervical trunk branch, not an external carotid branch — it is the counterpart to (and often confused with) the external carotid's own superior thyroid artery, together forming the thyroid gland's dual blood supply.

## answer_b
Transverse cervical artery

## answer_a
At L5, by the union of the two common iliac veins

## explanation_a
Correct. Behind the right sternoclavicular joint, by the union of the right internal jugular and right subclavian veins
