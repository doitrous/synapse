Glucose and galactose are:

## question
Glucose and galactose are:

## subject
fnd

## status

## owner
Admin team

## vignette


## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Anomers

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Anomers differ only at the anomeric carbon (C1 in an aldose), and glucose and galactose differ at C4, not at the anomeric carbon — so this pair cannot be anomers.

## answer_b
Constituents of sucrose

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Sucrose is built of glucose and fructose, not glucose and galactose — that pairing describes lactose.

## answer_c
D & L isomers

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Both glucose and galactose are D-sugars in human biochemistry; D/L describes mirror-image forms at the highest-numbered chiral centre, not this pair's relationship.

## answer_d
Epimers

## explanation_d
Correct. Glucose and galactose differ in configuration at exactly one carbon, C4, and nowhere else — that is the definition of epimers. This is the standard trap: students check that the two sugars 'look similar' rather than confirming they differ at exactly one specified, non-anomeric carbon.

Aldaric acid

## explanation_b
Incorrect. An aldaric (saccharic) acid results from oxidation at both ends of the chain (C1 and the terminal carbon), not at the terminal carbon alone.

Saccharic acid

Incorrect. 'Saccharic acid' is another name for an aldaric acid (oxidised at both ends), not for oxidation at one terminal carbon alone.

## question
Which of the following statements about carbohydrate attachment in glycoconjugates is correct?

## subject
fnd

## vignette


## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Carbohydrate in glycoproteins is always O-linked, through serine or threonine

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This overstates the rule: N-linked attachment, through the amide nitrogen of asparagine, is also common in glycoproteins, which is exactly what makes 'always O-linked' false.

Correct. Glycoprotein carbohydrate attaches by either linkage chemistry — O-glycosidic to the hydroxyl of serine or threonine, or N-glycosidic to the amide nitrogen of asparagine — so 'always O-linked' is false for glycoproteins specifically. Proteoglycans, by contrast, usually attach their GAG chains to the core protein by an O-linkage, which is the specific contrast the department's own bank draws between the two classes.

## answer_c
Carbohydrate in proteoglycans is always N-linked, through asparagine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Proteoglycan GAG chains are usually O-linked to their core protein, not N-linked; N-linkage through asparagine is the glycoprotein-side mechanism, and even there it is only one of two options, not the exclusive one.

## answer_d
Neither glycoproteins nor proteoglycans carry a glycosidic attachment to their protein core

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Both classes are defined by a covalent carbohydrate-to-protein attachment; that link is what makes each of them a glycoconjugate in the first place.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A deoxy sugar has lost a hydroxyl-bearing oxygen at one position; myoinositol's defining feature is instead its multiple hydroxyl groups on a cyclic ring.

## answer_b
Sugar Acid

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A sugar acid carries an oxidised carboxyl group (as in glucuronic or ascorbic acid); myoinositol carries no such acidic group.

## answer_c
Amino Sugar

## explanation_c
Incorrect. An amino sugar (glucosamine, galactosamine, mannosamine) carries a substituted amino group; myoinositol carries none.

## answer_d
Sugar Alcohol

## explanation_d
Correct. Myoinositol is classed among the sugar alcohols, alongside sorbitol, mannitol, ribitol and galactitol, despite being cyclic rather than a straight-chain reduction product — its defining feature is its multiple hydroxyl groups, which is what the sugar-alcohol class is built on.
