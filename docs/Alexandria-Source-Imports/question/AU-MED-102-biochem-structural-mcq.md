Glucose and galactose are:

## question
Glucose and galactose are:

## subject
fnd

## status

## vignette


## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Anomers

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Anomers differ only at the anomeric carbon (C1 in an aldose), and glucose and galactose differ at C4, not at the anomeric carbon — so this pair cannot be anomers.

## answer_b
Constituents of sucrose

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Sucrose is built of glucose and fructose, not glucose and galactose — that pairing describes lactose.

## answer_c
D & L isomers

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Both glucose and galactose are D-sugars in human biochemistry; D/L describes mirror-image forms at the highest-numbered chiral centre, not this pair's relationship.

## answer_d
Epimers

## explanation_d
Correct. Glucose and galactose differ in configuration at exactly one carbon, C4, and nowhere else — that is the definition of epimers. This is the standard trap: students check that the two sugars 'look similar' rather than confirming they differ at exactly one specified, non-anomeric carbon.

Incorrect. An aldaric (saccharic) acid results from oxidation at both ends of the chain (C1 and the terminal carbon), not at the terminal carbon alone.

Saccharic acid

Incorrect. 'Saccharic acid' is another name for an aldaric acid (oxidised at both ends), not for oxidation at one terminal carbon alone.

## question
Which of the following statements about carbohydrate attachment in glycoconjugates is correct?

## vignette


## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Carbohydrate in glycoproteins is always O-linked, through serine or threonine

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This overstates the rule: N-linked attachment, through the amide nitrogen of asparagine, is also common in glycoproteins, which is exactly what makes 'always O-linked' false.
