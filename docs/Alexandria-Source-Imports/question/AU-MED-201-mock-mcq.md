## title
Finding in primary hyperaldosteronism

## question
A young man presents with a blood pressure of 175/110 mmHg. He has a high circulating aldosterone level. Which of the following may be present in this patient?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Decreased cardiac output

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Aldosterone excess expands extracellular volume through renal sodium retention, which tends to raise, not lower, cardiac output and blood pressure — it does not depress cardiac output.

## answer_b
Hypokalemia

## explanation_b
Correct. Aldosterone acts on collecting duct principal cells to increase sodium reabsorption in exchange for increased potassium (and hydrogen) secretion. In sustained aldosterone excess this potassium wasting produces hypokalemia, the classic biochemical finding paired with the hypertension described here, and is the finding most directly explained by a high circulating aldosterone level.

## answer_c
Acidosis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The hydrogen ion secretion that accompanies aldosterone's sodium-potassium exchange tends to produce a mild metabolic alkalosis, not acidosis.

## answer_d
Hypercalcemia

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Aldosterone acts on renal sodium, potassium and hydrogen handling; it has no direct effect on calcium handling, so hypercalcemia is not an expected finding of aldosterone excess.

## title
Process inhibited in type I diabetes mellitus

## question
Which of the following is inhibited in diabetes mellitus type I?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Ketogenesis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. With insulin deficiency, cells rely on fatty-acid oxidation for fuel, and hepatic ketogenesis is enhanced (not inhibited), producing the ketoacidosis characteristic of untreated type I diabetes.

## answer_b
Gluconeogenesis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Unopposed glucagon and low insulin promote hepatic gluconeogenesis, raising blood glucose further; gluconeogenesis is enhanced, not inhibited, in insulin deficiency.

## answer_c
Glycogenolysis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Glycogen breakdown (glycogenolysis) is disinhibited when insulin is low, contributing to hyperglycemia; it is not the process that is inhibited.

## answer_d
Glycogenesis

## explanation_d
Correct. Insulin is the principal hormone driving glycogenesis (glycogen synthesis) in liver and muscle. In type I diabetes mellitus, absolute insulin deficiency removes this anabolic drive, so glycogenesis specifically is inhibited, while ketogenesis, gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis are all instead enhanced.

## title
Defining feature of the bicarbonate buffer system

## question
Which of the following best describes the bicarbonate buffer system?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The buffer releases more protons if pH of blood drops

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Buffering consumes free hydrogen ions as pH falls; the bicarbonate pair does not release additional protons in response to a falling pH.

## answer_b
The concentration of its components can be independently regulated

## explanation_b
Correct. The bicarbonate/carbonic-acid pair is physiologically powerful because it is an 'open system': CO2 (via carbonic acid) is regulated by the lungs through ventilation, while HCO3- is regulated independently by the kidneys through tubular reabsorption and regeneration. This independent, two-organ regulation — rather than the pair's intrinsic chemistry — is what makes it the body's most important extracellular buffer.

## answer_c
Its pKa is equal to blood pH

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The bicarbonate buffer's pKa is about 6.1, well below blood pH of 7.4; a buffer is normally most effective when its pKa is close to the working pH, so this is not what gives the bicarbonate system its physiological power.

## answer_d
Requires 1-2 days to readjust the blood pH

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The respiratory component of bicarbonate buffering (via ventilation) can adjust pH within minutes; a full renal compensation may take longer, but the system as a whole does not require a uniform 1-2 days to act.

## title
Nephron segment affected by ADH deficiency

## question
Deficiency of antidiuretic hormone will cause loss of water through which segment of the nephron?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Descending limb of Henle

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The descending limb of the loop of Henle is freely permeable to water independent of ADH, so its water handling is not the segment affected by ADH deficiency.

## answer_b
Ascending limb of Henle

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The thick ascending limb is water-impermeable regardless of ADH status (it is the segment that dilutes tubular fluid by reabsorbing solute without water); ADH has no water-permeability effect there to lose.

## answer_c
Distal convoluted tubules

## explanation_c
Correct. This source's own printed key attributes ADH-deficiency water loss to the distal convoluted tubule. ADH acts via V2 receptors to insert aquaporin-2 water channels in this segment, and without ADH the segment stays relatively water-impermeable, so the filtrate reaching it is not concentrated and water is lost in the urine — producing the polyuria of diabetes insipidus. (Most texts describe this ADH-sensitive water reabsorption as occurring in the late distal tubule together with the collecting duct as one functional unit; this source's key names the distal convoluted tubule specifically.)

## answer_d
Proximal convoluted tubules

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Proximal convoluted tubule water reabsorption (about 65% of the filtered load) is obligatory and essentially independent of ADH; it is not the ADH-regulated segment.

## title
Histological feature of pancreatic islet alpha cells

## question
Which of the following is a histological feature of a cells of pancreatic islets?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer

## answer_a
Peripherally located

## explanation_a
Correct. Alpha (A) cells, which secrete glucagon, are characteristically located at the periphery of the pancreatic islet, forming an outer rim around the more centrally-placed, more numerous beta cells. This peripheral position is the histological feature that identifies alpha cells on a stained section.

## answer_b
The least numerous

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Being the least numerous islet cell type describes delta (D) cells, which secrete somatostatin, not alpha cells (which are the second most numerous islet cell type after beta cells).

Small in size

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Alpha cells are not characteristically distinguished by small size; their distinguishing feature on histology is their peripheral position, not their dimensions.

## answer_d
Surrounded by sinusoidal capillaries

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Fenestrated sinusoidal capillaries supply the islet as a whole, shared by all islet cell types, not a feature specific to alpha cells.

## title
Intracellular messenger of alpha-1 adrenergic receptors

## question
Which of the following is the intracellular messenger of a1-adrenergic catecholamines?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
cGMP

## explanation_a
Incorrect. cGMP is the second messenger of the nitric oxide/atrial natriuretic peptide signalling pathways, not of alpha-1 adrenergic receptor stimulation.

## answer_b
Calcium or phosphatidyl inositol

## explanation_b
Correct. Alpha-1 adrenergic receptors are Gq-protein-coupled: catecholamine binding activates phospholipase C, which hydrolyses membrane phosphatidylinositol to generate IP3 and diacylglycerol, raising intracellular calcium. This calcium/phosphatidylinositol pathway is the a1 receptor's intracellular messenger system.

## answer_c
cAMP

## explanation_c
Incorrect. cAMP is the second messenger of beta-adrenergic (Gs-coupled, cAMP-raising) receptors, and is instead lowered by alpha-2 (Gi-coupled) receptors — a1 receptors do not signal through cAMP.

## answer_d
Tyrosine kinase

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Tyrosine kinase signalling is characteristic of growth factor and insulin receptors, not of alpha-1 adrenergic receptors, which are G-protein-coupled rather than enzyme-linked receptors.

GH

## explanation_b

## title
Muscles at risk from perineal body damage

## question
If the perineal body was damaged during an incision to prevent uncontrolled tearing during labor, the function of which of the following muscles might be impaired?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Deep transverse perineal and obturator internus

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The deep transverse perineal muscle sits in the deep perineal pouch above the perineal membrane, not inserting into the perineal body, and obturator internus is a pelvic sidewall muscle unrelated to the perineal body.

## answer_b
Ischiocavernosus and sphincter urethrae

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Ischiocavernosus attaches to the ischiopubic ramus and the crus of the clitoris/penis, not the perineal body, and sphincter urethrae (external urethral sphincter) sits in the deep perineal pouch, not inserting into the perineal body.

## answer_c
Bulbospongiosus and ischiocavernosus

## explanation_c
Incorrect. While bulbospongiosus does insert into the perineal body, ischiocavernosus does not — it attaches instead to the ischiopubic ramus and crus, so this pairing is incorrect.

## answer_d
Bulbospongiosus and superficial transverse perineal

## explanation_d
Correct. The bulbospongiosus and superficial transverse perineal muscles, both of the superficial perineal pouch, converge into and interlace with the perineal body (the central tendon of the perineum). Because these two muscles physically insert into it, an episiotomy or tear through the perineal body during labor risks their function specifically, unlike the deep-pouch muscles or ischiocavernosus.

## title
Hormonal deficiency causing proportionate short stature with hypogonadism

## question
A mother observed that her 16-year-old boy is much shorter than her 10-year-old sister but his grades in school were fine and were matching with his colleagues. Physical examination reveals a proportionate growth rate, normal mental functions, and hypogonadism. Which of the following hormonal deficiencies could be responsible for this condition?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
GH & GnRH

## explanation_a
Correct. Proportionate short stature with normal cognition plus hypogonadism/delayed puberty fits combined growth hormone (GH) and gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH)/gonadotropin deficiency. GH deficiency explains the proportionate growth failure without impairing intelligence, while GnRH/gonadotropin deficiency separately explains the hypogonadism. Together the two deficiencies account for the full clinical picture, which is why this combination — rather than either hormone alone — is the best answer.

## answer_b
GH

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Isolated GH deficiency alone would explain the proportionate short stature but does not account for the hypogonadism described in this vignette.

## answer_c
Insulin

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Insulin deficiency produces the metabolic derangements of diabetes mellitus, not proportionate short stature with normal cognition and hypogonadism.

## answer_d
Thyroid hormones

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Thyroid hormone deficiency (hypothyroidism) would typically also impair mental function and school performance, and produce disproportionate rather than purely proportionate growth failure, unlike this vignette's normal grades and proportionate growth.

Physiology

## subtopic

Function of the distal convoluted tubule

## question
What is the function of the distal convoluted tubules?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Determine the final composition of urine

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Determining the final composition of urine better describes the collecting duct's role as the last major site of regulated adjustment, downstream of the distal convoluted tubule.

## answer_b
Reabsorb sodium in exchange for hydrogen or potassium ions

## explanation_b
Correct. The distal convoluted tubule, particularly its aldosterone-sensitive late segment, reabsorbs sodium via the thiazide-sensitive Na+/Cl- cotransporter and principal-cell sodium channels. This sodium reabsorption is functionally coupled to potassium and hydrogen ion secretion into the tubular lumen under aldosterone's control. Secondary active secretion of hydrogen ions into the tubular lumen

Reabsorb about 50 percent of the water filtered by the glomeruli

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Reabsorbing about 50 percent of filtered water understates the proximal tubule's roughly 65% obligatory water reabsorption, and the distal convoluted tubule's early segment is instead relatively water-impermeable.

## question
A 50-year-old male patient was brought to the emergency unit in coma. His wife mentioned that he had been diabetic for 10 years. Deep & rapid breathing was noticed by the physician. ## correct_answer

Uncompensated metabolic alkalosis

## explanation_a

Compensated metabolic acidosis

## explanation_b
Correct. The low HCO3- (18 mEq/L) identifies the primary disturbance as metabolic acidosis, from accumulating ketoacid anions in this diabetic patient. Uncompensated respiratory acidosis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Respiratory acidosis would show an elevated, not decreased, PaCO2 as the primary disturbance; this patient's low PaCO2 instead reflects compensation for a metabolic (not respiratory) acidosis.
