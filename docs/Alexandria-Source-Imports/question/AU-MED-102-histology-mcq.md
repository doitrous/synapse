## question
The process where the cell breaks down its malfunctioning organelles

## subject

## status

## vignette


## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
autophagy

## explanation_a
Correct. Autophagy is the lysosome-dependent pathway by which a cell encloses its own worn-out or malfunctioning organelles in a membrane-bound vacuole and digests them, recycling the components. It is the cell's own internal quality-control mechanism, distinct from any process that brings material in from outside.

## answer_b
phagocytosis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Phagocytosis is the uptake of large particulate material — bacteria, cell debris — from outside the cell. This question is about the cell disposing of its own internal organelles, which never crossed the plasma membrane from outside, so this is the wrong direction of traffic entirely.

## answer_c
Receptor mediated endocytosis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Receptor-mediated endocytosis, like phagocytosis, brings a specific extracellular ligand into the cell via a coated vesicle. It is still material entering from outside, not the cell degrading its own organelles.

## answer_d
Pinocytosis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Pinocytosis is the non-selective uptake of extracellular fluid and small solutes. Like the other two distractors, it moves material inward from outside the cell rather than describing the cell recycling its own components.

## question
The central core of the nucleosome is……

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Alexandria content lane (W1-102-HIST)

## vignette


## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
DNA molecules

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The DNA is what winds around the nucleosome's core; it is not the core itself. Calling the DNA the core reverses the structural relationship the question is testing.

## answer_b
Eight histones

## explanation_b
Correct. The nucleosome's central core is an octamer of eight histone molecules (two each of H2A, H2B, H3 and H4), around which roughly two turns of DNA are wound. This packaging unit, repeated along the chromosome, is what gives chromatin its 'beads on a string' appearance.

## answer_c
Four histone

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Four histones would be half an octamer — one of each core histone type rather than two of each. The core is built from eight molecules, not four.

## answer_d
DNA linker

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Linker DNA is the stretch of DNA between two adjacent nucleosomes, associated with histone H1 — it is a separate structure from the nucleosome's own eight-histone core.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Mitosis has the spindle-assembly checkpoint, which checks chromosome attachment to the spindle — a different checkpoint entirely from the G1 restriction point.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The exocrine pancreas is a merocrine gland — its acinar cells release secretory vesicles by exocytosis without losing any apical cytoplasm, which is the defining feature merocrine secretion is contrasted against apocrine secretion on.

## answer_b
Sebaceous gland

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The sebaceous gland is the classic example of holocrine secretion: the whole cell disintegrates to release its lipid content, which is a more extreme loss of cellular material than the apical pinching-off seen in apocrine glands.

## answer_c
Mammary gland

## explanation_c
Correct. The mammary gland secretes by the apocrine mechanism: part of the apical cytoplasm pinches off together with the secretory product, so the cell loses a small portion of itself with each release (unlike merocrine secretion, which loses none, or holocrine secretion, which loses the whole cell). This is also the mechanism behind apocrine sweat glands, whose secretion is viscous and initially odourless but becomes offensive once skin bacteria act on it.

## answer_d
Salivary gland

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The salivary glands are merocrine glands, releasing their secretion by vesicle exocytosis with no loss of apical cytoplasm — the same mechanism as most exocrine glands, and the opposite of the apocrine 'pinch-off' this question tests.
