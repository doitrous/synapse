## title
Muscle spared in facial nerve palsy

## question
Which of the following muscle is spared in case of facial nerve palsy?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Sternomastoid

## explanation_a
Correct. Sternocleidomastoid is a spinal accessory nerve (CN XI) muscle, not a facial-nerve (CN VII) muscle, so it is spared even in a complete lower motor neuron facial nerve palsy (Bell's palsy). This is a useful bedside distinguisher: weakness confined to the facial-nerve muscles (forehead, eye closure, cheek, lips) with a normal shoulder shrug and head-turn points to CN VII, not CN XI, pathology.

## answer_b
Stapedius

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Stapedius, which dampens ossicular chain movement, is a second-arch (facial nerve) muscle and is paralysed in a facial nerve lesion proximal to its branch, producing hyperacusis.

## answer_c
Buccinator

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Buccinator is a muscle of facial expression, a facial-nerve (CN VII) muscle, and is weakened in facial nerve palsy, contributing to food pooling in the cheek.

## answer_d
Orbicularis oculi

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Orbicularis oculi is a facial-nerve muscle; its weakness in facial palsy causes incomplete eye closure (lagophthalmos).

## title
Left S1 destruction and contralateral astereognosis

## question
A patient had destruction of somatic sensory area I on the left side. This will show:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Total lack of pain sensation on the same side

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A cortical S1 lesion does not abolish crude pain sensation, which is registered subcortically via the spinothalamic tract's thalamic and brainstem relays; it is also on the wrong (same, not opposite) side for any spinothalamic-mediated deficit.

## answer_b
Total lack of temperature sensation of the same side

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Crude temperature sensation similarly survives a cortical lesion via subcortical relay, and would in any case be contralateral (right-sided), not same-sided, if a spinothalamic deficit were being described.

## answer_c
Inability to identify objects put in the left hand

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Since somatosensory input has already crossed the midline before reaching the cortex, a LEFT S1 lesion produces its discriminative-touch deficit in the RIGHT hand, not the left.

## answer_d
Inability to identify objects put in the right hand

## explanation_d
Correct. Left S1 receives touch, pressure and proprioceptive input that has already decussated in the medial lemniscus, so its destruction abolishes cortical discriminative touch on the RIGHT side, producing astereognosis — inability to identify an object by touch alone — in the right hand, while crude pain and temperature sensation survive via subcortical pathways. This contralateral pattern is the same logic used to localise any unilateral primary somatosensory cortex lesion.

Destruction of the circular area and word blindness

## question
Which of the following may occur due to destruction of the circular area?

## subject

## status

## vignette

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Broca's aphasia results from a lesion of the inferior frontal gyrus (Broca's area), an expressive-language area well anterior to the parieto-temporo-occipital "circular area" this question tests.

## answer_b
Agraphia

## explanation_b
Incorrect as the single best answer here — agraphia can accompany a parieto-temporo-occipital junction lesion, but the classic, specifically-named deficit of this circular area/angular gyrus region is alexia (word blindness), the option this source's key selects.

## answer_c
Word blindness

## explanation_c
Correct. The angular gyrus and the surrounding parieto-temporo-occipital junction — the region sometimes drawn on teaching diagrams as a border-zone "circular area" around the posterior end of the lateral (Sylvian) fissure — integrate visual, auditory and somatosensory association cortex needed for reading. Its destruction classically produces alexia (word blindness), the inability to read written language despite intact vision, distinct from Broca's expressive aphasia or a primary hearing deficit.

## answer_d
Loss of hearing

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Loss of hearing would require a primary auditory cortex (superior temporal gyrus) or auditory pathway lesion, not destruction of the parieto-temporo-occipital circular area.

## title
The exception in Brown-Séquard syndrome

## question
Which of the following isn't related to Brown-Séquard syndrome?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Loss of vibration sense on the opposite side below the level of the lesion

## explanation_a
Correct — this is the false statement, the one 'not related to' the syndrome. Vibration and proprioception are carried by the dorsal columns, which ascend UNCROSSED until they reach the gracile/cuneate nuclei of the medulla. A cord hemisection therefore loses these modalities IPSILATERAL (same side), not the opposite side, to the lesion, below its level — the reverse of what this statement claims.

## answer_b
Loss of voluntary movements on the same side below the level of the lesion

## explanation_b
This statement IS true, so it is not the answer: the corticospinal tract has already crossed above the spinal cord, so hemisection produces ipsilateral upper motor neuron weakness below the lesion.

## answer_c
Loss of reflex movements on the same side at the level of the lesion

## explanation_c
This statement IS true, so it is not the answer: at the exact segmental level of the lesion, ipsilateral lower motor neuron signs (including loss of segmental reflexes) occur from direct damage to the anterior horn/root at that level.

## answer_d
Loss of pain sensation on the opposite side below the level of the lesion

## explanation_d
This statement IS true, so it is not the answer: the spinothalamic tract crosses within a segment or two of entry, so its loss (pain/temperature) is CONTRALATERAL below the lesion — the exact mirror-image pattern to the ipsilateral dorsal-column loss described in option A.

## title
Structure preventing alpha motor neuron activity

## question
Which of the following is used to prevent the activity of alpha motor neurons?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

C

Gamma motor neuron

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Gamma motor neurons innervate the muscle spindle's own intrafusal fibers, keeping the spindle sensitive to stretch; they do not directly inhibit alpha motor neurons.

Primary motor area

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The primary motor area is a source of voluntary EXCITATORY drive to alpha motor neurons via the corticospinal tract, not an inhibitory structure.

Golgi tendon organ

## explanation_c
Correct. The inverse myotatic (autogenic inhibition) reflex, which protects the muscle-tendon unit from excessive tension.

Flower spray afferent

Incorrect. The flower-spray (group II) afferent is a sensory ending of the muscle spindle itself, signalling static muscle length; it participates in the excitatory stretch reflex, not in inhibiting alpha motor neurons.

## title
The exception in the cerebellar cortex molecular layer

## question
Which of the following isn't found in the molecular layer of the cerebellar cortex?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Stellate cells

## explanation_a
This IS found in the molecular layer, so it is not the answer: stellate cell bodies and processes reside in the molecular layer.

## answer_b
Dendrites of granule cells

## explanation_b
Correct. Granule cell DENDRITES remain confined to the granular layer, where they synapse with incoming mossy fibers within cerebellar glomeruli; it is the granule cell's own T-shaped AXONS (the parallel fibers) that ascend into and run through the molecular layer, not their dendrites. This distinction is a classic source of confusion, since both structures belong to the same granule cell but occupy different cerebellar cortex layers.

## answer_c
Purkinje dendrites

## explanation_c
This IS found in the molecular layer, so it is not the answer: Purkinje cell dendritic trees fan out extensively through the molecular layer.

## answer_d
Dendrites of Golgi cells

## explanation_d
This IS found in the molecular layer, so it is not the answer: Golgi cell dendrites extend up into the molecular layer to receive parallel fiber input.

## title
Rods vs cones — low-intensity light sensitivity

## question
When compared with the cones of the retina, the rods:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Are more sensitive to low-intensity light

## explanation_a
Correct. Rods have a lower threshold and higher amplification (via their phototransduction cascade and greater convergence onto bipolar/ganglion cells) than cones, making them far more sensitive to low-intensity (scotopic, dim-light/night) vision. This greater sensitivity comes at the cost of spatial resolution, since many rods converge onto a single ganglion cell, unlike the near one-to-one wiring of foveal cones.

## answer_b
Adapt to darkness before the cones

## explanation_b
Incorrect — reversed. Cones adapt to darkness FIRST (over a few minutes) and reach a plateau, after which rods continue adapting more slowly to reach an even lower final threshold; rods do not adapt before cones.

## answer_c
Are most highly concentrated on the fovea

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Cones, not rods, are most highly concentrated at the fovea; rods are instead concentrated in the peripheral retina.

## answer_d
Are primarily involved in color vision

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Cones, not rods, mediate color vision; rods are monochromatic (scotopic) receptors.
