## title
The following statements on the glucuronic acid pathway are true EXCEPT

## question
The following statements on the glucuronic acid pathway are true EXCEPT:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## format
single best answer

## derived_from
AFM Biochemistry Questions, Carbohydrate Metabolism Q1 (src_01ab4268402d32d4d111, p25).

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
It can be synthesised in the human body from active glucose

## explanation_a
Incorrect as an EXCEPT answer — this statement is true, not the exception. The pathway does run from glucose 6-phosphate through UDP-glucose (activated, or "active," glucose) to UDP-glucuronic acid in every human, so this is not the false statement the question is looking for.

## answer_b
It can be used in the synthesis of L-ascorbic acid in the human body

## explanation_b
Correct — this is the false statement. In most mammals the pathway continues one further step, from UDP-glucuronic acid to L-ascorbic acid, but humans (and other primates and guinea pigs) lack the enzyme that carries out that last conversion, L-gulonolactone oxidase. The pathway itself is not switched off — it still runs fully for conjugation — only this one branch is closed, which is exactly why vitamin C must come from the diet in humans and does not need to in most other mammals. The thing to hold onto is that a missing single enzyme, not a missing pathway, explains the whole fact.

## answer_c
It is used in detoxication of benzoate

## explanation_c
Incorrect as an EXCEPT answer — this is true. UDP-glucuronic acid is the activated glucuronic acid donor for conjugating a wide range of xenobiotics, including benzoate, so the liver can excrete them; this is one of the two jobs the pathway keeps doing in humans even without the ascorbate branch.

## answer_d
It conjugates with bilirubin in the liver

## explanation_d
Incorrect as an EXCEPT answer — this is true and is the pathway's other main human job: UDP-glucuronyl transferase conjugates bilirubin with glucuronic acid to make it water-soluble for biliary excretion, a step that fails in neonatal and obstructive jaundice.

## title
The malate shuttle is necessary for

## question
Malate shuttle is necessary for:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## format
single best answer

## derived_from
AFM Biochemistry Questions, Carbohydrate Metabolism Q17 (src_01ab4268402d32d4d111, p27).

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Gluconeogenesis

## explanation_a
Correct. Pyruvate carboxylase makes oxaloacetate inside the mitochondrion, but oxaloacetate has no transporter of its own across the inner mitochondrial membrane, and gluconeogenesis needs it in the cytosol for the next step. The malate shuttle solves this: mitochondrial malate dehydrogenase reduces oxaloacetate to malate, which crosses the membrane, and cytosolic malate dehydrogenase oxidises it back to oxaloacetate — the only route this carbon skeleton has out of the mitochondrion. This is why gluconeogenesis specifically, and not glycolysis or glycogen breakdown, depends on this shuttle.

## answer_b
Providing NADPH

## explanation_b
Incorrect. NADPH for biosynthesis comes mainly from the hexose monophosphate pathway, not from this shuttle; the malate shuttle's cytosolic reaction actually produces NADH, not NADPH, and that NADH is consumed by gluconeogenesis itself.

## answer_c
ATP generation

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The malate shuttle moves a carbon skeleton across a membrane; it neither consumes nor generates ATP directly, unlike the substrate-level phosphorylation steps of glycolysis or the citric acid cycle.

## answer_d
Glycogenolysis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Glycogen breakdown releases glucose 1-phosphate directly from glycogen and has no mitochondrial step at all, so it has no need for a shuttle moving material out of the mitochondrion.

## title
ATP molecules required to convert 2 pyruvate into glucose

## question
How many ATP molecules are required to convert 2 molecules of pyruvate into glucose?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## format
single best answer

## derived_from
AFM Biochemistry Questions, Carbohydrate Metabolism Q20 (src_01ab4268402d32d4d111, p27).

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Two

## explanation_a
Incorrect — this counts only the two ATP spent at the two pyruvate carboxylase reactions (one per pyruvate) and forgets the two further spending steps gluconeogenesis needs downstream.

## answer_b
Three

## explanation_b
Incorrect — this does not match any consistent accounting of the pathway's three ATP/GTP-spending steps run twice (once per pyruvate); it undercounts the true total.

## answer_c
Six

## explanation_c
Correct. Building one glucose from two pyruvate costs six high-energy phosphate bonds: two ATP at the two pyruvate carboxylase reactions, two GTP at the two phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase reactions, and two more ATP at the two reversed phosphoglycerate kinase reactions. Every one of the three spending steps happens twice because two three-carbon pyruvate molecules are needed to build one six-carbon glucose. This is why gluconeogenesis only proceeds when the liver's energy state — usually driven by active fatty acid oxidation — can afford it.

## answer_d
Eight

## explanation_d
Incorrect — this overcounts the true six-bond total, most likely by double-counting one of the three spending steps or by adding an ATP the pathway does not actually spend.

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Defective enzyme is aldolase-B

## explanation_a
Incorrect as an EXCEPT answer — this is true. Hereditary fructose intolerance is deficiency of aldolase B, the enzyme that splits fructose 1-phosphate after fructokinase has already made it.

## answer_b
Fructose-1-phosphate accumulates

## explanation_b
Incorrect as an EXCEPT answer — this is true and is the central mechanism. With aldolase B missing, fructose 1-phosphate cannot be split further and accumulates in the liver, sequestering phosphate and depleting ATP.

## answer_c
Glycogen phosphorylase is inhibited

## explanation_c
Incorrect as an EXCEPT answer, per the department's own key — the disease's ATP and phosphate depletion is taught here as inhibiting glycogenolysis (via glycogen phosphorylase) as well as gluconeogenesis, contributing to the hypoglycaemia that follows fructose ingestion.

## answer_d
Urine is free from fructose

## explanation_d
Correct — this is the false statement, and the point the question turns on. Because fructose cannot be processed past the aldolase B block, it is not cleared metabolically and spills into the urine — fructosuria is a feature of the disease, not its absence. A student who reasons "the enzyme block should stop fructose appearing anywhere abnormal" has the direction backwards: the block is what causes the abnormal buildup and spillover in the first place.

## title
Classifying a patient's oral glucose tolerance test result

## question
When a standard oral glucose tolerance test is done, the blood glucose levels of the patient were found to be as follows: 0 min (fasting) = 120 mg/dl; 60 min = 170 mg/dl; 120 min = 150 mg/dl. The patient has:

## subject
endo

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette
A patient undergoes a standard 75 g oral glucose tolerance test as part of a diabetes work-up.

## format
single best answer

## derived_from
AFM Biochemistry Questions, Carbohydrate Metabolism Q79 (src_01ab4268402d32d4d111, p36), tagged "Practical" in the source.

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Normal glucose tolerance

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A two-hour value of 150 mg/dL is above the normal upper limit of 140 mg/dL, so this result cannot be classified as normal.

## answer_b
Impaired glucose tolerance

## explanation_b
Correct. The test is classified mainly by the two-hour value: 150 mg/dL falls in the 140-199 mg/dL band, which is impaired glucose tolerance, not diabetes. The one-hour peak of 170 mg/dL is higher, but the test's interpretation turns on the two-hour sample, not the peak — reading the peak instead of the two-hour value is the commonest way to misclassify a result like this one. The fasting value (120 mg/dL) is also above the usual normal cut-off, but it remains below the diabetic threshold of 126 mg/dL, so it does not push the classification into diabetes on its own.

## answer_c
Mild diabetes mellitus

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Diabetes requires a two-hour value of 200 mg/dL or more (or a fasting value of 126 mg/dL or more); 150 mg/dL at two hours does not meet that threshold, however elevated the one-hour peak looked.

## answer_d
Severe diabetes mellitus

## explanation_d
Incorrect for the same reason as mild diabetes, and additionally there is no "severe" diabetes category defined by an OGTT reading — diabetes is diagnosed as a single threshold-based category, not graded by the test itself.

## title
Which derivative of choline forms phosphatidylcholine with diacylglycerol

## question
Phosphatidyl choline is synthesized by combining diacylglycerol with activated choline. Which derivative of choline is used for this reaction?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## format
single best answer

## derived_from
AFM Biochemistry Questions, Lipid Metabolism Q49 (src_01ab4268402d32d4d111, p50).

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
UDP-choline

## explanation_a
Incorrect. UDP is the activating nucleotide for glucose and galactose in glycogen and lactose synthesis, not for choline; this option tests whether a student has correctly kept the nucleotide carriers of different pathways separate.

## answer_b
GDP-choline

## explanation_b
Incorrect. GDP is the nucleotide carrier in a small number of other biosynthetic contexts (such as GDP-mannose or GDP-fucose in glycoprotein synthesis), never for choline activation.

## answer_c
ATP-choline

## explanation_c
Incorrect. ATP does phosphorylate choline to phosphocholine as the first step, but that is not yet the "activated choline" that reacts with diacylglycerol — the further activation step uses CTP, not ATP directly, to make the true donor molecule.

## answer_d
CDP-choline

## explanation_d
Correct. Phosphocholine (made from choline and ATP by choline kinase) is activated by CTP to CDP-choline, and it is this CDP-choline that combines with diacylglycerol — releasing CMP — to form phosphatidylcholine (lecithin). Naming the correct nucleotide is the entire point of this question, since the reaction's other two participants (diacylglycerol, choline) are given in the stem.

## question
For the direct synthesis of lecithin from diacylglycerol, the following compound is required:

## subject

## correct_answer

## answer_a
CDP-inositol

## explanation_a
Incorrect. CDP-inositol-type activation is not part of mainstream human phospholipid synthesis in the way CDP-choline and CDP-ethanolamine are; this option is a plausible-sounding but incorrect distractor built on the same "CDP-" pattern.

## answer_b
UDP-G

## explanation_b
Incorrect. UDP-glucose is the activated sugar donor for glycogen synthesis and other glucosyl-transfer reactions; it has no role in attaching a choline head group to diacylglycerol.

CDP-choline

## explanation_c
Correct. Direct synthesis of lecithin from diacylglycerol requires CDP-choline, which donates its phosphocholine group to diacylglycerol, releasing CMP and forming phosphatidylcholine. This is the same fact tested from a third angle in this bank — naming the required activated compound rather than the product it makes or the nucleotide within it.

## answer_d
ATP

## explanation_d
Incorrect. ATP is consumed earlier in the pathway, phosphorylating choline to phosphocholine, but it is not itself the compound that reacts directly with diacylglycerol.

## title
Death due to cyanide poisoning is a result of

## question
Death due to cyanide poisoning is a result of:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## format
single best answer

## derived_from
AFM Biochemistry Questions, Bioenergetics Q3 (src_01ab4268402d32d4d111, p54).

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Cyanide hemoglobin complex formation

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This is the mechanism of carbon monoxide poisoning, not cyanide — cyanide does not form a stable complex with haemoglobin, and this is the specific misconception the question is built to catch.

## answer_b
Cyanide inhibiting complex I of respiratory chain

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Cyanide's target is Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase), not Complex I; naming the wrong complex would still leave the "blocked electron transport chain" idea right but the specific site wrong, which is the finer distinction this question and its companion (Q12) test.

## answer_c
Cyanide inhibiting cytochrome oxidase

## explanation_c
Correct. Cyanide binds the ferric iron of cytochrome a3 within cytochrome c oxidase (Complex IV), the electron transport chain's terminal, oxygen-accepting enzyme. With this step blocked, electrons back up through the whole chain, oxidative phosphorylation stops, and cells switch to anaerobic glycolysis and lactate production despite blood oxygen content being essentially normal — histotoxic hypoxia. Tissues most dependent on oxidative ATP production, brain and heart, fail within minutes, which is why cyanide poisoning is so rapidly lethal.

## answer_d
Cyanide blocking oxygen transport in blood

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Cyanide's lethal action is intracellular and enzymatic; blood oxygen carriage and delivery are essentially unaffected, which is exactly what makes cyanide poisoning a diagnostic trap — a patient can have normal-looking oxygen saturation while cellular energy production has already stopped.

QST-FND-CYANIDE-COMPLEX-IV-002

## title
Location where cyanide inhibits cytochrome oxidation in the electron transport chain

## question
Cyanide poisoning inhibits cytochrome oxidation at which of the following locations in the electron transport chain?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## format
single best answer

## derived_from
AFM Biochemistry Questions, Bioenergetics Q12 (src_01ab4268402d32d4d111, p55).

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Complex I

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase) is where NADH enters the chain; cyanide does not act there, and inhibiting Complex I alone would still allow FADH2-linked electrons to enter downstream, which is not what happens in cyanide poisoning.

## answer_b
Complex II

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Complex II (succinate dehydrogenase) feeds electrons from FADH2 into the chain but is not a proton-pumping or cyanide-sensitive site; it is not where cytochrome oxidation is blocked.

## answer_c
Complex III

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Complex III (cytochrome bc1) sits upstream of the true target and is not where cyanide acts, even though it also carries cytochromes; confusing it with Complex IV is a common error because both are "cytochrome-containing" complexes.

## answer_d
Complex IV

## explanation_d
Correct. Complex IV, cytochrome c oxidase, is the electron transport chain's terminal enzyme, transferring electrons to molecular oxygen at cytochrome a3's ferric iron. Cyanide binds this iron and blocks that final transfer, which is why the whole chain backs up upstream of it — Complex IV is the one and only site of cyanide's inhibition, not any of the three complexes before it.
