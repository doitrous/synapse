# Item

## id
QST-MSK-AU105-ANAT-FEMSHEATH

## title
The one structure that is not a content of the femoral sheath

## question
All of the following are contents of the femoral sheath EXCEPT:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Import queue

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Femoral artery

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The femoral artery occupies the sheath's own lateral compartment, one of the three the sheath is divided into by its two vertical septa.

## answer_b
Femoral nerve

## explanation_b
Correct. The femoral nerve lies lateral to and outside the femoral sheath entirely, in the groove between iliacus and psoas major, because it is covered only by fascia iliaca rather than being enclosed within the sheath's fascia transversalis/fascia iliaca tube. All three of the sheath's own compartments — artery, vein and canal — are accounted for by the other three options, which is what makes the nerve the one genuine exception. This is also why a femoral nerve block is placed lateral to the femoral artery, outside the sheath, while anaesthetising the vessels themselves means entering the sheath directly.

## answer_c
Femoral vein

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The femoral vein occupies the sheath's intermediate compartment, between the artery laterally and the canal medially.

## answer_d
Femoral canal

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The femoral canal is the sheath's medial compartment — the empty one, containing only fat and lymphatics (including the node of Cloquet) — which is exactly what makes it the route of a femoral hernia.

QST-MSK-AU105-ANAT-SAPHOPENING

## title
The one false statement about the saphenous opening

## question
All of the following are TRUE as regards the saphenous opening EXCEPT:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Import queue

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
It is covered by the cribriform fascia

## explanation_a
Incorrect (i.e. this is true, so not the exception). The cribriform fascia is the sieve-like layer that bridges the opening, perforated by the great saphenous vein and lymphatics.

## answer_b
It lies one and a half inches below and lateral to the pubic tubercle

## explanation_b
Incorrect (true, not the exception). This is the standard surface-anatomy location given for the saphenous opening, and the same landmark used to distinguish a femoral hernia's exit point from an inguinal one.

## answer_c
Its medial margin (falciform margin) is sharp

## explanation_c
Correct — this is the false statement. The falciform margin is the opening's lateral edge, continuous with the iliotibial tract, and it is that lateral margin which is sharp; the medial margin is rounded. Reversing which margin is sharp and which is medial is the exact substitution this item is testing. A surgeon exposing the saphenofemoral junction relies on this distinction to identify the lateral, sharp falciform edge without mistaking the softer medial border for it.

## answer_d
The great saphenous vein passes through it

## explanation_d
Incorrect (true, not the exception). The great saphenous vein crosses the saphenous opening to pierce the cribriform fascia and drain into the femoral vein at the saphenofemoral junction.

## title
The one structure that does not pass through the greater sciatic foramen

## question
All of the following pass through the greater sciatic foramen EXCEPT:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer

## answer_a
Piriformis muscle

## explanation_a
Incorrect (true, not the exception). Piriformis itself passes through the greater sciatic foramen, dividing it into a compartment above and below the muscle.

## answer_b
Inferior gluteal nerve

## explanation_b
Incorrect (true, not the exception). The inferior gluteal nerve leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen, below piriformis, to supply gluteus maximus.

## answer_c
Pudendal nerve

## explanation_c
Incorrect (true, not the exception). The pudendal nerve does leave the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen (below piriformis) — but it does not stay in the gluteal region; it hooks around the ischial spine and re-enters the perineum through the lesser sciatic foramen, the classic "in through the big door, out through the small one" pattern.

## answer_d
Tendon of obturator internus muscle

## explanation_d
Correct. The tendon of obturator internus leaves the pelvis through the lesser sciatic foramen, not the greater one — it is the nerve to obturator internus that travels with piriformis through the greater foramen before also re-entering through the lesser foramen to reach the muscle's tendon in the gluteal region. The muscle belly itself sits inside the pelvis on obturator internus's own fascia, so only its nerve, never its tendon, ever passes through the greater foramen.

All of the following muscles are dorsiflexors for the foot EXCEPT:

## subject

## vignette

## answer_a

## explanation_a

## answer_b

## explanation_b
Incorrect (true, not the exception). Tibialis anterior is the prime dorsiflexor of the ankle and also inverts the foot.

## title
Which arch(es) of the foot the calcaneus forms

## question
The calcaneus participates in the formation of which ONE of the following arch(es) of the foot?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The calcaneus is the posterior pillar of the medial longitudinal arch, but it also forms part of the lateral longitudinal arch — "only" is what makes this wrong.

## answer_b

## explanation_b
Correct. The calcaneus is the posterior pillar shared by both longitudinal arches: the medial arch runs calcaneus–talus–navicular–cuneiforms–medial three metatarsals, and the lateral arch runs calcaneus–cuboid–lateral two metatarsals. Transverse arch only

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The transverse arch is formed by the cuneiforms, cuboid and the metatarsal bases — the calcaneus, well behind these bones, plays no part in it.

## answer_d
Medial longitudinal and transverse arches

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The calcaneus does contribute to the medial longitudinal arch, but not to the transverse arch (formed anteriorly by the cuneiforms, cuboid and metatarsal bases) — and this option omits the lateral longitudinal arch, which the calcaneus does form part of.

## subject
