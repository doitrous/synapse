# Item

## id
QST-MSK-SYNOVIAL-JOINT-DEFINITION-01

## title
A joint at the end of a movable long bone with a joint cavity is which type?

## question
A joint found at the end of movable long bones, and containing a joint cavity, would be classified as a:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Synovial joint

## explanation_a
Correct. A synovial joint is freely mobile, present mostly in the limbs, and is defined by seven named components built around a joint cavity — a fibrous capsule, a synovial membrane lining it, synovial fluid, articular (hyaline) cartilage over the bone ends, ligaments, ligamentous reinforcements and, in some joints, intra-articular structures such as a disc or meniscus. That joint cavity is the feature this stem is pointing at: it is present only in synovial joints, which is exactly why a joint 'at the end of movable long bones, containing a joint cavity' can only be a synovial joint. Fibrous and cartilaginous joints have no such cavity — their bones are held directly together by connective tissue or cartilage — so the cavity itself is the single most reliable feature separating synovial joints from the other two classes.

## answer_b
Epithelial joint

## explanation_b
Incorrect. 'Epithelial joint' is not a recognised joint classification; epithelium lines body surfaces and cavities generally, not the articulating surfaces of bone.

## answer_c
Articular joint

## explanation_c
Incorrect. 'Articular joint' is not a distinct class of joint — articular surfaces exist at synovial joints, but the class defined by a joint cavity is named synovial, not articular.

## answer_d
Fibrous joint

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A fibrous joint unites bones directly by fibrous tissue and has no joint cavity, the opposite of what this stem describes.

## question

## subject
msk

## status

A fibrous joint unites bone surfaces by fibrous tissue and is fixed or immobile, and it comes in three named types: syndesmosis (bones held by an interosseous ligament, as at the inferior tibiofibular joint), gomphosis (a tooth root held in its socket by the periodontal ligament), and suture (skull bones held by a thin sutural ligament, which obliterates in old age). Synovial

## title
Which is the only temporary joint?

## question
The only temporary joint, among the following, is:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## answer_a
Gomphosis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Gomphosis is a fibrous joint (a tooth root held in its socket by the periodontal ligament) and is permanent, not temporary.

## answer_b
Secondary cartilaginous

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A secondary cartilaginous joint is permanent — the opposite of the primary cartilaginous joint's temporary, ossifying nature.

## answer_c
Primary cartilaginous

## explanation_c
Correct. A primary cartilaginous joint (synchondrosis) unites bones by hyaline cartilage, is usually temporary, and ossifies as growth finishes — the epiphyseal (growth) plate is its type example, and once it ossifies the two bone ends it joined become one continuous bone. Gomphosis (a fibrous joint) and plane synovial joints are both permanent structures with no such ossifying end-point, which is why neither is a candidate for 'the only temporary joint' in this list.

## answer_d
Plane synovial

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A plane synovial joint is a permanent, freely mobile joint with a cavity; it does not ossify away with age.

## title
Which is NOT a character of the synovial joint?

## question
The synovial joint has the following characters, EXCEPT:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A considerable range of movement is a genuine character of synovial joints, not the exception.

## answer_b

## explanation_b
Correct. The articular surfaces of a synovial joint are covered by hyaline cartilage, not elastic cartilage — smooth, avascular and lubricated by synovial fluid, which nourishes it in the absence of a direct blood supply. Elastic cartilage is a different histological type entirely, found where a structure needs to bend and spring back (the auricle of the ear, the epiglottis), not where it needs a slick, load-bearing joint surface. The other three statements are all genuine synovial-joint properties — a considerable range of movement, a surrounding fibrous capsule, and a joint cavity that normally holds only a thin (minimal) film of fluid rather than a large pooled volume — which is exactly why the false one has to be spotted among three true statements.

## answer_c
Covered by a capsule

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Being covered by a fibrous capsule is a genuine character of synovial joints, not the exception.

## answer_d
Minimal synovial fluid within its cavity

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The joint cavity of a synovial joint is potential, holding only a thin, minimal film of fluid under normal conditions; this is a genuine character, not the exception.

## title
Which joint type is the most fixed, with no potential for movement?

## question
The most fixed type of joint, which does not allow any range of movement and has no potential ever to do so, is:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Syndesmosis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A syndesmosis is a fibrous joint held by an interosseous ligament (as at the inferior tibiofibular joint); it is very restricted but is a fibrous union, not the joint type the bank's own key names as most fixed.

## answer_b
Primary cartilaginous

## explanation_b
Correct. Of the two cartilaginous joint types, only the primary cartilaginous joint permits no movement at all by definition — its hyaline cartilage forms a rigid, temporary union (the epiphyseal plate) that ossifies away rather than ever loosening, so there is no 'potential' for movement to develop the way there is at a secondary cartilaginous joint (which permits slight movement) or even a fibrous joint. This is the department bank's own convention for ranking fixity, and it turns on the word 'potential': a suture or a syndesmosis is also very restricted in movement, but neither is defined as permitting literally none, and both remain living fibrous unions rather than the rigid cartilage-to-bone conversion a primary cartilaginous joint undergoes. Reading this item alongside 'the only temporary joint' elsewhere in the bank makes the intended logic clear — both point at the same primary-cartilaginous-joint fact from different angles: complete fixity, and eventual ossification, are two sides of the same property.

## answer_c
Secondary cartilaginous

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A secondary cartilaginous joint permits slight movement by definition (as at an intervertebral disc), which is the opposite of allowing no movement at all.

## answer_d
Sutures

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Sutures are fibrous joints holding the skull bones together; they are very restricted but are not the bank's own answer for the joint type with no potential ever to move.

QST-MSK-BALL-SOCKET-FULL-MOVEMENT-01

## title
Which synovial joint type allows the fullest range of movement, including circumduction?

## question
Which of the following types of synovial joint allows adduction, abduction, flexion, extension, medial and lateral rotation, and circumduction?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Pivot

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A pivot joint is uniaxial and permits rotation only (as at the superior and inferior radio-ulnar joints), not the full list of movements this stem describes.

## answer_b
Hinge

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A hinge joint is uniaxial and permits flexion and extension only, never abduction, adduction or rotation.

## answer_c
Ball and socket

## explanation_c
Correct. The movements possible at a synovial joint follow directly from how many axes its shape allows it to move around, and a ball-and-socket joint is triaxial — its spherical head sitting in a matching socket lets it move around three perpendicular axes at once, giving adduction, abduction, flexion, extension, and medial and lateral rotation, and circumduction as the smooth combination of all of them. A hinge joint, by contrast, is uniaxial and permits only flexion and extension; a pivot joint is also uniaxial but for rotation alone (as at the radio-ulnar joints); and a plane joint permits only small gliding movements with no fixed axis at all. Naming the joint type from its full list of possible movements — rather than from a single movement in isolation — is exactly the skill this item and the department's other joint-classification items are built to test.

## answer_d
Plane

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A plane joint permits only small gliding movements between flat articular surfaces, with no single fixed axis and no rotation or circumduction.

The pneumatic bones are called so because they contain:

Bone marrow content, lymphatic richness and cell density are not what defines any of the six shape classes, which is why none of them is the reason a pneumatic bone carries that name.
