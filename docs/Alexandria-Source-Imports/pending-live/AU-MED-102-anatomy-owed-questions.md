## id
QST-MSK-ANATOMICAL-POSITION-CRITERIA-01

## title
Which of the following does NOT describe the anatomical position?

## question
All of the following describe the standard anatomical position EXCEPT:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Standing erect with the eyes looking forward

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Standing erect with the eyes looking forward (the head level, gaze directed to the horizon) is one of the defining criteria of anatomical position.

## answer_b
Upper limbs hanging at the sides with the palms facing forward

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The upper limbs hanging at the sides with the palms facing forward is a defining criterion of anatomical position, not an exception to it.

## answer_c
Lower limbs together with the feet pointing forward

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The lower limbs together with the feet pointing forward is a defining criterion of anatomical position, not an exception to it.

## answer_d
Forearms flexed at the elbow with the palms facing the trunk

## explanation_d
Correct. The anatomical position holds the upper limb fully extended at the elbow and hanging by the side with the palm rotated to face forward (fully supinated), not flexed at the elbow with the palm turned toward the trunk. Every anatomical description — every 'superior', 'anterior', 'medial' — is written as if the body stands in this one fixed reference posture, which is exactly why its criteria are fixed rather than left to whatever position a patient happens to be lying in. A flexed elbow with the palm facing the trunk is the forearm's default resting (pronated) posture, and anatomical position specifically corrects for that default by extending the elbow and supinating the forearm so both sides of the body can be described symmetrically.

## id
QST-MSK-ANATOMICAL-PLANE-TRANSVERSE-01

## title
Which anatomical plane divides the body into upper and lower parts?

## question
Which anatomical plane divides the body into upper and lower parts?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Sagittal (median) plane

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The sagittal (median) plane divides the body into right and left parts, not upper and lower.

## answer_b
Coronal (frontal) plane

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The coronal (frontal) plane divides the body into front (anterior) and back (posterior) parts, not upper and lower.

## answer_c
Transverse (horizontal) plane

## explanation_c
Correct. The transverse (horizontal) plane runs at right angles to the body's long axis, and of the three cardinal anatomical planes it is the one that leaves an upper (superior) part and a lower (inferior) part behind. The sagittal plane instead cuts along the long axis to leave a right and a left part, and the coronal plane cuts along the long axis the other way to leave a front (anterior) and a back (posterior) part. Naming each plane by the two parts it leaves behind — transverse leaves upper and lower — is the quickest way to keep the three from being confused under exam pressure, since 'oblique' names no fixed pair of parts at all.

## answer_d
Oblique plane

## explanation_d
Incorrect. An oblique plane is any plane not aligned with the three cardinal planes; it is not one of the three named planes and defines no fixed pair of parts.

QST-MSK-PROXIMAL-DISTAL-TERMS-01

## title
Which pair of terms describes distance from the root of a limb?

## question
Which pair of terms of position is used to describe how far a point lies from the root of a limb?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Medial and lateral

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Medial and lateral measure distance from the median plane, not from the root of a limb.

## answer_b
Superior and inferior

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Superior and inferior measure position along the body's vertical (up/down) axis, not distance from a limb's root.

## answer_c
Proximal and distal

## explanation_c
Correct. Proximal and distal measure distance from the root of a limb — proximal nearer the trunk, distal further from it — and are the terms of position used whenever two structures along a limb's own long axis are compared, for example describing the plantar surface as more distal than a point on the leg. Medial and lateral instead measure distance from the median plane, not from a limb's root, so the two pairs answer different questions even though both are called terms of position. Keeping the reference point straight — the median plane for medial/lateral, the limb's own root for proximal/distal — is the exact distinction the department book's terms-of-position items are built to test.

## answer_d
Superficial and deep

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Superficial and deep measure distance from the body's surface, not distance from a limb's root.

Which of the following bones is part of the appendicular skeleton?

## subject

## answer_c

## explanation_c
Correct. The humerus is a bone of the upper limb, and the limbs together with their girdles (pectoral and pelvic) make up the appendicular skeleton — the part of the skeleton attached to, rather than forming, the body's central axis. Sorting a bone correctly into axial or appendicular is the single most-tested classification in this chapter, precisely because a few bones (the pectoral and pelvic girdles) sit at the join between the two and are easy to place wrongly.

## subject
msk

## status

## vignette

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Correct. The patella develops within the tendon of quadriceps femoris and is the largest of the body's sesamoid bones — one of the six shape classes bones fall into, alongside long, short, flat, irregular and pneumatic bones, each with its own worked example in the department book. A sesamoid bone forms inside a tendon where it crosses a joint under high compressive load, protecting the tendon and improving its mechanical leverage, and the patella is the clearest illustration of both roles at once, sitting over the knee. Sacrum

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The sacrum is an irregular bone formed by the fusion of five vertebrae, not a sesamoid bone.

## answer_d
Scaphoid

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The scaphoid is a carpal (wrist) bone, not a sesamoid bone.

QST-MSK-PNEUMATIC-BONE-FUNCTION-01

## title
Which is NOT a function of a pneumatic bone's air cavities?

## question
Which of the following is NOT a function of the air cavities in a pneumatic bone?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Lightening the weight of the skull

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Lightening the skull is one of the genuine functions of pneumatic bone air cavities.

## answer_b
Giving resonance to the voice

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Giving the voice its resonance is one of the genuine functions of pneumatic bone air cavities.

## answer_c
Warming inspired air

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Warming inspired air is one of the genuine functions of pneumatic bone air cavities.

## answer_d
Increasing the bone's mechanical strength

## explanation_d
Correct. The air-filled cavities of a pneumatic bone do the opposite of adding strength: any mechanical strength the skull's pneumatic bones retain comes from their outer compact-bone shell, not from the air spaces themselves, which exist despite reducing the bone's mass rather than because they reinforce it. What the cavities buy the body instead is a lighter skull, a resonating chamber that gives the voice its individual quality, and a lining surface that warms and humidifies inspired air on its way to the lungs. A bone that needed maximum mechanical strength — a weight-bearing long bone, for instance — is never built pneumatic, which is itself evidence that air cavities and strength trade against, rather than reinforce, one another.

## id
QST-MSK-EPIPHYSIS-DIAPHYSIS-SEPARATOR-01

## title
What separates the epiphysis from the diaphysis of a growing long bone?

## question
What separates the epiphysis from the diaphysis of a growing long bone?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Periosteum

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The periosteum is the fibrous covering on the outer surface of the whole bone; it does not separate the epiphysis from the diaphysis.

## answer_b
Epiphyseal (growth) cartilage

## explanation_b
Correct. A long bone is an epiphysis at each end, a diaphysis (shaft) between them, and a metaphysis where the two meet — and while the bone is still growing, the epiphysis and diaphysis are separated at that junction by a layer of epiphyseal (growth-plate) cartilage rather than being continuous bone. This cartilage plate is where the shaft actually lengthens, by cartilage cells proliferating and then being replaced by bone on the diaphyseal side, until it finally ossifies once growth is complete and the epiphysis fuses to the shaft. Until that fusion, an X-ray of a growing long bone shows a visible radiolucent line at exactly this epiphysis-diaphysis boundary, which is the growth plate the question is naming.

## answer_c
Medullary cavity

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The medullary cavity is the marrow-containing space inside the diaphysis; it does not sit at the epiphysis-diaphysis junction.

## answer_d
Articular cartilage

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Articular cartilage covers the joint surface at the end of the epiphysis; it does not separate the epiphysis from the diaphysis.

## title
Which long bone has its growing end at its upper extremity?

## question
Which of the following long bones has its growing end (the end that ossifies later) at its upper extremity?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Femur

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The femur's growing end is at its lower (distal, knee) end, not its upper end.

## answer_b
Tibia

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The tibia's growing end is at its upper (proximal, knee) end, but the tibia is a lower-limb bone following the opposite half of the same rule, not the humerus.

## answer_c
Radius

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The radius's growing end is at its lower (distal, wrist) end, not its upper end.

Humerus

The femur (lower end) and tibia (upper end) follow the lower-limb half of that same rule, which is exactly why they are the two distractors most likely to be confused with the humerus here.

## title
Which direction does a long bone's nutrient artery run?

The right lymphatic duct drains one quadrant of the body - the right side of the head and neck, the right upper limb, and the right side of the thorax - while the thoracic duct drains the other three-quarters, including both lower limbs, so lymph from the right lower limb reaches the venous system via the thoracic duct, not the right lymphatic duct. The thoracic duct begins at the cisterna chyli in the abdomen, ascends through the thorax, and empties at the junction of the left subclavian and left internal jugular veins, so the cisterna chyli is a way-station on this route rather than lymph's final destination. This asymmetric split - one small duct for one quadrant, one large duct for the other three - is the single fact the EOM paper's own item on the thoracic duct is built to test.

## answer_c
Cisterna chyli, with no further drainage beyond it

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The cisterna chyli is a dilated sac at the lower end of the thoracic duct, not the endpoint of lymph drainage; lymph continues from it through the thoracic duct to the venous system.

## answer_d
The left subclavian vein directly, bypassing any lymphatic duct

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Lymph from the right lower limb reaches the venous system via the thoracic duct, which empties into the venous system at the left subclavian/internal jugular junction - it does not bypass the duct.

## title
Which fibre arrangement gives skeletal muscle the greatest range of movement?

## question
Which pattern of skeletal muscle fibre arrangement gives the greatest range of movement?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Unipennate

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Unipennate fibres angle onto one side of a central tendon, trading range of movement for force - the opposite of the widest-range answer this item asks for.

## answer_b
Multipennate

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Multipennate fibres angle onto a central tendon from multiple directions, giving the greatest force of the listed arrangements but the least range of movement, not the greatest.

## answer_c
Parallel (strap-like)

## explanation_c
Correct. Skeletal muscles are classified by the direction of their fibres, from strap-like (parallel) to multipennate, and parallel fibres run the full length of the muscle in line with its long axis, so the muscle as a whole can shorten by roughly the same proportion each individual fibre shortens - giving parallel muscles the widest range of movement of any fibre arrangement. Pennate muscles (unipennate, bipennate and multipennate) instead angle their shorter fibres obliquely onto a central tendon, which packs in more fibres for a given muscle volume and so generates more force, but at the cost of each fibre contributing less to the muscle's overall length change. This is a genuine trade-off, not a simple hierarchy: parallel architecture is built for range of movement, and increasingly pennate architecture is built for increasing force, which is exactly why the exam item asks for the widest range specifically rather than the strongest muscle.

## answer_d
Bipennate

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Bipennate fibres angle onto both sides of a central tendon, trading range of movement for force, like the other pennate arrangements.
