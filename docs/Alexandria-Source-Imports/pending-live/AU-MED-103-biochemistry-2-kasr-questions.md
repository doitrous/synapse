## title
How many ATP does one glucose molecule yield under full aerobic oxidation, versus anaerobic glycolysis alone, and how much of the aerobic total is made at the substrate level within glycolysis itself?

## question
How many ATP does one glucose molecule yield under full aerobic oxidation, versus anaerobic glycolysis alone, and how much of the aerobic total is made at the substrate level within glycolysis itself?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
32 ATP aerobically, 2 ATP anaerobically; 4 of the aerobic ATP are made at the substrate level within glycolysis

## explanation_a
Correct. Complete aerobic oxidation of one glucose molecule, via glycolysis, the citric acid cycle and oxidative phosphorylation, yields about 32 ATP using the modern shuttle-corrected accounting; anaerobic glycolysis alone, without the mitochondria to reoxidise NADH, nets only 2 ATP. Within glycolysis itself, four ATP are generated by substrate-level phosphorylation (two per triose, at the phosphoglycerate kinase and pyruvate kinase steps), though two of those four are spent paying back the pathway's own priming steps, leaving glycolysis's own net contribution at 2 ATP either way.

## answer_b
38 ATP aerobically, 8 ATP anaerobically; none of it is made at the substrate level

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The commonly quoted older figure of 38 ATP overcounts the cost of shuttling cytosolic NADH into the mitochondrion; the modern, shuttle-corrected total is about 32. Some ATP genuinely is made at the substrate level (four within glycolysis, one more in the citric acid cycle), so 'none' is also wrong.

## answer_c
32 ATP aerobically, 0 ATP anaerobically; all of it is made at the substrate level

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Anaerobic glycolysis does yield ATP (2 net), just far less than the aerobic total; and while substrate-level phosphorylation contributes ATP, most of the aerobic total actually comes from oxidative phosphorylation at the electron transport chain, not from substrate-level reactions alone.

## answer_d
2 ATP aerobically, 32 ATP anaerobically; 4 are made at the substrate level

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This reverses the aerobic and anaerobic totals; aerobic oxidation yields far more ATP than anaerobic glycolysis, not less.

## title
Why does anaerobic glycolysis convert pyruvate to lactate, when lactate itself has no further metabolic use in that cell?

## question
Why does anaerobic glycolysis convert pyruvate to lactate, when lactate itself has no further metabolic use in that cell?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
To regenerate the NAD+ that glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase needs, since anaerobic conditions leave no mitochondrial route to reoxidise NADH

## explanation_a
Correct. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase, one of glycolysis's own steps, consumes NAD+ and produces NADH; without oxygen and functioning mitochondria to reoxidise that NADH back to NAD+ via the electron transport chain, the cell's small NAD+ pool would be exhausted within seconds and glycolysis would grind to a halt. Converting pyruvate to lactate via lactate dehydrogenase regenerates NAD+ directly in the cytosol, at the cost of no extra energy, purely so glycolysis itself can keep running — the lactate produced is not being made for its own sake, it is a by-product of solving this redox problem.

## answer_b
To generate one additional ATP directly from the reaction

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The lactate dehydrogenase reaction produces no ATP of its own; its only purpose is regenerating NAD+, not adding to the pathway's energy yield.

## answer_c
To detoxify pyruvate, which is otherwise harmful to the cell

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Pyruvate is not toxic to the cell; the reaction exists to solve a redox (NAD+ supply) problem, not a detoxification one.

## answer_d
To provide the substrate for gluconeogenesis in the same cell

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Lactate produced this way is typically exported for use elsewhere (such as the Cori cycle, where the liver converts it back to glucose); it is not retained by the same cell as a gluconeogenic substrate.

## title
The citric acid cycle is described as a "cycle" even though several of its individual reactions are irreversible. What actually makes it cyclic?

## question
The citric acid cycle is described as a "cycle" even though several of its individual reactions are irreversible. What actually makes it cyclic?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Every one of its reactions is freely reversible in both directions

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Several citric acid cycle reactions (citrate synthase, isocitrate dehydrogenase, alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase) are strongly irreversible under cellular conditions; the cycle's circularity does not depend on every step running backwards.

## answer_b
Oxaloacetate is regenerated at the end of every turn, ready to condense with a fresh acetyl-CoA

## explanation_b
Correct. What makes the pathway a cycle is that oxaloacetate, condensed away at the very first step to form citrate, is regenerated by the end of each full turn (via succinate to fumarate to malate to oxaloacetate) and is then ready to accept another acetyl-CoA and start the next turn. The cycle's irreversible steps drive it forward in one direction each turn; it is oxaloacetate's continuous regeneration, not step-by-step reversibility, that closes the loop.

## answer_c
The cycle only runs cyclically under anaerobic conditions

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The citric acid cycle is an obligately aerobic pathway (it depends on the electron transport chain to reoxidise the NADH and FADH2 it produces); it does not run under anaerobic conditions at all.

## answer_d
Citrate is regenerated at the end of every turn instead of oxaloacetate

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Citrate is the product of the first step, consumed as the cycle proceeds; it is oxaloacetate, not citrate, that is regenerated at the end of each turn.

## title
A well-fed liver cell has abundant fatty acids available for oxidation. How does this state affect the balance between glycolysis and gluconeogenesis in that cell?

## question
A well-fed liver cell has abundant fatty acids available for oxidation. How does this state affect the balance between glycolysis and gluconeogenesis in that cell?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
It has no effect; glycolysis and gluconeogenesis are regulated entirely independently of fatty acid oxidation

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Fatty acid oxidation is a major input into the reciprocal glycolysis/gluconeogenesis switch in liver, not an unrelated process.

## answer_b
Fatty acid oxidation raises acetyl-CoA and ATP, which inhibit glycolysis's regulatory enzymes and activate gluconeogenesis's, tipping the balance towards making glucose

## explanation_b
Correct. When the liver is oxidising fatty acids briskly (as it does during fasting, or when supplied with a high fat load), the resulting rise in acetyl-CoA and ATP inhibits pyruvate dehydrogenase and PFK-1 (glycolysis's key enzymes) while activating pyruvate carboxylase (a gluconeogenic enzyme, allosterically switched on by acetyl-CoA). The net effect is that active fatty acid oxidation tips the liver's own glucose metabolism towards making glucose rather than breaking it down — physiologically sensible, since a fasting liver oxidising fat for its own energy needs should be exporting glucose to the rest of the body, not consuming it.

## answer_c
Fatty acid oxidation always tips the balance towards glycolysis, regardless of energy state

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This is the opposite of the actual regulation; fatty acid oxidation tips the balance towards gluconeogenesis, not glycolysis.

## answer_d
Fatty acid oxidation is only relevant to muscle, never to liver glucose metabolism

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This reciprocal relationship is specifically a liver phenomenon central to whole-body glucose homeostasis during fasting, not a muscle-only effect.

## title
Which two tissues does the body's glucose supply exist mainly to protect, and why does this drive the need for gluconeogenesis?

## question
Which two tissues does the body's glucose supply exist mainly to protect, and why does this drive the need for gluconeogenesis?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Red blood cells and the brain, since red cells have no mitochondria and depend on glucose absolutely, and the brain relies very heavily on it too

## explanation_a
Correct. Red blood cells, lacking mitochondria entirely, can only make ATP by glycolysis and are therefore absolutely dependent on a continuous glucose supply with no alternative fuel option; the brain, while it can adapt to ketone bodies during prolonged starvation, relies very heavily on glucose under normal conditions and cannot tolerate a sudden drop. Gluconeogenesis exists largely to keep blood glucose from falling below the level these two tissues need, during the hours between meals and especially during fasting or starvation, when dietary glucose is unavailable.

## answer_b
Skeletal muscle and adipose tissue, since both depend on glucose absolutely and cannot use any other fuel

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Skeletal muscle and adipose tissue can both use fatty acids as an alternative fuel and are not absolutely glucose-dependent the way red cells are.

## answer_c
The liver and kidney, since both organs cannot survive without a constant external glucose supply

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The liver is in fact the organ that performs gluconeogenesis to supply glucose to other tissues, not a tissue that depends on an external glucose supply itself; the kidney also contributes some gluconeogenesis rather than depending entirely on outside glucose.

## answer_d
The skin and bone marrow, since both organs are the most glucose-dependent tissues in the body

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Skin and bone marrow are not singled out as the tissues gluconeogenesis exists to protect; the defining absolutely-glucose-dependent tissue is the red blood cell, with the brain as the other major glucose-reliant tissue.

## title
Substrate-level phosphorylation makes ATP directly, without the electron transport chain. At which three reactions does this happen in central carbohydrate metabolism?

## question
Substrate-level phosphorylation makes ATP directly, without the electron transport chain. At which three reactions does this happen in central carbohydrate metabolism?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Phosphoglycerate kinase and pyruvate kinase in glycolysis, and succinate thiokinase (succinyl-CoA synthetase) in the citric acid cycle

## explanation_a
Correct. Substrate-level phosphorylation directly transfers a high-energy phosphate from a reaction intermediate to ADP, without any involvement of the electron transport chain or a proton gradient. In glycolysis this happens twice per glucose, once at phosphoglycerate kinase (1,3-bisphosphoglycerate to 3-phosphoglycerate) and once at pyruvate kinase (phosphoenolpyruvate to pyruvate); in the citric acid cycle it happens once, at succinate thiokinase (succinyl-CoA synthetase), which actually produces GTP rather than ATP directly (interconvertible with ATP via nucleoside diphosphate kinase).

## answer_b
Hexokinase and phosphofructokinase-1 in glycolysis, and citrate synthase in the citric acid cycle

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Hexokinase and PFK-1 consume ATP rather than produce it, and citrate synthase produces no high-energy phosphate at all; none of these three is a substrate-level phosphorylation site.

## answer_c
All ten glycolytic reactions and all eight citric acid cycle reactions equally

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Most glycolytic and citric acid cycle reactions do not generate ATP or GTP directly; only the three specific reactions named above do.

## answer_d
Only the electron transport chain's ATP synthase step

## explanation_d
Incorrect. ATP synthase's action is oxidative phosphorylation, driven by the proton gradient the electron transport chain builds -- the opposite of substrate-level phosphorylation, which makes ATP without that gradient.

## title
The hexose monophosphate shunt has two phases. What does its non-oxidative phase produce, and why does the cell need this product?

## question
The hexose monophosphate shunt has two phases. What does its non-oxidative phase produce, and why does the cell need this product?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Ribose-5-phosphate, needed for nucleotide and nucleic acid synthesis

## explanation_a
Correct. The hexose monophosphate shunt's oxidative phase (glucose-6-phosphate through 6-phosphogluconate to ribulose-5-phosphate) generates NADPH and is irreversible; its separate non-oxidative phase then rearranges the resulting pentose sugars, via transketolase and transaldolase reactions, to yield ribose-5-phosphate, the sugar every cell needs to build the ribose backbone of nucleotides, RNA and DNA. A cell that needs mainly NADPH (for fat synthesis, for example) can run the oxidative phase alone and let the non-oxidative phase's reactions run in reverse to recycle any excess pentose back to glycolytic intermediates; a rapidly dividing cell that needs mainly ribose-5-phosphate can favour the non-oxidative route instead.

## answer_b
NADPH, needed for reductive biosynthesis and antioxidant defence

## explanation_b
Incorrect. NADPH is the product of the shunt's oxidative phase, not its non-oxidative phase; the two phases have genuinely different outputs.

## answer_c
ATP, needed to drive the oxidative phase forward

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The hexose monophosphate shunt produces no ATP at any point, in either phase.

## answer_d
Glucose-6-phosphate, regenerated to restart the shunt

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Glucose-6-phosphate is the shunt's starting substrate, consumed at the very first step of the oxidative phase, not a product regenerated by the non-oxidative phase.

## title
Epinephrine and glucagon both raise blood glucose by acting on liver glycogen. What is the shared covalent mechanism by which they do this?

## question
Epinephrine and glucagon both raise blood glucose by acting on liver glycogen. What is the shared covalent mechanism by which they do this?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
A cAMP-driven protein kinase A cascade that phosphorylates and activates glycogen phosphorylase, while phosphorylating and inactivating glycogen synthase at the same time

## explanation_a
Correct. Both epinephrine and glucagon bind their own receptors and raise intracellular cAMP, which activates protein kinase A; protein kinase A then phosphorylates glycogen phosphorylase (switching it ON, since phosphorylase is active in its phosphorylated form) and, in the same cascade, phosphorylates glycogen synthase (switching it OFF, since synthase is active in its dephosphorylated form). The red cell's glutathione system depends on a steady supply of NADPH. Where does this NADPH come from, and what does reduced glutathione then do?

## question
The red cell's glutathione system depends on a steady supply of NADPH. Where does this NADPH come from, and what does reduced glutathione then do?

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
NADPH from the hexose monophosphate shunt's oxidative phase; reduced glutathione neutralises peroxides and other oxidants that would otherwise damage haemoglobin and the cell membrane

## explanation_a
Correct. Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, the hexose monophosphate shunt's first and rate-limiting oxidative-phase enzyme, is the red cell's main source of NADPH. Glutathione reductase then uses that NADPH to keep the cell's glutathione pool in its reduced (GSH) form, and reduced glutathione is what neutralises hydrogen peroxide and other oxidants (via glutathione peroxidase) before they can oxidise hameoglobin's iron or damage the red cell membrane -- which is exactly the protection that fails when G6PD deficiency limits NADPH supply, leading to oxidant-induced haemolysis.

## answer_b
NADPH from glycolysis's substrate-level phosphorylation steps; reduced glutathione generates ATP directly

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Glycolysis's substrate-level phosphorylation steps produce ATP, not NADPH (they use ADP as the phosphate acceptor, not NADP+); and reduced glutathione's role is antioxidant defence, not ATP generation.

## answer_c
NADPH from the citric acid cycle; reduced glutathione regenerates 2,3-BPG

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The citric acid cycle is not relevant to the mature red cell, which has no mitochondria; the red cell's NADPH comes specifically from the hexose monophosphate shunt.

## answer_d
NADH, not NADPH, from anaerobic glycolysis; reduced glutathione binds oxygen in place of haemoglobin

## explanation_d
Incorrect. It is specifically NADPH, not NADH, that the glutathione system depends on, and glutathione does not bind oxygen -- that remains haemoglobin's role.
