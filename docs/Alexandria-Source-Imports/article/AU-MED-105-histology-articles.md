## summary
Cartilage is a resilient, avascular connective tissue that comes in three histological types told apart by their matrix, and it is covered (except as fibrocartilage) by a two-layered perichondrium that supplies it and lets it grow. Because it has no blood vessels of its own, it heals poorly, and worse with age.

## sections
### Definition
Cartilage is a tough, resilient connective tissue built around chondrocytes sitting in lacunae within a firm extracellular matrix, avascular, aneural and alymphatic throughout. Three histological types are told apart by their matrix: hyaline cartilage has a translucent, glass-like matrix with numerous small rounded cells and is the most widespread type; white fibrocartilage has an opaque matrix rich in collagen bundles and few cells, and carries no perichondrium; yellow elastic fibrocartilage has a matrix rich in elastic fibres and abundant cells.

### Mechanism
The perichondrium is the dense connective-tissue sheath that covers hyaline and elastic cartilage (fibrocartilage has none). It has two layers: an outer fibrous layer of dense irregular connective tissue carrying the blood vessels and nerves that supply the cartilage by diffusion, and an inner, more cellular chondrogenic layer whose flattened cells can differentiate into chondroblasts for appositional growth. Only the outer fibrous layer is vascular; the cartilage matrix itself, and the chondrogenic layer immediately next to it, are avascular. Cartilage enlarges by two mechanisms that can occur together. Interstitial growth happens from within: chondrocytes already embedded in lacunae divide mitotically and lay down new matrix around themselves, pushing existing matrix apart — this is how young, more pliable cartilage (e.g. at an epiphyseal plate) grows in length. Appositional growth happens from the outside: chondrogenic cells in the perichondrium's inner layer differentiate into chondroblasts, which lay down new matrix on the existing surface, adding to the cartilage's girth. Appositional growth is the only route available once a cartilage is surrounded by a perichondrium that has lost much of its chondrogenic potential.

### Key determinants
What a given piece of cartilage can do is set by which of the three matrix types it is (hyaline, fibrocartilage or elastic — each with its own typical sites) and by whether it still carries a perichondrium with active chondrogenic potential.

### Clinical significance
Cartilage is avascular, aneural and alymphatic: it is nourished entirely by diffusion of nutrients and gases from the vessels of the perichondrium (or, at a joint, from the synovial fluid) through the matrix to the chondrocytes. Because there is no direct blood supply to bring in an inflammatory and reparative response, a cartilage injury heals slowly and incompletely, usually by fibrous rather than hyaline tissue, and this capacity declines further with age as the perichondrium's chondrogenic potential falls. A structure such as the nasal septum, which depends on its perichondrium for any repair at all, heals particularly poorly once that supply is disrupted.

## summary
Bone tissue is maintained by four cell types — osteoprogenitor cells, osteoblasts, osteocytes and osteoclasts — and covered, outside its joint surfaces, by a two-layered periosteum whose outer layer is vascular and whose inner layer supplies the osteoblasts needed for growth and repair.

## sections
### Definition
Bone cells include osteogenic (osteoprogenitor) cells, osteoblasts, osteocytes, and osteoclasts, each with a distinct role in forming, maintaining or resorbing bone matrix.

### Mechanism
The periosteum is the connective-tissue covering of bone everywhere except at joint surfaces. It has two layers: an outer fibrous layer of dense connective tissue that carries the blood vessels supplying the bone and is anchored into the underlying bone by Sharpey's (perforating) fibres, and an inner, more cellular osteogenic layer containing osteoprogenitor cells that can differentiate into osteoblasts for appositional bone growth and fracture repair. The vascular supply belongs to the outer fibrous layer; the inner layer is the reservoir of bone-forming cells. Osteocytes, the bone's resident maintenance cells, sit within lacunae inside the mineralised matrix, connected to each other and to the Haversian canal's blood supply by fine canaliculi.

### Key determinants
Which of the four cell types a photomicrograph is showing is read from its location (surface-lining versus embedded in a lacuna versus at a resorption pit) and from its size and nuclearity (a large multinucleated cell at a scalloped bone surface is an osteoclast, not an osteoblast).

### Clinical significance
Because the periosteum's outer layer carries the blood supply and its inner layer carries the osteoprogenitor cells, a periosteum stripped from bone (surgically or by trauma) removes both that segment's nutrition and its main local source of new osteoblasts for repair.

## published_sections

Treating osteocytes as if they still actively secrete matrix the way osteoblasts do, rather than maintaining it.

## summary
Compact and cancellous bone are the same lamellar matrix and the same cells in two different architectures: compact bone is organised into Haversian systems each fed by a central canal, joined by transverse Volkmann's canals, while cancellous bone is an open lattice of trabeculae nourished by the marrow instead.

## sections
### Definition
On section, compact bone is dense, with few and small marrow spaces, and its lamellae are organised into Haversian systems (osteons) each with a central canal, plus interstitial lamellae between osteons and circumferential lamellae at its outer and inner surfaces. Cancellous bone is a loose, irregular lattice of trabeculae with wide, marrow-filled spaces between them and no Haversian systems. Both are built of the same lamellar bone matrix and the same cell types; the difference is architectural, not compositional.

### Mechanism
A Haversian (central) canal runs along the long axis of an osteon and is surrounded by concentric bony lamellae. Volkmann's (perforating) canals run obliquely or transversely, at right angles to the Haversian canals, connecting them to each other, to the periosteum, and to the marrow cavity; both carry blood vessels and nerves, but only the Haversian canal is surrounded by its own ring of concentric lamellae — a Volkmann's canal is not, which is how the two are told apart on a transverse section. Cancellous (spongy) bone forms an irregular lattice of anastomosing bony trabeculae with marrow-filled spaces between them, found at the ends of long bones and as the diploe between the two tables of a flat bone. Its trabeculae are built of parallel lamellae but do not organise into Haversian systems: no trabecula is thick enough to need its own internal blood supply, so its osteocytes are nourished by diffusion from the vessels of the adjacent marrow rather than from vessels running through the bone itself.

### Key determinants
Whether a section shows Haversian systems at all is the first branch point between compact and cancellous bone; within compact bone, whether a canal carries its own ring of concentric lamellae is what separates a Haversian canal from a Volkmann's canal.

### Clinical significance
Cancellous bone's dependence on diffusion from the marrow, rather than on vessels running through the bone itself, is part of why it remodels faster than compact bone and why marrow disease can affect trabecular bone directly.

## summary
Flat bones form by intramembranous ossification — a mesenchymal condensation, an ossification centre, osteoid, then mineralisation — and the same matrix is continuously renewed throughout adult life by coupled osteoclast resorption and osteoblast deposition. Sharpey's fibres anchor the periosteum, and a fracture heals through a fixed sequence from haematoma to remodelled bone.

## sections
### Definition
Sharpey's (perforating) fibres are coarse bundles of collagen continuous with the periosteum's outer fibrous layer that penetrate the outer circumferential lamellae of the underlying bone. They are what physically anchors the periosteum to the bone and prevents it lifting off; they are especially numerous where a tendon or ligament attaches. Newly secreted bone matrix (osteoid) is soft and unmineralised.

### Mechanism
Intramembranous ossification forms bone directly within a condensation of mesenchyme, without an intervening cartilage model — this is how the flat bones of the skull and the clavicle form. Mesenchymal cells cluster and differentiate directly into osteoblasts at an ossification centre; those osteoblasts secrete osteoid along trabeculae radiating from the centre; and the osteoid is then mineralised by calcium and phosphate deposition, trapping some osteoblasts as osteocytes and converting the trabeculae into woven bone, later remodelled into either compact or cancellous bone. Mineralisation is the process by which calcium and phosphate ions are deposited onto the collagenous osteoid as hydroxyapatite crystals, converting it into hard, calcified bone; it follows matrix deposition by a short lag and proceeds outward from nucleation sites toward a mineralisation front. Alkaline phosphatase is secreted by active osteoblasts as part of laying down new bone matrix: by hydrolysing organic phosphate esters, it raises the local concentration of free phosphate ions around the newly secreted osteoid, which is what allows calcium and phosphate to precipitate there as hydroxyapatite.

### Key determinants
Adult bone remodelling is the continuous, lifelong replacement of old bone by new bone: osteoclasts resorb a small volume of existing matrix, and osteoblasts then deposit new matrix in the resorbed space. In healthy adult remodelling, resorption and deposition are coupled and roughly balanced, so bone mass is maintained rather than net-gained.

### Clinical significance
Fracture healing proceeds through four overlapping stages. First, a haematoma forms at the fracture site as torn vessels bleed. Second, a soft (procallus/fibrocartilaginous) callus bridges the fragments without giving them rigid support. Third, a hard (bony) callus forms as the soft callus is progressively replaced by woven bone — this is the stage a repeat X-ray at around a month is looking for. Fourth, remodelling over months to years replaces the woven bone with lamellar bone and reshapes the callus toward the bone's original contour.

## published_sections

## hold_these
Intramembranous ossification skips the cartilage model entirely: mesenchyme differentiates straight into osteoblasts.
Alkaline phosphatase raises local phosphate so mineral CAN deposit; it is not the deposition step itself.
Adult remodelling is a balanced cycle, not a steady net gain in bone mass.
A one-month follow-up X-ray after a fracture is looking for the hard (bony) callus stage, not full remodelling.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing the stage sequence of intramembranous ossification with endochondral ossification's cartilage-model route.
Expecting a one-month fracture X-ray to already show the bone remodelled to its original shape.
Treating osteoid and mineralised matrix as the same substance rather than its unmineralised precursor and mineralised product.

## summary
A neuron's shape and the way its axon is myelinated both classify it. Peripheral axons are myelinated one-to-one by Schwann cells, while central axons are myelinated several-to-one by oligodendrocytes — a difference that also decides which of the two supports regeneration after injury.

## sections
### Definition
Neurons are classified by how many processes leave the cell body — unipolar (including the pseudounipolar sensory-ganglion neuron), bipolar, or multipolar — and by location: bipolar neurons in the olfactory mucosa and retina, pseudounipolar neurons in sensory (dorsal root) ganglia, and multipolar neurons as the commonest type, including cerebral cortical and anterior horn motor neurons.

### Mechanism
Schwann cells, in the peripheral nervous system, and oligodendrocytes, in the central nervous system, are the two myelin-forming glial cell types, but they differ in how they relate to the axons they myelinate. One Schwann cell wraps and myelinates a single internode of a single peripheral axon; one oligodendrocyte sends out several cytoplasmic processes, each of which myelinates one internode on a different nearby axon.

### Key determinants
A neuron's own cell body carries basophilic Nissl bodies (rough endoplasmic reticulum) and a Golgi apparatus, both absent from its axon — the feature used to tell perikaryon from axon on a stained section, and one of the ways a nerve fibre's myelination and location are read together to name its type.

### Clinical significance
This difference matters clinically: after peripheral nerve injury, surviving Schwann cells proliferate and form a guiding column that supports axon regeneration, whereas the central nervous system's oligodendrocytes do not support comparable regeneration, which is a major reason CNS axon injury recovers so poorly.

## summary
The central nervous system's four neuroglial cell types each have one distinguishing job. A peripheral nerve trunk is wrapped by three named connective-tissue coverings, of which only the perineurium forms the blood-nerve barrier. After a peripheral axon is cut, its distal stump degenerates in a fixed sequence that Schwann cells then use to guide regeneration.

## sections
### Definition
Central-nervous-system neuroglia are functionally distinct. Astrocytes are the most numerous glial cell and, through end-feet applied to capillary walls, induce and maintain the tight junctions of the blood-brain barrier, besides supporting neurons metabolically. Microglia are the CNS's resident phagocytes and immune cells; unlike the other glia, which are neuroectodermal, microglia derive from monocyte/macrophage precursors, making them members of the mononuclear phagocytic system. Oligodendrocytes myelinate CNS axons. Ependymal cells are the ciliated or cuboidal epithelial-like cells that line the cavities containing cerebrospinal fluid — the ventricles and central canal — and contribute to CSF circulation.

### Mechanism
A peripheral nerve trunk is wrapped, from outside in, by the epineurium (dense connective tissue around the whole trunk), the perineurium (around each individual fascicle), and the endoneurium (fine connective tissue around each nerve fibre within a fascicle). The perineurium is built of several concentric layers of flattened, epithelium-like perineurial cells joined to each other by tight junctions. Those tight junctions make the perineurium selectively permeable and constitute the blood-nerve barrier, which keeps the endoneurial fluid environment around the nerve fibres distinct from the surrounding tissue, much as the blood-brain barrier does for the central nervous system.

### Key determinants
Which neuroglial cell a question is describing is read from its distinguishing job — barrier formation for the astrocyte, phagocytosis and monocyte lineage for microglia, myelination for the oligodendrocyte, CSF-cavity lining for the ependymal cell — not from appearance alone.

### Clinical significance
When a peripheral axon is cut, the segment distal to the injury undergoes Wallerian degeneration: the axon and its myelin fragment and are cleared by Schwann cells and recruited macrophages. The Schwann cells along the distal stump then proliferate and align into a guiding column inside the persisting basal lamina tube. Meanwhile the axon's cell body undergoes chromatolysis as it switches to a growth programme, and the proximal stump sprouts a growth cone that, if it can find the Schwann-cell column, regenerates along it back toward the original target.

## published_sections

## hold_these

Wallerian degeneration affects the whole distal stump, and Schwann cells along that whole stump form the regeneration-guiding column, not only at the cut.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming all neuroglia share one embryological origin, missing that microglia alone come from monocyte precursors.
Confusing the perineurium with the epineurium or endoneurium when asked which covering forms the blood-nerve barrier.

## summary
A skeletal muscle fibre's striations come from its sarcomeres, whose A, I and H bands change predictably on contraction, and each fibre is wrapped by three named layers of connective tissue and activated at a motor end plate. Because mature fibres cannot divide, severe injury is repaired more by fibrosis than by regeneration.

## sections
### Definition
A skeletal muscle fibre is wrapped by three connective-tissue coverings: the epimysium around the whole muscle, carrying its major vessels and nerves; the perimysium around each fascicle; and the endomysium's reticular fibres around each individual fibre, carrying small vessels and fine nerves.

### Mechanism
Contraction shortens the sarcomere's I band and abolishes its H zone while the A band's length is preserved, because the thick and thin filaments slide past each other rather than shortening themselves. At the motor end plate, the neuromuscular junction where a motor neuron activates the fibre, acetylcholinesterase is positioned to hydrolyse acetylcholine after it has triggered the end-plate potential, terminating the signal.

### Key determinants
Skeletal muscle fibres are terminally differentiated and cannot divide, so repair after injury depends on satellite cells — quiescent myogenic stem cells sitting under the basal lamina of each fibre — activating, proliferating and fusing to reconstitute damaged segments.

### Clinical significance
This capacity is limited, and after a severe injury it is commonly overwhelmed: fibroblasts proliferate faster than satellite cells can regenerate muscle, so the injured area is repaired predominantly by dense collagenous scar tissue rather than by a full restoration of normal-sized, normal-numbered muscle fibres.

## summary
Smooth muscle fibres (leiomyocytes) are spindle-shaped, unstriated cells whose thin filaments lack troponin and whose contractile apparatus is anchored to dense bodies rather than Z lines, with caveolae standing in for the T-tubules of skeletal muscle.

## sections
### Definition
Smooth muscle fibres are arranged into bundles surrounded by connective tissue carrying collagen and elastic fibres, and fine reticular fibres surround each individual fibre within the bundle. Smooth muscle has no epimysium, perimysium or endomysium named the way skeletal muscle's coverings are, only this simpler connective-tissue wrapping. Each leiomyocyte is fusiform, with a single, oval, central nucleus and an eosinophilic, relatively homogeneous sarcoplasm.

### Mechanism
Smooth muscle's myofilaments differ from skeletal muscle's: the proportion of actin to myosin is greater, the thin filaments lack troponin complexes, and both filament types are not organised into parallel myofibrils or sarcomeres but instead run obliquely in bundles. Dense bodies — fusiform densities of alpha-actinin, scattered in the sarcoplasm or attached to the sarcolemma — are the functional equivalent of the Z lines that are absent here, and desmin intermediate filaments connect them. Caveolae are the smooth-muscle equivalent of the T-tubules of skeletal muscle.

### Key determinants
Because the thick and thin filaments are not registered into sarcomeres, smooth muscle shows no cross-striations on light microscopy, even though it still contracts by a sliding-filament mechanism.

### Clinical significance
Contraction in a smooth muscle fibre is based on the sliding-filament mechanism: sliding of thin filaments over thick filaments pulls on the sarcoplasmic and subsarcolemmal dense bodies, shortening the fibre as a whole even without a sarcomere to shorten.

## published_sections

## hold_these
Smooth muscle's thin filaments lack troponin; regulation of contraction does not run through the troponin-tropomyosin system used in striated muscle.
Dense bodies (Z-line equivalent) and caveolae (T-tubule equivalent) are smooth muscle's structural substitutes for the named skeletal-muscle structures.
Smooth muscle has no epimysium, perimysium or endomysium — only connective tissue around bundles and fine reticular fibres around individual fibres.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming smooth muscle is unstriated because it lacks thin filaments — it has them, they are simply not organised into registered sarcomeres.
Naming smooth muscle's connective-tissue coverings epimysium/perimysium/endomysium as if it were skeletal muscle.
