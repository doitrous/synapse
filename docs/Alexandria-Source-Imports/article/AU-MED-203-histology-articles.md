## summary
Alexandria's Nervous System histology department examines the layered organisation of neural tissue at both the cortical and retinal level, and what electron microscopy does and does not show within the neuropil between cell bodies.

## sections
### Definition
The neocortex has six histological layers — molecular/plexiform (I), external granular (II), external pyramidal (III), internal granular (IV), internal pyramidal with giant Betz cells in motor cortex (V), and multiform/polymorphic (VI). Between neuronal and glial cell bodies, grey matter contains neuropil: a dense feltwork of unmyelinated axons, dendrites, axon terminals and glial processes where most synapses form. The retina is the light-sensitive neural layer of the eye, itself organised into its own stack of nuclear and plexiform layers built around three orders of neurons (photoreceptors, bipolar cells, ganglion cells).

### Mechanism
By electron microscopy neuropil shows abundant neuronal and glial cell PROCESSES — axons, dendrites, dendritic spines, glial extensions — but, by definition, excludes the cell bodies (perikarya) of both neurons and glia, which sit outside the neuropil proper, surrounded by it. The retina's neurons are wired in an inverted architecture relative to the direction light travels: photoreceptors (rods and cones) sit at the very back, against the pigment epithelium, so that light entering the eye must cross every other retinal layer before reaching them.

### Key determinants
Layer VI, the multiform (polymorphic) layer, is both the deepest layer of the six-layered neocortex, bordering the subcortical white matter, and in most cortical areas also the broadest layer by thickness, its heterogeneous cell population projecting chiefly to the thalamus — distinct from layer I (plexiform, the thinnest, most superficial layer) and layer V (internal pyramidal, site of the giant Betz cells in motor cortex specifically). Because of the retina's inverted layering, light entering the eye passes through the retinal ganglion cell layer first, then the inner plexiform, inner nuclear and outer plexiform layers, and only last reaches the photoreceptor layer itself.

### Clinical significance
Because the multiform layer borders the subcortical white matter directly, it is often the cortical layer affected earliest as white-matter disease spreads toward the cortex. The retina's inverted wiring, despite seeming inefficient, preserves visual acuity because the inner layers crossed by light first are thin and transparent, and are displaced laterally out of the direct light path at the fovea — the retinal region of sharpest vision.

## hold_these
The six neocortical layers in order: molecular, external granular, external pyramidal, internal granular, internal pyramidal (Betz cells), multiform — layer VI (multiform) is deepest and broadest.
Neuropil = neuronal + glial processes between cell bodies; it excludes the cell bodies themselves.
Light crosses the ganglion cell layer first because the retina is inverted, reaching the photoreceptors last.

## lose_the_mark
Naming the plexiform (layer I, most superficial) layer as the deepest/broadest instead of the multiform (layer VI) layer.
Including neuronal or glial cell bodies as part of neuropil on EM — only their processes belong there.
Assuming light reaches the photoreceptor layer first because photoreceptors are the light-sensing cells.
