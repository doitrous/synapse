## summary
Haemoglobin is haem (iron protoporphyrin IX) joined to globin, four polypeptide chains that
each carry one haem group. How the porphyrin ring holds iron, how many chains globin has and
how long they are, and which chain pairs with which to make HbA, HbA2 or HbF together explain
why haemoglobin can bind oxygen reversibly, why one molecule carries four oxygens, and why
fetal blood pulls oxygen from maternal blood across the placenta.

## sections
### Definition
Haemoglobin is a tetrameric protein built from haem (iron protoporphyrin IX) bound to globin. Adult hemoglobin (HbA) is made of two alpha chains and two beta chains, each chain folded around one haem group, giving four oxygen-binding sites per molecule.

### Mechanism
Haem is a Type III porphyrin isomer: four pyrrole rings joined by methenyl bridges, with iron held in the ferrous (Fe2+) state at the centre. Iron makes four bonds to the ring's own nitrogens, plus two further bonds perpendicular to the ring plane. The 5th coordination bond links iron permanently to the nitrogen of the proximal histidine's imidazole ring, anchoring haem to the globin fold. The 6th coordination bond is empty in deoxyhaemoglobin and occupied by oxygen in oxyhaemoglobin — this is the bond that actually does the carrying, and the globin pocket around it is what keeps the interaction reversible rather than letting the iron oxidise irreversibly the moment it meets oxygen.

Globin itself is four polypeptide chains: two identical alpha chains of 141 amino acids each, folded into 7 alpha helices, and two identical beta chains of 146 amino acids each, folded into 8 alpha helices. Each chain carries exactly one haem, so haem to globin runs 4:1 and haem to polypeptide chain runs 4:4 — one haem per chain, four chains per molecule, four oxygens per haemoglobin.

The normal adult spectrum has three members that differ only in which chain pairs with alpha. Major adult haemoglobin, HbA (α2β2), makes up 95-97% of total haemoglobin. Minor adult haemoglobin, HbA2 (α2δ2), makes up 2-4%; its delta chain differs from beta by more than one residue. Fetal haemoglobin, HbF (α2γ2), predominates before birth and is replaced by HbA over the first seven months of life, falling to under 5% of total haemoglobin by then. HbF's gamma chain binds 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG) far less tightly than beta does; since 2,3-BPG is what lowers haemoglobin's oxygen affinity, HbF ends up with a higher oxygen affinity than adult HbA — exactly the property that lets the fetus pull oxygen across the placenta from the mother's own, lower-affinity, HbA.

### Key determinants
Which chain pairs with alpha (beta, delta or gamma) sets which haemoglobin type is present. Developmental stage sets the proportion of HbF versus HbA. 2,3-BPG binding, itself a property of the beta chain, sets oxygen affinity, which is why gamma-chain haemoglobin (HbF) behaves differently from beta-chain haemoglobin (HbA) at the same oxygen tension.

### Clinical significance
Every haemoglobin variant and every haemoglobinopathy is a variation on this same plan — a different chain, a different pairing, or a different amino acid within a chain. Reading an electrophoresis strip or interpreting a glycosylated-haemoglobin result both start from knowing which normal chain combination is being compared against.

## summary
Three families of abnormal haemoglobin fail in three different ways. Sickle cell disease
substitutes one amino acid and lets deoxygenated haemoglobin polymerise. Thalassaemia makes
too little of a structurally normal chain. Methaemoglobinaemia oxidises the iron itself so it
can no longer carry oxygen at all — and only some of its causes respond to treatment.

## sections
### Definition
A haemoglobinopathy is any inherited or acquired change to haemoglobin that alters its structure, its synthesis, or its oxidation state, with functional consequences.

### Mechanism
Sickle cell disease (HbS) is caused by a single substitution in the beta globin gene: glutamic acid at position 6 (polar) is replaced by valine (non-polar). This creates a sticky, non-polar patch on the surface of deoxygenated HbS that matches a complementary groove on other deoxy-HbS molecules. When blood is deoxygenated, these sticky patches and grooves bind together and the molecules polymerise into long fibrous precipitates, forcing the red cell into the sickle shape. Sickled cells are fragile, causing haemolytic anaemia, and can become trapped in small vessels, causing thrombosis and tissue damage.

Thalassaemia is a different kind of fault: a mutation in a regulatory gene reduces or abolishes synthesis of one globin chain, rather than making an abnormal chain. In alpha-thalassaemia, reduced alpha synthesis leaves excess beta or gamma chains, which combine with themselves into abnormal tetramers — HbH (four beta chains) or Hb Bart's (four gamma chains). In beta-thalassaemia, reduced beta synthesis leaves excess alpha chains, which combine instead with delta or gamma chains, raising HbA2 or HbF above their normal share. Because the chains themselves are structurally normal, thalassaemia is fundamentally a quantity problem, not a quality problem — the opposite of sickle cell disease.

Methaemoglobinaemia is a third, distinct fault: haemoglobin iron is oxidised from ferrous (Fe2+) to ferric (Fe3+), and ferric iron cannot bind oxygen. Congenital haemoglobin M results from a mutation that replaces the proximal or distal histidine with tyrosine, stabilising the ferric state through a tight ionic bond with the tyrosine's phenolate anion — reducing agents cannot reverse this, so the only treatment is conservative (transfusion). Congenital deficiency of the NADH-cytochrome b5 reductase system, by contrast, responds well to reducing agents such as methylene blue, because the enzyme system that would normally keep iron reduced is simply missing, not chemically blocked. Acquired (toxic) methaemoglobinaemia follows exposure to oxidising drugs (phenacetin, sulphonamides) or chemicals (nitrites) and also responds to reducing agents.

### Key determinants
Which globin gene is mutated (structural vs. regulatory) and which chemistry is disturbed (an amino acid substitution, a synthesis rate, or an oxidation state) separates these three families and predicts which treatment, if any, will work.

### Clinical significance
Recognising which of the three mechanisms is in play from a vignette — a specific amino acid substitution, an imbalance in chain ratios on electrophoresis, or a colour change that does or does not respond to a reducing agent — is what lets a student pick the right diagnosis and, for methaemoglobinaemia, the right treatment.

## summary
Folic acid and vitamin K are unrelated vitamins that both matter for blood: folic acid, via
tetrahydrofolate, supplies the one-carbon units DNA synthesis needs, so its deficiency stops
red cells maturing properly. Vitamin K, via a carboxylase, activates four clotting factors so
they can bind calcium — without it, clotting cannot proceed even though the factors are
present.

## sections
### Definition
Folic acid (folacin) is a water-soluble vitamin reduced in the body to tetrahydrofolic acid (THF), the coenzyme for one-carbon metabolism. Vitamin K is a fat-soluble vitamin required for the post-translational activation of specific clotting factors.

### Mechanism
THF carries a one-carbon unit — methyl, methylene, methenyl or formyl — donated mainly by the beta carbon of serine, by glycine, or by formate from tryptophan metabolism. This one-carbon pool is needed to synthesise glycine, serine, methionine and histidine, to build carbons 2 and 8 of the purine ring, and to methylate deoxyuridylate to deoxythymidylate (dTMP) via thymidylate synthase. Folate deficiency — whether from poor intake or as a knock-on effect of B12 deficiency — starves this last step in particular, slowing DNA synthesis and the maturation of red cells; the result is a macrocytic anaemia with megaloblastic changes in the bone marrow, plus glossitis and gastrointestinal disturbance. Because folate's role is DNA synthesis rather than nerve myelination, folate deficiency, unlike vitamin B12 deficiency, does not itself produce neurological signs — the clinical detail that separates the two on a vignette.

Vitamin K works through an entirely different mechanism, on an entirely different target. Clotting factors II (prothrombin), VII, IX and X are made in the liver as inactive precursors. Vitamin K is the essential cofactor for the carboxylase enzyme that converts specific glutamic acid residues on these precursors into gamma-carboxyglutamic acid. These carboxylated residues are good calcium chelators, and it is calcium-binding that lets the activated factor attach to a phospholipid membrane, where the rest of the clotting cascade can proceed. Without vitamin K, the factors are still present in the blood, but they cannot bind calcium or the membrane, so clotting fails — the coagulation time is prolonged, not shortened, and this is diagnostic of vitamin K deficiency.

### Key determinants
For folic acid: dietary intake, and whether the deficiency is primary or secondary to B12 deficiency, both determine severity. For vitamin K: dietary intake, intestinal synthesis by gut flora, and fat absorption (since it is fat-soluble) all determine how much reaches the liver's carboxylase.

### Clinical significance
A macrocytic anaemia with neurological signs points away from folate and towards B12; a prolonged coagulation time with normal platelet count points towards vitamin K deficiency or liver disease, and towards antagonists such as warfarin, which exploit this exact pathway.

## summary
Iron's journey from the gut to a red cell — absorption, transport on transferrin, and storage
as ferritin — sets what falls or rises in a full blood count and an iron panel. Myoglobin's
single haem contrasts with haemoglobin's four, explaining why one stores oxygen and the other
transports it; and a simple reducing-agent test tells oxyhaemoglobin apart from
carboxyhaemoglobin at the bedside.

## sections
### Definition
Iron metabolism covers dietary absorption, plasma transport, and tissue storage of iron. Myoglobin is a single-chain, single-haem oxygen-storage protein. Oxyhaemoglobin and carboxyhaemoglobin are two haemoglobin derivatives distinguished by what occupies the 6th coordination bond.

### Mechanism
About 10% of dietary iron is absorbed, mainly in the duodenum. Acidity helps liberate iron from organic combinations; reducing agents such as vitamin C and glutathione convert ferric iron (Fe3+) to the absorbable ferrous form (Fe2+); proteins with sulfur-containing amino acids do the same; and dietary haem iron is absorbed directly, without needing reduction. Achlorhydria, phytates and oxalates all inhibit absorption. Inside the enterocyte, iron is transported across the apical membrane as Fe2+ by the divalent metal transporter (DMT-1), while dietary haem is taken up separately by a haem transporter and then stripped of its iron by haem oxygenase; the released iron either joins the intracellular ferritin pool or crosses the basolateral membrane, via ferroportin, into plasma.

In plasma, iron is oxidised back to Fe3+ by ceruloplasmin (serum ferroxidase) and binds transferrin, the iron-transport protein; transferrin normally runs about 35% saturated, with protein-bound iron of 50-150 µg/dL against a total iron-binding capacity (TIBC) of 300-360 µg/dL. Of the body's total 5 g of iron, roughly 60% is in haemoglobin; most of the rest is stored as ferritin, mainly in the liver, with any excess stored as haemosiderin.

Myoglobin, in contrast to haemoglobin's four-chain, four-haem structure, is a single polypeptide carrying a single haem and therefore a single oxygen-binding site. This single-site structure gives myoglobin a hyperbolic (not sigmoid) oxygen-dissociation curve and a higher oxygen affinity than haemoglobin at low oxygen tensions, fitting its role as an oxygen store within muscle rather than a bulk transporter.

Two haemoglobin derivatives that look alike but are not are oxyhaemoglobin and carboxyhaemoglobin. Both are bright red, but they are told apart by adding sodium dithionite, a reducing agent: oxyhaemoglobin is reduced to deoxyhaemoglobin and darkens, while carboxyhaemoglobin resists reduction, because carbon monoxide is bound roughly 200 times more tightly than oxygen and stays put. A blood sample that fails to darken with sodium dithionite despite its bright-red colour is the laboratory sign of carbon monoxide poisoning.

### Key determinants
Dietary form of iron (haem vs. non-haem), gastric acidity, and reducing agents in the diet determine how much iron is absorbed. Chain number and haem number determine whether an oxygen-carrying protein transports (haemoglobin) or stores (myoglobin) oxygen. The binding affinity of a ligand for the 6th coordination bond (oxygen vs. carbon monoxide) determines whether a reducing agent can displace it.

### Clinical significance
Ferritin and TIBC track iron deficiency and overload from opposite directions: low ferritin with high TIBC is iron deficiency; high ferritin with low TIBC is iron overload (haemochromatosis, "bronze diabetes"). The sodium dithionite test is a quick, low-technology way to catch carbon monoxide poisoning that a visual inspection of blood colour alone would miss.

## summary
Building and breaking down glycogen each need a dedicated enzyme beyond the obvious one:
glycogen synthase cannot branch the molecule on its own, and glycogen phosphorylase cannot
finish breaking one down on its own. A third, unrelated pathway — the uronic acid pathway —
activates glucose into a form that makes bilirubin, steroids and drugs excretable.

## sections
### Definition
Glycogenesis is the synthesis of glycogen from glucose; glycogenolysis is its breakdown. The uronic acid pathway is a minor, ATP-independent route from glucose-6-phosphate that produces UDP-glucuronic acid.

### Mechanism
Glycogen synthase, the key enzyme of glycogenesis, transfers the glucose unit of UDP-glucose onto the non-reducing end of an existing glycogen primer, forming an alpha1,4 glucosidic bond and elongating the chain. Once a chain reaches a minimum length of about 11 glucose units, the branching enzyme (amylo-1,4→1,6 transglucosidase) transfers a block of at least 6 glucose units from the end of that chain onto a nearby chain, attaching it there by a new alpha1,6 bond and creating a branch point; glycogen synthase then continues to elongate the new branch. Repeated many times, this is what makes glycogen a compact, heavily branched molecule rather than one long chain — and a heavily branched molecule has far more free ends from which glucose can be released quickly when it is needed.

Breaking glycogen down needs the reverse partnership. Glycogen phosphorylase, the rate-limiting enzyme of glycogenolysis, acts on the alpha1,4 bonds of the outer branches, releasing glucose-1-phosphate units one at a time by phosphorolysis (using inorganic phosphate, not water) — but it stops four glucose residues short of every alpha1,6 branch point and cannot go further. The debranching enzyme finishes the job in two steps: first it transfers a block of the remaining chain to expose the single alpha1,6-linked glucose at the branch point, and then it hydrolyses that one bond with water, releasing ordinary free glucose rather than glucose-1-phosphate. Without the debranching enzyme, glycogen breakdown would stall at every branch point, leaving a molecule studded with short, un-mobilisable stubs.

The uronic acid pathway is unrelated to glycogen metabolism but shares the same starting point, glucose-6-phosphate. Via glucose-1-phosphate and UDP-glucose, it produces UDP-glucuronic acid — the activated form of glucuronic acid that conjugates less-polar compounds such as bilirubin, steroids and many drugs, making them water-soluble enough to excrete. The same pathway also supplies the glucuronic acid used to build glycosaminoglycans such as heparin and hyaluronic acid, though in humans, unlike some other species, it cannot go on to make vitamin C.

### Key determinants
Chain length determines when the branching enzyme, rather than glycogen synthase alone, is needed; branch-point proximity determines when the debranching enzyme, rather than phosphorylase alone, is needed. What the uronic acid pathway's product needs to conjugate (bilirubin, a steroid, a drug) determines how much flux the pathway carries.

### Clinical significance
A glycogen-storage disease can arise from a fault in any one of these enzymes, each with a different biochemical signature; and reduced glucuronyl transferase activity (as in neonatal or Gilbert's/Crigler-Najjar hyperbilirubinaemia) is a direct consequence of this same conjugation step failing.

## summary
A result is only as good as the specimen it came from. Haemolysis, the wrong anticoagulant,
and a mislabelled tube each corrupt a result in a different, recognisable way — and knowing
which stage of testing an error happened at is what separates a preanalytical mistake from an
analytical one.

## sections
### Definition
Specimen handling covers everything that happens to a blood sample between collection and analysis: how it is drawn, what additive it is collected into, and how it is stored, all of which can alter the result before the analyser ever sees it.

### Mechanism
Red cells hold potassium, AST and LDH at concentrations far higher inside the cell than in plasma. If the sample is haemolysed — during a difficult draw, rough handling, or prolonged storage — these intracellular analytes leak out and are falsely reported as raised, even though the patient's true circulating level is normal.

Different additives prevent clotting by different chemistry. Ammonium chloride causes haemolysis (when used experimentally to lyse red cells for a test) because the red cell membrane lets ammonium ions in but keeps chloride ions out; ammonium diffuses in, water follows it osmotically, and the cell ruptures. Ether, by contrast, lyses the cell by dissolving the lipid part of the membrane directly, with no osmotic step at all. In an anticoagulant tube proper, potassium oxalate and sodium fluoride prevent clotting by precipitating the sample's calcium as an insoluble salt, removing the calcium the clotting cascade depends on — a mechanism distinct from heparin (which potentiates antithrombin) or citrate (which chelates calcium in solution rather than precipitating it).

Laboratory errors are classified by where in the testing process they occur. Mislabelling a patient's sample is a preanalytical error, since it happens before analysis. A breakdown in the instrument performing the test is an analytical error, since it happens during measurement. Most laboratory errors, in practice, are preanalytical — which is why specimen identification and collection technique receive as much quality attention as the analyser itself.

### Key determinants
Collection technique and storage time determine how much haemolysis occurs. The additive chosen determines which chemistry prevents clotting, and therefore which tests that specimen remains valid for. The stage at which a mistake happens — before, during, or after analysis — determines its classification.

### Clinical significance
A clinician who sees an unexpectedly high potassium or LDH should ask whether the sample was haemolysed before acting on it; a laboratory investigating a wrong result should ask at which stage the error entered, since a preanalytical fix (better collection technique) is different from an analytical one (instrument maintenance).

## summary
Two related instruments answer two different questions about a sample: a spectrophotometer
reads how much of one wavelength a coloured solution absorbs, and a nephelometer reads how
much light a turbid (particle-laden) solution scatters. Confusing them, or confusing the
cuvette that holds the sample with the filter that selects the wavelength, is the commonest
mistake this topic produces.

## sections
### Purpose and indications
Spectrophotometry measures the concentration of a coloured compound in solution — the basis of most colorimetric clinical chemistry assays (glucose, bilirubin and many others). Turbidimetric and nephelometric methods instead measure the concentration of suspended particles, used for assays such as immunoprecipitation reactions where the analyte itself is not coloured.

### Principle
A spectrophotometer passes light through the sample and measures how much of one selected wavelength is absorbed, which is proportional to the concentration of the coloured compound (Beer-Lambert principle). A colorimeter or nephelometer measuring turbidity instead reads light that has been scattered by suspended particles rather than absorbed along the direct beam; a nephelometer, built specifically to detect scattered light at an angle from the main beam, outperforms a simple colorimeter for this purpose, because a colorimeter is built to read absorbance along the direct path, not scatter to the side.

### How it is performed
The sample, already made to react and develop colour where needed, is placed in a cuvette — an optically transparent cell that holds the solution in the light path without itself absorbing or scattering light. Before the light reaches the sample, a filter (a simpler instrument) or a monochromator (a more precise one) selectively transmits the one wavelength the assay needs and absorbs the rest, so only light the compound of interest actually absorbs reaches the detector on the far side of the cuvette.

### Normal findings
Not applicable in the usual clinical sense — the "normal" here is an instrument reading zero absorbance/scatter for a blank (no-analyte) sample, against which the test sample's reading is compared.

### Abnormal findings and interpretation
A reading outside the expected range reflects the analyte's own concentration, not an instrument fault, provided the correct component (filter/monochromator for colour, nephelometer for turbidity) was used for the assay in question — using a colorimeter where a nephelometer was needed will under-read a turbid sample.

### Limitations and pitfalls
Selecting the wrong wavelength, or using an absorbance-based instrument for a particle- scatter assay, both make a physically correct instrument give a clinically wrong number. Confusing the cuvette's job (holding the sample) with the filter's job (selecting the wavelength) is the specific pitfall this topic tests for.

## summary
A cluster of laboratory tests answer different questions about glucose handling: the OGTT
resolves diagnostic uncertainty (but is pointless once diabetes is confirmed); a low renal
threshold gives glucosuria despite a normal OGTT; HbA1c and fructosamine report control over
different windows of time; and microalbuminuria catches diabetic kidney damage before it
becomes irreversible.

## sections
### Definition
This cluster of tests investigates glucose homeostasis and its complications: the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), renal glucosuria, glycated-protein monitoring (HbA1c and fructosamine), microalbuminuria, and the fructose-specific Seliwanoff's test.

### Mechanism
The OGTT measures the plasma glucose response to a standard oral glucose load, and is indicated to resolve equivocal cases — for example, distinguishing benign renal glucosuria from true diabetes, or clarifying a borderline result in pregnancy — rather than to confirm a diagnosis that is already clear. It is not advised in patients whose diabetes is already confirmed, nor in acutely hospitalised patients, whose glucose handling is disturbed by illness rather than by the diagnostic question the test exists to answer.

Renal glucosuria is glucose appearing in the urine while plasma glucose stays at or below the normal renal threshold (about 180 mg/dL) — a low tubular threshold for glucose reabsorption, not hyperglycaemia, and it is ruled out precisely by an OGTT that shows a normal plasma glucose curve despite the glucosuria. For glycaemic control day to day, serum or plasma is the specimen glucose is estimated from, while a separate whole-blood sample is what point-of-care glucometers typically read — the two numbers are not interchangeable.

HbA1c and fructosamine both track glycaemic control by measuring how much glucose has become non-enzymatically attached to a protein, but they differ in which protein and therefore in which time window they report. HbA1c tracks glucose bound to haemoglobin inside the 120-day-lived red cell, giving a two-to-three-month window. Fructosamine tracks glucose bound to plasma proteins, chiefly albumin, whose shorter half-life gives a two-to-three-week window — useful when a more recent picture is needed than HbA1c can provide, such as after a recent treatment change or in pregnancy.

Microalbuminuria is albumin in urine at concentrations too low for a standard dipstick (which detects overt proteinuria) to catch. Because it is the earliest detectable sign of diabetic nephropathy, testing for it lets treatment begin before structural kidney damage becomes irreversible; a first-morning-void sample is preferred because the albumin-to-creatinine ratio in it is less variable than in a random daytime sample.

Finally, Seliwanoff's test detects fructose specifically in urine. In a child with recurrent vomiting after fruit or sucrose, a positive Seliwanoff's test confirms fructosuria and points towards a deficiency of fructokinase (essential fructosuria) or aldolase B (hereditary fructose intolerance) as the underlying cause.

### Key determinants
Whether the diagnostic question is already answered (ruling out an OGTT in confirmed diabetics), how recent a picture of control is needed (fructosamine vs. HbA1c), and how early a complication is being screened for (microalbuminuria before overt proteinuria) all determine which test in this cluster is the right one to order.

### Clinical significance
Ordering the wrong test from this cluster wastes time and can mislead: an OGTT in a confirmed diabetic adds nothing; relying on HbA1c alone can miss a recent deterioration that fructosamine would catch; and waiting for a positive urine dipstick before checking for microalbuminuria means nephropathy has already progressed past the point this screen is designed to catch it at.

## summary
Every immunoglobulin shares one Y-shaped, two-light/two-heavy-chain plan, with the
variable regions doing the recognising and the constant regions doing the rest. The five
classes vary this one plan by heavy-chain type, and each does a different job: IgG guards the
long game, IgA guards the mucosa, IgM leads the first response, IgD sits on the B cell, and
IgE drives allergy.

## sections
### Definition
An immunoglobulin (antibody) is a Y-shaped glycoprotein made of two identical light chains and two identical heavy chains, joined by disulphide bonds, secreted by plasma cells on antigen stimulation.

### Mechanism
Both light and heavy chains carry a variable region, at the amino-terminal end, and a constant region, at the carboxy-terminal end. The variable regions have a variable amino acid sequence and form the antigen-binding site — each immunoglobulin molecule has two identical binding sites. Constant regions have a constant sequence within a class and carry the carbohydrate that makes immunoglobulins glycoproteins.

Five classes are distinguished by their heavy-chain type: gamma (IgG), alpha (IgA), mu (IgM), delta (IgD) and epsilon (IgE). IgG, a monomer and about 75% of serum immunoglobulin, has a low enough molecular weight to cross blood vessel walls into tissue and to cross the placenta, giving the newborn passive immunity for its first six months; it dominates the secondary immune response. IgA exists as a serum monomer and, more importantly, as a secretory dimer that protects mucosal surfaces and is passed to the newborn in colostrum and milk. IgM, a pentamer, is the first antibody made in a primary immune response and gives a strong agglutination reaction with antigen. IgD is mainly a receptor for antigen on the surface of B lymphocytes. IgE, present at very low concentration, binds to receptors on mast cells and basophils via its Fc portion; when antigen cross-links bound IgE, the cell degranulates, releasing histamine and other mediators responsible for type I (immediate) hypersensitivity — urticaria from vasodilating mediators, bronchoconstriction from others.

### Key determinants
Heavy-chain type sets the class and therefore the role. Whether the response is primary (first exposure, IgM-dominant) or secondary (repeat exposure, IgG-dominant) sets which class predominates at a given moment. Location — serum vs. mucosal surface — sets whether IgA appears as a monomer or a secretory dimer.

### Clinical significance
Which class of antibody a laboratory or vaccine strategy is trying to raise (mucosal IgA for an inhaled or oral vaccine, IgG for lasting systemic immunity) follows directly from which class does that job physiologically; and an allergic reaction is, mechanistically, IgE doing exactly what it is built to do, just against an antigen that need not be a genuine threat.

IgM dominates the primary response; IgG dominates the secondary response — the two classes that examiners most often swap.

## lose_the_mark
Placing the antigen-binding site on the constant region instead of the variable region.
Reversing which class (IgM vs. IgG) dominates the primary vs. secondary response.
