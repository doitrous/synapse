## summary
Almost every medical word is assembled from a small, reusable set of parts: a word root carrying the core meaning, an optional prefix and suffix around it, and a combining vowel that joins them when two consonants would otherwise collide. Knowing this system, and the separate rule for pluralising the Latin and Greek nouns it produces, turns an unfamiliar word into one that can be decoded rather than memorised — and tells a descriptive term apart from an eponym, which cannot be decoded at all.

## sections
### Definition
A medical term is built from up to four kinds of piece. The word root carries the core meaning — cardi(o) for heart, nephr(o) for kidney. A prefix, in front of the root, narrows or modifies that meaning — hyper- for excessive, peri- for around. A suffix, after the root, states what is happening to it or names a condition — -itis for inflammation, -ectomy for surgical removal. A combining vowel, almost always "o", is inserted only when it is needed to join two consonant sounds smoothly, and dropped when the next piece already starts with a vowel.

### Mechanism
Read a term as pieces, not as one block. "Cardiomegaly" splits into cardi(o) — the heart — joined by its combining vowel to "megaly", enlargement: an enlarged heart. "Cardiac" needs no combining vowel, because the suffix -ac already begins with a vowel. The department's own bank treats "-megaly" here as a second word root rather than a suffix, since it can stand as a free-standing meaning ("enlargement") rather than only ever attaching to something else — a reminder that the root/suffix boundary is read off the specific word, not assumed from a fixed list.

A term's plural follows the rule of the language it was borrowed from, not English. A noun ending -x takes -ces (thorax → thoraces); -is takes -es (testis → testes); -um takes -a (bacterium → bacteria); -a takes -ae (vertebra → vertebrae); -on takes -a (ganglion → ganglia). Adding an English "-s" to any of these is wrong, and it is the distractor the department's own bank offers on almost every noun it asks about.

### Key determinants
Not every term follows this system. A descriptive term is built exactly as above and can be decoded from its parts. An eponym instead names the person credited with first describing the structure or condition — "fallopian tube", after Gabriele Falloppio — and carries no internal clue to its meaning. An eponym has to be learned as a whole word; trying to split it into a root and suffix, the way a descriptive term is split, finds nothing there.

### Clinical significance
A student who holds the word-part system can meet an unfamiliar term in an exam or on a ward round and work out roughly what it means, rather than needing to have seen that exact word before. The same system also explains why some pairs of terms that sound similar mean different things once their parts are read separately, and why a plural that "sounds right" in English can still be the wrong one for a Latin or Greek noun.

## summary
Gravida and para are the two numbers every obstetric history opens with, and they are not the same count: gravida is every pregnancy a woman has had, and para is only the ones that reached a viable gestation. A woman with two children after four pregnancies is charted "gravida 4, para 2" — reversing the two numbers, or treating them as one, misreads her whole obstetric history at a glance.

## sections
### Definition
Gravida is the total number of times a woman has been pregnant, including the current pregnancy and any that ended in miscarriage or termination. Para is the number of those pregnancies that reached a viable gestation, whether or not the child was born alive. The two numbers are written together as "gravida X, para Y".

### Mechanism
Every pregnancy counts once toward gravida, at the moment it is confirmed. Whether it later counts toward para depends only on whether it reached viability, not on the outcome of the delivery — a stillbirth after viability is still a para; a miscarriage before viability never becomes one. A woman pregnant for the first time is a primigravida (gravida 1). One who has never been pregnant at all is a nulligravida; one who has been pregnant but never carried to viability is a nullipara, even if she is not a nulligravida. A woman who has been pregnant, or given birth, more than once is a multigravida or multipara respectively.

### Key determinants
The department's own bank fixes the worked case: two children, four pregnancies, charted "gravida 4, para 2". The determining question for para is always "did this pregnancy reach viability", never "was there a live child at the end of it" and never "how many children are there now" — twins from one pregnancy add one to para, not two.

### Clinical significance
Gravida and para are read together at the start of almost every obstetric encounter, and a term built the same way an ordinary medical word is (word root plus suffix — see the word-building article) sits underneath both: "-gravida" and "-para" are themselves suffixes attached to a count or a qualifier (nulli-, primi-, multi-). Getting the two numbers backwards, or collapsing them into one, changes what the rest of the history is understood to mean before a single further question is asked.
