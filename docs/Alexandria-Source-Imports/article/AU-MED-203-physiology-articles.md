## summary
Alexandria's Nervous System physiology department pairs each mechanism with the specific alternative its own Workshop Quiz bank tests it against — what a muscle spindle actually detects and how it is innervated, which hypothalamic lesion causes which feeding/aggression syndrome, which vestibular organ senses which plane of movement, and which classic drug potentiates which inhibitory transmitter.

## sections
### Definition
The muscle spindle is a fusiform, encapsulated stretch receptor running parallel to extrafusal fibers, signalling muscle length and the rate of change of length — distinct from the Golgi tendon organ, which signals muscle tension. Reflex circuits, hypothalamic feeding/satiety centres, the special senses (vision, hearing, vestibular sensation), and the pharmacology of CNS inhibitory neurotransmission are the physiological systems this Workshop Quiz bank draws its questions from.

Pupillary reflex physiology adds two closely related definitions the bank tests as a contrasting pair: Argyll-Robertson pupil is a light-near dissociation (light reflex absent, near/accommodation reflex preserved) classic in neurosyphilis, whose lesion is NOT considered to sit within the Edinger-Westphal nucleus itself (a true nuclear lesion would abolish both reflexes); and the near (triad) response itself has three components — convergence, accommodation (via increased ANTERIOR lens curvature, not corneal curvature) and pupillary constriction (which increases depth of focus).

### Mechanism
The muscle spindle's intrafusal fibers (about two nuclear bag fibers and several nuclear chain fibers, not six to eight bag fibers) sit within a gelatinous fluid-filled periaxial space inside the spindle capsule, and carry both sensory (Ia, II) and motor (gamma efferent) innervation. Reflex circuits built on such receptors include the crossed extensor reflex, in which a painful stimulus produces ipsilateral flexor withdrawal together with contralateral extensor activation (via cord-crossing interneurons) to support body weight — distinct from simple reciprocal inhibition or Golgi tendon organ-mediated autogenic inhibition. Two classic CNS-depressant/convulsant mechanisms act on opposite sides of inhibitory neurotransmission: strychnine is a competitive antagonist at glycine receptors — glycine being the principal inhibitory neurotransmitter of spinal interneurons — so blocking it removes spinal inhibition and produces convulsions, while barbiturates instead potentiate GABA-A receptor-mediated inhibition, prolonging the chloride channel's open time in the presence of GABA (and, at higher doses, opening it directly).

The gate-control theory of pain describes a further inhibitory mechanism: large-diameter, fast type A-beta tactile afferents activate inhibitory interneurons in the substantia gelatinosa that suppress onward transmission of pain signals carried by smaller A-delta/C fibers — the physiological basis for pain relief from rubbing a painful area or from TENS. Dopamine, beyond its role in Parkinson disease (below), is also central to prefrontal working memory and to schizophrenia's pathophysiology (mesolimbic excess driving positive symptoms, mesocortical deficiency contributing to negative symptoms); its principal metabolite is homovanillic acid (HVA), and low-dose 'renal-dose' dopamine has no established, evidence-based benefit for renal failure despite historical use.

### Key determinants
Parkinson disease results from degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta projecting via the nigrostriatal pathway to the striatum; the resulting dopamine deficiency underlies bradykinesia, rigidity and tremor, distinguishing Parkinson's defining neurochemical lesion from other basal-ganglia/dementia disorders. The ventromedial hypothalamic nucleus acts as a satiety centre; its bilateral destruction removes this restraint, producing hyperphagia, obesity, and increased rage/aggression — the opposite lesion syndrome from the lateral (feeding) hypothalamic nucleus. Descending motor control follows a general rule that the corticobulbar tract supplies most cranial nerve motor nuclei bilaterally, with the lower facial nucleus and hypoglossal genioglossus supply as the clinically important exceptions. In vestibular sensation, the saccule macula is maximally sensitive to linear head acceleration in the vertical plane, complementing the horizontally-oriented utricle macula, while the cristae ampullares of the semicircular ducts instead detect angular (rotational) acceleration. In audition, the superior olivary complex in the pons is the first site where binaural cochlear-nucleus inputs converge to localise a sound's direction — distinct from the similarly-named but functionally unrelated inferior olivary nucleus, which projects climbing fibers to the cerebellum. In vision, retinal ganglion cells are the retina's only true action-potential-generating neurons; photoreceptors, bipolar cells and horizontal cells signal instead by graded potentials.

Parkinsonian rigidity is specifically lead-pipe or cog-wheel (uniform, full-range resistance in both flexor and extensor groups, without hyperkinesia or exaggerated tendon jerks), a different hypertonia pattern from upper motor neuron spasticity's clasp-knife quality and brisk reflexes. In sleep physiology, REM sleep is marked by near-total skeletal muscle atonia (protecting against dream enactment), vivid well-recalled dreaming, and INCREASED, more irregular heart and respiratory rate, contrasting with the otolith organs' (utricle, saccule) role in detecting static head position and linear acceleration via gravity, distinct from the semicircular canals' angular acceleration detection. The hippocampus, a medial temporal lobe/limbic structure, is essential for declarative memory consolidation and spatial navigation — its principal, most heavily tested function, illustrated classically by bilateral hippocampal damage (patient H.M.) producing severe anterograde amnesia. Neocerebellar (lateral hemisphere) dysfunction produces dysdiadochokinesia, hypotonia and intention tremor — the mirror image, in tone and tremor pattern, of Parkinsonism's rigidity, resting tremor and mask face.

### Clinical significance
Because bilateral corticobulbar innervation has exceptions at the lower facial nucleus and hypoglossal genioglossus supply, a central (upper motor neuron) facial palsy classically spares the forehead while a peripheral (lower motor neuron) palsy does not — a standard bedside localising sign. Because Parkinson's dopamine deficiency is presynaptic (loss of the dopamine-producing neurons themselves rather than the postsynaptic receptor), replacing the precursor with levodopa remains effective even late in disease. Barbiturate overdose is more dangerous than pure benzodiazepine overdose because, at high dose, barbiturates open the GABA-A chloride channel directly rather than only potentiating GABA's own action, causing fatal respiratory depression. Strychnine poisoning was historically a cause of death by asphyxiation from sustained respiratory muscle spasm, which is why the drug is no longer used therapeutically.

Low CSF chloride, alongside low glucose and raised protein/white cells, is the classic CSF signature of bacterial (or tuberculous) meningitis, distinguishing it from viral encephalitis's normal-chloride, normal-glucose lymphocytic pleocytosis. Spinal shock produces hypotension with a marked decrease in venous return from loss of sympathetic vasomotor tone (not a rise in arterial pressure), with reflexes — including the plantar response — typically absent during the acute phase; the exaggerated mass reflex and a positive Babinski sign only re-emerge once spinal shock resolves.

Workshop Quiz 3 and 4 extend the muscle spindle/reflex material this bank already draws on. The muscle spindle receives gamma efferent, Ia and group II afferent innervation, but NOT A-alpha fibers, which instead supply the extrafusal fibers outside the spindle capsule; during active contraction, parallel alpha-gamma co-activation keeps the spindle's intrafusal fibers taut and responsive to stretch, preserving proprioceptive feedback, whereas ALPHA discharge alone (without gamma coactivation) instead unloads and silences the spindle as the muscle shortens. The Golgi tendon organ, in series with the muscle at the musculotendinous junction, produces autogenic (Ib-mediated) inhibition of the SAME muscle's own alpha motor neurons — the functional opposite of the spindle's own excitatory (Ia-mediated) stretch reflex. A unilateral lesion of the primary somatosensory area (S1) produces astereognosis in the CONTRALATERAL hand, since somatosensory input has already decussated before reaching the cortex; and in Brown-Séquard (spinal cord hemisection) syndrome, vibration and proprioception are lost IPSILATERAL to the lesion (dorsal columns, uncrossed until the medulla), the mirror image of the CONTRALATERAL pain/temperature loss from the already-crossed spinothalamic tract.

A further cluster covers pain and integrative spinal physiology. The classic descending pain-control transmitters are serotonin, norepinephrine and enkephalin — acetylcholine is not among them — and enkephalin itself acts presynaptically, closing voltage-gated Ca2+ channels in the central terminals of nociceptive afferents to reduce transmitter release onto the dorsal horn's transmission (T) cell; the gate-control theory builds on this same synapse, since large A-beta tactile afferents excite inhibitory interneurons that in turn inhibit the transmission cell itself. Occlusion is the reverse of facilitation: it occurs when two afferent inputs share an overlapping (already-firing) neuronal pool, producing a LESS-than-additive combined response, while after-discharge describes a response that persists after the triggering stimulus itself has stopped (typically via reverberating circuits), distinguishing it from simple stimulus-locked adaptation. In chronic (post-shock) spinal cord injury, the mass reflex arises from irradiation of afferent impulses spreading widely through the isolated cord below the lesion, producing an exaggerated, multi-system reflex response to a trivial stimulus. Primary cutaneous hyperalgesia, unlike the secondary hyperalgesia of the surrounding normal skin, results from a lowered peripheral pain-receptor threshold within the injured tissue itself.

Special-senses and cortical physiology round out the Quiz 3-4 material. In both the primary motor and primary sensory cortex, the body's somatotopic map is inverted (leg superomedial, face inferolateral) — a shared feature of both areas, not one that distinguishes them. Rods are more sensitive to low-intensity (scotopic) light than cones, the basis of night vision, while increased amplitude of basilar membrane vibration (not decreased amplitude, and not fewer stimulated hair cells) is what the auditory system interprets as increased loudness. This source's own Workshop Quiz 3 key marks the neocerebellum, rather than the flocculonodular lobe (archicerebellum), as the balance-related cerebellar division, and its Workshop Quiz 4 key marks encephalitis, rather than bacterial meningitis, as the cause of decreased CSF glucose — standard teaching instead attributes balance to the flocculonodular lobe's direct vestibular connections and low CSF glucose specifically to bacterial (not viral) CNS infection; see each concept's own field_notes for this discrepancy. Cogwheel rigidity is a basal ganglia (extrapyramidal), not a corticospinal tract, sign — corticospinal disease instead produces spasticity, an extensor plantar response and exaggerated tendon jerks. Glutamate, not GABA (the CNS's principal inhibitory transmitter), is the CNS's most abundant excitatory neurotransmitter. And among substances compared between CSF and plasma, folate is a genuine exception, actively transported by the choroid plexus to a HIGHER concentration in CSF than in plasma, unlike urea, protein or calcium.

The Week 1 EOM Final adds a general sensory-receptor physiology cluster the earlier Quizzes' muscle-spindle-specific material builds on. Modality discrimination depends on the SPECIFICITY of the receptor activated (the law of specific nerve energies), while locality discrimination depends instead on the SPECIFIC (topographic) PROJECTION of the pathway to its own cortical territory — two distinct coding principles often confused with one another. Sensory receptors as a class show adaptation (a declining response to a constant, maintained stimulus), ranging from tonic/slowly-adapting types (pain receptors, muscle spindle, baroreceptors) to phasic/rapidly-adapting types (touch receptors such as Meissner's and Pacinian corpuscles); mechanoreceptors specifically (baroreceptors, proprioceptors, atrial stretch receptors) are transduced by mechanical deformation, distinct from thermoreceptors such as cold receptors. The receptor (generator) potential itself is a graded, local, non-propagated event, generated by stimulus-gated (e.g. mechanically-gated) rather than voltage-gated channels, and — unlike the action potential — has no refractory period; the action potential, by contrast, is generated by voltage-gated channel activation, and codes increasing stimulus intensity as increased firing FREQUENCY, never amplitude, since it remains all-or-none. Nociceptors (free nerve endings) are densely distributed in richly-innervated surfaces such as the buccal mucosa; among named corpuscular receptors, Ruffini's corpuscle functions as a sustained-pressure receptor (stimulated by mechanical displacement of the collagen fibers it is embedded within), while the Pacinian corpuscle sits deepest of the cutaneous mechanoreceptors, in the hypodermis, detecting deep pressure/vibration. Two paired cardiovascular receptor locations are conceptually contrasted: baroreceptors sense vessel-WALL stretch, while peripheral chemoreceptors are functionally paired with sampling the vessel LUMEN's blood composition; osmoreceptors regulating thirst and ADH release are located in the hypothalamus.

The muscle spindle and Golgi tendon organ receive further, more granular treatment in this cluster, building on the pair's basic length-vs-tension distinction already established. Muscle tissue as a whole carries pain receptors/nociceptors and Pacinian-type receptors alongside its classical proprioceptors. The intrafusal fiber's central equatorial region is specialised by being filled with a gelatinous substance (most pronounced in the nuclear bag fiber) rather than ordinary contractile sarcomeres; the muscle spindle is the ONLY receptor with both sensory AND motor innervation, its motor supply specifically via MYELINATED gamma efferent fibers (not alpha efferents, which supply extrafusal fibers instead), while the Golgi tendon organ remains purely sensory. Functionally, the muscle spindle monitors the degree of muscle STRETCH while the Golgi tendon organ monitors the FORCE of muscle contraction, and it is the muscle spindle, not the tendon organ, that is responsible for the (myotatic) stretch reflex. Among the spindle's own sensory endings, the flower-spray (secondary, group II) ending characteristically encircles the more peripheral part of the nuclear region, chiefly on nuclear chain fibers, complementing the annulospiral (primary, Ia) ending's central/equatorial position already established.

Continuing past Q50, the Week 1 EOM Final's second half (Q51-100) turns from single-receptor physiology to the ascending pathways that carry their signals centrally, plus two further general receptor-coding facts. Fine, discriminative touch (together with vibration and conscious proprioception) ascends via the dorsal column-medial lemniscus pathway — uncrossed to the gracile/cuneate nuclei, then crossing as internal arcuate fibers to the medial lemniscus and thalamus — a higher-resolution route than the cruder anterolateral (spinothalamic) system already established for pain, temperature and crude touch. This source separately credits position sense to the muscle spindle together with both cutaneous (tactile) and joint capsule receptors, and restates the module's stimulus-intensity coding rule (action potential frequency rising directly, not inversely, with stimulus strength) alongside a complementary fact: among receptor, end-plate and postsynaptic potentials — all graded and summating — only the action potential itself remains genuinely all-or-none (non-graded). A lesion-localisation question on thermal sensation from the left foot is also drawn from this source, with a printed key ('right thalamic ablation spares it') that runs against the standard contralateral spinothalamic pathway (a right-thalamic lesion, receiving already-crossed left-body input, would be expected to abolish rather than spare left-foot thermal sensation); the printed key is kept per the module's sourcing rules, with the discrepancy flagged on the concept's own record.

Attributing a refractory period or voltage-gated-channel generation to the receptor potential (those are action-potential properties), or amplitude-based (rather than frequency-based) intensity coding to the action potential.
Assuming hair follicles or visceral connective tissue are as densely nociceptor-supplied as skin/mucosa, or confusing Ruffini's (sustained pressure) with Meissner's (light flutter) function.
Reversing the baroreceptor (wall)/chemoreceptor (lumen) functional pairing, or placing osmoreceptors outside the hypothalamus.
Describing the intrafusal fiber's equatorial region as ordinary contractile muscle, or assuming any receptor besides the muscle spindle has both sensory and motor innervation.
Confusing which proprioceptor (spindle=stretch, tendon organ=force) does which job, or which receptor drives the stretch reflex.
Placing the flower-spray ending centrally (that is the annulospiral ending's position) or the annulospiral ending peripherally.
