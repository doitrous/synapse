## sections
### Definition
Group I hormones (steroids, thyroid hormone and other lipid-soluble hormones) diffuse into the target cell and bind intracellular or nuclear receptors that act directly as transcription factors, increasing transcription of specific target genes -- distinct from Group II (peptide/catecholamine) hormones, which bind membrane receptors and signal through second messengers such as cAMP. The insulin receptor is itself a receptor tyrosine kinase: insulin binding activates its intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity, the proximal signalling event upstream of the PI3-kinase and MAP-kinase branches. The heterotrimeric Gs protein cycles between a resting, GDP-bound state and an active, GTP-bound state that stimulates adenylate cyclase; glucagon-receptor binding catalyses the GDP-to-GTP exchange that activates the pathway. Thyroid hormones (T3 and T4) are lipid-soluble and, once bound to their intracellular receptor, the hormone-receptor complex itself acts on DNA as the effective intracellular signal, without a separate membrane second-messenger cascade. Obstructive jaundice raises conjugated (water-soluble) bilirubin, which is filtered by the kidney and appears in urine as bile pigments. The renal cortex is a major site of gluconeogenesis, especially in prolonged fasting or acidosis, and glutamine is its main substrate, also fuelling renal ammoniagenesis.

### Mechanism
Because Group I hormone receptors are themselves DNA-binding transcription factors, their signal does not require amplification through a second-messenger cascade -- the hormone-receptor complex is the signal, which is also why thyroid hormone (T3/T4) is credited, over TSH or glucagon, as using a "hormone-receptor complex" as its effective second messenger. Insulin's own receptor tyrosine kinase activity, triggered by autophosphorylation on insulin binding, is the proximal step that this source's key credits over its own downstream branches (PI3-kinase, MAP kinase) or unrelated gluconeogenic enzymes (PEPCK). Glucagon-receptor binding drives Gs-alpha's GDP-to-GTP exchange; this source's own key credits the resting, GDP-bound state as "true regarding glucagon action," ahead of the GTP-bound active state that more directly explains adenylate cyclase stimulation -- kept as printed, flagged as a divergence from the more commonly emphasised active-state teaching. Conjugated bilirubin's water solubility is what allows it, unlike the unconjugated form bound to albumin, to be filtered at the glomerulus and colour the urine dark in obstructive/post-hepatic jaundice. Renal cortical gluconeogenesis from glutamine is distinct from the liver's own dominant substrate (alanine, via the glucose-alanine cycle from muscle).

### Key determinants
Distinguishing Group I hormones' direct-transcription mechanism from Group II hormones' second-messenger/enzyme-phosphorylation mechanism is the core discriminator tested by the "group I hormone action" item. The insulin receptor's intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity is distinguished from its own downstream signalling branches (MAP kinase, PI3-kinase) and from unrelated gluconeogenic enzymes (PEPCK) that insulin regulates but does not signal through. Gs-alpha's GDP-bound (resting) and GTP-bound (active) states are distinguished from each other and from cAMP itself, which Gs does not bind. Bile pigments (conjugated bilirubin) are distinguished from sugar, ketone bodies and protein as the urinary constituent specific to obstructive jaundice. Glutamine is distinguished from alanine (the liver's dominant gluconeogenic substrate), glutamate and glycerol as the renal cortex's own main substrate.

### Clinical significance
Recognising Group I hormones' direct nuclear-receptor mechanism explains why steroid and thyroid hormone effects are slower in onset but longer-lasting than peptide-hormone, second-messenger effects. Understanding the insulin receptor's own tyrosine kinase activity underlies the pathophysiology of insulin resistance, where post-receptor signalling defects (not necessarily the tyrosine kinase step itself) blunt the response to a given insulin level. Knowing the Gs-GDP/GTP cycle is the basis for understanding both physiological glucagon signalling and pathological Gs mutations (e.g. McCune-Albright syndrome, cholera toxin's action). Recognising bile pigments (conjugated bilirubin) in urine as a marker of obstructive/post-hepatic jaundice is a bedside clue distinguishing it from unconjugated (pre-hepatic/hepatic) hyperbilirubinemia, where urine typically stays clear. And understanding the renal cortex's use of glutamine for gluconeogenesis and ammoniagenesis explains how the kidney contributes to acid-base regulation during metabolic acidosis.

## hold_these
Group I (lipid-soluble: steroid, thyroid) hormones act via intracellular/nuclear receptors that directly increase transcription of specific target genes -- NOT via DNA replication, enzyme dephosphorylation, or a vague "cell growth" effect.
The insulin receptor is itself a receptor TYROSINE KINASE; MAP kinase and PI3-kinase act downstream of this proximal step, and PEPCK is a regulated enzyme, not a signalling messenger.
This source's key: Gs is GDP-bound in its RESTING state, and this is what the item credits as "true regarding glucagon action" -- the GTP-bound state is the one that then stimulates adenylate cyclase.
T3 & T4 (not TSH or glucagon) use their own hormone-receptor complex as the effective intracellular signal, since they are lipid-soluble and act via intracellular receptors rather than a membrane second-messenger cascade.
Obstructive jaundice produces BILE PIGMENTS (conjugated bilirubin) in urine, not sugar, ketone bodies or protein.
GLUTAMINE, not alanine, glutamate or glycerol, is the renal cortex's main gluconeogenic substrate (alanine is the liver's own dominant one).

## lose_the_mark
Attributing Group I hormone action to DNA replication, enzyme dephosphorylation or generic "cell growth" instead of increased transcription of specific genes.
Naming MAP kinase, PI3-kinase or PEPCK instead of the insulin receptor's own intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity as the mechanism credited by this source's key.
Selecting GTP-bound Gs or cAMP instead of this source's own GDP-bound-Gs key for the glucagon-action item.
Choosing thyroxine alone, TSH or glucagon instead of "T3 & T4" for the hormone-receptor-complex-as-signal item.
Naming sugar, ketone bodies or protein instead of bile pigments as the urinary constituent in obstructive jaundice.
Selecting alanine, glutamate or glycerol instead of glutamine as the renal cortex's main gluconeogenic substrate.
