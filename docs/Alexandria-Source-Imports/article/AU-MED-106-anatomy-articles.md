## summary
The larynx is a cartilaginous voice box whose intrinsic muscles all adduct or tense the vocal folds except the posterior cricoarytenoid, the sole abductor — the muscle whose bilateral paralysis is a surgical emergency. Its cavity is layered into a vestibule, a middle compartment with the laryngeal saccule, and an infraglottic space, bounded at its inlet by the epiglottis and aryepiglottic folds. The nasal cavity is divided by a three-part septum (ethmoid, vomer, cartilage) and walled laterally by three conchae overhanging three meatuses, into which the paranasal sinuses drain by fixed anatomical routes rather than by gravity — the maxillary sinus, despite sitting low in the face, drains high into the middle meatus.

## sections
### Definition
The laryngeal skeleton is built from the thyroid cartilage, the cricoid cartilage (the airway's only complete cartilaginous ring), the paired arytenoid cartilages carrying the vocal folds' attachments, and the epiglottis. Five pairs of intrinsic muscles (plus the unpaired transverse arytenoid) move the vocal folds: the posterior cricoarytenoid abducts (the only abductor); the lateral cricoarytenoid and the arytenoid muscles adduct; the cricothyroid tenses and lengthens the folds (raising pitch) and is the one intrinsic muscle supplied by the external laryngeal nerve rather than the recurrent laryngeal nerve; the thyroarytenoid relaxes them. The laryngeal inlet is bounded by the epiglottis in front, the aryepiglottic folds on each side, and the interarytenoid fold behind. Inside, the vestibular (false) and vocal (true) folds divide the cavity into a vestibule above, a middle compartment (with the laryngeal ventricle and its saccule) between the two pairs of folds, and an infraglottic cavity below, continuous with the trachea. The nasal septum is built from the perpendicular plate of the ethmoid above and behind, the vomer below and behind, and the septal cartilage in front. The lateral wall carries the superior, middle and inferior conchae, each overhanging its own meatus (plus the sphenoethmoidal recess above the superior concha).

### Mechanism
Airway protection and phonation both depend on the same muscle set working in opposite directions: the posterior cricoarytenoid must actively abduct to keep the airway open at rest and during quiet breathing, while every other intrinsic muscle narrows the rima glottidis for swallowing or phonation — which is why a lesion that spares only the abductor (a partial recurrent laryngeal nerve injury with selective posterior cricoarytenoid weakness) causes stridor from unopposed adduction, while a lesion of the whole recurrent laryngeal nerve on one side is often better tolerated because the paralysed fold rests in a paramedian position. Paranasal sinus drainage follows fixed anatomical channels set up embryologically, independent of the sinus's own position in the skull: the frontal sinus (high in the forehead) and the maxillary sinus (low in the cheek) both drain into the same middle meatus via the hiatus semilunaris, while the much smaller, deeper sphenoidal sinus bypasses the meatal system entirely and opens directly into the sphenoethmoidal recess.

### Key determinants
Which laryngeal muscle is affected by a nerve lesion is set by which nerve is involved: recurrent laryngeal nerve injury threatens every intrinsic muscle except cricothyroid; external (superior) laryngeal nerve injury affects cricothyroid alone, weakening pitch control without threatening the airway. Which meatus a sinus drains into is fixed by embryological origin, not by the sinus's adult position — testable directly from "this sinus opens where" spot questions.

### Clinical significance
Bilateral recurrent laryngeal nerve injury (e.g. in thyroid surgery) removes the only abductor bilaterally, producing life-threatening airway obstruction from unopposed adduction — the single most clinically urgent fact in this anatomy. Because the maxillary sinus's ostium sits high on its medial wall, an infected maxillary sinus drains poorly while upright, which is why maxillary sinusitis characteristically worsens on bending forward. A fracture through the cribriform plate (the roof of the nasal cavity, shared with the anterior cranial fossa floor) can cause cerebrospinal fluid rhinorrhoea and anosmia from olfactory nerve filament damage.

## summary
A typical rib makes two synovial joints with the vertebral column — its head with two vertebral demifacets and the disc between them, its tubercle with the transverse process of the same-numbered vertebra — and carries a costal groove along its lower border holding the intercostal vein, artery and nerve in that order (VAN). The first rib is atypical: grooved on its upper surface by the subclavian vessels either side of the scalene tubercle, with the stellate ganglion and the brachial plexus's lower trunk crossing its neck. A typical thoracic vertebra's transverse process, not its pedicle or lamina, carries the costal facet for the rib tubercle.

## sections
### Definition
A typical rib (ribs 2-9) makes a costovertebral joint at its head (two demifacets plus the intervertebral disc between two vertebral levels) and a costotransverse joint at its tubercle (with the transverse process of the numerically corresponding vertebra), stabilised by the costotransverse ligaments. Its lower border carries the costal groove, sheltering the intercostal neurovascular bundle in Vein-Artery-Nerve order from above down. The first rib is short, flat and atypical: its head has a single facet (articulating only with T1), and its upper surface is divided by the scalene tubercle (scalenus anterior's insertion) into a subclavian vein groove in front and a combined subclavian artery/brachial-plexus-lower-trunk groove behind; the stellate ganglion lies at the rib's neck. A typical thoracic vertebra has a heart-shaped body with costal demifacets, pedicles and laminae forming the vertebral arch, a long downward-sloping spinous process, and a transverse process carrying the costal facet for the rib tubercle — the sole vertebral contribution to the costotransverse joint.

### Mechanism
The costovertebral joint's two-demifacet arrangement means a typical rib's head spans two vertebral levels (its own numbered vertebra and the one above), while the costotransverse joint at the tubercle involves only the single, numerically matching vertebra — so "which vertebra does this rib articulate with" has two different correct answers depending on which joint is meant. At the first rib, the scalene tubercle physically separates the subclavian vein (anterior, alone) from the artery and the brachial plexus's lower trunk (posterior, together) — the anatomical basis for scalenus anterior syndrome selectively compressing the artery and nerve while sparing the vein.

### Key determinants
Whether a labelling question about a rib-vertebra joint is testing the costovertebral (head, two demifacets, disc) or costotransverse (tubercle, transverse process) relationship determines which vertebral level(s) are correct. Whether a first-rib structure lies anterior or posterior to the scalene tubercle determines whether it is the vein (anterior, alone) or the artery-plus-nerve (posterior, together).

### Clinical significance
Cervical rib or scalenus anterior hypertrophy can compress the subclavian artery and the brachial plexus's lower trunk in the groove behind the scalene tubercle, producing thoracic outlet syndrome with arm ischaemia and lower-trunk sensory/motor signs, while sparing the vein anterior to the tubercle — a pattern that localises the compression to the first-rib/scalene-tubercle region rather than elsewhere along the neurovascular bundle. The costal groove's VAN order and its position along the rib's lower border is the anatomical basis for aiming a chest-drain or thoracocentesis needle just above the lower rib's upper border, in the "safe triangle."

## summary
The subclavian artery's first part gives the vertebral artery, internal thoracic artery and thyrocervical trunk before continuing as the axillary artery at the first rib; the common carotid artery bifurcates at the thyroid cartilage into the internal carotid (no neck branches) and the external carotid (eight branches, including the vessels this bank tests). Each brachiocephalic vein forms behind its own sternoclavicular joint from an internal jugular plus a subclavian vein — a different union from the inferior vena cava, which forms at L5 from the two common iliac veins, and from the superior vena cava, which forms from the two brachiocephalic veins uniting behind the first right costal cartilage. The heart's apex beat is normally felt in the left fifth intercostal space at the midclavicular line, and the four valve areas are auscultated along the direction of blood flow rather than at each valve's true anatomical position.

## sections
### Definition
The subclavian artery's first part (medial to scalenus anterior) gives the vertebral artery, the internal thoracic artery and the thyrocervical trunk (inferior thyroid, transverse cervical, suprascapular arteries); its third part continues at the outer border of the first rib as the axillary artery. The common carotid artery ascends in the carotid sheath with the internal jugular vein and vagus nerve, giving no neck branch, and bifurcates at the upper border of the thyroid cartilage into the internal carotid (no neck branches) and the external carotid, whose branches include the superior thyroid, lingual, facial and maxillary arteries. Each brachiocephalic vein forms behind its own sternoclavicular joint by the union of that side's internal jugular and subclavian veins; the left, longer of the two, crosses the midline behind the manubrium to join the right, forming the superior vena cava behind the first right costal cartilage. The inferior vena cava forms separately, at L5, from the union of the two common iliac veins, ascending to pierce the diaphragm's caval opening at T8. The heart's surface outline runs from the third right costal cartilage to the second left, down to the sixth right costal cartilage and the apex in the left fifth intercostal space at the midclavicular line — the site of the apex beat and the mitral auscultation area — with the aortic, pulmonary and tricuspid areas at the right second, left second, and lower-left-sternal-border positions respectively.

### Mechanism
Two arterial trees, not one, supply the neck: the subclavian artery's branches (vertebral, internal thoracic, thyrocervical) serve deep/posterior structures and the upper limb's continuation, while the carotid system (common → internal/external) serves the head and face — a question naming "which artery" a structure comes from is really asking which of these two systems it belongs to. Three separate venous unions are tested in this same region and are easy to conflate because each involves "two vessels joining": the brachiocephalic vein (internal jugular + subclavian, in the neck), the superior vena cava (the two brachiocephalic veins, behind the manubrium) and the inferior vena cava (the two common iliac veins, at L5, in the abdomen) — naming the wrong pair for the wrong vein is the single most common error this article corrects.

### Key determinants
Whether a branch belongs to the subclavian or the carotid system determines which trunk or bifurcation it is grouped under. Which two-vessel union is being asked about (brachiocephalic, SVC or IVC) is set by the vein's own location, not by "two veins uniting" being a generic answer that fits all three.

### Clinical significance
The apex beat's displacement (beyond the midclavicular line, or below the fifth space) is a bedside sign of cardiomegaly or mediastinal shift. Auscultating each valve area rather than its true anatomical position is standard clinical practice because each area is where that valve's sound radiates loudest along the direction of blood flow, not where the valve itself lies. A caval mix-up (stating that the IVC forms from a jugular-plus-subclavian union) is a genuine, recorded printed-key error in one addendum source — the kind of error that costs marks precisely because the two unions sound similar but occur in entirely different body regions.
