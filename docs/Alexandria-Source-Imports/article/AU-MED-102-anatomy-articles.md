## summary
A typical vertebra is a body and a neural arch enclosing the vertebral foramen, with seven named processes for muscle and joint attachment. The thirty-three vertebrae fall into five regions — cervical, thoracic, lumbar, sacral and coccygeal — and the exam-tested way to tell a cervical from a thoracic from a lumbar vertebra is by its foramen shape, its costal facets and its body size, not by counting.

## sections
### Overview and position
The vertebral column is the central axial support of the trunk, running from the base of the skull to the coccyx and enclosing the spinal cord throughout its length. It is built of thirty-three vertebrae in five regions: seven cervical, twelve thoracic, five lumbar, five sacral (fused into the sacrum) and three or four coccygeal (fused into the coccyx).

### Structure
Every typical vertebra has the same two-part plan. The body is the anterior, weight-bearing block, increasing in size from cervical to lumbar as the load it carries increases. Behind it, a pedicle and a lamina on each side form the neural arch, and body plus arch together enclose the vertebral foramen — the opening that, stacked vertebra on vertebra, becomes the vertebral canal holding the spinal cord. The arch carries a single spinous process behind, two transverse processes at the sides, and four articular processes (superior and inferior, one pair each side) that meet the vertebra above and below.

### Relations
Adjacent vertebral bodies are separated by an intervertebral disc, and the superior and inferior vertebral notches of adjacent pedicles together bound the intervertebral foramen, through which one spinal nerve leaves the canal on each side at each level — a different opening from the vertebral foramen itself, and the one point students most often confuse with it.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
The vertebral column is supplied segmentally, and its joints follow Hilton's law like any other joint: the nerve that crosses a vertebral joint also supplies the muscles acting on it.

### Development
Each somite's sclerotome contributes the vertebrae and intervertebral discs of its level, migrating to surround the neural tube and notochord — the notochord itself does not become the vertebral column, and its only postnatal remnant is the nucleus pulposus of the intervertebral disc.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
The five regions are distinguished on a plain film or in a cadaver dissection by the same three features taught here: foramen shape and size, the presence or absence of costal facets, and body size. A cervical vertebra shows a wide, roughly triangular foramen and a foramen transversarium punched through each transverse process for the vertebral vessels; a thoracic vertebra shows a small circular foramen and facets for rib articulation; a lumbar vertebra shows the largest, kidney-shaped body and no costal facets at all.

### Clinical correlations
Because the vertebral foramen and the vertebral canal it forms hold the spinal cord, while the intervertebral foramen at each level transmits one spinal nerve root, a lesion at the two sites produces different clinical pictures — a cord-level problem in the canal versus a single root lesion at one intervertebral foramen — and telling them apart starts from knowing which opening is which.

## summary
Male bones are longer, heavier and stronger than female bones because male sex hormones drive greater bone deposition. Osteoporosis — bone losing calcium and other minerals and becoming lighter and more fragile — is commoner in women, is slowed by moderate weight-bearing exercise and adequate dietary calcium, and its classic complication is fracture of the neck of the femur.

## sections
### Definition
Bone strength differs by sex from the growth period onward: male bones are, on average, longer, heavier and denser than female bones. Osteoporosis is a disease of reduced bone mass and mineral content that leaves bone lighter and more fragile than normal for the patient's age.

### Mechanism
Male sex hormones stimulate greater bone deposition throughout skeletal growth, which is why the sex difference in bone strength is present well before old age. Osteoporosis develops when bone resorption outpaces bone formation over time, most markedly after the menopause removes oestrogen's protective effect on bone turnover in women.

### Key determinants
Sex, age and the menopause are the non-modifiable determinants; moderate weight-bearing exercise and an adequate dietary calcium intake are the two modifiable factors that slow the disease's progress, while immobility accelerates bone loss instead.

### Clinical significance
The complication osteoporosis is best known for is fracture of the neck of the femur, a fracture that in an elderly patient carries substantial morbidity and mortality — which is why the two protective factors (exercise, calcium) are worth remembering as a pair, not as isolated facts.

## summary
The spleen sits against the left ninth to eleventh ribs, its visceral surface carrying four named impressions — gastric, colic, renal and pancreatic — around a hilum reached through the lienorenal ligament, which also carries the splenic vessels. The gastrosplenic ligament ties it to the stomach. Knowing the rib relation is what explains why a fractured left lower rib threatens the spleen.

## sections
### Overview and position
The spleen is the largest lymphoid organ and an intraperitoneal viscus of the left upper quadrant, lying obliquely along the long axis of the left ninth, tenth and eleventh ribs, behind the mid-axillary line.

### Structure
It has a medial end directed upwards and backwards and a broad lateral end directed downwards and forwards. Its upper border is sharp and notched; its lower border is broad; a middle border runs from the medial end to the hilum. Two surfaces meet at these borders: a convex diaphragmatic surface facing the diaphragm, and a concave visceral surface carrying the hilum and, around it, four named impressions — the gastric impression above the hilum, the colic impression near the lateral end, the renal impression below the hilum, and the pancreatic impression below the lateral end.

### Relations
The convex diaphragmatic surface is separated by the diaphragm from the left costodiaphragmatic pleural recess, the base of the left lung, and the posterior parts of the ninth, tenth and eleventh ribs — the relation that puts the spleen at risk when one of those ribs fractures. On the visceral surface, the gastric impression faces the fundus of the stomach, the colic impression the splenic flexure of the colon, the renal impression the left kidney, and the pancreatic impression the tail of the pancreas.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
The splenic artery and splenic vein both reach the hilum by running within the lienorenal ligament, the peritoneal fold that also attaches the spleen to the anterior surface of the left kidney. A second peritoneal fold, the gastrosplenic ligament, attaches the spleen to the greater curvature of the stomach and carries the short gastric and left gastro-omental vessels.

### Development

### Surface and imaging anatomy
On a plain film or in a trauma survey, the spleen's projection onto the left ninth to eleventh ribs is the reason a fracture there raises the possibility of splenic injury, and its position tucked under the left costal margin is also why it is not normally palpable unless enlarged.

### Clinical correlations
Splenic rupture from a fractured left ninth, tenth or eleventh rib is the clinical consequence of the rib relation described above, and can present with left upper quadrant pain and haemoperitoneum. The two peritoneal attachments (gastrosplenic and lienorenal ligaments) are also the surgical handles a splenectomy divides in turn, and each carries a named vessel that must be controlled as it is cut.

## summary
The upper limb's skeleton is four regions in series: a shoulder girdle of two bones (clavicle, scapula), an arm of one bone (humerus), a forearm of two bones (radius, ulna), and a hand of three rows (8 carpals, 5 metacarpals, 14 phalanges). Naming the region tells you which bones belong to it — and tells you, just as usefully, which bones (the femur, tibia and fibula of the lower limb) do not.

## sections
### Overview and position
The appendicular skeleton is built of the limb bones and their girdles, as opposed to the axial skeleton (skull, vertebral column, ribs, sternum). The upper limb's own share of the appendicular skeleton runs proximal to distal in four named regions.

### Structure
The shoulder girdle attaches the upper limb to the trunk and is two bones: the clavicle in front and the scapula behind. The arm, between shoulder and elbow, is a single long bone, the humerus. The forearm, between elbow and wrist, is two long bones side by side: the radius laterally (thumb side) and the ulna medially. The hand itself is three rows of bone: proximally eight carpal bones arranged in two rows of four, in the middle five metacarpal bones (one per digit), and distally fourteen phalanges — two for the thumb (proximal and distal) and three for each of the other four fingers (proximal, middle and distal).

### Relations
Region by region, the upper limb's own bones never repeat a name with the lower limb's bones — the humerus (arm) has no lower-limb counterpart sharing its name, and the femur (thigh), tibia and fibula (leg) belong only to the lower limb. Recognising a bone by which limb it belongs to, not only by its general shape as a long bone, is what an exception item ("which of the following is NOT a bone of the upper extremities") is built to test.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
Each region carries its own named vessels and nerves (for example the brachial artery in the arm, the radial and ulnar arteries in the forearm) rather than one shared supply for the whole limb — a detail this general bone-list article only flags, since the vascular and nerve anatomy of each region is its own separate topic.

### Development
Each upper-limb bone ossifies from its own primary and secondary centres, appearing and fusing on a predictable timetable used clinically to estimate skeletal age from a hand-and-wrist radiograph — the same ossification process (primary centre in utero, secondary centres before or after birth) that applies to long bones generally.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
On a plain radiograph of the upper limb, the four regions are read off in the same proximal-to-distal order they are named: girdle, arm, forearm, then the three rows of the hand — a useful checklist for not missing a bone when reading a trauma film.

### Clinical correlations
Recognising which bone belongs to which region is the first step in describing any upper-limb fracture correctly (a "distal radius fracture" versus a "proximal humerus fracture" locate the injury by region before any further detail is added), which is exactly the discipline this exception-style exam item is built to instil early.

## summary
Bone is more than a rigid scaffold: it supports the body, acts as a lever the muscles crossing a joint pull against to produce movement, stores minerals (chiefly calcium and phosphorus), and forms blood cells in its marrow. It is richly vascularised but, despite that, poorly supplied by lymphatics — the property this department's own bank most often tests as a false-statement distractor.

## sections
### Overview and position
Before any regional anatomy, the department bank tests bone at the level of the tissue itself: what it is for, in general, wherever in the body it is found.

### Structure
Bone's mineral matrix (chiefly calcium and phosphorus salts deposited on a collagen framework) is what gives it both its rigidity and its role as a mineral reservoir the body can draw on and redeposit as physiological demand changes.

### Relations
Bone's role as a lever depends entirely on its relation to the muscles crossing the joint it forms part of: a muscle's tendon attaches to bone on either side of a joint, and the bone's own rigidity is what lets the muscle's pull translate into movement at that joint rather than simply deforming the attachment site.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
Bone carries an extensive blood supply — nutrient arteries, periosteal vessels, and (in a long bone) separate epiphyseal and metaphyseal supplies — but is poorly supplied by lymphatics. This asymmetry, a rich blood supply paired with a poor lymphatic one, is precisely what the department bank's own "which statement is false" item isolates.

### Development
Bone itself is a living, actively remodelled tissue throughout life, not an inert scaffold laid down once — a fact underlying its ability to keep serving as a mineral reservoir and a site of blood-cell formation from fetal life into old age.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
None of bone's four general functions is itself a surface landmark; this record's own imaging relevance is regional (a fracture line, a lytic lesion) rather than a feature of the tissue-level definition covered here.

### Clinical correlations
Bone's mineral-storage role is why disorders of calcium and phosphorus homeostasis (for example in renal or parathyroid disease) show up radiologically and biochemically in bone before they show up anywhere else — the store is mobilised first.

## summary
Inversion and eversion are the named pair of twisting movements of the foot, produced mainly at the subtalar and transverse tarsal joints. Inversion turns the sole to face medially; eversion turns it to face laterally — the direction the sole itself faces, not which way the leg leans, is what names each movement.

## sections
### Overview and position
Alongside flexion/extension, abduction/adduction and rotation, inversion and eversion are a movement pair specific to the foot, describing a twist about the foot's own long axis rather than a movement at the ankle joint proper.

### Structure
Inversion and eversion occur mainly at the subtalar (talocalcaneal) and transverse tarsal (talocalcaneonavicular and calcaneocuboid) joints, not at the talocrural (ankle) joint, which is a hinge permitting only plantarflexion and dorsiflexion.

### Relations
Inversion turns the sole to face medially, toward the midline; eversion turns it to face laterally, away from the midline. Because the definition is stated in terms of the sole's own direction, it stays correct regardless of which leg is being examined or how the limb as a whole is positioned.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
The muscles producing inversion (chiefly tibialis anterior and tibialis posterior) and eversion (chiefly the peroneus/fibularis group) are supplied by the deep and superficial peroneal nerves respectively — a distinct regional fact from the movement definition itself, which this article states only to flag rather than to teach in full here.

### Development
No distinct developmental process attaches to inversion and eversion themselves; the joints that permit them (the subtalar and transverse tarsal joints) develop as synovial joints of the tarsus, part of the general limb-bud skeletal development the foot shares with the rest of the lower limb.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
Inversion and eversion are examined clinically by passively or actively moving the foot and observing which way the sole comes to face, rather than by any fixed surface landmark — the movement itself is the sign.

### Clinical correlations
A sprained ankle most often follows a forced inversion injury, which stretches or tears the lateral ligament complex — recognising inversion as the mechanism is part of why that injury pattern damages the lateral, not the medial, side of the ankle.

## summary
Cartilage comes in three types — hyaline, elastic and fibrocartilage — sorted by what the tissue needs to do. The auricle (pinna) of the external ear is the department book's own worked example of elastic cartilage: a matrix dense with elastic fibres that lets the ear bend under pressure and spring straight back, rather than crack or stay deformed.

## sections
### Overview and position
Cartilage type follows function: hyaline cartilage (smooth, glassy) covers joint surfaces and forms costal cartilage; fibrocartilage (tough, fibrous) forms the intervertebral disc and pubic symphysis; elastic cartilage (flexible, resilient) is found wherever a structure must bend repeatedly and recoil, such as the auricle and the epiglottis.

### Structure
Elastic cartilage's chondrocytes sit in a matrix built around a dense meshwork of elastic fibres, in addition to the collagen every cartilage type carries. That elastic-fibre meshwork is what gives the tissue its defining property — recoil after bending — that neither hyaline cartilage's smooth rigidity nor fibrocartilage's tensile toughness provides.

### Relations
The auricle's elastic-cartilage framework is covered directly by skin with almost no intervening subcutaneous fat, which is why the cartilage's own shape — helix, antihelix, tragus, lobule — is visible and palpable through the skin surface.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
Like cartilage generally, the auricle's elastic cartilage itself is avascular and is nourished by diffusion from the overlying perichondrium, rather than by vessels running through the cartilage matrix — a property shared with hyaline and fibrocartilage despite their different mechanical roles.

### Development
The auricle develops from six mesenchymal proliferations (the auricular hillocks) around the first pharyngeal groove in the embryo, which fuse and remodel to form its adult elastic-cartilage framework — detail that belongs to the Embryology department's own scope rather than this gross-anatomy record.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
The auricle's cartilage is directly palpable end to end, which is why its landmarks (helix, tragus) are used as external reference points in ear examination and in describing nearby surgical incisions.

### Clinical correlations
Because elastic cartilage keeps its shape after being bent rather than fracturing, the auricle can be folded flat against the head and spring back — the same property that makes cartilage-only injuries of the ear (as opposed to full-thickness lacerations) more forgiving to manage than bone injuries would be.
