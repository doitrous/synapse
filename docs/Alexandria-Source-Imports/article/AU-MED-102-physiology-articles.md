## summary
The Foundation physiology course opens with the ionic basis of the membrane potential, the definition of osmosis, the electrolyte differences between the extracellular and intracellular compartments, the relative sizes of the body-fluid compartments, and homeostasis itself — defined through its negative-feedback loop and contrasted with the rarer positive-feedback pattern. Every later fact about a specific organ's regulation assumes this vocabulary is already secure.

## sections
### Definition
Homeostasis is the dynamic regulation of the body's internal environment within narrow, physiologically acceptable limits, not a state of perfectly fixed, unaltered conditions. It is achieved through continuous corrective responses coordinated by feedback loops, and it depends on the same basic facts about membrane transport and fluid compartments that this article sets out.

### Mechanism
Every cell's resting membrane potential rests on an asymmetric ion distribution: sodium and chloride are concentrated outside the cell, potassium and the cell's own proteins inside, a gradient the Na+/K+ pump maintains. The same asymmetry, restated by named ion: calcium, sodium and chloride run higher in extracellular fluid than intracellular fluid, while potassium, phosphate and protein are higher inside.

Osmosis is the net movement of solvent (water) across a semipermeable membrane from a region of lower solute concentration toward a region of higher solute concentration — the solvent moves, not the solute, and it moves toward the more concentrated side to dilute it.

Of the named body-fluid compartments — intracellular, interstitial, intravascular and transcellular — the intracellular compartment is by far the largest and transcellular fluid (cerebrospinal, synovial, intraocular and similar epithelium-bounded fluids) is the smallest.

A negative-feedback loop runs through a detector that senses a deviation from the set point, a control centre that compares the deviation to the set point and computes a response, and an effector that carries out the corrective response. The suckling reflex during lactation is the standard physiological example of the rarer, opposite pattern — positive feedback — where the response (further prolactin and oxytocin release, driving more milk production) amplifies rather than opposes the original stimulus.

### Key determinants
What decides each of these facts is a direction or a ranking that is easy to reverse under exam pressure: which ions sit outside versus inside; which way the solvent moves in osmosis relative to the solute gradient; which fluid compartment is smallest, not merely which sounds smallest by name; and whether a given physiological loop amplifies (positive) or opposes (negative) its own trigger.

### Clinical significance
Every subsequent physiological system taught in this curriculum — cardiovascular, renal, endocrine — is described in terms of set points, feedback loops and fluid compartments defined here. A student who has not fixed these definitions will misread every later homeostatic mechanism built on top of them.

## summary
The autonomic nervous system controls involuntary function through two thoracolumbar/craniosacral outflows, ganglionic relays and a defined receptor pharmacology. This article sets out where the sympathetic outflow originates in the cord, what the anterior hypothalamus's stimulation produces, what a sacral cord lesion or a cervical sympathetic-chain lesion does to a patient, and where the nicotinic receptor sits and how it works — the vocabulary the module's autonomic vignettes are built from.

## sections
### Definition
The autonomic nervous system controls involuntary, visceral function — smooth muscle, cardiac muscle and glands — in contrast with the somatic nervous system's voluntary, skeletal-muscle remit. Its sympathetic division arises from the thoracolumbar spinal cord and its parasympathetic division from cranial nerves and the sacral cord (craniosacral outflow), each relaying through its own set of ganglia before reaching an effector.

### Mechanism
Preganglionic sympathetic neurons have their cell bodies in the lateral horn (intermediolateral cell column) of thoracolumbar spinal-cord segments, the anatomical basis for the 'thoracolumbar outflow' name.

The anterior hypothalamus is functionally linked to parasympathetic-type responses: its stimulation produces effects such as copious watery salivary secretion, whereas the posterior hypothalamus is more closely linked to sympathetic responses.

Erection is driven by the sacral parasympathetic outflow (S2-S4); destruction of the sacral spinal cord removes this outflow and so abolishes the capacity for reflex erection.

The cervical sympathetic chain supplies the upper eyelid's smooth muscle, the pupillary dilator and facial sweat glands on that side; its injury removes that supply and produces a Horner's-type picture — ptosis, pupillary constriction and reduced sweating — rather than mydriasis or eyeball protrusion.

Nicotinic acetylcholine receptors are ligand-gated ion channels — not G-protein-coupled receptors — found at the skeletal-muscle motor end-plate and in the autonomic ganglia, where they mediate fast synaptic transmission between pre- and postganglionic neurons.

### Key determinants
The thoracolumbar/craniosacral division is anchored in the location of the preganglionic cell body (lateral horn, thoracolumbar segments for sympathetic; cranial nuclei and sacral cord for parasympathetic) — get the level wrong and the division collapses. The anterior/posterior hypothalamic pairing with parasympathetic/sympathetic response is a fixed pairing worth holding together, not memorising separately. A cervical sympathetic-chain lesion produces the opposite signs to what an unwary guess predicts (constriction and drooping, not dilation and protrusion). Nicotinic receptors are defined by being ligand-gated ion channels, which is what makes their synaptic action fast.

### Clinical significance
A sacral cord injury's effect on erection, a cervical sympathetic lesion's Horner's-type picture, and myasthenia gravis's attack on the nicotinic receptor at the neuromuscular junction are all direct clinical readouts of this outflow-and-receptor map — which is why the module examines them as vignettes rather than as isolated facts.
