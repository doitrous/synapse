## summary
Pulmonary function testing separates disease into a restrictive pattern (low volumes, preserved or high FEV1/FVC ratio, normal RV/TLC%) and an obstructive pattern (low FEV1/FVC ratio, raised RV/TLC% from air trapping). Reading a spirogram or flow-volume loop means finding the FEV1/FVC ratio and the curve's own shape, not FVC alone. Two dead-space measurement methods answer different questions: Bohr's method (CO2 dilution) measures physiological dead space, and Fowler's method (single-breath N2 washout) measures anatomical dead space only. Breathing reserve and MVV describe ventilatory capacity in reserve during exercise, distinct from the resting spirometric volumes (VC, IC, ERV, TV) that make up the lung's basic bookkeeping.

## sections
### Purpose and indications
Spirometry and its derived tests answer a specific bedside question: is a patient's breathlessness or abnormal chest finding explained by a restrictive process (the lung or chest wall cannot expand normally), an obstructive process (airway narrowing slows expiratory flow), or neither. The same tests also measure how much of each breath is wasted on non-exchanging airway (dead space) and how much ventilatory capacity remains in reserve during exertion.

### Principle
A forced expiration after maximal inspiration is plotted as a volume-time curve (FVC on the y-axis, time on the x) or a flow-volume loop. FEV1 is the volume expired in the first second; the FEV1/FVC ratio, not FEV1 alone, separates the two basic patterns, because FEV1 falls in both. In restriction, TLC and VC fall together and the ratio is preserved (often even increased); RV/TLC% stays normal because RV falls in proportion with TLC. In obstruction, airway collapse traps air, so RV rises disproportionately to TLC — RV/TLC% rises — while the FEV1/FVC ratio itself falls below about 70%. Bohr's method exploits the fact that only alveolar gas exchanges CO2 with blood: comparing arterial (or end-tidal) CO2 against mixed-expired CO2 gives the physiological dead-space fraction, which includes any alveolar dead space from ventilated-but-underperfused alveoli. Fowler's method instead washes out nitrogen after a single breath of 100% O2 and reads anatomical dead space off the midpoint of the rapidly-rising transition in the N2-concentration-versus-volume curve — it cannot see alveolar dead space at all, only the conducting-airway volume. Breathing reserve (MVV minus exercise minute ventilation, as a percentage of MVV) shows how much ventilatory capacity is left unused at maximal exertion; MVV itself is measured by sustained maximal-rate, maximal-depth breathing for a short timed interval.

### How it is performed
Spirometry: the patient inhales maximally, then exhales as hard and fast as possible into the spirometer for the full FVC manoeuvre; volume and flow are recorded continuously. Bohr's method: mixed expired gas is collected over several breaths and its CO2 fraction compared against arterial or end-tidal CO2. Fowler's method: the patient inspires one full breath of 100% O2, then exhales steadily while a rapid N2 analyser plots expired N2 against expired volume. MVV/breathing reserve: the patient breathes maximally fast and deep for 12-15 seconds (extrapolated to L/min) at rest, and minute ventilation is separately measured at maximal exercise for comparison.

### Normal findings
FEV1/FVC is normally about 75-80% or higher. Anatomical dead space is normally about 150 mL in an adult (roughly 2.2 mL/kg). MVV in a young healthy adult male is roughly 150-180 L/min (bank value: about 160 L/min for a 20-year-old). Minute respiratory volume (tidal volume × respiratory rate) is normally about 6-8 L/min; the bank's own worked example (VT 2.8 L, RR 23/min) gives 64.4 L/min — an unusually large tidal volume/rate combination in the bank's numbers, recorded as printed rather than corrected, since the arithmetic itself (VT×RR) is what is being tested.

### Abnormal findings and interpretation
A restrictive pattern (e.g. silicosis, interstitial fibrosis, late pregnancy) shows low TLC/VC/FVC with a preserved-or-high FEV1/FVC ratio and normal-or-decreased RV/TLC%. An obstructive pattern (e.g. bronchial asthma, emphysema, COPD, bronchiectasis) shows a low FEV1/FVC ratio (bank example: 33%, clearly below 70%) and a raised RV/TLC% from air trapping. A "scooped" concave expiratory limb on a flow-volume loop is the obstructive signature; a small loop with a normal or increased relative peak flow is the restrictive signature. An exhausted breathing reserve at only moderate exertion signals that ventilatory capacity, not the cardiovascular system, is limiting exercise.

### Limitations and pitfalls
FVC or TLC alone cannot classify the pattern — restriction lowers both, so only the FEV1/FVC ratio (together with RV/TLC%) discriminates the two. Bohr's and Fowler's methods are not interchangeable: Bohr's (CO2) captures physiological dead space including alveolar dead space; Fowler's (N2 washout) captures anatomical dead space only, and reading the washout curve's plateau instead of the transition midpoint mislocates the boundary. MVV and breathing reserve are functional, effort-dependent tests distinct from the resting lung volumes (VC, IC, ERV, TV) — a low resting VC does not by itself predict a low breathing reserve or vice versa.

## summary
A 12-lead ECG is read by working through rhythm, then intervals, then wave morphology, then lead-specific patterns. Rate is calculated as 300 divided by the number of large squares between successive R waves (regular rhythm only). PR interval reflects AV conduction, QRS duration and amplitude reflect ventricular conduction speed and muscle mass respectively, and QT interval reflects total ventricular systole. ST-segment depression and T-wave inversion signal reversible ischaemia; ST elevation and a pathological Q wave signal an evolving infarct. Lead aVR is normally inverted; the six chest leads view the horizontal plane. Two teaching claims from this bank (VSD/absent Q wave) are recorded as printed, sourced local teaching rather than established general fact.

## sections
### Purpose and indications
A 12-lead ECG is requested to characterise rhythm and rate, screen for conduction disease, detect ischaemia or infarction, and assess chamber enlargement — the same tracing answers all of these depending on which feature is read.

### Principle
Standard paper calibration (25 mm/s, 10 mm/mV) makes one small square 0.04 sec/0.1 mV and one large square 0.2 sec/0.5 mV, so duration and amplitude are both read directly off the grid. Rate: for a regular rhythm, heart rate = 300 / (large squares between two successive R waves); an irregular rhythm needs a longer-strip count instead. PR interval reflects the time from atrial depolarisation through AV-nodal delay to the start of ventricular depolarisation; QRS duration reflects how long ventricular depolarisation takes (widened beyond 0.12 sec by a bundle branch block, because one ventricle must then be activated slowly, cell-to-cell, from the other side) while QRS amplitude reflects the mass of tissue depolarising (increased in ventricular hypertrophy). Q wave represents septal depolarisation and is normally small; a deep, wide Q wave instead signals infarcted, electrically silent tissue that the electrode "sees through". ST depression and T-wave inversion are the reversible signs of subendocardial ischaemia; ST elevation, evolving to a pathological Q wave, signals a fully occluded vessel progressing to transmural infarction. P-wave enlargement reflects atrial hypertrophy, the atrial parallel of the ventricular QRS-amplitude sign. Hypercalcaemia shortens the QT interval by shortening the calcium-dependent plateau of the ventricular action potential. Lead aVR views the heart roughly opposite the mean depolarisation vector and is normally inverted; the precordial (V1-V6) leads are placed at fixed anatomical points and together view the horizontal plane, complementing the limb leads' frontal-plane view.

### How it is performed
Electrodes are placed at the four limbs (for leads I, II, III, aVR, aVL, aVF) and six fixed chest positions (V1-V2 at the 4th intercostal space either side of the sternum, V4 at the 5th left intercostal space mid-clavicular line, V6 at the 5th left intercostal space mid-axillary line) per the standard colour-coded convention, and the tracing is recorded at standard speed and gain.

### Normal findings
Normal QRS duration is 0.06-0.10 sec (widened beyond 0.12 sec in complete bundle branch block); normal PR interval is 0.12-0.20 sec; a small septal Q wave is normal in several leads; lead aVR is normally fully inverted.

### Abnormal findings and interpretation
ST elevation with an evolving deep/wide Q wave = infarction; ST depression/T-wave inversion alone = ischaemia without infarction; QRS >0.12 sec = bundle branch block; increased QRS amplitude = ventricular hypertrophy; enlarged P wave = atrial hypertrophy; shortened QT = hypercalcaemia. This bank (and, independently, the Telegram addendum bank) marks "absent Q wave" as a sign of ventricular septal defect — recorded as a printed, cross-corroborated local teaching claim, not verified mainstream cardiology.

### Limitations and pitfalls
The 300/large-squares rate formula only works for a regular rhythm. Amplitude (hypertrophy) and duration (bundle branch block) are independent measurements that are easy to conflate. The VSD/absent-Q-wave claim is taught locally by two independent banks but is not how VSD's ECG correlates are conventionally described, and no mechanism is given in either source — treat it as a flagged local teaching point, not a settled fact.

## summary
Correct auscultatory BP technique needs the device at heart level, correct cuff position, and a preceding palpatory estimate to avoid missing the auscultatory gap. Korotkoff sounds pass through a recognised phase sequence during cuff deflation. Pulse examination assesses rate, rhythm, force/volume and arterial-wall condition; pulsus deficit (pulse rate below auscultated heart rate) is a classic sign of atrial fibrillation. Diastolic pressure matters more than systolic for organ perfusion because most of the cardiac cycle, and virtually all coronary flow, happens in diastole. The four heart-sound auscultation areas and S1/S3/S4's mechanisms and age-dependent significance complete the practical exam.

## sections
### Purpose and indications
Blood pressure and pulse examination, and cardiac auscultation, are the core bedside cardiovascular assessment — together they screen for hypertension, arrhythmia and valvular/heart-failure signs without any equipment beyond a sphygmomanometer and stethoscope.

### Principle
Auscultatory BP technique requires the device at heart level (a height error introduces a hydrostatic offset), the cuff bladder centred over the brachial artery about 2.5 cm proximal to the cubital fossa, and the stethoscope diaphragm under the cuff edge. A preceding palpatory estimate (inflate while palpating the radial pulse until it disappears, then reinflate 20-30 mmHg above that point) sets the correct starting pressure so the auscultatory gap — a real, patient-specific silent interval within Korotkoff phase II, more likely with a stiff aorta — is not mistaken for the true systolic reading. Korotkoff sounds themselves pass from first tapping (systolic) through softer/murmurish, louder/banging, abrupt muffling and silence (diastolic) as the cuff deflates. Pulse examination assesses rate, rhythm, force/volume (how large the wave feels, from rolling the artery under the examining finger) and the condition of the arterial wall — never "condition of the vein", which is not part of the arterial pulse at all. An irregular rhythm such as atrial fibrillation can produce a pulsus deficit: some beats generate too little stroke volume to produce a palpable peripheral pulse at all, so the palpated pulse rate undercounts the auscultated heart rate. Pulse pressure (systolic minus diastolic) rises physiologically with a meal, exercise and age, and falls during sleep. Diastolic pressure is described as more important than systolic for organ perfusion because diastole occupies roughly two-thirds of the cardiac cycle (and virtually all of coronary flow), so adequate diastolic pressure sustains perfusion through most of each heartbeat. Blood flow velocity is fastest in the aorta and slowest in the capillaries, inversely proportional to each level's total cross-sectional area. Heart sounds: S1 (mitral/tricuspid closure, 25-40 Hz) marks the start of systole; the four auscultation areas (mitral at the apex, tricuspid at the left lower sternal border, pulmonary and aortic at the 2nd intercostal spaces) are named for where each valve's sound transmits best, not its anatomical position; S3 can be a normal finding in children/young adults but usually signals heart failure in an older adult, while S4 is never normal at any age.

### How it is performed
Auscultatory BP: palpate the radial pulse while inflating to estimate systolic, deflate fully, reinflate 20-30 mmHg above the palpatory estimate, then deflate slowly (2-3 mmHg/s) while auscultating over the brachial artery for the Korotkoff phases. Pulse: palpate the radial (or another accessible) artery, noting rate, rhythm, force and wall character over several beats. Auscultation: place the stethoscope diaphragm/bell in turn at the mitral, tricuspid, pulmonic and aortic areas, listening for S1, S2 and any additional sounds.

### Normal findings
Normal adult BP is roughly 90-140/60-90 mmHg; normal paediatric systolic pressure is roughly 90-120 mmHg. S1 is 25-40 Hz; a physiological S3 is normal in children and young adults.

### Abnormal findings and interpretation
A pulsus deficit points to atrial fibrillation. An S4 at any age, or an S3 in an older adult, points to reduced ventricular compliance/heart failure. Hypertension is arterial pressure persistently elevated above the normal range for an individual; when a cause is found, endocrine disease and kidney disease are the categories this bank names.

### Limitations and pitfalls
Skipping the palpatory estimate risks missing the auscultatory gap and underestimating systolic pressure. Confusing pulsus deficit with true bradycardia, or treating any S3 as automatically pathological regardless of the patient's age, are the two most common interpretive errors this bank tests.
