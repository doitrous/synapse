## sections
### Definition
The perineal body (central tendon of the perineum) is the fibromuscular node at the midpoint of the perineum into which the bulbospongiosus and superficial transverse perineal muscles of the superficial perineal pouch converge and interlace, together with the external anal sphincter and part of levator ani. The male urethra is conventionally divided into four parts — prostatic, membranous, bulbar and penile — of which the membranous urethra, surrounded by the external urethral sphincter within the deep perineal pouch, is the narrowest and least dilatable segment. The ureter runs retroperitoneally and, near the cervix, passes directly beneath the uterine artery close to the lateral vaginal fornix — the "water under the bridge" relation. The pudendal nerve, the principal somatic nerve of the perineum, arises from the anterior rami of S2, S3 and S4.

### Mechanism
Because the bulbospongiosus and superficial transverse perineal muscles physically insert into the perineal body, an episiotomy or a tear through it during labor puts their function at risk specifically — the deep transverse perineal muscle and the external urethral sphincter sit in the deep perineal pouch, above the perineal membrane, and are not perineal-body insertions, and ischiocavernosus attaches instead to the ischiopubic ramus and the crus of the clitoris/penis. The membranous urethra's narrowness and rigidity come from its being encircled by the striated external urethral sphincter as it traverses the deep perineal pouch — a fixed muscular ring, unlike the more distensible prostatic urethra (surrounded by glandular tissue) or the spongy, dilatable penile urethra. During hysterectomy, clamping and ligating the uterine artery at the level of the internal os brings the operative field within 1-2 cm of the ureter's crossing point beneath the vessel, making the ureter — not the higher-running ovarian artery in the infundibulopelvic ligament, nor the more lateral iliac vessels — the structure most at risk of accidental injury. The pudendal nerve leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen, hooks around the ischial spine/sacrospinous ligament, and re-enters through the lesser sciatic foramen into the pudendal (Alcock's) canal to supply the perineum.

### Key determinants
The perineal body's specific superficial-pouch insertions (bulbospongiosus, superficial transverse perineal, external anal sphincter) distinguish it from the deep pouch's own muscles (deep transverse perineal, sphincter urethrae), which sit at a different anatomical level and are not damaged by a perineal-body-directed episiotomy. Among the four described parts of the male urethra, only the membranous urethra carries the "narrowest and least dilatable" description as a whole segment — the prostatic urethra is the widest, and the penile urethra is dilatable along most of its length (its own narrowest point, the external meatus, is a separate landmark, not the whole penile segment). The ureter's proximity to the uterine artery, rather than the ovarian vessels or the iliac arteries, is what makes it the classic structure at risk during hysterectomy — the ovarian vessels run higher, away from the cervical field, and the iliac arteries lie too far lateral to be directly threatened by standard uterine vessel ligation. The pudendal nerve's S2-S4 root value distinguishes it from the sciatic nerve's broader L4-S3 range and from the sacral plexus's own wider L4-S4/S5 span.

### Clinical significance
Recognising the perineal body's specific muscle insertions guides which muscles' function to assess after an episiotomy or a perineal tear during labor. Identifying the membranous urethra as the narrowest, least dilatable segment explains why urethral strictures and traumatic urethral injury (e.g. pelvic fracture) classically localise there, producing the difficult, hesitant voiding this item's vignette describes. Awareness of the ureter's course beneath the uterine artery is a core patient-safety point in gynaecological surgery, since inadvertent ureteric ligation during hysterectomy is a recognised, largely preventable complication. And the pudendal nerve's S2-S4 root value is the anatomical basis for the pudendal nerve block used to anaesthetise the perineum for episiotomy and instrumental vaginal delivery.

## hold_these
Perineal body: bulbospongiosus + superficial transverse perineal muscles converge into it; deep transverse perineal and sphincter urethrae sit in the deep pouch instead, not perineal-body insertions.
Membranous urethra = narrowest, least dilatable part of the male urethra (surrounded by the external urethral sphincter); prostatic urethra is the widest part.
Ureter passes beneath the uterine artery near the cervix ("water under the bridge") — the structure most at risk of accidental ligation during hysterectomy.
Pudendal nerve root value = S2, S3, S4; basis of the pudendal nerve block for perineal anaesthesia.

## lose_the_mark
Attributing deep transverse perineal or sphincter urethrae damage to a perineal-body injury — both sit in the deep pouch, not the perineal body itself.
Choosing the prostatic or penile urethra instead of the membranous urethra as the narrowest, least dilatable segment.
Choosing the ovarian artery or an iliac vessel instead of the ureter as the structure at risk during hysterectomy.
Misremembering the pudendal nerve's root value as S1-S3, S3-S5, or a lumbosacral mix instead of S2-S4.
