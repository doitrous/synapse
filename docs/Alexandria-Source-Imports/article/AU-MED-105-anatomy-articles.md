## summary
The femoral triangle houses the femoral sheath, which sorts the femoral vessels into three compartments — the medial one being the empty femoral canal, the route of a femoral hernia. The femoral nerve supplies the anterior thigh muscles and, through the saphenous nerve, the medial leg and foot; the obturator nerve supplies the medial (adductor) compartment instead.

## sections
### Definition
The femoral triangle is bounded by the inguinal ligament above, sartorius laterally and adductor longus medially, with a floor of iliopsoas and pectineus and a roof of fascia lata. It contains, from lateral to medial, the femoral nerve, femoral artery, femoral vein and lymphatics ("NAVEL": Nerve, Artery, Vein, Empty space, Lymphatics). The femoral sheath is a short funnel-shaped prolongation of the abdominal fascial layers around the femoral vessels for about 4 cm below the inguinal ligament, divided by two septa into a lateral compartment (artery), an intermediate compartment (vein) and a medial compartment — the femoral canal — that contains only fat and lymphatics, including the node of Cloquet. The femoral ring, the canal's upper opening, is bounded by the inguinal ligament in front, the pectineal ligament behind, the lacunar ligament medially and the femoral vein's sheath laterally.

### Mechanism
Because the femoral canal is otherwise empty, it is the weak point through which abdominal or pelvic contents can herniate — a femoral hernia, which appears below and lateral to the pubic tubercle (unlike an inguinal hernia, which appears above and medial to it). Nearby, the saphenous opening — a separate deficiency in the deep fascia, floored by the cribriform fascia — is where the great saphenous vein pierces to join the femoral vein at the saphenofemoral junction; it sits close to the femoral ring but is a different aperture in a different fascial layer, and only the ring is a hernia route. The femoral nerve, lying outside the femoral sheath, supplies the quadriceps femoris (knee extension, tested as the patellar reflex), sartorius and pectineus, and gives two cutaneous branches: the anterior cutaneous nerves of the thigh, and the saphenous nerve, which follows the femoral artery through the adductor canal and continues down the medial leg and foot — the only branch of the lumbar plexus to reach the foot, and purely sensory. The obturator nerve, by contrast, leaves the pelvis through the obturator foramen and supplies the adductor (medial) compartment: its anterior division supplies gracilis, adductor longus and adductor brevis (and a small patch of medial-thigh skin), and its posterior division supplies obturator externus and the adductor part of adductor magnus — the hamstring part of adductor magnus being supplied instead by the tibial division of the sciatic nerve, a dual innervation worth holding onto explicitly.

### Key determinants
Whether a swelling in the groin is a femoral or an inguinal hernia is set by its relationship to the pubic tubercle (below and lateral versus above and medial). Which nerve is responsible for a sensory or motor deficit in the thigh or medial leg is set by which of the femoral, saphenous or obturator territories is involved.

### Clinical significance
A femoral hernia, though less common than an inguinal hernia, is more likely to strangulate because the femoral ring is a rigid, unyielding opening. A femoral nerve lesion weakens knee extension and abolishes the patellar reflex, with any accompanying sensory loss below the knee attributable specifically to the saphenous nerve, not to "the femoral nerve" as a motor label. An obturator nerve lesion weakens hip adduction, with sensory loss confined to a small medial-thigh patch — and spares the hamstring part of adductor magnus, which the tibial division of the sciatic nerve still drives.

## summary
The greater and lesser sciatic foramina, subdivided by piriformis, route the gluteal nerves and vessels, the sciatic nerve and the pudendal nerve out of the pelvis. Gluteus maximus, medius/minimus and the six short lateral rotators each have distinct actions and nerve supplies, and the hip joint's own spiral ligaments make it far more stable — and far less often dislocated — than the shoulder.

## sections
### Definition
The greater sciatic foramen, bounded by the sacrotuberous and sacrospinous ligaments, the greater sciatic notch and the sacrum, is subdivided by piriformis (which passes through it) into a compartment above piriformis (superior gluteal vessels and nerve) and one below it (sciatic nerve, inferior gluteal vessels and nerve, pudendal nerve and vessels, nerve to obturator internus, nerve to quadratus femoris, posterior cutaneous nerve of the thigh). The lesser sciatic foramen, below the ischial spine, re-admits the pudendal nerve and vessels into the perineum and also carries the obturator internus tendon and its nerve — so the pudendal nerve is the one structure common to both foramina, leaving through the greater and re-entering through the lesser. Gluteus maximus (inferior gluteal nerve) extends and laterally rotates the extended hip, inserting mostly into the iliotibial tract with only a minority of fibres reaching the gluteal tuberosity. Gluteus medius and minimus (superior gluteal nerve) abduct the hip and, critically, stabilise the pelvis in single-leg stance. The six short lateral rotators — piriformis, obturator internus with the superior and inferior gemelli, quadratus femoris, and obturator externus — run roughly horizontally from the pelvis or ischium to the region of the greater trochanter.

### Mechanism
Superior gluteal nerve weakness abolishes pelvic stabilisation on the affected side: because gluteus medius/minimus normally contract on the stance-leg side to stop the opposite (swing-leg) side of the pelvis dropping, their failure lets the pelvis drop toward the side **opposite** the weak muscle whenever the affected limb bears weight — the Trendelenburg sign — and the patient compensates by lurching the trunk toward the weak side. The hip joint itself is a deep ball-and-socket joint reinforced by three ligaments that all spiral to wind tighter in extension: the iliofemoral (anteriorly, checking hyperextension, the strongest ligament in the body), the pubofemoral (anteroinferiorly) and the ischiofemoral (posteriorly). The intracapsular ligamentum teres carries a small artery contributing to the femoral head's blood supply, mainly in childhood. Because the hip's bony and ligamentous fit is so much deeper than the shoulder's, dislocation needs major trauma and is overwhelmingly posterior (as in a "dashboard injury"), the reverse of the shoulder's usual anterior pattern.

### Key determinants
Which gluteal muscle is responsible for a given deficit is set by the movement lost (extension/lateral rotation → gluteus maximus/inferior gluteal nerve; abduction/pelvic stabilisation → gluteus medius-minimus/superior gluteal nerve) and by which side of the pelvis drops in a Trendelenburg gait (the side opposite the weak abductors).

### Clinical significance
A superior gluteal nerve injury (e.g. from a poorly placed intramuscular injection in the wrong gluteal quadrant, or hip surgery) produces a Trendelenburg gait. A posterior hip dislocation, from trauma driving a flexed femur backward, risks sciatic nerve injury and disruption of the trochanteric anastomosis supplying the femoral head, risking avascular necrosis.

## summary
The hamstrings extend the hip and flex the knee under the tibial division of the sciatic nerve, except the short head of biceps femoris, which answers to the common peroneal division instead. Three unrelated muscles converge as the pes anserinus on the medial tibia, and popliteus must unlock the fully extended knee before the hamstrings can flex it at all.

## sections
### Definition
The hamstrings — semitendinosus, semimembranosus, and the long head of biceps femoris — arise from the ischial tuberosity, cross both hip and knee, and so extend the hip and flex the knee; all, with the hamstring (ischial) part of adductor magnus, are supplied by the tibial division of the sciatic nerve. The short head of biceps femoris is the exception: it arises from the linea aspera, acts only at the knee, and is supplied by the common peroneal division — so it alone survives a tibial-division-selective sciatic injury, allowing some residual knee flexion. Sartorius, gracilis and semitendinosus converge as the pes anserinus onto the upper medial tibia despite arising from three different nerves (femoral, obturator, and the tibial division of the sciatic nerve respectively) and three different compartments. The popliteal fossa's deepest structure is the popliteal artery (superficial to it lie, in order, the tibial nerve then the popliteal vein).

### Mechanism
As the knee reaches full extension, the larger medial femoral condyle drives a terminal rotation — the femur medially rotating on the fixed tibia (the "screw-home mechanism") — which tightens the ligaments and locks the knee into a stable, low-effort standing position. To flex the knee again from this locked position, popliteus (arising from the lateral femoral condyle, inserting on the posterior tibia) must first contract to laterally rotate the femur on the tibia, unlocking the joint, before the hamstrings can do any flexing at all. A knee that "cannot be unlocked" therefore points to popliteus, not to hamstring weakness, however strong the hamstrings are.

### Key determinants
Whether a hamstring deficit spares knee flexion depends on which sciatic division is damaged: a tibial-division-selective lesion spares the short head of biceps femoris (common peroneal division) and so spares some flexion. Whether a knee can begin to flex from full extension depends on popliteus, not the hamstrings.

### Clinical significance
Sciatic nerve injury proximal to its division classically weakens the hamstrings except the short head of biceps femoris — a clinically useful localising sign. A patient reporting inability to "unlock" a fully extended knee to begin bending it should raise suspicion of popliteus dysfunction rather than isolated hamstring weakness.

## summary
The deep peroneal nerve drives the whole anterior compartment of the leg and continues onto the one intrinsic muscle of the dorsum of the foot, extensor digitorum brevis. The ankle joint only dorsiflexes and plantarflexes; inversion and eversion belong to the subtalar and transverse tarsal joints. The foot's three arches are held up by bone shape, plantar ligaments and long tendons, and the superior and inferior tibiofibular joints are different joint types entirely.

## sections
### Definition
The anterior compartment of the leg — tibialis anterior, extensor hallucis longus, extensor digitorum longus and peroneus tertius — dorsiflexes the ankle and extends the toes, and is supplied throughout by the deep peroneal nerve, which enters the compartment by piercing extensor digitorum longus after winding round the neck of the fibula. The same nerve continues onto the dorsum of the foot to supply extensor digitorum brevis, the one intrinsic muscle there (the sole has four full muscle layers by contrast). The superior tibiofibular joint is a small plane synovial joint between the fibular head and the lateral tibial condyle; the inferior tibiofibular joint is a fibrous syndesmosis just above the ankle, held by the interosseous ligament and anterior/posterior tibiofibular ligaments, with no synovial cavity. The true ankle (talocrural) joint is a hinge permitting only dorsiflexion and plantarflexion; inversion and eversion occur instead at the subtalar (talocalcaneal) joint acting with the transverse tarsal joint.

### Mechanism
Because one nerve (the deep peroneal) supplies the entire anterior compartment, a common peroneal nerve injury at the fibular neck — where it is superficial and vulnerable — paralyses dorsiflexion completely, producing foot drop and a high-stepping gait to clear the foot while walking. The three arches of the foot — medial longitudinal (calcaneus, talus, navicular, three cuneiforms, medial three metatarsals), lateral longitudinal (calcaneus, cuboid, lateral two metatarsals) and transverse (cuneiforms, cuboid, metatarsal bases) — distribute body weight and let the foot act as a spring. The medial longitudinal arch, the highest, is supported chiefly by the spring (plantar calcaneonavicular) ligament, tibialis posterior and the plantar aponeurosis; the calcaneus is the posterior pillar common to both longitudinal arches; the transverse arch is maintained by the tie-beam action of peroneus longus crossing the sole.

### Key determinants
Whether a foot-drop presentation localises to the common peroneal nerve (fibular neck) or a root/plexus lesion depends on whether sensory loss follows the nerve's own distinctive territory and whether inversion (tibial nerve territory, via tibialis posterior) is spared. Whether a movement belongs to the ankle joint proper or to the subtalar/transverse tarsal joints depends on whether it is dorsiflexion/plantarflexion (ankle) or inversion/eversion (subtalar/transverse tarsal).

### Clinical significance
Foot drop from a fibular-neck common peroneal injury is a classic complication of prolonged leg-crossing, tight plaster casts, or fibular neck fracture, and is distinguished from an L5 radiculopathy partly by whether foot inversion (tibialis posterior, tibial nerve) is preserved. Collapse of the medial longitudinal arch's supporting structures produces flat foot (pes planus).

## summary
Profunda femoris, not the femoral artery itself, supplies the bulk of the thigh muscles through its circumflex and perforating branches, which also form the trochanteric and cruciate anastomoses guaranteeing collateral flow to the hip. The popliteal artery, deepest structure in the popliteal fossa, divides into the anterior and posterior tibial arteries; the great and small saphenous veins drain the superficial lower limb into the femoral and popliteal veins respectively, close to the deep inguinal lymph nodes.

## sections
### Definition
Profunda femoris, the largest branch of the femoral artery, arises in the femoral triangle and gives the medial and lateral circumflex femoral arteries (supplying the hip joint) and three or four perforating arteries (piercing adductor magnus to supply the posterior/hamstring compartment). The trochanteric anastomosis, around the greater trochanter, links the superior and inferior gluteal arteries with the circumflex femoral arteries' ascending branches; the cruciate anastomosis, at the lesser trochanter, links the first perforating artery with the inferior gluteal and circumflex femoral arteries. The popliteal artery, the femoral artery's continuation beyond the adductor hiatus, is the deepest structure in the popliteal fossa (superficial to it: tibial nerve, then popliteal vein), gives genicular branches to the knee anastomosis, and ends at popliteus's lower border by dividing into the anterior and posterior tibial arteries. The great saphenous vein runs in front of the medial malleolus and up the medial leg and thigh to the saphenofemoral junction through the saphenous opening; the small saphenous vein runs behind the lateral malleolus and up the back of the calf to the saphenopopliteal junction with the popliteal vein. The deep inguinal lymph nodes, including the node of Cloquet, lie within the femoral canal medial to the femoral vein.

### Mechanism
Because profunda femoris — not the femoral artery, which mostly passes through to become the popliteal artery — supplies most of the thigh's muscle bulk, an occlusion of the femoral artery above the origin of profunda femoris is far more dangerous than one below it, since the perforating branches and the cruciate anastomosis can partly compensate for a more distal block. Similarly, the trochanteric anastomosis's contribution to femoral head perfusion is why a femoral neck fracture threatens avascular necrosis — the fracture line can disrupt the ascending cervical branches arising from it.

### Key determinants
The severity of a femoral or hip-region vascular injury depends on its level relative to the origin of profunda femoris and to the trochanteric/cruciate anastomoses, since these determine how much collateral flow survives.

### Clinical significance
Varicose veins classically affect the great and small saphenous systems because their valves are prone to becoming incompetent; the saphenofemoral and saphenopopliteal junctions are the surgical ligation points. Femoral neck fracture is a well-known cause of avascular necrosis of the femoral head via disruption of the trochanteric anastomosis's contribution.

## summary
A fractured clavicle displaces in a predictable way, driven by sternocleidomastoid on one fragment and the arm's own weight (plus pectoralis major, with trapezius too weak to stop it) on the other. The dorsal scapular and suprascapular nerves are both root/upper-trunk branches of the brachial plexus, given off early, each supplying a distinct set of scapular muscles.

## sections
### Definition
The clavicle typically fractures at the junction of its two curvatures, its structurally weakest point, from a fall onto the shoulder or outstretched hand. The dorsal scapular nerve arises directly from the C5 root (before the trunks form), pierces scalenus medius, and supplies levator scapulae and both rhomboids, muscles that retract and help elevate the scapula. The suprascapular nerve arises from the upper trunk (C5, C6), passes through the suprascapular notch under the superior transverse scapular ligament (the suprascapular artery passes above it), and supplies supraspinatus and infraspinatus plus sensory branches to the shoulder joint capsule itself — but no cutaneous branch to the overlying skin.

### Mechanism
After a clavicle fracture, the medial fragment is pulled upward and backward by sternocleidomastoid, now unopposed by the intact bone's counterweight. The lateral fragment is displaced downward, forward and medially by the weight of the whole upper limb and by pectoralis major; trapezius, though still attached to the lateral fragment, is too weak against the limb's dead weight to prevent the drop, producing the classic "dropped shoulder". Because the dorsal scapular and suprascapular nerves both leave the brachial plexus so early (at root or upper-trunk level, before the "big five" terminal branches), they are tested as answers to "which root/trunk gives rise to the nerve supplying X", rather than as sites of common isolated injury.

### Key determinants
Which fragment of a fractured clavicle moves which way, and why trapezius fails to prevent the lateral fragment's drop despite being attached there, is set by relative muscle strength against the limb's weight, not by attachment alone.

### Clinical significance
Recognising the typical clavicle fracture displacement pattern matters for reduction and for anticipating injury to the subclavian vessels or brachial plexus trunks that lie just deep to the middle third of the bone. A suprascapular nerve lesion weakens the initiation of abduction (supraspinatus) and lateral rotation (infraspinatus) without numbing the overlying skin — a point that distinguishes it from injuries to nerves that do carry cutaneous branches.

## summary
Pronator teres's two heads sandwich the median nerve as it enters the forearm, and the median nerve then supplies every anterior-compartment muscle except flexor carpi ulnaris and the medial half of flexor digitorum profundus, which the ulnar nerve takes. The anterior interosseous nerve, a purely motor branch of the median nerve, supplies flexor pollicis longus, the lateral half of flexor digitorum profundus and pronator quadratus.

## sections
### Definition
Pronator teres has a larger humeral head (medial epicondyle) and a smaller ulnar head (coronoid process), with the median nerve passing between them to enter the forearm — making pronator teres the classic entrapment site for the median nerve (pronator syndrome). It forms, with flexor carpi radialis, palmaris longus and flexor carpi ulnaris, the superficial layer of the anterior compartment. The anterior interosseous nerve branches from the median nerve just distal to pronator teres and runs down the front of the interosseous membrane, supplying flexor pollicis longus, the lateral (radial) half of flexor digitorum profundus (index and middle fingers), and pronator quadratus — a purely motor nerve with no cutaneous branch of its own.

### Mechanism
Having passed between pronator teres's two heads, the median nerve supplies every anterior-compartment muscle **except** flexor carpi ulnaris and the medial half of flexor digitorum profundus, both of which the ulnar nerve supplies instead — the deliberate two-muscle exception to "median nerve = front of forearm". An isolated anterior interosseous nerve lesion is tested clinically by the "OK sign": asked to pinch the thumb and index fingertip to tip, the patient cannot flex the interphalangeal joints of either digit fully (flexor pollicis longus and the index-finger part of flexor digitorum profundus being weak) and instead makes a triangular pinch rather than a round "O" — with no sensory loss, since the nerve is motor only.

### Key determinants
Whether a forearm flexor is supplied by the median or ulnar nerve depends on the two-muscle exception (flexor carpi ulnaris and the ulnar half of flexor digitorum profundus are ulnar; everything else anterior is median), not on which compartment the muscle sits in.

### Clinical significance
Anterior interosseous nerve syndrome (Kiloh–Nevin syndrome) presents with a positive "pinch sign" and no sensory complaint, distinguishing it from more proximal median nerve lesions that do cause sensory loss.

## summary
The tibial nerve alone drives the posterior compartment of the leg (superficial calf muscles plus the deep long flexors and tibialis posterior), producing plantarflexion and inversion — the mirror image of the deep-peroneal-driven anterior compartment. Five nerves divide the skin of the leg and foot between them: saphenous (medial leg), superficial peroneal (anterolateral leg and most of the dorsum), deep peroneal (only the first web space), sural (posterolateral leg and lateral foot), and the tibial nerve's plantar branches (the sole).

## sections
### Definition
The posterior compartment of the leg has a superficial group — gastrocnemius, soleus and plantaris, converging on the tendo calcaneus to insert on the calcaneus — and a deep group — tibialis posterior, flexor digitorum longus, flexor hallucis longus and popliteus. Every muscle in both groups is supplied by the tibial nerve, which enters between the two heads of gastrocnemius and runs deep to soleus with the posterior tibial vessels. Cutaneously, the leg and foot are divided among five nerves: the saphenous nerve (sensory continuation of the femoral nerve) covers the medial leg down to, but not behind, the medial malleolus; the superficial peroneal nerve covers the anterolateral leg and almost the whole dorsum of the foot; the deep peroneal nerve, despite its motor dominance of the anterior compartment, is reduced cutaneously to the first web space; the sural nerve covers the lower posterolateral leg, passes behind the lateral malleolus with the small saphenous vein, and reaches the lateral border of the foot to the little toe; and the tibial nerve's medial and lateral plantar branches cover the whole sole.

### Mechanism
Because the whole posterior compartment answers to one nerve, a tibial nerve injury paralyses plantarflexion and inversion together and produces a calcaneovalgus-type gait, the functional opposite of the foot-drop pattern from a common peroneal (anterior/lateral compartment) injury. Because the cutaneous map does not track the motor map one-for-one — the deep peroneal nerve's huge motor territory corresponds to a tiny sensory patch — sensory findings alone can mislocalise a nerve injury unless the examiner checks the correct nerve's actual skin field. A sciatic nerve injury spares the medial leg specifically because that strip is saphenous (femoral-derived) territory, not sciatic, which is the standard way this bank distinguishes a sciatic-level lesion from a more distal one.

### Key determinants
Whether a foot's dorsiflexion/plantarflexion deficit is common-peroneal or tibial in origin depends on whether inversion (tibial, via tibialis posterior) or eversion (superficial peroneal, via peroneus longus/brevis) is also lost, since the two compartments' actions run in parallel pairs. Whether a patch of numb skin localises to a specific nerve depends on matching its exact borders (which malleolus, front or back, how far up the leg) to the five-nerve map rather than assuming "the leg" is a single territory.

### Clinical significance
Tibial nerve injury (rare compared with common peroneal injury, since it lies deeper and is better protected) causes loss of plantarflexion, inversion, and sole sensation, and is tested by asking the patient to stand on tiptoe. Sural nerve biopsy is a classical diagnostic procedure in peripheral neuropathy because the nerve is purely sensory and superficial behind the lateral malleolus, so its loss causes only a tolerable patch of numbness on the lateral foot.

## summary

## sections
### Definition
The sole's intrinsic muscles form four layers: layer 1 (abductor hallucis, flexor digitorum brevis, abductor digiti minimi), layer 2 (the long flexor tendons, quadratus plantae, the lumbricals), layer 3 (flexor hallucis brevis, adductor hallucis, flexor digiti minimi brevis) and layer 4 (the interossei, plus the tibialis posterior and peroneus longus tendons crossing the sole). The medial plantar nerve supplies only abductor hallucis, flexor digitorum brevis, flexor hallucis brevis and the first lumbrical; the lateral plantar nerve supplies everything else, including quadratus plantae and adductor hallucis. In the knee, both cruciate ligaments and both menisci lie inside the fibrous capsule but outside the true synovial cavity; the medial meniscus is fused to the medial collateral ligament while the lateral meniscus is separated from the lateral collateral ligament by popliteus; the suprapatellar bursa communicates with the joint cavity, the popliteal (semimembranosus) bursa does not. The anterior tibial artery becomes dorsalis pedis in front of the ankle and ends by diving into the sole to complete the deep plantar arch; the posterior tibial artery gives the peroneal artery (the leg's main supply and the fibula's nutrient artery) and ends behind the medial malleolus as the medial and lateral plantar arteries. In the thigh, adductor longus separates the femoral artery from profunda femoris, whose medial circumflex femoral branch and the femoral vein's deep tributaries (profunda femoris, circumflex femoral and deep external pudendal veins, but not the small saphenous vein) complete the region's vascular picture.

### Mechanism
The sole's nerve split follows a hand-like pattern (median-like medial plantar nerve, ulnar-like lateral plantar nerve), which is why quadratus plantae and adductor hallucis — despite sounding medial by name or position — are lateral-plantar-supplied exceptions worth memorising deliberately rather than inferring from position. The knee's intracapsular-but-extrasynovial arrangement of the cruciates and menisci explains why an isolated meniscal tear can be diagnosed and treated arthroscopically without opening the true joint cavity's synovial lining elsewhere, and why the medial meniscus's fixation to the medial collateral ligament (unlike the free lateral meniscus) makes it far more often torn in the same injury. The dorsalis-pedis/posterior-tibial split explains the two sites where a foot pulse is checked clinically (dorsum of foot, behind medial malleolus) and why occlusion proximal to the origin of profunda femoris in the thigh is far more dangerous than a distal one.

### Key determinants
Whether a sole muscle is medial- or lateral-plantar-supplied depends on the "one and a half muscles are medial, the rest are lateral" rule, not on the muscle's superficial-versus-deep layer. Whether a knee ligament/meniscus injury pattern links the medial or lateral side depends on which meniscus is fused to its collateral ligament (medial) versus free (lateral, separated by popliteus).

### Clinical significance
Absent dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial pulses together suggest peripheral arterial disease proximal to both branch points; the "unhappy triad" (ACL, medial collateral ligament, medial meniscus) reflects the medial meniscus's fixation to its ligament. A femoral or hip vascular injury's severity depends on its level relative to the origin of profunda femoris, since its perforating branches supply most of the thigh's muscle bulk once past the origin.
