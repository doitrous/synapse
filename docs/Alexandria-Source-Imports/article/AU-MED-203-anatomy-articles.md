## summary
Alexandria's Nervous System anatomy department examines named structures against their exact course, origin and clinical correlate, not just their existence — which pharyngeal arch gives which cartilage, which groove a cranial nerve runs in, which thalamic nucleus relays which body region, and which spinal segment a nucleus actually sits in. This article states each fact once, alongside the specific alternative the department's own Workshop Quiz bank tests it against.

## sections
### Definition
The pharyngeal (branchial) arches are paired mesenchymal bars that appear in the fourth and fifth weeks in utero on either side of the developing pharynx, each with its own cartilage bar, muscle group, nerve and artery. Of the cartilage derivatives, the ventral portions of the fourth arch cartilage fuse to form the thyroid cartilage, while the sixth arch cartilage forms the cricoid, arytenoid and corniculate cartilages; there is no persisting fifth-arch cartilage, and the stapes and styloid process instead derive from the second (hyoid) arch, whose cartilage also gives the lesser cornu and upper body of the hyoid bone (the third arch cartilage gives the greater cornu and lower body). Muscularly, the second arch (facial nerve, CN VII) gives the muscles of facial expression, stapedius, stylohyoid and the posterior belly of digastric, distinct from the first arch's (trigeminal, V3) anterior belly of digastric and mylohyoid, the third arch's (glossopharyngeal, IX) stylopharyngeus, and geniohyoid, which is not an arch muscle at all but a hypoglossal-innervated derivative of the occipital myotomes. Separately from the pharyngeal apparatus, the scalp has five layers (Skin, dense subcutaneous Connective tissue, epicranial Aponeurosis, Loose areolar tissue, Pericranium — "SCALP"); the connective tissue layer, not the tendinous aponeurosis, is the vascular layer, carrying the scalp's arteries and cutaneous nerves.

Workshop Quiz 2 extends the pharyngeal-apparatus grid to the fourth pouch: its dorsal wing continues as the ultimobranchial body (which fuses with the thyroid to give the calcitonin-secreting parafollicular C cells), while its ventral wing gives the superior parathyroid gland, mirroring the third pouch's own dorsal/ventral split (inferior parathyroid, thymus). Tongue development supplies a parallel embryology fact: the anterior two-thirds of the tongue forms from paired lateral lingual swellings and a small midline tuberculum impar, all first-arch derivatives, and failure of the lateral swellings to fuse over the tuberculum impar leaves a bifid (cleft) tongue. Separately, cerebral white matter fibers are classed as association (intra-hemispheric, e.g. the superior longitudinal, uncinate and inferior longitudinal fasciculi) or commissural (inter-hemispheric); the corpus callosum, despite its length and prominence, is commissural, not a long association fiber.

### Mechanism
Several named nerves are examined for their exact course and territory. The vagus nerve (CN X) descends within the carotid sheath in the groove between the internal jugular vein and the internal/common carotid artery, distinct from the hypoglossal nerve, which crosses superficial to the carotid vessels rather than running within the sheath. Preganglionic parasympathetic fibers to the lacrimal gland leave the facial nerve as the greater petrosal nerve, joining the deep petrosal nerve to form the nerve of the pterygoid canal and synapsing in the pterygopalatine ganglion before postganglionic fibers reach the gland via the zygomatic and lacrimal nerves — a different facial-nerve parasympathetic route from the chorda tympani, which instead supplies the submandibular and sublingual glands. The ansa cervicalis, a cervical plexus (C1-C3) loop, supplies the infrahyoid strap muscles sternohyoid, sternothyroid and omohyoid; thyrohyoid, despite being an infrahyoid muscle, instead receives direct C1 fibers riding on the hypoglossal nerve, bypassing the ansa. The great auricular nerve (C2-C3), a cutaneous branch of the cervical plexus, supplies the skin over the parotid gland, auricle and angle of the mandible — territory that the greater/lesser occipital nerves (posterior scalp) and the trigeminal buccal branch (anterior cheek) do not reach.

The hypoglossal nerve (CN XII), on its descent to the tongue, crosses superficial to the internal carotid artery and the first part of the lingual artery before passing deep to the posterior belly of digastric — a constant relationship surgeons use to identify it during neck dissections, distinct from the vagus, which instead runs within the carotid sheath itself. The auriculotemporal nerve, a mandibular (V3) branch running close to the temporomandibular joint and parotid gland, supplies the external auditory meatus and the outer surface of the tympanic membrane, whose INNER surface is instead glossopharyngeal (Jacobson's nerve) territory. Digastric is a genuine exception to the usual one-muscle-one-nerve rule: its anterior belly (first arch) is supplied by the mylohyoid nerve (V3), while its posterior belly (second arch) is supplied directly by the facial nerve.

### Key determinants
The caudate nucleus arches over the thalamus; specifically its body, running along the floor of the body of the lateral ventricle directly above the thalamus, is the suprathalamic part of the caudate, distinct from its head (anterolateral to the anterior horn) and tail (curving into the temporal horn). The ventral posterior thalamic nuclear complex is somatotopically split: the ventral posterolateral (VPL) nucleus relays touch, proprioceptive and pain/temperature information from the body and limbs to the corresponding somatosensory cortex, while the ventral posteromedial (VPM) nucleus performs the equivalent role for the face and oral cavity (including taste), relaying to the face representation near area 43. In the primary motor cortex (precentral gyrus), the inverted somatotopic homunculus places the leg/foot superiorly and onto the medial surface, the trunk and arm at the mid-convexity, and the face — needing the finest motor control — most laterally and inferiorly near the lateral fissure, with disproportionately large cortical territory. The tectum, the dorsal midbrain formed by the superior and inferior colliculi, sits above the tegmentum and crus cerebri, and is distinct from the pons, diencephalon or medulla. The hypoglossal nerve (CN XII) rootlets emerge from the anterolateral (preolivary) sulcus between the medullary pyramid and olive, distinct from the postolivary sulcus, which instead carries the glossopharyngeal, vagus and accessory rootlets. Functionally, the cerebellar vermis and flanking intermediate (paravermal) zone form the spinocerebellum, carrying a topographical somatotopic body map from spinal proprioceptive input, unlike the lateral hemispheres (movement planning, no direct map) or the flocculonodular lobe (balance). Within the dural venous system, the superior sagittal sinus runs in the superior, attached border of the falx cerebri from the crista galli to the internal occipital protuberance, distinct from the inferior sagittal sinus (free inferior border of the falx) and the straight sinus (falx-tentorium junction). Thoracic spinal cord grey matter genuinely contains the nucleus proprius, Clark's (dorsal) nucleus (T1-L2, origin of the dorsal spinocerebellar tract) and the intermediolateral sympathetic nucleus (T1-L2); the phrenic nucleus, giving rise to the phrenic nerve supplying the diaphragm, instead sits in the cervical cord (chiefly C3-C5), not in any thoracic segment. In the dorsal column-medial lemniscus pathway, upper-limb (including finger) proprioceptive and fine-touch fibers ascend in the fasciculus cuneatus to their second-order neuron in the cuneate nucleus of the lower medulla, while lower-limb fibers instead synapse in the gracile nucleus via the fasciculus gracilis; both give rise to internal arcuate fibers that decussate to form the medial lemniscus. In the anterolateral (spinothalamic) system carrying pain, temperature and crude touch from the body, second-order neurons decussate near their level of entry and ascend to terminate in the ventral posterolateral (VPL) nucleus of the thalamus — the corresponding facial pathway instead terminates in the VPM.

Brodmann area 22 occupies the superior temporal gyrus, largely overlapping Wernicke's area, the receptive-language counterpart to Broca's area (areas 44/45) across the lateral fissure. The internal capsule's own regional organisation extends further: its genu carries corticobulbar fibers (distinct from the anterior limb's frontopontine fibers and the posterior limb's corticospinal and sensory radiation fibers), and it is supplied by the anterior choroidal, middle cerebral (lenticulostriate) and anterior cerebral (Heubner) arteries, but NOT by the posterior cerebral artery, which instead supplies the occipital lobe and, via separate branches, the thalamus. In the spinal cord grey matter, the substantia gelatinosa at the tip of the dorsal horn is the first major relay/modulation site for pain transmission, distinct from the nucleus thoracicus (Clarke's column, T1-L2), which instead gives rise to the dorsal spinocerebellar tract carrying unconscious proprioception to the cerebellum; in the white matter, the lateral corticospinal tract specifically controls fine, discrete voluntary movement. On the medial cerebral surface, the paracentral lobule — the continuation of the pre/postcentral gyri — represents not only the leg and foot but, further medially, the perineum, including voluntary sphincter control. In the visual pathway, a lesion of the optic tract (carrying a mixed, already-partly-crossed representation of the contralateral field) produces a contralateral homonymous hemianopia, distinct from the chiasm's bitemporal hemianopia.

### Clinical significance
A mass lesion compressing the preolivary sulcus characteristically produces ipsilateral tongue weakness and deviation on protrusion, the classic bedside localiser for a lower motor neuron hypoglossal lesion. The abducens nerve's unique free course within the cavernous sinus (rather than its lateral dural wall, like the trochlear and trigeminal divisions) makes it the cranial nerve most vulnerable to compression from cavernous sinus pathology such as thrombosis or a carotid-cavernous fistula. Because the phrenic nucleus is cervical (C3-C5) rather than thoracic, a high cervical cord injury above this level is immediately life-threatening without ventilatory support, while a lower thoracic injury spares diaphragmatic breathing. A cerebellar lesion confined to the vermis produces truncal or gait ataxia, whereas a more lateral intermediate-zone lesion instead produces limb ataxia on the same side — a direct clinical consequence of the vermis/intermediate zone's somatotopic organisation described above.

Because the corticospinal/corticobulbar fibers running through the internal capsule have not yet decussated, a unilateral internal capsule lesion produces an upper motor neuron syndrome — spastic paralysis, exaggerated tendon jerks and an extensor plantar response — on the CONTRALATERAL side of the body, not the ipsilateral or flaccid pattern of a lower motor neuron lesion. The basilar artery, formed by the vertebral arteries' union at the pontomedullary junction, supplies the pons and midbrain via paramedian and circumferential branches; its occlusion produces brainstem ischemia ranging from isolated cranial nerve/long tract signs to locked-in syndrome, a different territory from the internal or external carotid arteries.

## lose_the_mark
Assigning the lesser cornu of the hyoid or the stapes to the third arch — both are second-arch derivatives.
Calling geniohyoid a second-arch muscle because it sits near stylohyoid — it is a non-arch, hypoglossal-innervated muscle.
Naming the aponeurotic (galea) layer as the scalp's vascular layer instead of the connective tissue layer beneath the skin.
Placing the hypoglossal nerve inside the carotid sheath alongside the vagus — it crosses external to the sheath.
Confusing the greater petrosal (lacrimal gland) and chorda tympani (submandibular/sublingual gland) parasympathetic routes of the facial nerve.
Assigning thyrohyoid to the ansa cervicalis rather than to its direct C1/hypoglossal supply.
Sending trigeminal (buccal) territory as far as the angle of the mandible — that territory belongs to the cervical plexus's great auricular nerve.
Swapping VPL and VPM, or assuming the whole caudate (not just its body) is suprathalamic.
Placing the hand, not the face, as the most lateral/inferior motor-cortex representation.
Placing the tectum in the diencephalon, or the hypoglossal rootlets in the postolivary (not preolivary) sulcus.
Attributing the cerebellar body map to the lateral hemispheres rather than the vermis/intermediate zone.
Treating the phrenic nucleus as thoracic because the diaphragm is a thoracic/abdominal muscle, or sending body spinothalamic fibers to VPM instead of VPL.
Assigning the ultimobranchial body to the fourth pouch's ventral wing (that is the superior parathyroid) rather than its dorsal wing.
Confusing bifid tongue (tuberculum impar fusion failure) with ankyloglossia (a short frenulum, an unrelated anomaly).
Classing the corpus callosum among the long association fibers because of its length — length alone does not make a bundle an association tract.
Placing the hypoglossal nerve within the carotid sheath alongside the vagus, or crossing it deep to (rather than superficial to) the carotid/lingual arteries.
Attributing sensation of the tympanic membrane's INNER surface to the auriculotemporal nerve — that is glossopharyngeal (Jacobson's nerve) territory.
Assuming digastric has a single nerve supply like most named muscles — its two bellies answer to two different cranial nerves.
Confusing area 22 (superior temporal gyrus, receptive language) with Broca's area (inferior frontal gyrus, expressive language).
Assigning the posterior cerebral artery a role in supplying the internal capsule because of its proximity to the thalamus.
Confusing the substantia gelatinosa (pain) with the nucleus thoracicus/Clarke's column (unconscious proprioception, dorsal spinocerebellar tract) as dorsal horn nuclei.
Attributing sensory ataxia (a dorsal column sign) to a lateral corticospinal tract lesion, whose own sign is loss of fine voluntary movement.
Limiting the paracentral lobule's representation to the leg/foot alone, omitting its perineal representation.
Reversing chiasmal (bitemporal) and optic tract (contralateral homonymous) visual field defects.
Placing the internal capsule lesion's motor deficit on the same side as the lesion, or describing it as flaccid rather than spastic.
Confusing the basilar artery's brainstem (posterior circulation) territory with the internal carotid artery's cerebral hemisphere (anterior circulation) territory.
