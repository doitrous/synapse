### Where the cough is coming from
Mrs Samia Fouad, 58, has coughed for three months. She has hypertension, treated for two years. The cough is dry, worse at night, and she has bought three different cough mixtures.
Concept: CON-RES-AF0A37842A4B44
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What should be considered as possible causes of her cough?
*= Upper or lower airway disease, reflux oesophagitis, and drugs such as ACE inhibitors
Why: Correct. Cough arises from disease anywhere in the airway, from reflux irritating the larynx, and from drugs — ACE inhibitors being the classic example, and directly relevant to a treated hypertensive.
* Lower airway disease only, since cough is a pulmonary symptom
Why: Restricts the differential to the lungs and would miss both the reflux and the drug, which between them account for a large share of chronic cough.
* Psychological causes, once a chest radiograph is normal
Why: A habit cough exists but is a diagnosis of exclusion, and a normal film excludes very little.
* Only infection, since cough is a symptom of inflammation
Why: Chronic cough is frequently non-infective, and treating every cough as infection is how a drug cause is missed for months.
Rationale: A cough is a symptom with a differential, and two of the commonest causes — reflux and an ACE inhibitor — are outside the lungs. The drug history is part of the respiratory history.

### What treatment should be aimed at
Concept: CON-RES-C386DA2E357D56
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What principle should govern the treatment of her cough?
*= Treat the underlying cause
Why: Correct. Cough is a symptom; suppressing it without addressing the cause leaves the disease untreated and may conceal it.
* Suppress the cough first, and investigate only if suppression fails
Why: Reverses the order and risks suppressing a protective reflex in someone with an unrecognised cause.
* Use a combination preparation to cover several possible causes at once
Why: Combination mixtures treat symptoms rather than causes, and covering several possibilities at once is not the same as identifying one.
* Reassure, since chronic cough is rarely significant
Why: Chronic cough may be the presenting feature of serious disease, and reassurance without a cause is not a treatment.
Rationale: The cough is the signal, not the disease. In this patient the treatment most likely to work is a review of her antihypertensive, not a bottle from the pharmacy.

### Matching the preparation to the cough
Concept: CON-RES-E54C71E3C8C556
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: How does the type of cough determine which preparation might be used?
*= A dry, non-productive cough may be treated with an antitussive, while a productive cough is managed with expectorants and mucolytics
Why: Correct. Suppression suits a cough that produces nothing; a cough that is clearing secretions is helped by making those secretions easier to move, not by abolishing it.
* Antitussives suit both types, since the aim is always to stop the cough
Why: Suppressing a productive cough retains secretions the patient is trying to clear.
* Expectorants suit a dry cough, since they add moisture
Why: Reverses the pairing; an expectorant acts on secretions that are already present.
* The type of cough is irrelevant to the choice of preparation
Why: It is the single most useful distinction in choosing one at all.
Rationale: Dry means suppress, productive means mobilise. Getting that the wrong way round is the commonest error in over-the-counter cough treatment, and it makes the patient worse.

### What a combination bottle is worth
Concept: CON-RES-2150FBD9FBEE6D
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: She has been taking mixtures containing two or three active preparations. How should those be regarded?
*= As adjuncts to treatment, not as treatment of the cause
Why: Correct. Combination cough mixtures are presented as adjuncts; they may relieve the symptom but do not address why the cough is there.
* As definitive treatment, provided the combination covers the likely mechanisms
Why: No combination treats reflux, an ACE inhibitor or airway disease, which are the causes actually in play here.
* As harmful in all circumstances and never to be used
Why: Overstates. They have a place in symptom relief; the error is in mistaking relief for cure.
* As equivalent to a single-agent preparation in every respect
Why: Combining agents adds exposure and interactions without adding a treatment for the cause.
Rationale: An adjunct helps alongside treatment; it is not treatment. Three bottles over three months is a sign that the cause has not been found.

### How a central antitussive works
Concept: CON-RES-9BCD8280370DF3
Difficulty: Hard
Q: If an antitussive were used, how do the centrally acting ones work, and what follows from that?
*= They inhibit the medullary cough centre directly, suppressing the reflex itself — so they suppress a protective mechanism regardless of what is provoking it
Why: Correct. Central antitussives act on the cough centre in the medulla, abolishing the reflex rather than treating its stimulus, which is why they are inappropriate when secretions need clearing.
* They act on the airway mucosa, reducing the stimulus reaching the centre
Why: That is the peripheral route, used by local anaesthetic agents and demulcents, not the central one.
* They increase the water content of secretions so less stimulus arises
Why: Describes an expectorant, which works on the secretions rather than the reflex.
* They relax bronchial smooth muscle, removing the mechanical trigger
Why: That is bronchodilatation, which relieves bronchoconstriction rather than suppressing the cough reflex.
Rationale: Central suppression is powerful and indiscriminate. Knowing where a drug acts is what tells you when it is inappropriate — and a productive cough in an infected patient is exactly that circumstance.
## debrief
The case is a differential in disguise: a chronic dry cough in a treated hypertensive should raise the ACE inhibitor before it raises the pharmacy shelf. Behind that sit two principles — treat the cause, and match the preparation to the type of cough — and one mechanism, since a central antitussive abolishes a protective reflex wherever the stimulus came from.

### What it does to mucus
On the same ward, one patient is receiving acetylcysteine for tenacious sputum and another for a paracetamol overdose.
Concept: CON-RES-80300BAF6E1772
Difficulty: Easy
Q: How does acetylcysteine act on respiratory secretions?
*= It splits the disulfide bonds of mucoproteins, reducing the viscosity and tenacity of secretions
Why: Correct. Breaking the disulfide cross-links between mucoprotein chains makes the mucus less viscous and easier to clear.
* It increases the water content of the secretions
Why: That is how an expectorant works; acetylcysteine acts chemically on the mucus structure itself.
* It suppresses the production of mucus by goblet cells
Why: It acts on mucus already produced rather than on its production.
* It anaesthetises the airway, so secretions are not perceived
Why: Describes a local anaesthetic antitussive, and would leave the secretions exactly as they were.
Rationale: The disulfide bond is the structural target. Naming it is what separates a mucolytic, which changes the mucus, from an expectorant, which dilutes it.

### Naming the class
Concept: CON-RES-DCD7BDC14AF7AE
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What do mucolytics do?
*= Break down and liquefy thick, tenacious secretions, improving airflow
Why: Correct. Mucolytics reduce the viscosity of secretions so they can be cleared, which improves airflow through airways that were partly occluded.
* Suppress the cough reflex so secretions are not expelled
Why: The opposite intention: mucolytics are given so that secretions can be cleared more easily.
* Reduce inflammation of the bronchial mucosa
Why: That is an anti-inflammatory effect, which mucolytics do not primarily provide.
* Dilate the bronchi mechanically
Why: Bronchodilatation is a different mechanism; a mucolytic improves airflow by changing what is inside the airway, not its calibre.
Rationale: Mucolytics work on the contents of the airway, bronchodilators on its walls. Both improve airflow, by different routes and for different patients.

### Which drugs belong to it
Concept: CON-RES-04CD9047E73472
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Which of these are listed as mucolytics?
*= Bromhexine, acetylcysteine, inhaled dornase alfa and erdosteine
Why: Correct. These are the mucolytic agents listed, acting to reduce the viscosity of secretions by differing mechanisms.
* Codeine, dextromethorphan and pholcodine
Why: These are central antitussives, which suppress the cough reflex rather than thin secretions.
* Salbutamol, salmeterol and ipratropium
Why: These are bronchodilators, acting on airway smooth muscle.
* Prednisolone, beclometasone and budesonide
Why: These are corticosteroids, acting on airway inflammation.
Rationale: Grouping drugs by what they do to the airway — thin the mucus, widen the tube, or damp the inflammation — is what makes a long list of names manageable and prevents one class being used for another's job.

### The other use entirely
Concept: CON-RES-9DA5B3FB92BC7A
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Why is acetylcysteine given in paracetamol poisoning?
*= It restores hepatic glutathione and binds the reactive hepatotoxic metabolite, protecting the liver
Why: Correct. Paracetamol toxicity is caused by a reactive metabolite that depletes glutathione; acetylcysteine replenishes glutathione and binds the metabolite.
* It breaks down paracetamol in the stomach before absorption
Why: It is not a binding agent in the gut; activated charcoal serves that purpose early after ingestion.
* It thins hepatic secretions, allowing the drug to be excreted in bile
Why: Transfers its respiratory mechanism to the liver, where it does not apply.
* It blocks the enzyme that metabolises paracetamol
Why: It does not act on the enzyme; it deals with the reactive metabolite once formed.
Rationale: One molecule, two indications, two mechanisms — a sulfhydryl group that splits mucus disulfide bonds in the airway and restores glutathione in the liver. Chemistry connects them; the clinical uses have nothing else in common.

### The third property
Concept: CON-RES-6F7E169B8BE108
Difficulty: Hard
Q: Acetylcysteine is also described as having an antioxidant effect. How does that relate to its other two actions?
*= All three follow from its free sulfhydryl group — it donates reducing equivalents, which splits mucus disulfide bonds, replenishes glutathione and scavenges oxidants
Why: Correct. The sulfhydryl group is the common chemical basis: reducing disulfide bonds in mucoproteins, supplying cysteine for glutathione synthesis, and acting directly as an antioxidant.
* The antioxidant effect is unrelated and arises from a separate metabolite
Why: Misses the shared chemistry that makes one molecule serve three purposes.
* The antioxidant effect is what liquefies mucus, and the two are the same action
Why: Related through the same chemical group but not the same action; mucolysis is a specific reduction of disulfide cross-links.
* The antioxidant effect only occurs at doses far above those used clinically
Why: Invents a threshold and disconnects the property from the molecule's ordinary behaviour.
Rationale: One functional group explains all three. That is the level of understanding that makes a drug's uses predictable rather than a list to be memorised.
## debrief
Two patients, one drug, and a single chemical property behind both uses. The sulfhydryl group splits the disulfide bonds that make mucus tenacious, supplies the cysteine that restores hepatic glutathione, and scavenges oxidants directly. Sorting drugs by what they do to the airway — thin the mucus, widen the tube, damp the inflammation, suppress the reflex — is what keeps the classes apart at the bedside.

### Making secretions easier to shift
Concept: CON-RES-5B33A2B1CB0210
Difficulty: Easy
Q: How do expectorants work?
*= They increase the water content of airway secretions, reducing stickiness so the secretions are easier to cough up
Why: Correct. An expectorant makes secretions wetter and less adherent, so the cough that is already occurring clears them more effectively.
* They break the chemical bonds within mucus
Why: That is mucolysis, a chemical action on the mucus structure rather than a change in its water content.
* They suppress the cough reflex once secretions are cleared
Why: Suppression is the opposite of what an expectorant is for.
* They reduce the volume of secretion produced
Why: They act on the character of secretions rather than reducing their production.
Explanation: Expectorant and mucolytic both make secretions easier to clear, by adding water and by breaking bonds respectively. Both depend on the patient still coughing, which is why neither is combined with a suppressant.

### Soothing the surface
Concept: CON-RES-BEFE4500100131
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What are demulcents such as liquorice pastilles, lozenges and syrups used for?
*= To soothe the pharyngeal mucosa in pharyngitis and sore throat
Why: Correct. Demulcents coat and soothe the inflamed mucosal surface, relieving the local irritation that provokes coughing.
* To suppress the medullary cough centre
Why: That is a central antitussive action; a demulcent acts locally on the mucosa.
* To liquefy secretions in the lower airway
Why: They act on the pharynx and do not reach or alter lower airway secretions.
* To dilate the bronchi and relieve wheeze
Why: They have no action on airway smooth muscle.
Explanation: A demulcent works where it is applied, which is why it helps a sore throat and does nothing for a productive cough arising below the larynx.

### Anaesthetising the trigger
Concept: CON-RES-3F6E8F02EBE7F3
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: How does benzonatate reduce cough?
*= By anaesthetising the stretch receptors in the respiratory tract, lung tissue and pleura
Why: Correct. Benzonatate acts peripherally, numbing the stretch receptors whose stimulation triggers the cough reflex.
* By inhibiting the medullary cough centre
Why: That is the central route used by other antitussives; benzonatate acts on the receptors.
* By reducing the viscosity of secretions
Why: A mucolytic action, which benzonatate does not have.
* By blocking histamine receptors in the airway mucosa
Why: An antihistamine action, and not the mechanism described.
Explanation: The cough reflex has a receptor, an afferent path, a centre and an efferent path, and a drug can be aimed at any of them. Naming the target is what distinguishes a peripheral from a central antitussive.

### The muscle in the bronchial wall
Concept: CON-RES-57BA3D5E03451B
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: How is the smooth muscle of the intrapulmonary bronchi arranged?
*= Spirally, encircling the airway in a helical pattern
Why: Correct. Intrapulmonary bronchial smooth muscle is arranged spirally, which is what allows it to shorten and narrow the airway together.
* Circularly, as complete rings around the lumen
Why: The circular arrangement belongs to the bronchioles; the intrapulmonary bronchi are spiral.
* Longitudinally, running parallel to the airway
Why: Longitudinal muscle would shorten the tube without narrowing it appreciably.
* There is no smooth muscle in the intrapulmonary bronchi, only cartilage
Why: Cartilage is present as irregular plates, but smooth muscle is present too and is what makes the calibre adjustable.
Explanation: A spiral arrangement in the bronchi and a circular one in the bronchioles both narrow the airway on contraction, which is why a bronchodilator acts along the whole length of the conducting tree rather than at one level.

### Where the glands run out
Concept: CON-RES-FD8740B1526869
Difficulty: Hard
Q: What happens to the seromucous glands of the airway wall distally, and what does that mean for a drug given to alter secretions?
*= They decrease progressively and disappear, so the secretions of the most distal airways are not glandular and a drug acting on gland secretion has less to act on there
Why: Correct. Serous and mucous glands become fewer with successive divisions and are absent distally, so distal airway lining fluid arises from surface cells rather than from submucosal glands.
* They increase distally, so drug effects on secretion are greatest in the bronchioles
Why: Reverses the gradient; glands are most abundant proximally, in the trachea and large bronchi.
* They remain constant throughout the conducting airways
Why: The progressive loss is one of the features distinguishing the levels of the airway.
* They are replaced distally by goblet cells, which increase in number
Why: Goblet cells also decrease distally and disappear, so no such replacement occurs.
Explanation: Everything in the airway wall thins out together — glands, goblet cells, cartilage, epithelium, lamina propria. That single gradient explains where mucus is produced, where it is cleared from, and where a drug acting on glandular secretion can and cannot work.

### The cell that covers the surface
Concept: CON-RES-AE642FB46D38E2
Difficulty: Easy
Q: Which cell forms most of the alveolar lining surface?
*= The type I pneumocyte
Why: Correct. Type I pneumocytes cover the great majority of the alveolar surface area, providing the thin barrier across which gas diffuses.
* The type II pneumocyte
Why: Type II cells are more numerous but far smaller, so they cover only a small fraction of the surface.
* The alveolar macrophage
Why: Macrophages move within the alveolar space; they are not part of its lining.
* The endothelial cell of the capillary
Why: Endothelium lines the capillary on the other side of the barrier, not the alveolar space.
Explanation: More type II cells but more type I surface. Holding both facts at once is what makes the alveolar wall's structure and its repair after injury understandable.

### Why that cell is shaped as it is
Concept: CON-RES-E08739F0C0C229
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What is the morphology of the type I pneumocyte, and why does it matter?
*= Extremely flattened and attenuated, so the diffusion distance for gas is minimal
Why: Correct. Its thin, sheet-like cytoplasm keeps the blood-gas barrier as thin as possible, which is what makes diffusion efficient.
* Cuboidal with abundant lamellar bodies for secretion
Why: That describes the type II pneumocyte, whose lamellar bodies store surfactant.
* Columnar and ciliated, sweeping particles towards the bronchioles
Why: Ciliated columnar epithelium belongs to the conducting airways; cilia end before the alveolus.
* Spherical and motile, phagocytosing inhaled particles
Why: Describes the alveolar macrophage.
Explanation: Function dictates shape. A cell whose whole purpose is to be crossed by gas is built to be as thin as a cell can be, which is also why it is so vulnerable to injury.

### What lies between two alveoli
Concept: CON-RES-C5513EC116B783
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What does the interalveolar septum contain?
*= A dense capillary network with a thin connective tissue framework, between the alveolar linings of two adjacent alveoli
Why: Correct. The septum between neighbouring alveoli carries the capillary bed and its supporting elastic and collagenous framework, sandwiched between two alveolar surfaces.
* Cartilage plates that keep the alveoli open
Why: Cartilage disappeared far proximally, at the transition to bronchiole.
* Smooth muscle arranged circularly around each alveolus
Why: Muscle persists only at the alveolar openings of the alveolar ducts, not around each alveolus.
* Lymphatic channels draining each alveolus directly
Why: Alveolar walls contain no lymphatics, which is why alveolar fluid must first reach the surrounding connective tissue.
Explanation: The septum is where gas exchange physically happens, and its thinness is why any thickening — oedema, fibrosis, inflammation — impairs diffusion so readily.

### Where the alveolar macrophage comes from
Concept: CON-RES-9B9526985BB441
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What is the origin of the alveolar macrophages?
*= They are derived from blood monocytes, which migrate into the alveolar space
Why: Correct. Alveolar macrophages arise from circulating monocytes of bone-marrow origin that enter the lung and mature within the alveoli.
* They are modified type II pneumocytes that have detached
Why: Type II cells are the progenitors of the alveolar *lining* after injury, not of macrophages.
* They are resident cells that arise from the interalveolar septum's fibroblasts
Why: Fibroblasts maintain the connective tissue framework and do not become macrophages.
* They are lymphocytes that have migrated from the bronchial lymphoid tissue
Why: Lymphocytes are a distinct lineage with a different function.
Explanation: Because the mucociliary escalator ends before the alveolus, defence at that level is cellular, and the cell is a monocyte that has come from the blood to do it.

### The film that lets the lung slide
Concept: CON-RES-B7C1C8079DFBE9
Difficulty: Challenging
Q: A small volume of fluid lies in the pleural cavity. What does it do, and what follows if it accumulates?
*= It lubricates the two pleural surfaces so they slide over one another while holding them together by surface tension — and an accumulation separates them, so the lung no longer follows the chest wall and collapses away from it
Why: Correct. The thin film both lubricates and couples the layers: it allows sliding while its surface tension resists separation. A large effusion abolishes that coupling and compresses the lung.
* It lubricates only, so an accumulation has no mechanical consequence beyond volume
Why: Misses the coupling function, which is what makes the lung follow the chest wall at all.
* It holds the layers apart, and a reduction in volume causes them to adhere
Why: Reverses the function; the film holds the layers together rather than apart.
* It supplies nutrients to the visceral pleura, which has no blood supply
Why: The visceral pleura is supplied by the bronchial circulation, so it does not depend on the pleural fluid for nutrition.
Explanation: Two functions from one film — sliding and sticking. That is why a small volume is essential and a large volume is disabling, and why the lung collapses inwards when the coupling is lost.
