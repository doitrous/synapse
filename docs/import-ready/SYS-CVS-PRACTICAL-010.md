### Why the ankles swell
Mr Mounir Askalany, 68, has known heart failure. He is dizzy when he gets out of bed, and by evening his ankles are swollen. His treatment was increased two weeks ago.
Concept: CON-CVS-26C64F6FB246BD
Difficulty: Easy
Q: His right ventricle cannot move all the blood delivered to it. What follows upstream?
*= Blood accumulates in the systemic veins and venous pressure rises behind the failing ventricle
Why: Correct. A ventricle that cannot eject what it receives leaves blood in the circuit feeding it, and the pressure behind it rises.
* Blood accumulates in the pulmonary veins, causing pulmonary oedema
Why: That is left ventricular failure. Which side fails determines which circuit backs up.
* Total blood volume falls, because less is circulating
Why: Volume is redistributed towards the venous side rather than lost.
* Arterial pressure rises, because the blood has to go somewhere
Why: Reasons about the wrong side of the pump; failure to eject reduces what is delivered forward.
Rationale: Right-sided failure raises systemic venous pressure, which is why the neck veins and the ankles report the same problem.

### Why the evening is worse
Concept: CON-CVS-6430B0D729893E
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: His ankles are normal on waking and swollen by evening, without any change in his heart. Why?
*= Being upright all day adds the weight of the blood column to venous pressure in the legs, raising capillary pressure there
Why: Correct. Venous pressure at any point includes the hydrostatic column above it, so an upright day raises pressure in the dependent veins and drives filtration into the tissue.
* His heart fails progressively through the day and recovers overnight
Why: Attributes a postural effect to a change in cardiac function, which would not reverse so reliably with a night in bed.
* He drinks more as the day goes on, adding volume
Why: Intake contributes to overall fluid balance but does not explain why the fluid collects specifically in the dependent parts.
* Standing reduces venous pressure in the legs, so fluid is drawn in from elsewhere
Why: Reverses the direction gravity acts on the column.
Rationale: The distribution of oedema follows the hydrostatic column. That is why it is in the ankles by evening and over the sacrum in a patient who has been in bed.

### The hormone the failing heart recruits
Concept: CON-CVS-A3B1CCD1E56CE7
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: His renin-angiotensin system is active. What is angiotensin II's overall effect on arterial pressure, and why is that unhelpful here?
*= It raises pressure, by constricting vessels and promoting salt and water retention — which adds volume a failing ventricle cannot move
Why: Correct. Both arms raise pressure, and in a failing heart the retained volume worsens the congestion the patient already has.
* It lowers pressure by promoting sodium excretion, which relieves the oedema
Why: Reverses the renal effect; angiotensin II promotes retention.
* It raises pressure by increasing heart rate alone
Why: Its effects are vascular and renal; rate is not the mechanism.
* It has no effect on pressure and acts only on thirst
Why: Thirst is one of its actions but far from the only one.
Rationale: A response built to defend pressure after a haemorrhage is recruited here against a heart that cannot use the extra volume. The same physiology helps in one setting and harms in the other.

### Why he is dizzy on standing
Concept: CON-CVS-E476EBADED4CE3
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: On standing, his pressure falls and he feels faint. What should the normal response to that threat be?
*= Heart rate and contractility rise and the vessels constrict, defending pressure and redirecting flow to brain and heart
Why: Correct. The response is coordinated — more output, more resistance, and flow redistributed to the organs that cannot tolerate interruption.
* Heart rate falls and the vessels dilate, reducing the work of the heart
Why: Describes a vasovagal response, which is the reflex going the wrong way rather than the normal defence.
* Only the heart responds, since the vessels play no acute part
Why: Vasoconstriction is at least as important, and in a standing adult contributes more.
* Blood is diverted to skin and muscle in preparation for movement
Why: Those beds constrict in this response; muscle flow rises only when the muscle works.
Rationale: The defence needs both a cardiac and a vascular arm. In a patient whose ventricle cannot increase its output much, the vascular arm carries more of the burden — and drugs that blunt it produce exactly this symptom.

### Reading the two symptoms together
Concept: CON-CVS-AEBDDFAB03B5DF
Difficulty: Hard
Q: He is congested when upright and hypotensive when he stands. Which two quantities set mean arterial pressure, and what does that combination suggest about his treatment?
*= Cardiac output and total peripheral resistance — the treatment has reduced one or both further than his compensations can offset on standing
Why: Correct. Mean arterial pressure is the product of output and resistance. Increased diuresis and vasodilatation lower filling and resistance, which relieves congestion but leaves less margin when he stands.
* Heart rate and stroke volume, so the treatment must have slowed his heart
Why: These determine output, which is one of the two terms. Leaving resistance out makes pressure look purely cardiac.
* Blood volume and vessel diameter, neither of which treatment changes
Why: Treatment changes both, and they act through output and resistance rather than directly.
* Systolic and diastolic pressure, which are averaged to give the mean
Why: Confuses how mean pressure is estimated from a trace with what determines it.
Rationale: One product, two terms, and a treatment that acts on both. The congestion and the postural dizziness are not contradictory findings — they are the same adjustment seen from two postures.
## debrief
The case holds two complaints together that sound opposite: too much fluid in the evening and too little pressure in the morning. The hydrostatic column explains where the fluid goes; the product of output and resistance explains what happens on standing; and angiotensin II explains why the body adds volume the ventricle cannot move. Treatment moves both terms of the same equation, which is why relief of one symptom can produce the other.

### Standing still, and what it costs
Mr Sameh Labib, 31, faints while standing in a long queue on a hot morning. He is well within two minutes of being laid flat. Pulse 62, blood pressure 118/74 lying.
Concept: CON-CVS-6430B0D729893E
Difficulty: Easy
Q: He had been standing motionless for twenty minutes. What does an upright posture do to the pressure in the veins of his legs?
*= It raises it, because the column of blood above them adds its own weight
Why: Correct. Venous pressure at any point depends on the height of the column of blood above it, so standing raises pressure in the dependent veins substantially.
* It lowers it, because gravity drains blood back towards the heart
Why: Reverses the direction gravity acts. Blood is pulled downwards, towards the feet, not upwards to the heart.
* It leaves it unchanged, because venous pressure is set by the right atrium alone
Why: Right atrial pressure is the reference point, but the hydrostatic column between it and the foot is exactly what changes with posture.
* It raises it only in the arteries, not the veins
Why: The hydrostatic column acts on both sides of the circulation. It is the veins, being compliant, in which the consequence shows.
Rationale: The height of the blood column is a pressure in its own right. Standing raises venous pressure in the legs, distends the compliant veins, and pools blood there — which is the first step in this faint.

### The sensor that should have corrected it
Concept: CON-CVS-A302CAFBD5028C
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Pooling reduces venous return and so arterial pressure. Which receptors detect that fall, and what do they sense?
*= Arterial baroreceptors, which sense stretch of the vessel wall in the carotid sinus and aortic arch
Why: Correct. They are stretch receptors: a fall in pressure reduces wall stretch, their discharge falls, and the reflex response follows.
* Arterial chemoreceptors, which sense the fall in oxygen delivery that follows
Why: Chemoreceptors respond to oxygen, carbon dioxide and pH, and act far more slowly than the second-by-second correction standing needs.
* Ventricular stretch receptors, which sense the reduced filling directly
Why: Cardiac receptors exist and do contribute, but the reflex that defends arterial pressure on standing is driven from the arterial baroreceptors.
* Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus, which sense the change in blood concentration
Why: Osmoreceptors govern water balance over hours. Nothing about standing changes plasma osmolality quickly.
Rationale: The baroreceptors report pressure by reporting stretch. Everything the body does in the first seconds of standing follows from their discharge falling.

### What the reflex does when it works
Concept: CON-CVS-E476EBADED4CE3
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: In a normal response to a sudden threat to perfusion, what does the cardiovascular system do?
*= Heart rate and contractility rise and the vessels constrict, defending arterial pressure and redirecting flow to the brain and heart
Why: Correct. The response is coordinated: more output and more resistance, with flow redistributed towards the organs that cannot tolerate interruption.
* Heart rate falls and the vessels dilate, to reduce the work of the heart
Why: Describes what happens in a vasovagal faint, which is the abnormal reflex that caused this patient's collapse — not the normal defence.
* Only the heart responds; the blood vessels play no part in the acute response
Why: Vasoconstriction is at least as important as the cardiac response, and in a standing adult it is the larger contributor.
* Blood is redirected preferentially to skin and muscle to prepare for movement
Why: Skin and splanchnic beds constrict in this response. Muscle flow rises only when the muscle itself is working.
Rationale: The intact response raises output, raises resistance and redistributes flow. Naming it precisely is what makes a vasovagal faint recognisable as the reflex going the wrong way.

### Where the pooled blood went
Concept: CON-CVS-37F13C09832A27
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Roughly half a litre of blood shifts into his legs on standing. Which property of the veins allows so much blood to move for so small a change in pressure?
*= Their high compliance — a thin, distensible wall accepts a large volume for a small pressure rise
Why: Correct. Compliance is volume accepted per unit pressure, and it is high in veins, which is what makes them the capacitance vessels.
* Their low compliance, which is why venous pressure stays low
Why: Inverts the definition. Low compliance means pressure rises steeply with volume, which is what arteries do.
* Their muscular wall, which actively draws blood into them
Why: Veins have some smooth muscle and can constrict, but they do not actively suck blood in; they are filled passively.
* Their valves, which trap blood in the legs on standing
Why: Valves prevent backflow and assist the muscle pump. They do not create the capacity that the pooled volume occupies.
Rationale: Compliance is why standing still is a haemodynamic event at all. The same property that makes the veins a useful reservoir is what allows half a litre to disappear into the legs when nothing is moving.

### Putting the pressure together
Concept: CON-CVS-AEBDDFAB03B5DF
Difficulty: Hard
Q: His mean arterial pressure fell despite an intact heart. Which two quantities determine mean arterial pressure, and which of them failed here?
*= Cardiac output and total peripheral resistance; output fell because venous return fell
Why: Correct. Mean arterial pressure is the product of cardiac output and total peripheral resistance. Pooling reduced venous return and so output, and the compensatory rise in resistance was not enough.
* Heart rate and stroke volume; the rate failed to rise
Why: These determine cardiac output, which is one of the two terms — not the whole equation. Leaving resistance out is what makes pressure seem to be a purely cardiac matter.
* Blood volume and vessel diameter, neither of which changed
Why: Both influence pressure, but only through output and resistance. Stated this way the relationship cannot be applied.
* Systolic and diastolic pressure, which are averaged to give the mean
Why: Confuses how mean pressure is estimated from a trace with what physiologically determines it.
Rationale: Two terms, and any fall in pressure is a fall in one of them that the other did not offset. Here output fell because the veins held the blood, and resistance could not rise far enough to compensate.
## debrief
The case is one column of blood and what it does. Standing raises venous pressure in the legs, high venous compliance lets half a litre collect there, venous return falls, and cardiac output with it. The baroreceptors report the fall and the normal response defends pressure — unless the reflex inverts, as it did here. Mean arterial pressure is the product of output and resistance, and every step in the case acts on one or the other.

### Where the blood banks up
Mrs Ilham Serry, 71, has three weeks of swelling of both ankles and a feeling of fullness in the abdomen. Her jugular venous pressure is visibly raised.
Concept: CON-CVS-26C64F6FB246BD
Difficulty: Easy
Q: Her right ventricle is failing to move the blood delivered to it. What happens upstream?
*= Blood accumulates in the systemic veins, raising venous pressure behind the failing ventricle
Why: Correct. A ventricle that cannot eject what it receives leaves the blood in the circuit feeding it, and venous pressure rises behind it.
* Blood accumulates in the pulmonary veins, causing pulmonary oedema
Why: That is the consequence of left ventricular failure. Which ventricle is failing determines which side backs up.
* Blood volume falls, because less is being circulated
Why: Total volume is unchanged; it is redistributed towards the venous side rather than lost.
* Arterial pressure rises, because the blood must go somewhere
Why: Reasons about the wrong side of the pump. Failure to eject lowers, not raises, what is delivered forward.
Rationale: A failing ventricle backs blood up into whatever feeds it. Right-sided failure raises systemic venous pressure — which is why the neck veins and the ankles report the same problem.

### The hormone that makes it worse
Concept: CON-CVS-A3B1CCD1E56CE7
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Her renin-angiotensin system is activated. What is the overall effect of angiotensin II on arterial pressure?
*= It raises it, by constricting vessels and by promoting sodium and water retention
Why: Correct. Angiotensin II raises pressure through a fast vascular route and a slower renal one, and both act in the same direction.
* It lowers it, by promoting sodium excretion
Why: Reverses the renal effect. Angiotensin II promotes retention, not excretion.
* It has no effect on pressure, acting only on thirst
Why: Thirst is one of its actions, and it does contribute — but it is far from the only one.
* It raises it only in the pulmonary circulation
Why: The systemic circulation is where its pressure effect is exerted and measured.
Rationale: Angiotensin II defends pressure, which is useful after a haemorrhage and harmful in a failing heart: the same response that would save a bleeding patient adds volume this patient cannot move.

### The faster of its two routes
Concept: CON-CVS-2886EDD0864ABC
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Which of its actions takes effect within minutes rather than hours?
*= Direct constriction of vascular smooth muscle, particularly in the arterioles
Why: Correct. The vascular action is immediate; the renal handling of sodium and water is the slower arm.
* Sodium retention in the proximal tubule
Why: A real and important action, and the one most students name first — but it acts over hours to days.
* Aldosterone-driven sodium retention in the collecting duct
Why: Slower still, since it requires aldosterone secretion and a change in gene expression.
* Stimulation of thirst and drinking
Why: Behavioural, and dependent on the patient acting on it. Not a minute-by-minute pressure mechanism.
Rationale: One hormone, two timescales. Separating them is what explains why blocking this system relieves congestion over days while changing vascular tone almost at once.

### Why the fluid she was given did not raise her pressure much
Concept: CON-CVS-245C56665C0240
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: She was given intravenous fluid before the diagnosis was clear. Her pressure rose less than expected. Which venous property accounts for that?
*= Stress relaxation — the veins widen slowly around the added volume
Why: Correct. Vascular smooth muscle relaxes when stretched, so mean systemic filling pressure, venous return, cardiac output and arterial pressure all rise less than the volume added would predict.
* Reverse stress relaxation, in which the veins narrow around the volume
Why: The same mechanism in the wrong direction: narrowing follows a volume loss, not a gain.
* Reduced venous compliance in the elderly, which limits the pressure rise
Why: Reduced compliance would produce a *larger* pressure rise for the same volume, not a smaller one.
* Immediate renal excretion of the fluid given
Why: The kidney does eventually remove it, but not within the timeframe described, and this is not a property of veins.
Rationale: The venous reservoir resizes itself. That is a safety margin when volume is added and a limitation when a clinician is relying on fluid to raise a pressure.

### Where the oedema actually forms
Concept: CON-CVS-D9759B3361EAEB
Difficulty: Hard
Q: Raised venous pressure is transmitted back to the microcirculation. Across which vessels does the fluid leave to become oedema?
*= The capillaries and postcapillary venules, where the wall is thin enough for exchange
Why: Correct. Exchange occurs across the thin-walled microvessels, and a raised downstream pressure raises capillary hydrostatic pressure, shifting the Starling balance towards filtration.
* The arterioles, because they carry the highest pressure into the tissue
Why: Arteriolar pressure is higher, which is why this is chosen, but the muscular wall permits no meaningful exchange.
* The large veins, because they are the most distended
Why: Distension is greatest there, but their thick walls exchange nothing.
* The lymphatic vessels, which fill from the plasma
Why: Lymphatics drain the interstitium rather than fill it. Their failure worsens oedema; they are not its source.
Rationale: The oedema forms where exchange happens and nowhere else. Raised venous pressure matters because it is transmitted upstream to the capillary bed, not because the large veins leak.
## debrief
One raised venous pressure explains the whole examination: the neck veins report it directly, the ankles show what it does at the capillary bed, and the abdomen shows it in the liver. Angiotensin II defends arterial pressure by two routes on two timescales and makes the congestion worse in the process. The veins widening slowly around a fluid load is why the fluid helped so little — and why it did less harm than it might have.

### What the number means
A 24-year-old man is brought in after a motorcycle collision. He is alert, cold peripherally, pulse 118, blood pressure 112/86.
Concept: CON-CVS-22A9DCDEA41021
Difficulty: Easy
Q: His systolic pressure is nearly normal. What does arterial blood pressure represent?
*= The pressure the blood exerts on the arterial wall, driven by the heart and opposed by the resistance ahead of it
Why: Correct. It is the pressure within the arterial system, produced by ejection against the resistance of the circulation, and it is not a measure of how much blood there is.
* The volume of blood in the circulation at that moment
Why: The misconception this case exists to break. Volume can fall substantially while pressure is defended.
* The force with which the heart muscle contracts
Why: Contractility contributes, but pressure is a property of the vessel contents, not a direct reading of muscle force.
* The rate of blood flow through the arteries
Why: Pressure and flow are different quantities, related through resistance. A high pressure can accompany a low flow.
Rationale: Pressure is not volume and not flow. A defended blood pressure in a bleeding patient is the compensations working, not evidence that little has been lost.

### The fluid that arrived from nowhere
Concept: CON-CVS-1E8F1FB07C77D0
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: An hour later his haemoglobin has fallen although he has been given no fluid. Why?
*= Interstitial fluid has moved into the capillaries as capillary hydrostatic pressure fell, diluting the blood
Why: Correct. Losing volume lowers capillary hydrostatic pressure, so the Starling balance shifts towards absorption and interstitial fluid enters the circulation.
* He has continued to bleed, and the haemoglobin reflects the additional loss
Why: Further bleeding lowers the volume but not the concentration: whole blood is lost with its cells, so the haemoglobin concentration is initially unchanged.
* The bone marrow has responded by releasing immature cells
Why: A marrow response takes days and would not lower the haemoglobin concentration.
* Laboratory error, since haemoglobin cannot change without fluid being given
Why: This is exactly the change to expect, and dismissing it is how an early normal haemoglobin gets used as false reassurance.
Rationale: Autotransfusion refills the circulation from the interstitium within minutes. It is also why the first haemoglobin in a bleeding patient understates the loss and the second one reveals it.

### The reservoir tightening around what is left
Concept: CON-CVS-59DE21D055FA34
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: His mean systemic filling pressure has fallen less than the volume lost alone predicts. What accounts for that?
*= Reverse stress relaxation — the veins slowly narrow around the reduced volume
Why: Correct. Venous smooth muscle takes up the slack over minutes to hours, limiting the falls in filling pressure, venous return, cardiac output and arterial pressure.
* Stress relaxation, in which the veins widen around the volume
Why: The same mechanism in the wrong direction; widening follows a volume gain.
* Arteriolar constriction, which raises the filling pressure of the system
Why: Arteriolar constriction raises resistance and defends arterial pressure, but it is downstream and does not set the filling pressure.
* Increased heart rate, which raises venous return
Why: A faster heart moves blood already returning; it does not change the pressure that drives the return.
Rationale: The capacitance vessels resize themselves in both directions. Narrowing around a smaller volume is a quiet, powerful defence, and it is part of why a young patient looks well until he suddenly does not.

### The symptom that is a sign
Concept: CON-CVS-5201719B770125
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: He asks repeatedly for a drink. How should that be interpreted?
*= As part of the volume response — thirst is driven by angiotensin II and reduced baroreceptor discharge
Why: Correct. Thirst is recruited by the same volume-loss pathways as vasoconstriction and fluid retention, and in this context it is a sign of hypovolaemia.
* As anxiety and a dry mouth after the accident
Why: Both are present and plausible, which is precisely why the sign is dismissed. It should not be explained away in a patient who is bleeding.
* As a rise in plasma sodium concentration from the blood lost
Why: Whole blood is lost isotonically, so sodium concentration does not rise.
* As a consequence of the oxygen given by the paramedics
Why: Oxygen therapy can dry the mouth, but it does not produce the drive to drink described here.
Rationale: Thirst belongs to the same response as the tachycardia and the cold peripheries. Reading it as a symptom to be relieved rather than a sign to be acted on is a recognised way of missing continuing blood loss.

### When the defences stop working
Concept: CON-CVS-1AD44A19DA47AD
Difficulty: Challenging
Q: Despite transfusion his pressure is restored but his skin stays mottled and his lactate keeps rising. What has happened, and why does the restored pressure not settle the question?
*= The microvasculature has stopped regulating, so perfusion remains inadequate even though the driving pressure is adequate
Why: Correct. In late shock the microcirculation no longer matches perfusion to demand. Pressure is the upstream driving force, not a measure of what reaches the tissue, so it can be normal while delivery is not.
* The transfused blood cannot carry oxygen, so the pressure is irrelevant
Why: Stored blood does carry oxygen. This substitutes a carriage problem for a distribution one.
* The lactate is from the injured muscle and carries no circulatory meaning
Why: Injured tissue does contribute, and this is a genuine confounder — but a rising lactate with mottled skin after resuscitation must be treated as a perfusion failure.
* Compensation is still working, and the pressure will normalise the skin shortly
Why: Compensation restoring pressure while the skin stays mottled is the finding, not the reassurance.
Rationale: Arterial pressure is what drives perfusion, not what measures it. Once the microcirculation fails to regulate, the two come apart — which is why skin, lactate and urine output are read alongside the blood pressure and not replaced by it.
## debrief
Four defences hold this patient's pressure up while his volume falls: the interstitium refilling the circulation, the veins narrowing around what remains, the arterioles raising resistance, and thirst driving him to replace it. Each buys time and each hides the loss. The last decision is the reason none of them can be trusted as an endpoint: a restored pressure is not a restored circulation.

### What sets the rhythm
Amira Hafez, 26, runs competitively. A routine insurance medical finds a resting pulse of 44. She has never blacked out and exercises without difficulty.
Concept: CON-CVS-C9E53B5A691D19
Difficulty: Easy
Q: Which property of the sinoatrial node makes it the pacemaker?
*= Automaticity and rhythmicity — it depolarises spontaneously, and regularly
Why: Correct. It reaches threshold by itself and does so at regular intervals, and it does this faster than any other pacemaker tissue, so it sets the rate.
* Conductivity — it conducts faster than the surrounding muscle
Why: Conduction spreads the impulse but does not generate it. Speed of conduction does not make a tissue a pacemaker.
* Excitability — it responds to a stimulus more readily than other tissue
Why: Excitability is responding to a stimulus. A pacemaker needs no stimulus, which is the whole point.
* Contractility — it contracts first and the rest follows
Why: Nodal tissue is specialised for electrical activity, not for force. It contributes almost nothing mechanically.
Rationale: The fastest automatic tissue sets the rate. Everything about bradycardia is a question about what has slowed that tissue, or what has replaced it.

### What slows it in an athlete
Concept: CON-CVS-A87E8F7EE8DC58
Difficulty: Easy
Q: Endurance training raises resting vagal tone. How does parasympathetic activity act on the pacemaker cells?
*= It slows their spontaneous depolarisation, so they take longer to reach threshold
Why: Correct. Vagal activity flattens the pacemaker potential, lengthening the interval between beats.
* It raises the threshold the cells must reach
Why: A plausible-sounding route to the same outcome, but the change is in the slope of depolarisation rather than the threshold.
* It blocks conduction from the node into the atrial muscle
Why: Vagal activity slows conduction at the atrioventricular node, not exit from the sinoatrial node, and that would be a different diagnosis.
* It reduces the force of atrial contraction, which slows the pulse
Why: Confuses a mechanical effect with the electrical one that sets the rate.
Rationale: A slower slope means a longer wait to threshold, and a slower heart. In a trained athlete that is a physiological adaptation, not a conduction disorder.

### What speeds it when she runs
Concept: CON-CVS-8452EED3F9CFB2
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Her rate reaches 180 during a race. What effect do circulating catecholamines have on the sinoatrial node?
*= They increase its firing rate, steepening the pacemaker depolarisation
Why: Correct. Circulating adrenaline and noradrenaline act on the node in the same direction as sympathetic nerve activity, steepening the slope so threshold is reached sooner.
* They slow it, opposing sympathetic nerve activity
Why: Inverts the effect. Circulating catecholamines and sympathetic nerves act in the same direction, not opposite ones.
* They act only on the ventricles, raising contractility without changing rate
Why: Contractility does rise, but nodal tissue responds too, which is how rate rises with exercise and emotion.
* They act only on vascular smooth muscle
Why: Their vascular effects are real, but a purely vascular action would leave the rise in heart rate unexplained.
Rationale: The same pacemaker slope is pushed both ways: vagal activity flattens it, catecholamines steepen it. An athlete's heart demonstrates both ends of the range within a single hour.

### What filling looks like at speed
Concept: CON-CVS-C2030AF413E220
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: At 180 beats per minute, what happens to the atrial contribution to ventricular filling?
*= It rises as a proportion, because the shortened diastole leaves less time for passive filling
Why: Correct. Diastole shortens disproportionately with rate, so the passive phase is curtailed and atrial contraction accounts for a larger share of a smaller total.
* It falls, because the atria have less time to contract
Why: Atrial systole occupies late diastole and is preserved; it is the long passive phase that is lost.
* It is unchanged, because the ratio of atrial to passive filling is fixed
Why: The ratio is not fixed — it is precisely what shifts with rate, which is why losing atrial contraction matters more during exercise.
* It becomes irrelevant, because filling is complete before the atrium contracts
Why: True only at rest, and only approximately. At high rates it is the atrial contribution that keeps filling adequate.
Rationale: Rate takes its time from diastole, and the atrium's share of filling rises to compensate. That is why a patient in atrial fibrillation copes at rest and struggles on exertion.

### Doing the arithmetic
Concept: CON-CVS-DB8585BDDB67E7
Difficulty: Hard
Q: At a resting rate of 75, how long is one cardiac cycle and how is it divided? Use it to explain what her rate of 44 gives her.
*= About 0.8 seconds, roughly 0.3 systole and 0.5 diastole — so at 44 the cycle is nearer 1.4 seconds, and almost all the extra time is diastole
Why: Correct. Sixty divided by the rate gives the cycle length, and systole changes comparatively little, so a slower rate lengthens diastole disproportionately — more filling time and more coronary perfusion time.
* About 0.8 seconds, split equally, so at 44 both phases lengthen equally
Why: The right cycle length, but systole is the shorter and the more fixed of the two phases; the extra time is not shared equally.
* About 1.3 seconds at 75, so at 44 the cycle is over 2 seconds
Why: Divides 100 rather than 60 by the rate — an arithmetic slip that doubles the answer.
* Cycle length cannot be calculated from rate without knowing the stroke volume
Why: Cycle length is the reciprocal of rate and needs nothing else. Stroke volume is a separate quantity.
Rationale: One division gives the cycle, and knowing which phase absorbs the change gives the physiology. A slow rate in a trained heart means a long diastole, which is more filling and better coronary perfusion — the opposite of a problem.
## debrief
A resting pulse of 44 in this athlete is the pacemaker slope being held flat by vagal tone, not a diseased node. The same slope is steepened by catecholamines when she races, and the arithmetic of the cycle explains why a slow rate serves her well: nearly all the extra time is diastole, which is when the ventricle fills and the coronary arteries are perfused.
