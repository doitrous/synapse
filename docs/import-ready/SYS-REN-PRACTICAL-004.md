### The covering
Concept: CON-REN-29ED086AF129A6
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What forms the capsule of the adrenal gland?
*= Thick dense fibrous connective tissue, covered externally by fat
Why: Correct. A dense fibrous capsule surrounds the gland, embedded in perirenal fat within the renal fascia.
* A thin serosal membrane continuous with the peritoneum
Why: The gland is retroperitoneal and has a fibrous capsule, not a serosal one.
* A layer of smooth muscle that expels secretion
Why: The adrenal is an endocrine gland releasing into the blood; it has no muscular expulsion mechanism.
* No capsule; the cortex lies directly against perirenal fat
Why: The capsule is a defining feature, and it is where the cortical stem-cell population lies.
Explanation: A dense capsule and a fatty bed, both within the renal fascia — which is why the adrenal is removed with the kidney in a radical nephrectomy unless deliberately separated.

### The three layers of the outer part
Concept: CON-REN-79B3F941F1FA97
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What are the zones of the adrenal cortex, from the capsule inwards?
*= Zona glomerulosa, zona fasciculata and zona reticularis
Why: Correct. The three cortical zones lie in that order beneath the capsule, and each has a characteristic architecture and secretory product.
* Zona fasciculata, zona glomerulosa and zona reticularis
Why: Reverses the first two; the glomerulosa is the outermost, immediately beneath the capsule.
* Zona reticularis, zona fasciculata and zona medullaris
Why: Reverses the order and invents a fourth zone; the medulla is not a cortical zone.
* Zona compacta and zona spongiosa
Why: Those terms describe endometrium, not adrenal cortex.
Explanation: Outer to inner: glomerulosa, fasciculata, reticularis. The order is worth fixing because it maps onto the order of the hormones — salt, sugar, sex.

### What lies inside them
Concept: CON-REN-3706A589FEDA42
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: How does the cellular organisation of the medulla differ from that of the cortex?
*= The cortex has three zones of secretory cells, whereas the medulla contains chromaffin cells together with sympathetic ganglion cells
Why: Correct. The medulla is not zoned; it consists of chromaffin cells with scattered sympathetic ganglion cells among them.
* The medulla also has three zones, arranged concentrically
Why: Zonation is a cortical feature; the medulla is not organised that way.
* The medulla contains only connective tissue and vessels
Why: It is a secretory tissue in its own right, and the source of circulating catecholamines.
* The medulla contains cortical cells that have migrated inwards
Why: The two have entirely different embryological origins, which is the subject of the next case.
Explanation: Zoned cortex, unzoned medulla with ganglion cells — the histological signature of two different tissues sharing one capsule.

### The stain that separates them
Concept: CON-REN-5135E0550312A3
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What does the chromaffin reaction show?
*= The medulla is chromaffin-positive and the cortex chromaffin-negative
Why: Correct. Medullary cells contain catecholamines that oxidise on exposure to chromium salts and stain brown; cortical cells do not.
* The cortex is chromaffin-positive and the medulla negative
Why: Reverses the reaction; the name chromaffin belongs to the medullary cells.
* Both are chromaffin-positive, differing only in intensity
Why: The reaction is a discriminator between the two, not a difference of degree.
* Neither is chromaffin-positive; the reaction identifies nerve fibres
Why: The reaction identifies catecholamine-containing cells.
Explanation: A single histochemical reaction separates the two tissues, which is why it named the cell type. It also identifies extra-adrenal chromaffin tissue, the site of extra-adrenal phaeochromocytoma.

### Telling the two medullary cells apart
Concept: CON-REN-6E40972A62BEB3
Difficulty: Hard
Q: The medulla contains adrenaline-secreting and noradrenaline-secreting cells. How do they differ under the electron microscope, and why does the distinction matter?
*= The adrenaline-secreting cells have less dense, more homogeneous granules, while noradrenaline-secreting granules are denser and eccentric — and the proportion matters because a tumour of either produces a different clinical picture
Why: Correct. The two populations are distinguishable by their granule appearance, and because adrenaline and noradrenaline have different receptor profiles, a predominantly noradrenaline-secreting tumour produces sustained hypertension while an adrenaline-secreting one produces more episodic symptoms with tachycardia.
* They are identical ultrastructurally and can only be distinguished chemically
Why: The granules differ in density and homogeneity, which is exactly the point being tested.
* Adrenaline-secreting cells have no granules at all
Why: Both populations store their product in granules.
* The distinction is purely academic and has no clinical correlate
Why: The secretory profile of a phaeochromocytoma determines its presentation, so the distinction is directly clinical.
Explanation: Two cell populations in one tissue, distinguishable by their granules and by what they release. That heterogeneity is why phaeochromocytomas do not all present the same way.

### Two origins
A student asks in a tutorial why the adrenal behaves as though it were two organs.
Concept: CON-REN-D14434FB35A811
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What are the embryological origins of the adrenal cortex and medulla?
*= The cortex is mesodermal and the medulla ectodermal, from neural crest
Why: Correct. The cortex arises from coelomic mesoderm, while the medulla is derived from neural crest cells that migrate in — two tissues of different origin enclosed in one capsule.
* Both are mesodermal, differing only in the time of differentiation
Why: A common origin would not explain the medulla's neuronal properties.
* The cortex is endodermal and the medulla mesodermal
Why: Endoderm gives rise to gut and its derivatives, not to the adrenal cortex.
* Both are neural crest in origin
Why: Only the medulla is neural crest; the cortex is not.
Rationale: Two origins, one capsule. Every functional difference in the case that follows is a consequence of that developmental fact.

### What the medullary cell really is
Concept: CON-REN-E96E5D3613BF68
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What are the chromaffin cells of the medulla, in developmental terms?
*= Modified sympathetic neurons that have lost their axons and become secretory
Why: Correct. They are postganglionic sympathetic neurons that secrete into the blood rather than onto a target, which is why they are innervated by preganglionic fibres directly.
* Modified cortical cells that migrated inwards during development
Why: The two tissues have separate origins; there is no such migration.
* Specialised endothelial cells lining the medullary sinusoids
Why: They are neuroendocrine cells, not endothelium.
* Macrophages that have acquired secretory granules
Why: They are of neural crest origin, not haematopoietic.
Rationale: Calling the medulla a modified sympathetic ganglion explains its preganglionic innervation, its neural crest origin, and why its secretion is part of the sympathetic response rather than a separate hormonal axis.

### What each part secretes
Concept: CON-REN-1CF1475C5DD060
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What does each part of the gland secrete?
*= The cortex secretes corticosteroids and sex hormones; the medulla secretes catecholamines
Why: Correct. Mineralocorticoid, glucocorticoid and adrenal androgens come from the three cortical zones, while adrenaline and noradrenaline come from the medulla.
* The cortex secretes catecholamines and the medulla corticosteroids
Why: Reverses the two, and would put steroid synthesis in a tissue of neural origin.
* Both secrete corticosteroids, differing only in quantity
Why: The medulla has no steroidogenic capacity.
* The cortex secretes only aldosterone; the rest comes from the medulla
Why: Each cortical zone has its own product, and cortisol and adrenal androgens are cortical too.
Rationale: Steroids from the cortex, catecholamines from the medulla. That division is why adrenal insufficiency and phaeochromocytoma are entirely different diseases of the same organ.

### How the blood reaches each
Concept: CON-REN-42009C41CA53DB
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: How does the blood supply differ between cortex and medulla?
*= The cortex receives arterial blood directly, while the medulla receives both arterial blood and blood that has already passed through the cortex
Why: Correct. Most medullary blood arrives through cortical sinusoids, so it is already rich in cortical steroids by the time it reaches the chromaffin cells.
* Both receive arterial blood only, with no shared circulation
Why: The shared portal-like flow is the important feature, and it has a functional consequence.
* The medulla receives arterial blood and supplies the cortex
Why: Reverses the direction; blood flows cortex to medulla.
* Neither has an arterial supply; both are supplied by the renal vein
Why: Veins drain rather than supply, and the adrenal has its own arterial supply from three sources.
Rationale: The medulla is bathed in cortical steroid before it secretes. That is why glucocorticoid is required for the enzyme that converts noradrenaline to adrenaline, and why the two parts are functionally coupled despite their separate origins.

### Which part can be lost
Concept: CON-REN-93248E6F5A3578
Difficulty: Hard
Q: If one part of the gland had to be lost, which is survivable, and why?
*= The medulla, because its catecholamine function is duplicated by the sympathetic nervous system — whereas loss of the cortex is fatal without hormone replacement
Why: Correct. The cortex is essential to life: without mineralocorticoid and glucocorticoid, salt wasting and circulatory collapse follow. The medulla's product is largely reproduced by sympathetic nerve endings, so bilateral medullary loss is tolerated.
* The cortex, because steroids are also produced by the gonads
Why: Gonadal steroids do not replace mineralocorticoid or glucocorticoid function.
* Neither; both are equally essential
Why: The asymmetry between them is the point, and it is why adrenalectomy requires steroid replacement but not catecholamine replacement.
* Both are dispensable, since their functions overlap with other organs
Why: Cortical loss is fatal untreated.
Rationale: One part has a backup and the other does not. That single asymmetry explains why the emergency after bilateral adrenalectomy is an adrenal crisis and never a catecholamine deficiency.
## debrief
The adrenal is two glands in one capsule: a mesodermal cortex making steroids and an ectodermal medulla of modified sympathetic neurons making catecholamines. Blood flows from one to the other, coupling them functionally. And only one of them is indispensable — which is why steroid replacement, not adrenaline, is what a patient needs after both glands are removed.

### What the medulla does at rest
Mrs Amina Zaki, 46, has episodes of severe headache, sweating and palpitation, with blood pressures recorded up to 220/120 during them and normal readings between.
Concept: CON-REN-CCABBC368078FA
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What does the adrenal medulla secrete under ordinary resting conditions?
*= Small amounts of catecholamine continuously
Why: Correct. There is a low continuous basal secretion, on which the large stress-related release is superimposed.
* Nothing at all until a stressor occurs
Why: A purely on-off gland would leave no basal contribution, and basal secretion is measurable.
* Corticosteroid, switching to catecholamine under stress
Why: The medulla has no steroidogenic capacity at any time.
* Acetylcholine, which is converted to adrenaline in the blood
Why: Acetylcholine is the preganglionic transmitter reaching the medulla, not its secretory product.
Rationale: A continuous low output with a large reserve is what allows the same gland to contribute at rest and to dominate under stress — and it is why plasma catecholamines are interpreted against the clinical state.

### What it does under stress
Concept: CON-REN-5D60B4B43BCCC3
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What does the medulla secrete during the fight-or-flight response?
*= Adrenaline and noradrenaline, in large amounts
Why: Correct. Both catecholamines are released, with adrenaline predominating in the human adrenal medulla.
* Noradrenaline only, adrenaline being confined to nerve endings
Why: The reverse: noradrenaline is the principal transmitter at sympathetic nerve endings, while the medulla is the main source of circulating adrenaline.
* Cortisol, which mediates the acute response
Why: Cortisol rises in stress but from the cortex and over a slower time course.
* Dopamine only, which is converted peripherally
Why: Dopamine is a precursor and a minor product; the principal secretions are adrenaline and noradrenaline.
Rationale: The medulla is the endocrine arm of the sympathetic system. Its episodic, massive release is exactly what produces the paroxysms this patient describes.

### Assessing her kidneys
Concept: CON-REN-31C3C4C47EF721
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Her renal function is checked. What is creatinine, and where does it come from?
*= An endogenous product of muscle creatine, produced at a fairly steady rate
Why: Correct. Creatinine is generated continuously from creatine in skeletal muscle, which is what makes its plasma level usable as an index of excretion.
* A dietary product absorbed from meat
Why: Diet contributes a little, but the dominant source is endogenous muscle turnover.
* A product of hepatic protein metabolism, like urea
Why: Urea comes from hepatic deamination; creatinine comes from muscle.
* A product of the renal tubules themselves
Why: The kidney excretes it; muscle produces it.
Rationale: A steady endogenous production is the whole basis for using creatinine at all. It is also why its level depends on muscle mass as much as on renal function.

### Calculating what the kidney cleared
Concept: CON-REN-DB9326762E58CC
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Her creatinine clearance is calculated from a timed urine collection. What is the calculation?
*= Urine concentration multiplied by urine flow rate, divided by the plasma concentration
Why: Correct. Clearance is the volume of plasma that would need to be completely cleared of the substance each minute to account for the amount excreted.
* Plasma concentration multiplied by urine flow, divided by urine concentration
Why: Inverts the ratio, and would rise as renal function fell.
* Urine concentration divided by plasma concentration
Why: Omits the flow rate, so a small concentrated volume and a large dilute one would give the same result.
* Plasma concentration multiplied by body weight
Why: That is not a clearance; it is the basis of some dosing calculations.
Rationale: Amount excreted per unit time, divided by the concentration it came from, gives a volume per unit time. Reading the formula that way makes it derivable rather than memorised.

### Why the answer is slightly too high
Concept: CON-REN-262E9A13219C74
Difficulty: Hard
Q: Her creatinine clearance is 118 mL/min, above the value inulin clearance would give. Why, and what would make the discrepancy larger?
*= Because the tubules secrete a small amount of creatinine in addition to what is filtered — and the discrepancy widens as filtration falls, since the secreted fraction becomes a larger share of the total excreted
Why: Correct. Tubular secretion adds to the filtered load, so measured clearance exceeds true filtration. As glomerular filtration declines, secretion contributes proportionally more, so creatinine clearance increasingly overestimates the true rate in advanced renal impairment.
* Because creatinine is partly reabsorbed, concentrating it in the urine
Why: Reabsorption would cause underestimation, not overestimation.
* Because the urine collection was incomplete, falsely raising the result
Why: An incomplete collection lowers the measured amount excreted and so lowers the calculated clearance.
* Because creatinine production rises during a hypertensive episode
Why: Production is comparatively steady and is not the source of this systematic error.
Rationale: Every practical marker fails one of the conditions that made inulin exact. Knowing which condition creatinine fails gives the direction of the error, and knowing why it worsens with declining function tells you when not to rely on it.
## debrief
The medulla secretes continuously at a low rate and massively under stress, which is the shape of this patient's paroxysms. The second half of the case is the routine assessment that accompanies any such presentation — and it carries its own trap, because the marker used to measure renal function is secreted as well as filtered, and increasingly so as function declines.

### The starting point for any marker
Concept: CON-REN-C5017B7B7C2F23
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What is meant by saying creatinine is freely filtered?
*= It crosses the glomerular filter without hindrance, so its concentration in the filtrate equals that in plasma
Why: Correct. Being small and not protein-bound, it passes the barrier freely, which is the first condition for a filtration marker.
* It is filtered only when plasma levels are high
Why: Free filtration is independent of concentration; the filtrate simply matches the plasma.
* It is filtered and then completely reabsorbed
Why: Complete reabsorption would leave none in the urine to measure.
* It is bound to albumin and filtered with it
Why: Protein binding would restrict filtration, which is why it disqualifies a marker.
Explanation: Free filtration is condition one; being neither reabsorbed nor secreted is condition two. Creatinine satisfies the first and very nearly the second.

### Reading the comparison
Concept: CON-REN-E4ECB5005A7A41
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A substance is found to have a clearance greater than the glomerular filtration rate. What does that reveal about its tubular handling?
*= That it is secreted into the tubule in addition to being filtered
Why: Correct. More is appearing in the urine than filtration alone can account for, so the tubule must be adding to it.
* That it is reabsorbed after filtration
Why: Reabsorption removes solute from the filtrate and lowers clearance below the filtration rate.
* That it is not filtered at all
Why: A substance neither filtered nor secreted would have a clearance of zero.
* That the filtration rate has been underestimated
Why: The comparison is the intended use of clearance rather than evidence of measurement error.
Explanation: Above the filtration rate means secretion, below means reabsorption, equal means neither. Three possibilities, one comparison — which is why clearance is the standard tool for characterising a substance's renal handling.

### The marker that is both
Concept: CON-REN-84F3AFF788329C
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What happens to para-aminohippurate at the glomerulus, and what happens to it afterwards?
*= It is freely filtered, and the fraction remaining in the peritubular blood is then almost entirely secreted by the tubule
Why: Correct. Filtration removes part of it and tubular secretion removes nearly all the remainder, which is why a single pass clears most of the plasma and its clearance approximates renal plasma flow.
* It is filtered only, and its clearance therefore equals the filtration rate
Why: If that were so it would be a filtration marker like inulin rather than a flow marker.
* It is secreted only, without being filtered at all
Why: It is freely filtered as well, and both routes contribute to its removal.
* It is reabsorbed after filtration, lowering its clearance
Why: Reabsorption would reduce its clearance below the filtration rate and destroy its usefulness as a flow marker.
Explanation: Filtered and then secreted is what makes near-complete extraction possible in a single pass. That is the entire reason one substance measures filtration and another measures flow.

### A clearance that exceeds plasma flow
Concept: CON-REN-C929E8998F6299
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Ammonia is reported to have a clearance above 650 mL/min, exceeding renal plasma flow. How can a clearance exceed the plasma flow?
*= Because ammonia is synthesised within the tubular cells and added to the urine, so its urinary appearance is not limited by delivery in the plasma
Why: Correct. Clearance calculated from urinary appearance assumes the substance arrived in the plasma. When the kidney itself manufactures and secretes it, that assumption fails and the calculated value can exceed plasma flow.
* Because the measurement is in error; no clearance can exceed plasma flow
Why: The value is real, and its impossibility under the usual assumption is what reveals renal synthesis.
* Because ammonia is filtered more than once as blood recirculates
Why: Clearance is calculated per unit time and does not double-count recirculation.
* Because ammonia is protein-bound and carried at a higher effective concentration
Why: Protein binding restricts filtration and would lower rather than raise clearance.
Explanation: A clearance above plasma flow is not an error but a signal — it means the kidney is a source rather than merely a route, which is exactly the case for the ammonia used in acid excretion.

### Why the figure is not a constant
Concept: CON-REN-C929E8998F6299
Difficulty: Challenging
Q: A patient with a metabolic acidosis is found to have a considerably higher ammonia clearance than a healthy control. What does that difference tell you, and over what timescale does it develop?
*= That renal ammonia production is regulated rather than fixed — ammoniagenesis from glutamine rises over days in acidosis to permit greater acid excretion, so the clearance reflects the acid-base state rather than a property of the molecule
Why: Correct. The kidney increases glutamine metabolism in acidosis, generating more ammonia to buffer secreted hydrogen ion and allowing more acid to be excreted. The adaptation takes days rather than minutes, which is why it distinguishes a chronic acidosis from an acute one.
* That the acidotic patient has renal impairment reducing ammonia handling
Why: Impairment reduces ammoniagenesis; the raised figure indicates an intact kidney responding appropriately.
* That the difference is an artefact of the higher urine flow in acidosis
Why: Flow appears in the clearance calculation, but the change being described is in production rather than in dilution.
* That ammonia is being filtered more efficiently because acidosis raises filtration
Why: The route is tubular synthesis and secretion, not increased filtration; filtration is not the limiting step for a substance the kidney makes itself.
Explanation: A clearance that changes with physiological state is reporting regulation, not measurement error. Renal ammoniagenesis rising over days in acidosis is the mechanism by which the kidney increases acid excretion, and it is why the response to a chronic acid load is greater than to an acute one.
