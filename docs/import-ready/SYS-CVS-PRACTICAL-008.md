### Why the heart is visible at all
Concept: CON-CVS-AD0D9E76B568F0
Difficulty: Easy
Q: On a frontal chest radiograph the heart appears as a dense central shadow. Where does it lie in relation to the sternum?
*= Behind the sternum, in the middle of the chest, with more of its bulk to the left
Why: Correct. The heart lies retrosternally, its mass projecting further to the left than to the right, which is why the silhouette is central but asymmetrical.
* Entirely within the left hemithorax, lateral to the sternum
Why: The apex points left, and that is why this is chosen, but a substantial part of the heart lies to the right of the midline behind the sternum.
* Behind the vertebral column, in the posterior mediastinum
Why: The posterior mediastinum lies behind the pericardium and contains the oesophagus and descending aorta.
* Above the sternal angle, in the superior mediastinum
Why: The great vessels occupy the superior mediastinum; the heart itself sits below that plane.
Explanation: The retrosternal position explains both the shape of the silhouette and why the sternum is the route taken to reach the heart surgically.

### Which chamber makes which border
Concept: CON-CVS-1D2C77D7421EF7
Difficulty: Easy
Q: The upper part of the right cardiac border on a frontal film is formed by which chamber?
*= The right atrium
Why: Correct. The right atrium forms the right border of the cardiac silhouette; the upper cardiac border is atrial rather than ventricular.
* The right ventricle
Why: The right ventricle lies anteriorly and forms the sternocostal surface, but it does not form the right border on a frontal projection.
* The left atrium
Why: The left atrium is the most posterior chamber and is normally hidden within the silhouette; it appears at the border only when enlarged.
* The superior vena cava throughout its length
Why: The superior vena cava contributes to the upper right mediastinal border above the heart, but the cardiac border itself is atrial.
Explanation: Each border belongs to a chamber, and reading the film is a matter of knowing which. That is what turns "the heart looks big" into a statement about which chamber has enlarged.

### The chamber against the sternum
Concept: CON-CVS-4B1B7D6AE18CC6
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A lateral film shows the anterior surface of the heart against the sternum. Which chamber forms most of it?
*= The right ventricle, which forms most of the sternocostal surface
Why: Correct. The right ventricle lies anteriorly against the sternum and costal cartilages, which is why it is the chamber most exposed in a penetrating anterior chest injury.
* The left ventricle, which is the largest chamber
Why: The left ventricle is the most muscular chamber, and its size is why this is chosen — but it lies posteriorly and to the left, forming the apex and the left border.
* The left atrium, which lies immediately behind the sternum
Why: The left atrium is the most posterior chamber, not the most anterior.
* The right atrium, which forms both the right border and the front
Why: The right atrium does form the right border, but the anterior surface is predominantly right ventricular.
Explanation: Anterior chamber, right ventricle. The same fact explains the position of the apex beat relative to the parasternal heave, and why a subxiphoid or parasternal approach reaches the right side of the heart first.

### Where the sac sits
Concept: CON-CVS-AE28ABD8CE2B0B
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A globular, symmetrically enlarged cardiac silhouette with clear lung fields is reported. Which compartment does the abnormality occupy, and what does the clear lung field suggest?
*= The middle mediastinum, and clear lungs suggest the enlargement is the pericardial sac rather than heart failure
Why: Correct. The heart and pericardium occupy the middle mediastinum; a large silhouette with clear lungs raises a pericardial effusion rather than a failing ventricle with pulmonary congestion.
* The superior mediastinum, and clear lungs exclude any cardiac cause
Why: Places the heart above its compartment, and clear lungs do not exclude cardiac disease.
* The posterior mediastinum, with the lungs displaced forward
Why: A posterior mediastinal mass has a different appearance and would not produce a globular cardiac silhouette.
* The pleural cavity, with the heart compressed by fluid around it
Why: A pleural effusion produces a meniscus at the costophrenic angle and does not enlarge the cardiac silhouette symmetrically.
Explanation: A big heart shadow with clear lungs and a big heart shadow with congested lungs point in different directions. The compartment tells you what can be enlarged there; the lung fields tell you whether the ventricle is failing.

### Why the shape changes with position
Concept: CON-CVS-10EC8DB8FAF3BF
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A film taken supine and portable shows a larger cardiac silhouette than the patient's previous erect film. What is the most important reason for caution?
*= Projection and position change the apparent size, so a supine portable film is not comparable with an erect one
Why: Correct. The shorter film distance and the anteroposterior projection both magnify the heart, so an apparently enlarged silhouette on a portable film must not be read as new cardiomegaly.
* The pericardium's anterior fixation prevents the heart moving, so any change is real
Why: The pericardium is indeed anchored anteriorly, and that limits movement — but it does not remove the magnification introduced by projection.
* Supine films underestimate heart size, so the true heart is even larger
Why: Reverses the effect: supine anteroposterior films overestimate, not underestimate.
* The difference is due to the phase of the cardiac cycle at exposure
Why: Cycle phase does change the silhouette slightly, but it is a far smaller effect than projection.
Explanation: Half of radiographic interpretation is knowing what the technique did to the image. Comparing an erect posteroanterior film with a supine portable one is the commonest way a normal heart is reported as enlarged.

### The valve behind the left sternal edge
Concept: CON-CVS-CD77400CEDD059
Difficulty: Easy
Q: Where does the pulmonary valve lie on the anterior chest wall?
*= Behind the medial end of the left third costal cartilage, at the left sternal edge
Why: Correct. The pulmonary orifice projects behind the medial end of the left third costal cartilage, the most superior and leftward of the four valve projections.
* Behind the right second costal cartilage, at the right sternal edge
Why: That is where the pulmonary valve is *listened to*, not where it lies. The confusion between projection and auscultation area is exactly what the set is about — and the second right space is the aortic area in any case.
* Behind the sternum at the level of the fourth costal cartilage
Why: Places it too low; the fourth costal cartilage level corresponds to the atrioventricular valves.
* At the apex, in the fifth left intercostal space
Why: The apex is where the mitral valve is auscultated, and is remote from the pulmonary orifice.
Explanation: All four valves lie close together behind the sternum, in an oblique line. Their projections are anatomical facts; where you place a stethoscope is a separate, haemodynamic matter.

### The valve behind the sternum on the right
Concept: CON-CVS-572235544548B5
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Where does the tricuspid valve project?
*= Behind the sternum, at about the level of the fourth and fifth costal cartilages, slightly to the right of the midline
Why: Correct. The tricuspid orifice lies behind the sternum in its lower part, projecting a little to the right of the midline.
* At the left fifth intercostal space in the midclavicular line
Why: That is the apex and the mitral auscultation area, on the opposite side of the heart from the tricuspid orifice.
* Behind the left second costal cartilage
Why: Too high and too far left; that region corresponds to the pulmonary projection.
* In the right fifth intercostal space in the midaxillary line
Why: Far too lateral; no cardiac valve projects that far from the midline.
Explanation: The tricuspid orifice sits low and just right of the midline, behind bone. That it lies behind the sternum is the practical reason it is not auscultated where it lies.

### Why the areas are elsewhere
Concept: CON-CVS-AA14CA86F39233
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: The four auscultation areas do not coincide with the four valve projections. Why are they placed where they are?
*= Sound travels in the direction of blood flow, so each area lies downstream of its valve where the chest wall is not covered by bone
Why: Correct. The areas are chosen where the sound generated at a valve is best transmitted, which is downstream in the direction of flow and away from the sternum.
* The areas mark where each valve actually lies, and the projections are theoretical
Why: Reverses which is which. The projections are the anatomical positions; the areas are chosen for transmission.
* The areas are placed by convention with no physiological basis
Why: Treats a reasoned arrangement as arbitrary, which makes it far harder to remember and impossible to reconstruct.
* The areas lie over the thinnest parts of the chest wall, regardless of the valve
Why: Chest-wall thickness matters, but it does not explain why each area lies specifically downstream of its own valve.
Explanation: Anatomy tells you where the valve is; haemodynamics tells you where to listen. Holding both, and knowing they differ, is what prevents a student from placing the stethoscope over the sternum and hearing nothing useful.

### The valve you listen for at the apex
Concept: CON-CVS-A6FBEEAD8427BD
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Where does the mitral valve project, and where is it auscultated?
*= It projects behind the left half of the sternum at the fourth costal cartilage, and is auscultated at the apex
Why: Correct. The projection is retrosternal on the left; the auscultation area is at the apex, downstream in the direction of flow into the left ventricle.
* It projects at the apex and is auscultated there
Why: Collapses the projection into the auscultation area, which is precisely the error this set exists to correct.
* It projects behind the right second costal cartilage and is auscultated at the apex
Why: The right second space is the aortic area, and no left-sided atrioventricular valve projects there.
* It projects at the left sternal edge and is auscultated in the axilla
Why: Radiation to the axilla is a real feature of mitral regurgitation, which is why the axilla is chosen — but the auscultation area itself is the apex.
Explanation: One valve, two locations, for two different reasons. The pairing is worth learning as a pair, because an examiner will ask for either and the answers are not interchangeable.

### Reading the aortic root
Concept: CON-CVS-7539FD9FAC1751
Difficulty: Hard
Q: On a cross-sectional image at the level of the aortic root, the aortic valve is seen centrally with the other three valves around it. What does that central position reflect?
*= The aortic orifice lies at the centre of the heart, wedged between the other three, which is why it has relations with all of them
Why: Correct. The aortic root sits centrally, in contact with the mitral valve behind, the tricuspid to its right and the pulmonary valve anteriorly and to the left — which is why disease of the root can affect the others.
* The aortic valve has been displaced centrally by the dilated root in this patient
Why: Reads a constant anatomical relationship as pathology.
* The imaging plane is oblique, and the true position is anterior and to the left
Why: An oblique plane is a real pitfall, but the central position of the aortic root is genuine rather than an artefact.
* The aortic valve lies centrally only in this projection; anatomically it is the most posterior valve
Why: The most posterior structure is the left atrium; the aortic root is central, not posterior.
Explanation: The central, wedged position of the aortic root is what makes the aortic valve the reference point on cross-sectional imaging, and what explains why root pathology so readily involves neighbouring structures.

### Where the catheter sits
Concept: CON-CVS-8424E2DDED2365
Difficulty: Easy
Q: The operator engages the catheter in the vessel of origin of the left coronary artery. Which vessel is that, and what else arises from it?
*= The ascending aorta, whose only branches are the two coronary arteries
Why: Correct. The coronary arteries are the sole branches of the ascending aorta, arising from its sinuses.
* The aortic arch, which also gives the brachiocephalic trunk
Why: The arch supplies head and upper limb. Engaging a catheter there would not reach a coronary ostium.
* The left ventricle, directly from the outflow tract
Why: The coronaries arise beyond the valve, from the aorta, not from the ventricular cavity.
* The pulmonary trunk, which lies immediately adjacent
Why: Adjacency is why this is chosen, and an anomalous coronary from the pulmonary trunk is a recognised rare lesion — but it is not the normal origin.
Explanation: Two ostia, one vessel. Everything about coronary catheterisation follows from the coronaries arising in the aortic sinuses just above the valve.

### The vessel in the anterior groove
Concept: CON-CVS-7FEAA20AB5CBD0
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A vessel is seen descending towards the apex in the anterior interventricular groove. Which is it, and what does it supply?
*= The anterior interventricular artery, supplying the anterior wall and most of the interventricular septum
Why: Correct. It runs in the anterior interventricular groove towards the apex, supplying the anterior wall and the anterior two-thirds of the septum.
* The circumflex artery, which also arises from the left coronary artery
Why: The circumflex is the other division of the left coronary artery, but it runs in the coronary sulcus, not the interventricular groove.
* The great cardiac vein, which accompanies it in the same groove
Why: The vein does share the groove, which is why it is chosen — but a vessel filling with injected contrast from an arterial catheter is the artery.
* The posterior interventricular artery, seen through the heart on this projection
Why: Structures are not seen through the heart on angiography; contrast fills only the vessel injected.
Explanation: Groove first, then vessel, then territory. The anterior interventricular groove carries the artery whose occlusion produces the anterior infarcts seen in leads V1 to V4.

### The vessel that turns the corner
Concept: CON-CVS-63D0C4219C40EA
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A second vessel from the same injection passes leftwards in the coronary sulcus and disappears round the left border of the heart. Which is it?
*= The circumflex artery, running in the coronary sulcus towards the posterior surface
Why: Correct. The circumflex runs in the anterior and then posterior parts of the coronary sulcus, curving around the left border.
* The right coronary artery, which runs in the same sulcus
Why: The right coronary artery does occupy the coronary sulcus, but on the right side, and would not fill from a left coronary injection.
* The anterior interventricular artery, angled by the projection
Why: The interventricular groove runs towards the apex, not around the border; no projection converts one into the other.
* The coronary sinus, which follows the same course posteriorly
Why: The sinus does share the posterior sulcus, and this is a genuinely close spatial call — but it is a vein and will not fill from an arterial injection.
Explanation: The two divisions of the left coronary artery take the two grooves: interventricular towards the apex, coronary sulcus around the border. Identifying which groove a vessel occupies names it without any need to recognise its shape.

### Who supplies the back
Concept: CON-CVS-69303DBED9D05C
Difficulty: Hard
Q: The right coronary injection is seen to supply the posterior interventricular artery. What does this establish?
*= A right-dominant circulation, in which the right coronary artery supplies the posterior part of the heart
Why: Correct. Dominance is defined by which artery gives the posterior interventricular branch, and this is the commonest pattern.
* A left-dominant circulation, since the posterior vessel is a branch of the left system
Why: Reverses the finding. The posterior interventricular artery is arising from the right coronary artery here.
* A balanced circulation, in which both arteries contribute
Why: A balanced pattern is one in which neither dominates; here the right has clearly supplied the posterior vessel.
* Nothing about dominance, which can only be assessed on cross-sectional imaging
Why: Dominance is conventionally established at angiography, which is exactly what this injection has done.
Explanation: Dominance is a single question with a single test: which artery gives the posterior interventricular branch. Answering it is what allows an inferior infarct to be attributed to a vessel.

### The vessel along the acute margin
Concept: CON-CVS-44B74F73B50D20
Difficulty: Hard
Q: A branch of the right coronary artery runs along the acute margin of the heart towards the apex. Which is it, and why does it matter in a patient with an inferior infarct and hypotension?
*= The right marginal artery, supplying the right ventricle — right ventricular involvement explains hypotension in an inferior infarct
Why: Correct. The right marginal branch supplies the right ventricular free wall along the acute margin, and right ventricular infarction is a recognised cause of hypotension complicating an inferior infarct.
* The posterior interventricular artery, supplying the inferior septum
Why: That vessel runs in the posterior interventricular groove, not along the acute margin.
* The circumflex artery, reaching the margin from the left
Why: The circumflex travels around the obtuse margin on the left, not the acute margin on the right.
* The small cardiac vein, which follows the same course
Why: The small cardiac vein does accompany the right coronary artery along the acute margin, which makes this the best of the wrong answers — but a vessel filling from an arterial injection is the artery.
Explanation: The clinical consequence sits directly on the anatomy: the branch that runs along the acute margin supplies the chamber whose failure explains the blood pressure. That is why a hypotensive inferior infarct prompts a right-sided ECG.

### The general rule
Concept: CON-CVS-41752651BA015F
Difficulty: Easy
Q: On a venous-phase study of the heart, what is the usual relationship between the cardiac veins and the coronary arteries?
*= The veins usually accompany the arteries, sharing the same grooves
Why: Correct. Cardiac veins generally run alongside an artery in the same groove, which is why identifying the groove identifies both.
* The veins run independently, crossing the arteries at right angles
Why: Would make the venous anatomy unlearnable and does not describe the heart.
* The veins run only on the posterior surface, and the arteries only anteriorly
Why: Both arteries and veins are found on both surfaces; the grooves run all the way round.
* The veins lie within the myocardium rather than on its surface
Why: Small venous channels do run within the muscle, but the named cardiac veins run on the surface in the grooves.
Explanation: Artery and vein together in a groove is the pattern. It makes the venous anatomy an extension of the arterial anatomy rather than a second set of facts.

### The vein along the right border
Concept: CON-CVS-BC694980C8B41C
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Which vein accompanies the right coronary artery, and where does it run?
*= The small cardiac vein, running with the right coronary artery in the right coronary sulcus and along the acute margin
Why: Correct. The small cardiac vein accompanies the right coronary artery, following the acute margin and the right part of the coronary sulcus.
* The great cardiac vein, which accompanies the right coronary artery posteriorly
Why: The great cardiac vein accompanies the anterior interventricular artery and then the circumflex, on the left side.
* The middle cardiac vein, which runs along the acute margin
Why: The middle cardiac vein runs in the posterior interventricular groove with the posterior interventricular artery, not along the margin.
* The anterior cardiac veins, which accompany the right coronary artery to the coronary sinus
Why: The anterior cardiac veins are precisely the ones that do *not* follow this rule, and do not drain to the sinus — which the next question addresses.
Explanation: Each named vein is identified by the artery it accompanies and the groove they share. The small cardiac vein belongs to the right coronary artery on the acute margin.

### The exception
Concept: CON-CVS-1470BCE2FE4215
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Which cardiac veins do not follow the usual pattern, and where do they drain?
*= The anterior cardiac veins, which cross the anterior surface of the right ventricle and open directly into the right atrium
Why: Correct. They do not accompany a named artery to the coronary sinus; they cross the anterior right ventricular surface and drain straight into the right atrium.
* The middle cardiac vein, which drains directly into the right ventricle
Why: The middle cardiac vein drains conventionally into the coronary sinus.
* The great cardiac vein, which drains into the superior vena cava
Why: The great cardiac vein becomes continuous with the coronary sinus; nothing from the heart's own drainage joins the superior vena cava.
* The small cardiac vein, which drains into the left atrium
Why: Would return venous blood into the systemic circulation, and the small cardiac vein drains to the coronary sinus in any case.
Explanation: The anterior cardiac veins are the exception worth carrying: a surgeon opening the anterior surface of the right ventricle meets veins that will not be found where the accompanying-vein rule predicts.

### Where nearly all of it collects
Concept: CON-CVS-480B355B4BFDA0
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: The coronary sinus is followed to its termination. Where does it open?
*= Into the right atrium, between the inferior vena caval opening and the atrioventricular orifice
Why: Correct. The coronary sinus opens into the right atrium in that position, usually with a rudimentary valve at its mouth.
* Into the right ventricle, below the tricuspid orifice
Why: Misplaces the opening by a chamber and would bypass the atrium entirely.
* Into the inferior vena cava, just before it reaches the heart
Why: The sinus opens beside the caval orifice, not into it, and the distinction matters when a catheter is being placed.
* Into the left atrium, alongside the pulmonary veins
Why: Would deliver deoxygenated blood into the systemic circulation. The error usually comes from associating the sinus with the left-sided circumflex artery it runs beside.
Explanation: The coronary sinus opening is a landmark inside the right atrium, and its position between the caval opening and the atrioventricular orifice is what allows it to be found deliberately during cannulation.

### Putting the venous map together
Concept: CON-CVS-7CD4CEA27DF356
Difficulty: Challenging
Q: On the posterior surface, a large venous channel is seen running in the coronary sulcus alongside an artery. Identify both, and explain how you would distinguish this vein from the great cardiac vein anteriorly.
*= The coronary sinus alongside the circumflex artery; the great cardiac vein is the same channel followed anteriorly in the interventricular groove, becoming the sinus as it passes into the posterior sulcus
Why: Correct. The great cardiac vein ascends the anterior interventricular groove, turns into the coronary sulcus and continues posteriorly as the coronary sinus, running there beside the circumflex artery.
* The middle cardiac vein alongside the posterior interventricular artery, which is a different groove entirely
Why: A correct pairing, but in the interventricular groove rather than the coronary sulcus described in the question.
* The small cardiac vein alongside the circumflex artery, distinguished by its smaller calibre
Why: The small cardiac vein accompanies the right coronary artery, not the circumflex.
* Two unrelated vessels; the great cardiac vein and coronary sinus have no continuity
Why: Removes the continuity that is the answer, and makes the venous anatomy far harder than it is.
Explanation: One channel, two names, according to which groove it is in. Recognising the great cardiac vein and the coronary sinus as a single continuous route around the heart is what makes the whole venous map a single structure rather than a list.
