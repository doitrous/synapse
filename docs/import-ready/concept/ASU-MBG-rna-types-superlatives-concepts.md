## definition
The three main RNA species differ from one another in several superlative facts commonly tested together. __Of the major RNA types, transfer RNA (tRNA) carries by far the highest percentage of chemically modified nucleotides.__ Bases such as **dihydrouridine**, **pseudouridine**, **inosine** and **ribothymidine** are added post-transcriptionally, concentrated in tRNA's D-arm, anticodon arm and TψC arm, supporting its compact cloverleaf fold and precise codon reading.

__Messenger RNA (mRNA) is the most heterogeneous RNA species in the cell__ — its unprocessed nuclear precursor was historically named **heterogeneous nuclear RNA (hnRNA)** for exactly this reason. Every protein-coding gene produces its own uniquely sequenced and uniquely sized transcript, unlike the comparatively few, highly conserved rRNA and tRNA sequences.

A human cell needs __at least roughly 20 distinct tRNA species__, one dedicated (via its own aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase) to charging each of the 20 standard amino acids. This holds even though the code's 61 sense codons and **wobble** pairing let a single tRNA often recognise more than one codon.

Of the RNA species routinely listed in an introductory course — rRNA, tRNA, snRNA and lncRNA — __only mRNA is translated into protein__. The rest are **non-coding RNA**: they act directly as RNA molecules (structural, catalytic, splicing or regulatory) rather than as a template read out into an amino acid sequence.

## explicit_objective
State that tRNA has the highest percentage of modified nucleotides among RNA types, that mRNA is the most heterogeneous ("hnRNA") RNA species, that a human cell needs at least ~20 tRNA species (one per amino acid), and that mRNA is the one coding RNA among rRNA/tRNA/snRNA/lncRNA.

## pitfalls
Confusing "most heterogeneous" (greatest sequence diversity, i.e. mRNA) with "most abundant" (rRNA, by mass) or "most modified" (tRNA, by modified-nucleotide percentage) — three different superlatives about three different RNA types, routinely built as distractors for one another. Also assuming the "except" framing of the coding/non-coding question is testing whether tRNA is non-coding (it is, uncontroversially) rather than testing whether the student can correctly spot mRNA as the single coding RNA in the list.

## definition
RNA's ribose sugar carries a **2'-hydroxyl (2'-OH)** group that DNA's deoxyribose lacks. Under alkaline conditions this 2'-OH attacks the adjacent phosphodiester bond intramolecularly, cleaving the backbone — __which is why RNA is alkali-labile while DNA, lacking this group, resists alkaline hydrolysis.__

RNA also differs from DNA in being generally **shorter** and **single-stranded**. A single RNA strand still routinely folds back on itself to form local intra-strand (secondary-structure) hydrogen bonds, exactly as in tRNA's cloverleaf and rRNA's stem-loops.

Its pyrimidine that pairs with adenine is **uracil** in place of DNA's thymine.

## explicit_objective
State that RNA, not DNA, is alkali-labile because of its ribose 2'-OH group, and list this alongside RNA's other standard distinguishing properties versus DNA: shorter length, single-strandedness with intra-strand base pairing, and uracil instead of thymine.

## pitfalls
Assuming "single-stranded" means RNA cannot have any base pairing at all — a single RNA strand still folds back on itself to hydrogen-bond intra-strand (secondary structure), which is exactly how tRNA's cloverleaf and rRNA's functional shapes form; single-stranded refers to there being one covalent backbone, not the absence of hydrogen bonding.
