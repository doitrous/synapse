## label
The perichondrium has an outer fibrous layer and an inner chondrogenic layer, and only the outer one is vascular

## canonical_key
perichondrium.structure.two-layers-and-vascularity

## aliases
Perichondrium
Fibrous and chondrogenic layers of the perichondrium
Chondrogenic layer

## arabic_label
السمحاق الغضروفي

## arabic_aliases
الطبقة الليفية والطبقة المولدة للغضروف

## definition
The perichondrium is the dense connective-tissue sheath that covers hyaline and elastic cartilage (fibrocartilage has none). It has two layers: an outer fibrous layer of dense irregular connective tissue carrying the blood vessels and nerves that supply the cartilage by diffusion, and an inner, more cellular chondrogenic layer whose flattened cells can differentiate into chondroblasts for appositional growth. Only the outer fibrous layer is vascular; the cartilage matrix itself, and the chondrogenic layer immediately next to it, are avascular.

## explicit_objective
State the perichondrium's two layers and identify which one is vascular and which one supplies new chondroblasts.

## pitfalls
Treating the perichondrium as a single uniform layer, or assuming the inner chondrogenic layer is the vascular one because it sits closer to the (avascular) cartilage — vascularity belongs to the outer fibrous layer only.

## label
Cartilage grows both from within, by chondrocytes dividing inside the matrix, and by apposition from the perichondrium's chondrogenic layer

## canonical_key
cartilage.growth.interstitial-and-appositional

## aliases
Interstitial growth of cartilage
Appositional growth of cartilage
Chondrogenic cells

## arabic_label
نمو الغضروف

## arabic_aliases
النمو الخلالي والنمو بالإضافة

## definition
Cartilage enlarges by two mechanisms that can occur together. Interstitial growth happens from within: chondrocytes already embedded in lacunae divide mitotically and lay down new matrix around themselves, pushing existing matrix apart — this is how young, more pliable cartilage (e.g. at an epiphyseal plate) grows in length. Appositional growth happens from the outside: chondrogenic cells in the perichondrium's inner layer differentiate into chondroblasts, which lay down new matrix on the existing surface, adding to the cartilage's girth. Appositional growth is the only route available once a cartilage is surrounded by a perichondrium that has lost much of its chondrogenic potential.

## explicit_objective
Distinguish interstitial from appositional cartilage growth by the cell of origin and the direction growth proceeds.

## pitfalls
Assuming all cartilage growth happens the same way, or crediting the perichondrium's outer fibrous layer (rather than its inner chondrogenic layer) with producing new chondroblasts.

## id
CON-MSK-B701B1C61D6E0D

## label
Cartilage has no blood vessels of its own, so its healing capacity is poor and worsens with age

## canonical_key
cartilage.avascularity.healing-capacity

## aliases
Cartilage avascularity
Poor cartilage healing
Nasal septum cartilage repair

## arabic_label
عدم تروية الغضروف بالدم

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Cartilage is avascular, aneural and alymphatic: it is nourished entirely by diffusion of nutrients and gases from the vessels of the perichondrium (or, at a joint, from the synovial fluid) through the matrix to the chondrocytes. Because there is no direct blood supply to bring in an inflammatory and reparative response, a cartilage injury heals slowly and incompletely, usually by fibrous (rather than hyaline) tissue, and this capacity declines further with age as the perichondrium's chondrogenic potential falls. A structure such as the nasal septum, which depends on its perichondrium for any repair at all, heals particularly poorly once that supply is disrupted.

## explicit_objective
Explain why a cartilage injury heals poorly, from its avascularity and its dependence on the perichondrium.

## pitfalls
Attributing poor cartilage healing to the chondrocytes being incapable of division (they are, in interstitial growth) rather than to the matrix being avascular, which is the real limit on a reparative response reaching the injury.

## definition
The periosteum is the connective-tissue covering of bone everywhere except at joint surfaces. It has two layers: an outer fibrous layer of dense connective tissue that carries the blood vessels supplying the bone and is anchored into the underlying bone by Sharpey's (perforating) fibres, and an inner, more cellular osteogenic layer containing osteoprogenitor cells that can differentiate into osteoblasts for appositional bone growth and fracture repair. The vascular supply belongs to the outer fibrous layer; the inner layer is the reservoir of bone-forming cells.

## explicit_objective
State the periosteum's two layers and identify which one is vascular and which one supplies new osteoblasts.

## pitfalls
Confusing the periosteum's two layers with the endosteum (which lines internal bone surfaces, not the external one), or assuming the inner osteogenic layer is the vascular layer because it is the more cellular one.

## label
Volkmann's canals run transversely to connect Haversian canals, the periosteum and the marrow cavity, and they alone carry no concentric lamellae

## canonical_key
bone.canals.volkmann-versus-haversian

## aliases
Volkmann's canals
Perforating canals of bone
Haversian canal versus Volkmann's canal

## arabic_label
قنوات فولكمان

## arabic_aliases
القنوات المخترقة للعظم المدمج

## definition
A Haversian (central) canal runs along the long axis of an osteon and is surrounded by concentric bony lamellae. Volkmann's (perforating) canals run obliquely or transversely, at right angles to the Haversian canals, connecting them to each other, to the periosteum, and to the marrow cavity; both carry blood vessels and nerves, but only the Haversian canal is surrounded by its own ring of concentric lamellae — a Volkmann's canal is not, which is how the two are told apart on a transverse section.

## explicit_objective
Distinguish a Volkmann's canal from a Haversian canal by its course and by the absence of its own concentric lamellae.

## pitfalls
Assuming any vascular canal seen in compact bone must be a Haversian canal — a canal without a surrounding ring of concentric lamellae, running across rather than along the bone's long axis, is a Volkmann's canal.

## label
Cancellous bone is a lattice of trabeculae with no Haversian systems, nourished by diffusion from the marrow rather than by its own blood vessels

## canonical_key
bone.cancellous.structure-and-nutrition

## aliases
Cancellous bone

Trabeculae of cancellous bone
Diploe

## arabic_label
العظم الإسفنجي

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Cancellous (spongy) bone forms an irregular lattice of anastomosing bony trabeculae with marrow-filled spaces between them, found at the ends of long bones and as the diploe between the two tables of a flat bone. Its trabeculae are built of parallel lamellae but, unlike compact bone, do not organise into Haversian systems: no trabecula is thick enough to need its own internal blood supply, so its osteocytes are nourished by diffusion from the vessels of the adjacent marrow rather than from vessels running through the bone itself.

## explicit_objective
Explain why cancellous bone has no Haversian systems and how its osteocytes are nourished instead.

## pitfalls
Expecting cancellous bone to show Haversian canals on section because compact bone does — its trabeculae are too thin to need one, and it is nourished by diffusion from the marrow instead.

Compact bone is told from cancellous bone by its organised Haversian systems and few, small marrow spaces, against cancellous bone's irregular trabeculae and open marrow spaces

## canonical_key

## arabic_label

## definition
On section, compact bone is dense, with few and small marrow spaces, and its lamellae are organised into Haversian systems (osteons) each with a central canal, plus interstitial lamellae between osteons and circumferential lamellae at its outer and inner surfaces. Cancellous bone is a loose, irregular lattice of trabeculae with wide, marrow-filled spaces between them and no Haversian systems. Both are built of the same lamellar bone matrix and the same cell types; the difference is architectural, not compositional.

## explicit_objective
List the histological features that separate compact from cancellous bone on a section.

## pitfalls
Treating compact and cancellous bone as different tissues rather than different architectural arrangements of the same lamellar bone matrix and the same cells.

This difference matters clinically: after peripheral nerve injury, surviving Schwann cells proliferate and form a guiding column (bands of Büngner) that supports axon regeneration, whereas the central nervous system's oligodendrocytes do not support comparable regeneration, which is a major reason CNS axon injury recovers so poorly.

## explicit_objective
Contrast a Schwann cell with an oligodendrocyte by how many axons each myelinates and by their regenerative role after injury.

## pitfalls
Assuming any myelinating glial cell relates to its axon(s) the same way a Schwann cell does — an oligodendrocyte myelinates internodes on several different axons at once, and, unlike a Schwann cell, does not support axon regeneration after injury.

## definition
When a peripheral axon is cut, the segment distal to the injury (cut off from the cell body) undergoes Wallerian degeneration: the axon and its myelin fragment and are cleared by Schwann cells and recruited macrophages over the following days. The Schwann cells along the distal stump then proliferate and align into a column of cells (bands of Büngner) inside the persisting basal lamina tube. Meanwhile the axon's cell body undergoes chromatolysis (dispersal of Nissl substance) as it switches to a growth programme, and the proximal stump sprouts a growth cone that, if it can find the Schwann-cell column, regenerates along it back toward the original target at a few millimetres a day.

## explicit_objective

## pitfalls
Treating degeneration and regeneration as if they only happen at the site of injury — Wallerian degeneration affects the whole distal stump, and it is the Schwann cells along that entire distal stump, not only at the cut, that form the guiding column for the regrowing axon.

## definition
