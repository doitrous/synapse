## title
History: breathlessness in a 64-year-old smoker
## subject

## candidate_instructions
Mr Sabry Mahmoud is 64 and has come to the chest clinic with breathlessness that has worsened over two years. Take a focused history and present your differential diagnosis to the examiner. You have eight minutes.
## actor_opening
I can't keep up with my grandson any more, doctor. I have to stop halfway up the stairs.
## actor_sections
Who you are: Sabry Mahmoud, 64, a retired bus driver.
When asked about your breathing: Say you are short of breath most days now, and it has crept up over about two years rather than starting suddenly.
When asked what you can still do: Say you can walk on the flat for about a hundred metres before you have to stop, and you cannot manage one flight of stairs without stopping.
When asked what it was like before: Say two years ago you could walk to the mosque and back, about a kilometre, without stopping.
When asked about wheeze: Say yes, there is a whistling sound, mostly when you breathe out, and it is worse in the mornings.
When asked about cough: Say you cough most mornings and have done for years. You bring up a small amount of clear or white phlegm.
When asked about the phlegm: Say it is usually white. Twice this winter it turned yellow-green and you felt feverish.
When asked about blood in the phlegm: Say no, never.
When asked about chest pain: Say no chest pain, only tightness when you are very short of breath.
When asked about weight: Say your weight is steady. You have not lost weight.
When asked about smoking: Say you started at 16 and smoked about 20 a day until two years ago, when you cut down to 10.
When asked about nights: Say you sometimes wake coughing, but you sleep on one pillow and do not wake gasping.
When asked about ankle swelling: Say no swelling.
When asked about occupation: Say you drove a bus for 30 years. No dust or chemical exposure.
When asked about previous treatment: Say you were given a blue inhaler two years ago. It helps a little for about an hour.
When asked how you use the inhaler: Say you press it and breathe in at the same time, as best you can. Nobody has ever watched you do it.
When asked about infections: Say you have had two chest infections this winter needing antibiotics.
When asked about family history: Say your father had a bad chest too, and also smoked.
Ideas, concerns and expectations: Say you are worried it is cancer, because a friend who smoked died of it. You want to know whether you will end up on oxygen.
## actor_flags
Speak in short sentences and pause for breath every few words, but do not appear distressed at rest.
Do not volunteer the smoking history, the inhaler or the two infections unless asked directly.
If the candidate asks an open question, describe the stairs and the grandson first, and then stop and wait.
If the candidate uses the words COPD, emphysema or spirometry without explaining them, ask what that means.
Near the end, ask directly whether this is cancer.
Do not use the words wheeze, sputum, exertional or dyspnoea. Say whistling, phlegm and short of breath.
## mark_scheme
Opening and structure (5): Introduces self, confirms patient identity and gains consent
Opening and structure (5): Begins with an open question and allows the patient to speak without interruption
Characterising the breathlessness (10): Establishes the onset and the two-year time course
Characterising the breathlessness (10): Quantifies exercise tolerance now and previously in the same terms
Characterising the breathlessness (10): Asks about diurnal variation and about waking at night
Associated respiratory symptoms (8): Asks about wheeze and when in the breathing cycle it occurs
Associated respiratory symptoms (8): Asks about cough, and about the volume and colour of sputum
Associated respiratory symptoms (8): Asks specifically about haemoptysis
Red flags and differential (7): Asks about weight loss and about chest pain
Red flags and differential (7): Asks about orthopnoea and ankle swelling to consider a cardiac cause
Risk and exposure (5): Quantifies smoking in pack-years and asks about occupational exposure
Treatment and its use (3): Establishes current inhaler use and asks the patient to describe the technique
Closing (2): Summarises accurately, addresses the concern about cancer honestly, and checks understanding

Miss Rana Adel is 22 and has come to the emergency department with wheeze and chest tightness that began this evening. Take a focused history and tell the examiner what you would do next. You have eight minutes.
## actor_opening
My chest went tight after I got to my cousin's flat, and I can't get a proper breath in.
## actor_sections
Who you are: Rana Adel, 22, a student.
When asked about your breathing: Say your chest feels tight, like a band, and you cannot get a full breath. It came on over about an hour.
When asked about wheeze: Say yes, there is a whistling when you breathe out.
When asked what started it: Say it began about an hour after arriving at your cousin's flat. She has two cats.
When asked whether this has happened before: Say yes, three or four times a year, usually around animals or in the spring.
When asked about previous treatment: Say you have a blue inhaler you use when it happens, and a brown one you are supposed to take every day.
When asked how often you use the blue inhaler: Say most days at the moment, sometimes three or four times a day.
When asked about the brown inhaler: Say you stopped it about two months ago because you felt well and forgot to reorder it.
When asked about nights: Say you have woken coughing two or three nights a week for the last month.
When asked about exercise: Say you get tight running for the bus, which is new this month.
When asked about hospital admissions: Say you came to hospital once at 17 and were kept overnight. You have never been in intensive care and never been ventilated.
When asked about steroid tablets: Say you had a course of tablets last spring.
When asked about hay fever or eczema: Say yes, hay fever every spring, and eczema as a child.
When asked about family history: Say your mother and younger brother both have asthma.
When asked about smoking: Say you have never smoked, but your cousin smokes indoors.
When asked about a peak flow reading: Say your best reading at home was 420, and today you have not measured it.
When asked whether you can speak in full sentences: Speak in short phrases and take a breath halfway through a sentence.
Ideas, concerns and expectations: Say you are frightened because it feels worse than the previous attacks, and you want to know whether you have to stay in.
## actor_flags
Speak in short phrases, pausing to breathe mid-sentence. Sit upright and lean slightly forward.
Do not volunteer the stopped preventer inhaler, the night waking or the increased reliever use unless asked.
If asked to take a deep breath in to speak, do so with visible effort.
If the candidate does not ask about previous admissions or steroid courses, do not offer them.
Ask near the end whether you will have to stay in hospital.
Do not use the words exacerbation, preventer or reliever. Say the blue one and the brown one.
## mark_scheme
Opening and structure (4): Introduces self, confirms identity and gains consent
Opening and structure (4): Recognises the patient is breathless and adapts the pace of questioning
Characterising the attack (10): Establishes onset, duration and speed of progression
Characterising the attack (10): Identifies the trigger and asks about previous triggers
Characterising the attack (10): Asks about wheeze, chest tightness and cough
Assessing control before today (10): Asks about night waking and about exercise symptoms
Assessing control before today (10): Establishes reliever frequency and whether the preventer is being taken
Assessing severity and risk (10): Asks about previous admissions, intensive care and ventilation
Assessing severity and risk (10): Asks about previous courses of oral steroids
Assessing severity and risk (10): Notes the ability to complete sentences as a marker of severity
Background (4): Asks about atopy — hay fever and eczema — and family history
Closing (2): Summarises, answers the question about admission honestly, and checks understanding

## actor_opening
There was blood in what I coughed up on Tuesday, and again yesterday.
## actor_sections
Who you are: Gamal Wahba, 58, a carpenter.
When asked about the blood: Say it was streaks of bright red blood mixed through the phlegm, about a teaspoon each time, on two occasions in the last week.
When asked whether it was vomited or coughed: Say it definitely came up with a cough, not from your stomach.
When asked about the phlegm itself: Say you have had a cough for about three months, bringing up a small amount most days.
When asked about weight: Say you have lost weight — your trousers are loose, and you think about six or seven kilograms over three months.
When asked about appetite: Say your appetite is poor.
When asked about night sweats: Say yes, you have woken with the sheets damp several times over the last month.
When asked about fever: Say you have felt hot on and off but have not measured a temperature.
When asked about breathlessness: Say you get more short of breath than you used to, but you can still do most of your work.
When asked about chest pain: Say there is a dull ache on the right side of your chest, present most of the time for the last few weeks.
When asked about hoarseness: Say your voice has been hoarse for about a month.
When asked about smoking: Say you have smoked 30 a day since you were 18.
When asked about tuberculosis contact: Say your brother-in-law was treated for tuberculosis two years ago and lives in the same building.
When asked about travel: Say you have not travelled outside Egypt.
When asked about occupation: Say you are a carpenter and work with wood dust. No asbestos that you know of.
When asked about previous illness: Say you have never had a chest problem before this.
When asked about anticoagulants: Say you take no regular medicines.
Ideas, concerns and expectations: Say your wife thinks it is tuberculosis because of your brother-in-law, but you are frightened it is cancer. You want to know how quickly you will find out.
## actor_flags
Look well but tired. Speak normally; you are not breathless at rest.
Volunteer the blood and the cough. Do not volunteer the weight loss, night sweats, hoarseness or the tuberculosis contact unless asked.
If asked an open question, describe the two episodes of blood and then stop.
If the candidate asks about tuberculosis contact, answer fully and then ask whether that is what it is.
Near the end, ask how long the tests will take.
Do not use the words haemoptysis or malignancy. Say blood and cancer.

Systemic red flags (12): Asks about appetite

Local red flags (8): Asks about breathlessness and change in a chronic cough

Risk assessment (6): Asks specifically about tuberculosis contact and about occupational exposure
Closing (2): Summarises, acknowledges both fears honestly, and explains the next step

## candidate_instructions
Mr Farid Nassif is 67 and has been admitted with breathlessness. Perform a respiratory examination and present your findings to the examiner. You do not need to take a history. You have eight minutes.
## actor_opening
Good morning, doctor. They said someone would come to examine my chest.
## actor_sections
Who you are: Farid Nassif, 67, a retired clerk.
When asked to sit forward: Do so slowly and cooperate fully.
When asked to take deep breaths: Breathe through your mouth as asked, but cough once or twice if asked to breathe deeply several times in succession.
When asked whether anything hurts: Say your chest is not painful, but you get short of breath when you lie flat.
When asked about your breathing during the examination: Say you feel short of breath if you talk for too long.
If asked to say ninety-nine: Say it clearly each time you are asked.
If the candidate does not expose your chest: Wait, and do not undress unprompted.
If the candidate forgets to reposition you at 45 degrees: Remain as you are.
Ideas and concerns: If asked, say you are worried because your breathing has been worse for a week.
## actor_flags
Cooperate fully but do not anticipate instructions.
Do not volunteer any symptom unless asked.
If the candidate examines you through your shirt, say nothing but do not help.
If the candidate thanks you and ends early, ask whether they have finished.
## mark_scheme
Preparation (6): Washes hands, introduces self, confirms identity and gains consent
Preparation (6): Positions the patient at 45 degrees and exposes the chest adequately while preserving dignity
General inspection (6): Comments on the general appearance, respiratory rate and use of accessory muscles
General inspection (6): Inspects the chest for scars, shape and symmetry of movement
Hands and face (6): Examines the hands for clubbing, tar staining, tremor and peripheral cyanosis
Hands and face (6): Assesses the pulse and checks the eyes and tongue for anaemia and central cyanosis
Palpation (8): Assesses tracheal position centrally and gently
Palpation (8): Assesses chest expansion, comparing the two sides at the same level
Percussion (6): Percusses both sides comparably, including the axillae and apices
Auscultation (6): Auscultates all zones front and back, comparing left with right at each level
Completion (4): Checks vocal resonance, examines for lymphadenopathy and offers to check peak flow and saturations
Presentation (4): Presents findings in a structured way and offers a differential consistent with them

## candidate_instructions
Miss Rana Adel is 22 and has asthma. Her symptoms are poorly controlled and the practice nurse has noted that her inhaler technique is poor. Teach her to use a metered-dose inhaler with a spacer, and address her concerns. You have eight minutes.
## actor_opening
The nurse said I'm doing it wrong, but nobody has ever shown me how.
## actor_sections
Who you are: Rana Adel, 22, a student with asthma.
When asked how you currently use it: Say you take the cap off, put it in your mouth, press it and breathe in at the same moment, then take the inhaler out straight away.
When asked whether you hold your breath: Say no, you breathe out again immediately.
When asked whether you shake it: Say no, you have never been told to.
When asked about the spacer: Say you were given one two years ago but have never used it because it is bulky and you were embarrassed to carry it.
When asked which inhalers you have: Say a blue one you use when you are wheezy and a brown one you are meant to take twice a day.
When asked whether you take the brown one: Say you stopped two months ago because you felt well.
When asked why you think the brown one matters: Say you assumed it was only for when you feel bad, like the blue one.
When asked about mouth rinsing: Say nobody has mentioned it.
When asked about your symptoms: Say you wake coughing two or three nights a week and use the blue inhaler most days.
Ideas, concerns and expectations: Say you are worried that the brown inhaler is a steroid and will affect your weight or your bones. You want to know whether you have to use the spacer in public.
## actor_flags
Be willing and cooperative, not defensive.
When asked to demonstrate, perform the technique exactly as described above — press and inhale simultaneously, no shake, no breath-hold.
When taught, attempt the technique again and make one deliberate error the candidate must correct, such as not holding your breath.
If the candidate uses the words preventer, reliever or corticosteroid without explaining them, ask what that means.
Raise the worry about steroids only if the candidate asks about your concerns, or near the end if they do not.
Do not accept "it's perfectly safe" without a reason.
## mark_scheme
Opening and assessment (6): Introduces self, confirms identity, and establishes what she already knows and does
Opening and assessment (6): Asks her to demonstrate her current technique before teaching
Explaining the two inhalers (8): Explains in plain language that one relieves symptoms and one prevents them
Explaining the two inhalers (8): Explains why the preventer must be taken regularly even when well
Teaching the technique (12): Demonstrates the sequence: shake, attach spacer, seal lips, actuate once
Teaching the technique (12): Explains breathing in slowly and deeply, or tidal breathing through the spacer
Teaching the technique (12): Explains holding the breath for about ten seconds afterwards
Teaching the technique (12): Explains waiting before a second dose and rinsing the mouth after the steroid inhaler
Checking (8): Asks her to demonstrate back, and corrects the remaining error specifically and kindly
Addressing concerns (4): Addresses the worry about steroids with a reason rather than bare reassurance
Closing (2): Checks understanding, agrees a follow-up and offers written or video instruction

Mr Sabry Mahmoud is 64 and has had spirometry after two years of worsening breathlessness. It shows an obstructive pattern that does not return to normal after a bronchodilator. Explain the result and what happens next. You have eight minutes.
## actor_opening
They did the blowing test last week. Nobody has told me what it showed.
## actor_sections
Who you are: Sabry Mahmoud, 64, a retired bus driver who smoked for 46 years.
When asked what you understand so far: Say you were told to blow into a machine three times, and that is all you know.
When asked what you think the problem is: Say you assume it is your chest being worn out from smoking.
When asked whether you want to know everything: Say yes, tell me straight.
When told the airways are narrowed: Ask whether that means you have emphysema.
When told the narrowing does not fully reverse: Ask whether that means it will keep getting worse.
When asked about smoking now: Say you cut down to 10 a day two years ago and have never managed to stop completely.
When asked whether you have tried to stop: Say you tried patches once for two weeks.
When the candidate mentions stopping smoking: Ask whether there is any point stopping now, since the damage is done.
When asked about your inhaler: Say the blue one helps a little for about an hour.
When asked about vaccination: Say you have never had a flu vaccine.
Ideas, concerns and expectations: Say your main fear is ending up on oxygen and being housebound. You want to know whether you will get better.
## actor_flags
Be calm and attentive. You want information, not reassurance.
Interrupt once if the candidate uses two technical terms in a row without explaining them.
Ask directly whether it is cancer if the candidate has not addressed that by the halfway point.
Do not accept "we'll see how it goes" as an answer about the future.
If told stopping smoking still helps, ask why, and expect a reason.
## mark_scheme

Explaining the result (12): Avoids jargon, or defines any term immediately in ordinary words
Answering the fears (10): Addresses the cancer question directly and honestly
Answering the fears (10): Explains the likely course without false reassurance and without fatalism
Management and prognosis (9): Explains that stopping smoking slows further decline, and gives the reason
Management and prognosis (9): Offers smoking-cessation support rather than only advice
Management and prognosis (9): Mentions vaccination, inhaled treatment and pulmonary rehabilitation as available options
Closing (4): Checks understanding by asking him to say back what he will tell his family, and arranges follow-up
