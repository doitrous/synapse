### Where the heart sits
Concept: CON-CVS-AD0D9E76B568F0
Difficulty: Easy
Q: On a frontal chest radiograph, where does the heart lie relative to the sternum?
*= Behind it, centrally, with more of its bulk projecting to the left
Why: Correct. The heart is retrosternal, its mass extending further left than right, which is why the silhouette is central but asymmetrical.
* Entirely within the left hemithorax, lateral to the sternum
Why: The apex points left, which is why this is chosen, but a substantial part of the heart lies to the right of the midline behind the sternum.
* Behind the vertebral column, in the posterior mediastinum
Why: The posterior mediastinum lies behind the pericardium and holds the oesophagus and descending aorta.
* Above the sternal angle, in the superior mediastinum
Why: The great vessels occupy the superior mediastinum; the heart lies below that plane.
Explanation: The retrosternal position explains the shape of the silhouette and why the sternum is the route to the heart surgically.

### The chamber against the front of the chest
Concept: CON-CVS-4B1B7D6AE18CC6
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: On a lateral film the anterior surface of the heart lies against the sternum. Which chamber forms most of it?
*= The right ventricle
Why: Correct. The right ventricle forms most of the sternocostal surface, which is why it is the chamber most exposed in a penetrating anterior chest wound.
* The left ventricle, being the largest chamber
Why: Its size is why this is chosen, but it lies posteriorly and to the left, forming the apex and left border.
* The left atrium
Why: The most posterior chamber, not the most anterior.
* The right atrium, which forms both the right border and the front
Why: It does form the right border, but the anterior surface is predominantly right ventricular.
Explanation: Anterior chamber, right ventricle. The same fact explains the parasternal heave and why a subxiphoid approach reaches the right side of the heart first.

### The upper right edge
Concept: CON-CVS-1D2C77D7421EF7
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Which chamber forms the upper part of the right cardiac border?
*= The right atrium
Why: Correct. The right atrium forms the right border of the silhouette, so the upper cardiac border on that side is atrial.
* The right ventricle
Why: It lies anteriorly and forms the sternocostal surface, not the right border on a frontal projection.
* The left atrium
Why: Normally hidden within the silhouette, appearing at a border only when enlarged.
* The ascending aorta throughout
Why: The aorta contributes to the mediastinal contour above the heart, but the cardiac border itself is atrial.
Explanation: Each border belongs to a chamber. Knowing which turns "the heart looks big" into a statement about a specific chamber.

### The valve behind the left third cartilage
Concept: CON-CVS-CD77400CEDD059
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Where does the pulmonary valve project onto the chest wall?
*= Behind the medial end of the left third costal cartilage
Why: Correct. The pulmonary orifice is the most superior and leftward of the four valve projections.
* Behind the right second costal cartilage
Why: That is the aortic *auscultation* area. Projection and listening area differ, which is the recurring lesson of these sets.
* At the apex, in the left fifth intercostal space
Why: The apex is the mitral auscultation area, remote from the pulmonary orifice.
* Behind the xiphisternum
Why: Far too low; the valve projections cluster around the third and fourth costal cartilages.
Explanation: The four valves sit close together behind the sternum in an oblique line, with the pulmonary highest and most to the left.

### The valve low and just to the right
Concept: CON-CVS-572235544548B5
Difficulty: Hard
Q: A student is asked to mark the tricuspid valve on a chest wall model and places it in the left fifth intercostal space in the midclavicular line. What is wrong, and where does it belong?
*= That is the mitral auscultation area; the tricuspid orifice projects behind the sternum at the fourth and fifth costal cartilages, slightly right of the midline
Why: Correct. The student has marked a listening area on the wrong side of the heart. The tricuspid orifice lies low behind the sternum, just right of the midline.
* Nothing is wrong; that is where the tricuspid valve lies
Why: That point is the apex, which corresponds to the mitral valve's auscultation area and to no valve's projection.
* It belongs in the second right intercostal space at the sternal edge
Why: That is the aortic auscultation area, and no valve projects there.
* It belongs in the left second intercostal space
Why: That region corresponds to the pulmonary projection, one valve too high and on the wrong side of the sternum.
Explanation: Two errors are being caught at once — confusing a projection with an auscultation area, and confusing the two atrioventricular valves. The tricuspid orifice is retrosternal, low, and just right of the midline.

### The vein on the right border
Concept: CON-CVS-BC694980C8B41C
Difficulty: Easy
Q: Which vein accompanies the right coronary artery, and where does it run?
*= The small cardiac vein, running with it along the acute margin and the right coronary sulcus
Why: Correct. The small cardiac vein accompanies the right coronary artery around the acute margin and in the right part of the sulcus.
* The great cardiac vein, accompanying it on the posterior surface
Why: The great cardiac vein accompanies the anterior interventricular artery and then the circumflex, on the left.
* The middle cardiac vein, running along the acute margin
Why: The middle cardiac vein occupies the posterior interventricular groove, not the margin.
* The anterior cardiac veins, which follow the right coronary artery to the coronary sinus
Why: The anterior cardiac veins are precisely the ones that do not follow that rule, and they do not reach the sinus.
Explanation: Each named vein is identified by the artery it accompanies and the groove they share. Naming the groove names the pair.

### The veins that break the rule
Concept: CON-CVS-1470BCE2FE4215
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Which cardiac veins do not accompany an artery to the coronary sinus, and where do they drain?
*= The anterior cardiac veins, which cross the front of the right ventricle and open directly into the right atrium
Why: Correct. They cross the anterior right ventricular surface and drain straight into the right atrium, bypassing the coronary sinus.
* The middle cardiac vein, which drains directly into the right ventricle
Why: The middle cardiac vein drains conventionally into the coronary sinus.
* The great cardiac vein, which drains into the superior vena cava
Why: The great cardiac vein becomes the coronary sinus; nothing from the heart's own drainage joins the superior vena cava.
* The small cardiac vein, which opens into the left atrium
Why: That would return venous blood to the systemic circulation, and the small cardiac vein drains to the sinus in any case.
Explanation: The exception is the clinically useful part: a surgeon opening the anterior right ventricle meets veins that are not where the accompanying-vein rule predicts.

### Where nearly all of it arrives
Concept: CON-CVS-480B355B4BFDA0
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: The coronary sinus is traced to its termination. Where does it open?
*= Into the right atrium, between the opening of the inferior vena cava and the atrioventricular orifice
Why: Correct. It opens there, usually guarded by a rudimentary valve.
* Into the right ventricle, below the tricuspid orifice
Why: Misplaces the opening by a chamber and would bypass the atrium.
* Into the inferior vena cava just before it reaches the heart
Why: The sinus opens beside the caval orifice, not into it — a distinction that matters when a catheter is being placed.
* Into the left atrium alongside the pulmonary veins
Why: Would deliver deoxygenated blood into the systemic circulation; the error usually comes from associating the sinus with the left-sided circumflex it runs beside.
Explanation: The opening is a landmark inside the right atrium, and its position between the caval orifice and the atrioventricular orifice is what allows it to be found deliberately.

### The pair on the posterior surface
Concept: CON-CVS-7CD4CEA27DF356
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: On the posterior surface, which artery runs alongside the coronary sinus?
*= The circumflex artery, in the posterior part of the coronary sulcus
Why: Correct. The circumflex continues into the posterior coronary sulcus, where the sinus also lies, so the two run together there.
* The posterior interventricular artery
Why: That runs in the interventricular groove, a different groove from the sulcus the sinus occupies.
* The right coronary artery, along its whole posterior course
Why: The right coronary artery occupies the right part of the sulcus; the segment beside the sinus is circumflex.
* The anterior interventricular artery
Why: It runs on the anterior surface towards the apex and does not reach the posterior sulcus.
Explanation: Grooves organise the surface: each carries an artery and its accompanying vein. Knowing which pair occupies which groove makes a posterior view readable.

### The rule and its exception together
Concept: CON-CVS-41752651BA015F
Difficulty: Hard
Q: State the general relationship between the cardiac veins and arteries, and explain why the exception matters surgically.
*= Veins usually accompany arteries in the grooves and drain to the coronary sinus; the anterior cardiac veins do neither, so they are encountered unexpectedly on the anterior right ventricle
Why: Correct. The rule makes the venous anatomy learnable from the arterial anatomy, and the exception is the one a surgeon meets when opening the anterior surface of the right ventricle.
* Veins never accompany arteries in the heart, so each must be learned separately
Why: Overturns the rule to accommodate the exception, and makes the anatomy far harder than it is.
* Veins accompany arteries without exception, and all drain to the coronary sinus
Why: The rule with no exception, which is exactly the gap that catches an operator.
* Veins accompany the cardiac nerves rather than the arteries
Why: Invents an arrangement that does not describe the heart's surface anatomy.
Explanation: A rule worth learning and an exception worth carrying. Together they are the whole venous map: everything follows an artery to the sinus, except the veins on the front of the right ventricle.

### The pockets behind the cusps
Concept: CON-CVS-CE1750C05B9C84
Difficulty: Easy
Q: A parasternal long-axis view shows dilatations of the aortic wall just above the cusps. What are they, and what arises from two of them?
*= The aortic sinuses, from two of which the coronary arteries arise
Why: Correct. The sinuses sit behind the cusps, and the right and left coronary arteries arise from two of them.
* The pericardial sinuses, which lie behind the ascending aorta
Why: A genuinely easy trap given the shared word, but those are recesses of the pericardial cavity, not of the aortic wall.
* The commissures, where adjacent cusps meet
Why: Commissures are the junctions between cusps, not dilatations of the wall.
* Dilatations produced by the jet of ejection, seen only in systole
Why: The sinuses are permanent structural features, present throughout the cycle.
Explanation: The sinuses are why coronary filling happens in diastole: as the cusps fall back, blood held in the sinuses is pressed into the coronary orifices behind them.

### The muscles in the cavity
Concept: CON-CVS-4ABA568F6D69F1
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A short-axis view shows two muscular projections into the left ventricular cavity. What are they attached to at each end?
*= The ventricular wall at their base, and the valve cusps through chordae tendineae at their tips
Why: Correct. Each papillary muscle arises from the wall and sends chordae to the cusps of the atrioventricular valve.
* The ventricular wall at the base and the fibrous ring at the tip
Why: The fibrous ring anchors the valve's base, not the papillary muscle tips.
* The septum at both ends, spanning the cavity
Why: That describes a trabecula, and in the right ventricle the septomarginal trabecula does span the cavity — which is why it is chosen — but it is not the papillary arrangement.
* The cusps directly, with no intervening cords
Why: Removes the chordae, which are the structures that fail in papillary rupture.
Explanation: Wall to muscle to chorda to cusp. Naming each link is what makes it possible to say which one has broken when a new murmur appears.

### How the cords are shared
Concept: CON-CVS-334F674BA1244E
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: To which cusps do the chordae from one papillary muscle attach?
*= To the adjacent halves of two neighbouring cusps
Why: Correct. Chordae from one muscle attach across a commissure to the adjoining halves of two cusps, so one muscle steadies both.
* To one cusp only, so each cusp has its own muscle
Why: The intuitive one-to-one arrangement, and the reason students underestimate what a single rupture does.
* To all cusps equally, so support is uniform
Why: Overstates redundancy and would make a single rupture almost inconsequential.
* To the valve ring rather than to the cusps
Why: The ring is part of the fibrous skeleton and is not what the chordae hold.
Explanation: One muscle, two cusps, adjacent halves. That is why losing a single papillary muscle produces severe regurgitation rather than a small local leak.

### The bigger of the two
Concept: CON-CVS-25C53C42DCA293
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Which left ventricular papillary muscle is the larger, and why does the answer matter?
*= The anterior one, and its size means it supports the most valve tissue, so its rupture is the most consequential
Why: Correct. The anterior papillary muscle is larger and supports more of the valve.
* The posterior one, which is larger because it bears the greater load
Why: Reverses the anatomy. The posterior muscle is the more *vulnerable*, because of its blood supply — a separate point from size.
* The septal one, which is the largest in both ventricles
Why: A septal papillary muscle is a right ventricular feature and is small.
* They are equal, so rupture of either is equally severe
Why: Removes the distinction being tested; size and blood supply differ and both have consequences.
Explanation: Size and blood supply are separate risk factors. The larger muscle supports more valve; the one with less redundant supply is the more likely to infarct.

### The valve on the other side, built the same way
Concept: CON-CVS-5E6A3445314E64
Difficulty: Challenging
Q: A four-chamber view shows the tricuspid valve. Describe how one of its cusps is anchored, and say what that shared plan means for learning the two valves.
*= Its base attaches to the fibrous ring while chordae from papillary muscles hold its free edge and ventricular surface — the same plan as the mitral valve, so learning one transfers to the other
Why: Correct. Both atrioventricular valves are ring-at-the-base, chordae-at-the-edge, papillary-muscle-beyond. The plan is shared, which is why an examiner may teach one and ask about the other.
* Its base attaches to a papillary muscle and its free edge to the fibrous ring
Why: Reverses the two ends of the anchoring.
* It attaches only at its base and closes by pressure alone
Why: Pressure closes it, but unrestrained cusps would evert into the atrium; the chordae prevent that.
* It is built on a different plan from the mitral valve, having three cusps rather than two
Why: The cusp count does differ, which is why this is tempting — but the anchoring plan is the same, and that is what the question asks about.
Explanation: One plan, two valves. The cusp count differs; the ring, chordae and papillary muscles do not — which halves what has to be learned and doubles what an examiner can ask.

### The two coats around the heart
Concept: CON-CVS-7C9D59D257AC65
Difficulty: Easy
Q: A thin low-attenuation line surrounds the heart, bounded outside by a fine dense line. What are these?
*= A small amount of fluid in the pericardial cavity, bounded by the fibrous pericardium with the parietal serous layer against it
Why: Correct. A physiological amount of fluid lies between the two serous layers, and the dense outer line is the fibrous pericardium.
* The myocardium, seen as a low-attenuation layer
Why: Myocardium is soft tissue of higher attenuation and lies inside the pericardium, not around it.
* Pleural fluid tracking around the heart
Why: The pleural cavities lie either side and do not surround the heart — the distinction between a pericardial and a pleural collection.
* Epicardial fat alone, with no pericardium visible
Why: Epicardial fat is present and genuinely hard to separate from a small effusion, but the dense bounding line is the fibrous pericardium.
Explanation: Naming the layers in order on a slice is what lets a pericardial collection be told from a pleural one, and a normal amount of fluid from an effusion.

### Which compartment
Concept: CON-CVS-AE28ABD8CE2B0B
Difficulty: Easy
Q: In which mediastinal compartment do the heart and pericardium lie?
*= The middle mediastinum
Why: Correct. The heart within its pericardium occupies the middle mediastinum, between the pleural cavities.
* The superior mediastinum
Why: That holds the great vessels above the sternal angle, not the chambers.
* The posterior mediastinum
Why: That lies behind the pericardium and holds the oesophagus and descending aorta.
* The anterior mediastinum
Why: A narrow space in front of the pericardium containing thymic remnant and fat.
Explanation: Naming the compartment orders every relation on the slice — pleura laterally, oesophagus and descending aorta behind, sternum in front.

### What holds the sac in place
Concept: CON-CVS-10EC8DB8FAF3BF
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Which attachment of the pericardium explains why a patient's chest pain changes with posture?
*= Its anterior fixation to the sternum, so posture alters how the inflamed surfaces are loaded against it
Why: Correct. Anterior fixation means the sac's relationship to the chest wall cannot simply shift, so posture changes contact and pain.
* Its attachment to the diaphragm alone, with no anterior fixation
Why: The diaphragmatic attachment is real and important, but treating it as the only one leaves posture-dependent anterior pain unexplained.
* It has no attachments and hangs freely, so the heart falls forward on leaning
Why: A free sac is the intuitive picture and the wrong one; the pericardium is anchored front and below.
* Its attachment to the vertebral column posteriorly
Why: Posterior structures are related to the pericardium but do not provide the fixation that makes posture matter anteriorly.
Explanation: The pericardium is tethered rather than floating, which is why posture changes the pain and why the sac cannot move out of the way when it fills.

### The vessels that change places
Concept: CON-CVS-930E4596CA5673
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: On one axial slice the pulmonary trunk lies left of the ascending aorta; several slices higher the relationship differs. Why?
*= The two vessels spiral around each other, so their relative positions change along their course
Why: Correct. The spiral means each axial level shows a different arrangement, which is normal rather than abnormal.
* The patient moved between the two acquisitions
Why: Motion artefact is a real consideration, but a consistent change along the course is anatomical.
* One vessel is aneurysmal and has displaced the other
Why: Reads a normal relationship as pathology — the error that knowing the spiral prevents.
* The slices were taken in different phases of the cardiac cycle
Why: Cycle phase alters chamber size, not the course of the great arteries around one another.
Explanation: No single axial slice defines the relationship between the great arteries. Expecting it to change with level is what stops normal anatomy being reported as displacement.

### When the connection itself is wrong
Concept: CON-CVS-770A1368E72DFB
Difficulty: Hard
Q: In a cyanosed newborn the aorta is seen arising from the right ventricle. Name the lesion and explain why the cyanosis is severe while the lungs are clear.
*= Transposition of the great arteries — the circulations run in parallel rather than in series, so systemic blood returns to the body without passing through the lungs, while the lungs themselves work normally
Why: Correct. The reversed ventriculo-arterial connections put the two circuits in parallel. Gas exchange is normal, which is why there is no respiratory distress and the chest is clear.
* Transposition of the great arteries — the lungs are clear because pulmonary blood flow is reduced
Why: The right lesion with the wrong mechanism: pulmonary flow in transposition is typically normal or increased.
* An overriding aorta above a septal defect, with mixing at the defect
Why: A different lesion. An overriding aorta straddles a defect and receives from both ventricles.
* Severe pneumonia, since cyanosis with a clear chest is a respiratory presentation
Why: Cyanosis with a clear chest and no distress points away from the lungs, which is the discriminating observation here.
Explanation: Cyanosis with a clear chest and no respiratory distress is a routing problem until proved otherwise. The heart's position between the two lungs is why a cardiac lesion can present with a completely normal respiratory examination.
