### Starting with the edges
Concept: CON-RES-044FB05CEE2B7A
Difficulty: Easy
Q: You are handed an isolated lung. Which border separates the surface that faced the ribs from the surface that faced the mediastinum?
*= The anterior border
Why: Correct. The anterior border is the sharp edge between the costal and mediastinal surfaces, and finding it orients the specimen.
* The inferior border
Why: The inferior border separates the costal from the diaphragmatic surface, at the base.
* The posterior border
Why: The posterior border is rounded rather than sharp and lies against the vertebral groove.
* The hilar margin
Why: The hilum is where structures enter; it is not a border between two surfaces.
Explanation: Orientation begins with the borders. Once the anterior border is found, the two surfaces it separates are identified and every other feature has a place to sit.

### Following it onto the chest wall
Concept: CON-RES-0624EEA6DB23DE
Difficulty: Easy
Q: Where do the anterior borders of both lungs lie in their upper part on the chest wall?
*= Running down from behind the sternoclavicular joints towards the sternal angle
Why: Correct. Both borders start behind the sternoclavicular joints and converge behind the upper sternum as far as the sternal angle.
* Along the mid-clavicular line down to the sixth rib
Why: That is part of the surface marking of the inferior border, not the anterior one.
* Behind the manubrium on the right only
Why: Both lungs have an anterior border and both begin at the sternoclavicular joint.
* Along the lateral sternal edge as far as the xiphisternum on both sides
Why: The left border deviates laterally below the sternal angle for the cardiac notch, so the two are not symmetrical below that level.
Explanation: The borders run together behind the upper sternum and part company at the sternal angle. That asymmetry below is the cardiac notch, and it is why the left lung has two lobes and a lingula rather than three.

### The mark left by the aorta
Concept: CON-RES-D10F9CAE51D91C
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A triangular impression is present anteriorly on the mediastinal surface of one lung. Which lung is it, and what made the mark?
*= The right lung, marked by the ascending aorta
Why: Correct. The ascending aorta leaves a triangular impression on the anterior part of the right lung's mediastinal surface.
* The left lung, marked by the aortic arch
Why: The arch grooves the left lung, but above the hilum and continuing behind it, not as an anterior triangle.
* The right lung, marked by the superior vena cava
Why: The cava does groove the right lung, but vertically towards the hilum rather than as this anterior triangle.
* The left lung, marked by the pulmonary trunk
Why: The pulmonary trunk lies at the hilum and does not produce this impression.
Explanation: Each mediastinal structure leaves a characteristic mark, and the marks side the specimen before any lobe is counted.

### The groove that arches and descends
Concept: CON-RES-912F4BEC0BBE15
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A groove arches above the hilum of one lung and continues downwards behind it. Which lung, and what made it?
*= The left lung, grooved by the aortic arch continuing as the descending aorta
Why: Correct. The arch grooves the left lung above the hilum, and that groove continues behind the hilum as the descending aorta's groove.
* The right lung, grooved by the azygos vein
Why: The azygos arch does groove the right lung above its hilum, but it joins the caval and azygos grooves rather than descending as a single aortic groove.
* The left lung, grooved by the oesophagus
Why: The oesophageal groove runs behind the hilum without arching above it.
* The right lung, grooved by the superior vena cava
Why: The caval groove is vertical and above the hilum anteriorly, not an arch continuing downwards behind it.
Explanation: One continuous vessel leaves one continuous groove, arching over the hilum and descending behind it. Recognising it identifies a left lung immediately.

### The rule that settles it
Concept: CON-RES-3587EEFEDA34BB
Difficulty: Hard
Q: Two lungs are presented with their lobes obscured. Which single feature reliably sides them, and why is it more dependable than counting lobes?
*= The structure arching over the root — azygos arch on the right, aortic arch on the left — because lobar anatomy varies and fissures may be incomplete, while the arching relation does not vary
Why: Correct. Each root is arched over by a different structure, and this relation is constant. Fissures are frequently incomplete or accessory, so lobe counting is the less reliable method.
* The number of lobes, which is always three on the right and two on the left
Why: Usually true but not invariable; incomplete and accessory fissures are common, which is exactly why the arch is preferred.
* The size of the lung, the right always being larger
Why: The right lung is generally larger, but size is a poor discriminator between two specimens of different people.
* The presence of a cardiac notch, which is found on both sides
Why: The cardiac notch is a left-sided feature, so stating it is found on both sides is wrong.
Explanation: Reliable anatomy beats usual anatomy. Choosing a feature that does not vary is what makes an identification defensible rather than probable.

### The arch on the right
Concept: CON-RES-7B3F9A26D5765F
Difficulty: Easy
Q: On the right lung, which groove arches above the hilum and joins two vertical grooves?
*= The azygos-arch groove, connecting the azygos groove behind with the caval groove in front
Why: Correct. The azygos arch grooves the lung above the hilum and links the vertical azygos and superior vena caval grooves.
* The aortic-arch groove, connecting the aortic and caval grooves
Why: The aortic arch is a left-sided relation and does not groove the right lung.
* The oesophageal groove, which arches above the hilum
Why: The oesophageal groove runs vertically behind the hilum; it does not arch.
* The tracheal groove, above the right main bronchus
Why: The trachea relates to the right lung above the hilum but does not produce an arching groove joining two others.
Explanation: The azygos arch is the hinge between two vertical grooves, which is what makes the right mediastinal surface read as one continuous route from the posterior wall to the cava.

### The nerve in the neighbourhood
Concept: CON-RES-4DCA05069A0480
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Which nerve is closely related to the azygos-arch groove?
*= The right vagus nerve
Why: Correct. The right vagus passes close to the azygos arch on its way to the posterior aspect of the lung root.
* The right phrenic nerve
Why: The phrenic nerve descends anterior to the root, on the pericardium, not in this groove.
* The left recurrent laryngeal nerve
Why: It hooks around the aortic arch on the left, not around the azygos arch on the right.
* The thoracic sympathetic chain
Why: The chain lies on the vertebral bodies, further posteriorly and laterally.
Explanation: A groove names a neighbourhood, not just a vessel. The vagus lying there is why the relationship is stated in surgical descriptions of the right hilum.

### Which lies behind which
Concept: CON-RES-B5B9265E8558D0
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: On the right lung, how does the azygos-vein groove relate to the oesophageal groove?
*= It descends behind the oesophageal groove
Why: Correct. At this level the azygos vein lies posterior to the oesophagus, and the grooves reflect that order.
* It descends in front of the oesophageal groove
Why: Reverses the relationship; the oesophagus is the more anterior structure.
* The two share a single groove
Why: They produce two distinguishable grooves, which is what allows the surface to be read in detail.
* It lies lateral to the oesophageal groove on the costal surface
Why: Both are mediastinal relations and neither grooves the costal surface.
Explanation: Front to back on the right: cava and heart, then oesophagus, then azygos vein. Holding that order makes an unlabelled right lung legible.

### Where the lymph cannot start
Concept: CON-RES-C139C75AFF0EA1
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Lymphatic vessels are absent from which part of the lung, and what follows clinically?
*= From the alveolar walls themselves — so alveolar fluid must first reach the connective tissue around the airways and vessels before it can be drained
Why: Correct. Lymphatics begin in the peribronchial and perivascular connective tissue, not in the alveolar wall, which is why alveolar oedema clears indirectly and slowly.
* From the visceral pleura, which has no lymphatic drainage
Why: The visceral pleura has a rich lymphatic plexus.
* From the bronchial walls, which drain only through veins
Why: Peribronchial lymphatics are precisely where the drainage begins.
* From the hilum, where lymphatics have not yet formed
Why: The hilum is where the lymphatics converge on the tracheobronchial nodes.
Explanation: Gas exchange needs the thinnest possible wall, and a lymphatic channel would thicken it. The cost of that design is that fluid entering the alveolus has no direct route out.

### Putting the surface together
Concept: CON-RES-D0E08FFB438AB5
Difficulty: Hard
Q: A neonatal lung is examined at post-mortem. It is firm, and it sinks in water. What does that establish, and what must be considered before relying on it?
*= That the lung has not been aerated — but consolidation, or freezing and thawing, can also sink an aerated lung, so the finding is interpreted with the rest of the examination
Why: Correct. An unaerated lung is firm and denser than water. The test answers one question, and other causes of increased density must be excluded before the answer is relied upon.
* That the infant was stillborn, which the test proves conclusively
Why: Overstates a single sign. Other causes of density exist, and the finding is corroborative rather than conclusive.
* That the lung is normal, since all lung tissue sinks
Why: Aerated lung floats, which is the basis of the comparison.
* Nothing, since density bears no relation to aeration
Why: Aeration is exactly what changes the density and is the only variable the test examines.
Explanation: One observation, one variable, and a list of confounders. Treating a single sign as proof is the error the question exists to prevent, in a setting where the conclusion carries legal weight.

### How far the nutritive supply reaches
Concept: CON-RES-91E3CD4EED8E76
Difficulty: Easy
Q: The bronchial arteries follow the bronchial tree. How far distally do they reach?
*= As far as the respiratory bronchioles
Why: Correct. The bronchial arteries accompany the airways to the level of the respiratory bronchioles, beyond which the alveolar region is supplied by the pulmonary circulation.
* To the alveolar capillaries themselves
Why: The alveolar capillary bed belongs to the pulmonary circulation.
* Only to the segmental bronchi
Why: They continue considerably further than that, along the bronchial tree.
* To the pleura only, not into the lung substance
Why: They supply the visceral pleura and also travel with the airways into the lung.
Explanation: The two circulations meet at the respiratory bronchiole. That handover point is why an embolus in one circulation does not necessarily kill tissue supplied by the other.

### What each circulation is for
Concept: CON-RES-47AF74612E619C
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What do the bronchial arteries nourish, and why does that matter after a pulmonary embolus?
*= The lung stroma, bronchial walls and visceral pleura — and because they supply the tissue itself, infarction after a pulmonary embolus is not inevitable
Why: Correct. The bronchial circulation is nutritive and independent of the pulmonary circulation, which is why an occluded pulmonary artery does not always cause infarction.
* The alveolar walls, so an embolus infarcts the lung immediately
Why: The alveolar walls are supplied by the pulmonary circulation; treating the bronchial supply as alveolar removes the double supply and the protection it offers.
* The parietal pleura and chest wall
Why: Those are supplied from the chest wall's own vessels.
* Nothing of consequence, since the pulmonary circulation supplies everything
Why: Ignores the nutritive circulation entirely, and with it the explanation of why lung infarction is comparatively uncommon.
Explanation: A double blood supply is a safety margin. It explains why pulmonary embolism causes infarction in only a minority, particularly in patients whose bronchial circulation is otherwise healthy.

### Where the nutritive blood returns
Concept: CON-RES-27B7D7F1915BFE
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What do the bronchial veins drain?
*= The territories supplied by the bronchial arteries, with part of that blood also reaching the pulmonary veins
Why: Correct. They return blood from the bronchial arterial territory, and some of that drainage joins the pulmonary venous return.
* The alveolar capillary bed
Why: That drains to the pulmonary veins as the functional circulation.
* The parietal pleura and intercostal spaces
Why: Those drain into the azygos system by the intercostal veins.
* The whole lung, replacing the pulmonary venous return
Why: The pulmonary veins carry the great majority of the return; the bronchial veins account for a small part of it.
Explanation: Part of the bronchial venous return entering the pulmonary veins is one reason a small amount of deoxygenated blood normally reaches the left atrium — a physiological shunt present in health.

### Finding them on a section
Concept: CON-RES-454D45D9ADFD50
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Where do the bronchial vessels lie in relation to a bronchus?
*= Along its posterior wall
Why: Correct. The bronchial vessels consistently follow the posterior walls of the bronchi, which makes them findable rather than incidental.
* Encircling the bronchus evenly
Why: Their relationship is consistent and posterior rather than circumferential.
* Within the bronchial lumen
Why: Vessels lie in the wall and surrounding tissue, never in the airway lumen.
* Only at the hilum
Why: They enter at the hilum and continue with the bronchial tree into the lung.
Explanation: A consistent relationship is a searchable one. Knowing where to look is what allows a student to find a structure on an unlabelled section instead of hunting at random.

### Putting the two together
Concept: CON-RES-C5513EC116B783
Difficulty: Challenging
Q: The interalveolar septum carries the pulmonary capillaries. Explain why an embolus occluding a pulmonary artery may leave the alveolar walls structurally intact, and what would change that.
*= The alveolar walls receive their oxygen from alveolar gas as well as from the pulmonary capillaries, and the surrounding tissue retains a bronchial supply — so infarction follows only when the bronchial circulation is also compromised, as in coexisting cardiac or lung disease
Why: Correct. The septum's cells can take oxygen directly from alveolar gas, and the lung has a second arterial supply. Infarction occurs when both routes fail, which is why it is more common in patients with pre-existing cardiopulmonary disease.
* The alveolar walls have their own arterial supply independent of both circulations
Why: There is no third supply; the protection comes from alveolar gas and from the bronchial circulation.
* Alveolar walls are metabolically inert and require no oxygen supply
Why: They are cellular and metabolically active, requiring oxygen like any tissue.
* An embolus never causes infarction under any circumstances
Why: Infarction does occur in a minority, and the question is what determines which patients.
Explanation: Three sources of oxygen for one tissue — pulmonary blood, bronchial blood and the gas in the alveolus itself. Lung infarction is uncommon precisely because all three must fail together.

### The cell that repairs the lining
Concept: CON-RES-0A1B54247E50C8
Difficulty: Easy
Q: After alveolar injury, which cell restores the alveolar lining?
*= The type II pneumocyte, which proliferates and differentiates into type I cells
Why: Correct. Type II pneumocytes are the progenitor cells of the alveolar epithelium as well as the source of surfactant.
* The type I pneumocyte, which divides to replace itself
Why: Type I cells are terminally differentiated and do not divide; that is why the repair falls to the type II cell.
* The alveolar macrophage, which transforms into lining cells
Why: Macrophages are haematopoietic in origin and do not become epithelium.
* The bronchiolar club cell, migrating distally
Why: Club cells contribute to bronchiolar repair, not to restoring the alveolar lining.
Explanation: One cell type does two jobs — secretion and repair. That is why diffuse alveolar damage is followed by a phase of type II cell hyperplasia.

### The cell that does the exchanging
Concept: CON-RES-AE642FB46D38E2
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Type I pneumocytes cover most of the alveolar surface but are far fewer than type II cells. How is that possible?
*= Each type I cell is extremely large and flattened, so a small number covers a great area
Why: Correct. Type I cells are broad, attenuated sheets; type II cells are small and cuboidal, so the numerical and areal proportions differ sharply.
* Type II cells are located only in the airways, not the alveoli
Why: Type II cells sit within the alveolar wall, typically at the corners.
* Type I cells divide continuously to maintain their coverage
Why: They do not divide; coverage is achieved by size, not by turnover.
* The two are present in equal numbers, and the statement is incorrect
Why: Type II cells outnumber type I cells while covering far less surface, which is the point being tested.
Explanation: More cells, less surface. Holding both facts prevents the common error of assuming the more numerous cell must be the one lining the alveolus.

### The cell that cleans
Concept: CON-RES-9B9526985BB441
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Where do alveolar macrophages come from, and why does the alveolus depend on them?
*= From blood monocytes — and the alveolus depends on them because the mucociliary escalator does not reach that far
Why: Correct. They derive from circulating monocytes of bone-marrow origin, and because cilia and mucus are absent distally, defence at alveolar level is cellular.
* From type II pneumocytes, which detach and become phagocytic
Why: Type II cells are epithelial progenitors, not the source of macrophages.
* From resident fibroblasts of the interalveolar septum
Why: Fibroblasts maintain the connective tissue framework and are a separate lineage.
* They are not needed, since surfactant itself clears particles
Why: Surfactant lowers surface tension; clearance at that level is the macrophage's job.
Explanation: Where the escalator ends, the macrophage begins. That handover explains why particles reaching the alveolus are cleared slowly and why they accumulate over a working lifetime.

### Muscle at the last airway
Concept: CON-RES-223676347B292B
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: How is smooth muscle arranged at the alveolar ducts?
*= Limited to the openings of the alveoli, as knob-like thickenings in the duct wall
Why: Correct. By this level muscle has become discontinuous, persisting only at the alveolar mouths.
* As a complete circular layer, as in the bronchioles
Why: A complete layer belongs to the bronchioles, and it has been lost by the alveolar duct.
* Absent entirely at and beyond the alveolar duct
Why: Reduced but not yet absent — the persistence at alveolar openings is the specific point.
* Replaced by elastic cartilage supporting the duct
Why: Cartilage disappeared several generations earlier.
Explanation: The muscle thins out gradually rather than stopping, and the last of it rings the alveolar mouths — the structural counterpart of the loss of cartilage and epithelial height along the same route.

### Following the loss of the escalator
Concept: CON-RES-B0D786E9E0644D
Difficulty: Hard
Q: Goblet cells and cilia are both lost distally. Why must they disappear together, and what would happen if only one did?
*= Mucus without cilia could not be moved and would obstruct, while cilia without mucus would have nothing to propel — so the escalator only works where both are present, and both end together
Why: Correct. The two are functionally interdependent: the mucociliary escalator requires a mucus layer and the ciliary carpet that moves it. Losing one alone would either obstruct the airway or leave the cilia useless.
* They are unrelated, and their coincident loss is a coincidence
Why: The coincidence is functional rather than accidental, which is the point of the question.
* Cilia persist to the alveolus, so only goblet cells are lost
Why: Both disappear distally; cilia do not reach the alveolus.
* Goblet cells persist so that surfactant can be produced
Why: Surfactant is produced by type II pneumocytes, not goblet cells.
Explanation: Two components of one mechanism, ending at the same place. Distal to that point defence becomes cellular, which is the whole reason the alveolar macrophage exists.
