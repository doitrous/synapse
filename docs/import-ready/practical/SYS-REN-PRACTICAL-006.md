### What the filter is made of
Concept: CON-REN-64E32F60E90072
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What is the glomerular basement membrane composed of?
*= Collagen, mostly type IV, with laminin and polyanionic proteoglycans of which heparan sulfate is the main one
Why: Correct. Type IV collagen provides the scaffold, laminin the adhesive component, and the anionic proteoglycans the fixed negative charge that makes the barrier selective for charge as well as size.
* Type I collagen with elastin, as in the dermis
Why: Type I collagen and elastin belong to interstitial connective tissue; basement membranes are built on type IV.
* A lipid bilayer with embedded transport proteins
Why: That describes a cell membrane; the basement membrane is extracellular matrix.
* Fibrin deposited from the plasma
Why: Fibrin appears in the glomerulus in disease, not as a normal constituent of the membrane.
Explanation: The anionic charge is why albumin, itself negatively charged, is held back more effectively than its size alone would predict. Losing that charge is one route to proteinuria without any hole appearing in the filter.

### Counting the glomeruli involved
Concept: CON-REN-D823DE43FBEFAA
Difficulty: Easy
Q: A biopsy shows proliferation in part of the tuft in some but not all glomeruli. How is that described?
*= Focal and segmental — focal because only some glomeruli are involved, segmental because only part of each affected tuft is
Why: Correct. The two words answer two different questions: how many glomeruli, and how much of each.
* Diffuse and global, since proliferation is present
Why: Diffuse means most or all glomeruli, and global means the whole of each tuft — the opposite of what is described.
* Focal and global, since the whole of each affected glomerulus is involved
Why: Only part of each affected tuft shows proliferation, which is segmental rather than global.
* Diffuse and segmental, since the process is widespread
Why: Diffuse would require most glomeruli to be affected, and only some are.
Explanation: Two independent axes — how many, and how much of each. Every glomerular pattern is named on both, which is why the four combinations are worth holding as a grid rather than a list.

### Uniform thickening
Concept: CON-REN-0E170769834796
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A second biopsy shows diffuse, uniform thickening of the basement membrane in every glomerulus. Which pattern is this?
*= Membranous nephropathy
Why: Correct. Membranous nephropathy thickens the basement membrane diffusely and uniformly, affecting all glomeruli and the whole of each capillary loop.
* Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis
Why: That produces sclerosis in part of some glomeruli, not uniform thickening in all.
* Minimal change disease
Why: In minimal change disease the light microscopic appearance is normal; the abnormality is podocyte effacement seen only on electron microscopy.
* Acute proliferative glomerulonephritis
Why: That shows hypercellularity rather than membrane thickening.
Explanation: Uniform and everywhere is as diagnostic as patchy and partial. Membranous nephropathy is the pattern in which the membrane itself is the abnormality, rather than the cells.

### When the capillaries are lost
Concept: CON-REN-02A832B899B605
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: How does long-standing uncontrolled diabetes reduce the filtration rate?
*= By reducing the functional glomerular capillary surface available for filtration
Why: Correct. Progressive glomerular damage reduces the surface area across which filtration can occur, and filtration rate falls with it.
* By lowering the glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure alone
Why: Pressure changes occur, but the loss of filtering surface is the mechanism named here, and it is not reversible.
* By raising the pressure in Bowman's space
Why: That is the mechanism of obstruction, not of diabetic glomerular disease.
* By increasing the oncotic pressure of the filtrate
Why: Protein in the filtrate rises in diabetic nephropathy, but filtration falls because the surface is lost.
Explanation: Filtration depends on the pressure difference and on the area available. Diabetes attacks the area, which is why the decline is progressive and why early hyperfiltration gives way to a falling rate.

### What the blood tests show
Concept: CON-REN-8E47567E9AC240
Difficulty: Hard
Q: As glomerular filtration falls, blood urea nitrogen and plasma creatinine rise. Why is that relationship not linear, and what follows for early detection?
*= Because plasma concentration is inversely related to filtration rate, so a large early fall in filtration produces only a small rise in creatinine while a later small fall produces a large rise — which is why substantial function can be lost before the blood test becomes abnormal
Why: Correct. The reciprocal relationship means the curve is flat at high filtration rates and steep at low ones. A patient can lose a considerable proportion of function with a creatinine still inside the reference range, which is why estimated filtration rate rather than creatinine alone is used for early detection.
* Because creatinine production falls as renal function declines, offsetting the rise
Why: Production depends on muscle mass rather than on renal function, and does not offset the rise in this way.
* Because urea and creatinine rise at different rates, making the sum non-linear
Why: They do behave differently, but the non-linearity being described applies to each individually.
* The relationship is linear, and a normal creatinine excludes renal impairment
Why: This is the belief the question exists to correct, and it is why early chronic kidney disease is missed.
Explanation: A reciprocal relationship is why a creatinine at the top of the reference range in an elderly patient can represent a substantially reduced filtration rate. It is the single most useful thing to understand about interpreting a renal profile.

### Who presents with this
Mr Nabil Shafik, 66, has passed visibly bloodstained urine twice in a fortnight. It is painless. He has smoked for forty years.
Concept: CON-REN-8A11F1D9204098
Difficulty: Easy
Q: Painless visible haematuria in this age group raises the possibility of bladder carcinoma. What is its age distribution?
*= About 80% of patients are between 50 and 80 years of age
Why: Correct. It is predominantly a disease of later life, with roughly four-fifths of cases presenting in that band — which is why painless visible haematuria in an older patient is investigated urgently.
* It occurs most often between 20 and 40 years of age
Why: Presentation before 40 is uncommon, and the age distribution is one reason this presentation is taken so seriously in older patients.
* It is distributed evenly across all adult ages
Why: The strong skew towards later life is what makes age a useful part of the risk assessment.
* It occurs almost exclusively after the age of 80
Why: The majority present before 80, and treating it as a disease of the very old would delay diagnosis in the group most affected.
Rationale: Age shifts the probability before any test is done. Painless visible haematuria in a smoker of this age is bladder carcinoma until proved otherwise.

### What most of these tumours look like
Concept: CON-REN-95F19D57F25A2B
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: At cystoscopy a frond-like lesion is seen. What is the architecture of the commonest bladder carcinomas?
*= Papillary, each frond formed of a delicate fibrovascular core covered by neoplastic urothelium
Why: Correct. About four-fifths are papillary, and the fronds have a slender vascular core — which is also why they bleed so readily and present with painless haematuria.
* Flat and solid, spreading along the mucosa without projecting
Why: That describes the non-papillary minority, which behaves quite differently.
* Ulcerated craters with heaped edges, like a gastric carcinoma
Why: That morphology belongs elsewhere; the characteristic bladder appearance is papillary.
* Submucosal nodules covered by intact normal urothelium
Why: Papillary tumours project into the lumen and are covered by neoplastic, not normal, urothelium.
Rationale: A delicate vascular core covered by tumour explains the presentation: it bleeds painlessly and intermittently, which is exactly what the patient describes.

### The minority that behaves badly
Concept: CON-REN-A6AEEA7A18934B
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: The remaining fifth of bladder carcinomas are flat and non-papillary. How do they differ in behaviour?
*= They are typically invasive at presentation and infiltrate the detrusor muscle
Why: Correct. The flat solid tumours are the aggressive group, already invading muscle when first found, which changes both staging and treatment.
* They are indolent and rarely progress beyond the mucosa
Why: That is closer to the behaviour of low-grade papillary tumours, not of the flat ones.
* They differ only in appearance, with identical prognosis
Why: The difference in behaviour is the reason the distinction is drawn at all.
* They arise only in patients with previous papillary tumours
Why: They can arise de novo, and their behaviour is what defines them.
Rationale: Architecture predicts behaviour. Papillary and superficial is a different disease from flat and muscle-invasive, and the distinction is made at the first cystoscopy.

### The other cause of blood in the urine
Concept: CON-REN-2926B6DCBB2134
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Stones are also being considered. Which stone types are favoured by an acid urine?
*= Uric acid and cystine stones
Why: Correct. Both are less soluble at low pH, so a persistently acid urine favours their formation — which is why alkalinising the urine is used to prevent and dissolve them.
* Calcium oxalate and calcium phosphate stones
Why: Calcium phosphate stones are favoured by alkaline urine, and calcium oxalate is relatively pH-independent.
* Struvite stones
Why: Struvite forms in alkaline urine, characteristically with urea-splitting organisms.
* All stone types equally, since pH does not affect solubility
Why: pH is one of the main determinants of solubility for several stone-forming solutes.
Rationale: Which stone forms depends on what is supersaturated and at what pH. That is why urine pH is measured and why alkalinisation helps two stone types and not the rest.

### An incidental finding
Concept: CON-REN-374A36F2DAF367
Difficulty: Hard
Q: Cystoscopy also shows a pouch in the bladder wall. What is it, what caused it, and why does it matter?
*= An acquired diverticulum, caused by chronic outflow obstruction — usually prostatic enlargement — and it matters because urine stagnates within it, predisposing to infection, stones and tumour
Why: Correct. Mucosa herniates between hypertrophied detrusor bundles under the pressure of chronic obstruction. Because a diverticulum has no muscle in its wall it empties poorly, and the resulting stasis predisposes to infection, stone formation and malignancy within it.
* A congenital diverticulum, present since birth and of no consequence
Why: Congenital diverticula exist but are not obstruction-related, and the association with prostatic enlargement points to the acquired form.
* A ureterocele, the dilated end of the ureter within the bladder
Why: A ureterocele is a cystic dilatation at the ureteric orifice, a different lesion in a defined position.
* Normal trabeculation of no significance
Why: Trabeculation accompanies obstruction, but a discrete pouch is a diverticulum, and its stasis has consequences.
Rationale: A pouch that cannot empty is a reservoir. Everything that follows — infection, stone, tumour — comes from urine sitting still, which is why a diverticulum found incidentally is still worth reporting.
## debrief
Painless visible haematuria in an older smoker is bladder carcinoma until excluded, and the epidemiology, the architecture and the behaviour of the two tumour types are what the first cystoscopy is trying to establish. Stones remain in the differential, and their type is predicted by urine pH. The diverticulum is the incidental finding that still matters, because stagnant urine is the origin of three separate problems.

### How function is being measured
Mrs Faiza Ramzy, 68, has advanced chronic kidney disease. Her bicarbonate is low and her urate is raised.
Concept: CON-REN-7E551969617857
Difficulty: Easy
Q: Which methods are used to assess glomerular filtration rate?
*= Clearance methods, using a marker whose renal handling is known
Why: Correct. Filtration rate is assessed by clearance — of inulin as the reference standard, or of creatinine, or by estimating equations derived from plasma creatinine.
* Direct measurement of blood flow through the renal artery
Why: That gives renal blood flow, from which filtration cannot be derived without a filtration fraction.
* Measurement of urine output over 24 hours
Why: Urine volume reflects water handling, not filtration; a patient can have a normal volume with a very low filtration rate.
* Ultrasound measurement of kidney size
Why: Size carries prognostic information but is not a measure of function.
Rationale: Every assessment of filtration is a clearance, whether measured directly or estimated. What differs between methods is only which marker and which assumptions.

### Why the estimate drifts as she deteriorates
Concept: CON-REN-262E9A13219C74
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Her creatinine clearance is measured. How does it compare with her true filtration rate at this stage of disease?
*= It overestimates it, and increasingly so as filtration falls, because tubular secretion contributes a larger share of what is excreted
Why: Correct. Creatinine is secreted as well as filtered. As filtration declines the secreted component becomes proportionally more important, so the overestimate widens exactly when accuracy matters most.
* It underestimates it, because tubular reabsorption increases in renal failure
Why: Creatinine is secreted rather than reabsorbed, and the error is in the opposite direction.
* It matches the true rate, since creatinine is freely filtered
Why: Free filtration is only the first condition; the secretion is what introduces the error.
* The discrepancy narrows as filtration falls
Why: It widens, which is why creatinine clearance becomes less reliable in advanced disease.
Rationale: A systematic error that grows as the disease advances is more dangerous than a constant one. It is why decisions about dialysis are not made on a creatinine clearance alone.

### Why she is acidotic
Concept: CON-REN-A33EEFD4C4A862
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What produces the metabolic acidosis of renal failure?
*= Retention of phosphates, sulphates and organic acids that the failing kidney can no longer excrete
Why: Correct. Normal metabolism generates acid anions which are excreted by the kidney; as filtration falls they accumulate, and impaired renal generation of bicarbonate compounds the problem.
* Excessive loss of bicarbonate in the urine alone
Why: Bicarbonate wasting occurs in renal tubular acidosis, a different mechanism from the retention of acid anions in advanced failure.
* Overproduction of lactate by hypoxic tissues
Why: Lactic acidosis is a separate cause, not the mechanism of the acidosis of renal failure.
* Accumulation of carbon dioxide from impaired ventilation
Why: That is a respiratory acidosis, and the ventilatory response here is compensatory rather than causal.
Rationale: The kidney both excretes acid and regenerates bicarbonate. Losing filtration impairs the first and losing tubular mass the second, which is why the acidosis of renal failure is progressive.

### Why her urate is high
Concept: CON-REN-B9E0531973510E
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Her urate is raised. Besides reduced excretion, which conditions cause a secondary metabolic gout by increased production?
*= Cancer, leukaemia and psoriasis, in which high cell turnover increases purine breakdown
Why: Correct. Rapid cell turnover releases nucleic acid, whose purines are catabolised to urate — which is why secondary gout accompanies these conditions and why treatment of a large tumour burden can precipitate it.
* Hypothyroidism and Addison's disease
Why: Neither is characterised by the high nucleic acid turnover that raises urate production.
* Iron deficiency and vitamin B12 deficiency
Why: These reduce rather than increase cell production.
* Type 1 diabetes and coeliac disease
Why: Neither is a recognised cause of secondary gout through increased purine turnover.
Rationale: Urate rises when production increases or excretion falls, and this patient has both. Recognising the two routes is what determines whether treatment aims at production or at excretion.

### Whether diet is the answer
Concept: CON-REN-13DF1A71B18999
Difficulty: Hard
Q: She asks whether avoiding foods containing nucleic acids would help. What is the position, and why?
*= Dietary nucleic acids and nucleotides are nonessential, because humans synthesise purines and pyrimidines de novo — so restriction is possible but has a limited effect on urate, most of which comes from endogenous turnover
Why: Correct. There is no dietary requirement for nucleic acid: the body makes its own purines and pyrimidines. That is why restriction is nutritionally safe, and also why it lowers urate only modestly — the endogenous supply continues regardless of what is eaten.
* Dietary nucleic acids are essential, so restriction would cause deficiency
Why: They are nonessential precisely because de-novo synthesis exists.
* Restriction would normalise the urate, since diet is the only source
Why: Endogenous turnover is the larger contributor, which is why diet alone rarely suffices.
* Diet has no effect at all on urate, so the question is irrelevant
Why: Diet contributes a real if minor part, and dietary advice remains worth giving alongside drug treatment.
Rationale: The same fact answers two questions at once — restriction is safe because the pathway is nonessential, and insufficient because that pathway keeps running. Understanding why explains both the advice and its limits.
## debrief
Advanced renal failure is several failures at once: filtration measured by an increasingly unreliable marker, acid anions retained, and urate rising by two routes at the same time. The final decision is the one patients ask about most, and the honest answer rests on a single biochemical fact — the body makes its own purines, so diet is safe to restrict and limited in what it can achieve.

### Phosphorylating a nucleotide
Concept: CON-REN-FA00DC78CFAD63
Difficulty: Easy
Q: How are nucleoside monophosphates converted to diphosphates?
*= By base-specific nucleoside-monophosphate kinases, using ATP as the phosphate donor
Why: Correct. Each kinase is specific for its base and transfers a phosphate from ATP, which is why ATP appears throughout the pathway as the universal donor.
* Spontaneously, without enzyme catalysis
Why: Phosphoryl transfer requires catalysis; it does not occur usefully unaided.
* By a single non-specific kinase acting on all four bases
Why: The monophosphate kinases are base-specific, which is one of the pathway's control points.
* By removal of a phosphate from the triphosphate form
Why: That is the reverse direction, and would consume rather than produce the diphosphate from the monophosphate.
Explanation: ATP as donor and a base-specific kinase at each step means the cell can regulate the supply of each nucleotide separately while drawing on one energy currency.

### The two purines regulating each other
Concept: CON-REN-091F22B55D3081
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: How are AMP and GMP synthesis cross-regulated?
*= ATP drives GMP formation and GTP drives AMP formation
Why: Correct. Each purine nucleotide provides the energy for synthesis of the other, so an excess of one promotes production of its partner and the two are kept in balance.
* ATP drives AMP formation and GTP drives GMP formation
Why: That would be self-reinforcing and would let one purine accumulate at the expense of the other.
* Both are driven by ATP alone
Why: The cross-dependence is the regulatory feature; a single donor would remove it.
* Neither requires a nucleotide donor
Why: Both branch reactions are energy-requiring.
Explanation: Cross-regulation is a design that maintains a ratio rather than a level. Whichever purine is in excess pays for the synthesis of the other, which keeps the pool balanced without any external controller.

### Regulating the pyrimidine branch
Concept: CON-REN-C4606D65B74F2E
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: How is carbamoyl-phosphate synthetase II regulated?
*= Inhibited by UTP and activated by PRPP
Why: Correct. The end-product of the pathway inhibits its first committed step, while the substrate that feeds it activates it — feedback inhibition and feed-forward activation on the same enzyme.
* Activated by UTP and inhibited by PRPP
Why: Reverses both, and would make the pathway accelerate as its product accumulated.
* Regulated only by the concentration of its substrate
Why: End-product inhibition by UTP is the principal control.
* Not regulated; flux depends only on substrate supply
Why: It is the committed step of pyrimidine synthesis and is regulated accordingly.
Explanation: Inhibited by what it produces, activated by what feeds it — the standard arrangement at a committed step, and the reason flux matches demand rather than substrate availability alone.

### The second action of allopurinol
Concept: CON-REN-42ED4D5025FB7B
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Besides inhibiting xanthine oxidase, how else does allopurinol reduce urate production?
*= By reacting with PRPP, which depletes the PRPP pool and reduces de-novo purine synthesis
Why: Correct. Consuming PRPP lowers the pool available for the first step of de-novo synthesis, so fewer purines are made and less urate results — a second route to the same end.
* By increasing urate excretion in the proximal tubule
Why: That is a uricosuric action, which allopurinol does not have.
* By inhibiting the base-specific monophosphate kinases
Why: Those act further along the pathway and are not its target.
* By binding urate directly and rendering it soluble
Why: It reduces production rather than acting on urate already formed.
Explanation: One drug, two mechanisms converging on the same output — block the final oxidation and reduce the raw material for synthesis. That is why it lowers urate more effectively than enzyme inhibition alone would predict.

### A drug aimed at the pyrimidine side
Concept: CON-REN-E6070C296322CB
Difficulty: Challenging
Q: How does 5-fluorouracil act, and why does that make it a cytotoxic agent?
*= It is converted to 5-FdUMP, which inhibits thymidylate synthase — so thymidylate cannot be made, DNA synthesis fails, and dividing cells are affected preferentially
Why: Correct. The active metabolite blocks the enzyme that makes thymidylate, the pyrimidine unique to DNA. Cells attempting to replicate their DNA are the ones that fail, which is what gives an antimetabolite its selectivity for dividing tissue and also its toxicity to marrow and gut.
* It is incorporated into protein in place of a normal amino acid
Why: It acts on nucleotide synthesis, not on translation.
* It inhibits xanthine oxidase, reducing purine turnover
Why: That is allopurinol's target, on the purine side of the pathway.
* It blocks PRPP synthesis, halting all nucleotide production
Why: Its target is specifically thymidylate synthase, which is why the effect falls on DNA rather than RNA synthesis.
Explanation: Thymidylate is required for DNA and not for RNA, so blocking its synthesis strikes at replication specifically. The same selectivity for dividing cells explains both the antitumour effect and the predictable toxicity to marrow, gut and hair.
