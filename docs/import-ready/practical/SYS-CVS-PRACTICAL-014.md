History: a blackout at Friday prayers

## candidate_instructions
Mr Sameh Labib is 31 and has come to the clinic after collapsing at Friday prayers last week. Take a focused history and tell the examiner what you think happened and what would make you change your mind. You have eight minutes.
## actor_opening
I passed out at the mosque last Friday and my wife says I looked grey.
## actor_sections
Who you are: Sameh Labib, 31, a schoolteacher. Fit, no regular medicines.
What happened: I had been standing in a long row for about twenty minutes. It was hot and crowded.
Before it: I felt hot and sick first, then my ears went muffled and my vision closed in from the edges. It came on over half a minute or so.
Warning: If asked whether there was any warning, say yes — I knew something was wrong for maybe thirty seconds before it happened.
Position: I was standing the whole time. It has never happened when I was sitting or lying down.
During it: My wife says I was out for a few seconds. She says I went stiff for a moment and there was a bit of twitching.
Tongue and continence: If asked directly, say no — I did not bite my tongue and I did not wet myself.
After it: I knew where I was straight away. I felt washed out for an hour but not confused.
Chest pain and palpitations: If asked, say no chest pain and no thumping or racing heart before it.
On exertion: If asked whether it happens on exertion, say no — I play football weekly with no trouble at all.
Before that day: I have felt light-headed standing up quickly a few times, but never blacked out before.
Fasting and fluids: If asked, say I had been fasting that day and had not drunk since dawn.
Family history: If asked about sudden death or heart problems in the family, say no one has died suddenly and no one has a heart condition.
Medicines: If asked, say I take nothing regularly and no one has started me on anything new.
Ideas and concerns: I am worried it is epilepsy, and I am worried about driving to work.
## actor_flags
Be relaxed and well. This is a clinic visit a week later, not an emergency.
Volunteer the hot crowded room and the long standing only if asked what you were doing at the time.
If the candidate asks an open question, give the warning symptoms first and wait.
If asked about the twitching, say it was brief and happened after you were already on the floor.
Do not use the words vasovagal, syncope, or seizure. Say passed out or blacked out.
If the candidate offers a diagnosis, ask whether you are allowed to drive.

Before the event (10): Asks about the setting — heat, crowding, fasting and fluid intake

During the event (8): Asks about tongue biting and incontinence
After the event (6): Establishes the speed of recovery and the absence of confusion
Cardiac red flags (6): Asks about chest pain and palpitations preceding the event
Cardiac red flags (6): Asks whether it occurred on exertion, and about family history of sudden death

## learning_objective
Take a blackout history that separates a reflex faint from an arrhythmic or seizure cause on the features that discriminate, rather than on the collapse itself.

Kind: clinical photograph
Purpose: Students misread post-collapse jerking as a seizure; seeing the sequence in real time is what corrects it.

Explaining a murmur found at a routine check
## subject

## candidate_instructions
Farida Kamal is 13 and was found to have a heart murmur at a school medical. She has no symptoms and plays sport without difficulty. Her mother has brought her to the clinic. Explain what a murmur is, what will happen next, and answer her mother's questions. You have eight minutes.
## actor_opening
The school nurse said there is a noise in her heart. Is there a hole in it?
## actor_sections
Who you are: Hoda Kamal, mother of Farida, 13. You are anxious but not hostile.
What you were told: The nurse said she heard an extra sound and that we should get it checked. Nobody explained what it meant.
What you are afraid of: If asked what worries you, say you are frightened she has a hole in the heart and will need an operation.
Her health: If asked, say she is completely well. Never breathless, never faints, never any chest pain.
Sport: If asked, say she plays handball twice a week and keeps up with everyone.
Growth and childhood: If asked, say she was a normal birth, has grown normally and has never been in hospital.
Family history: If asked, say no one in the family has a heart problem and no one has died suddenly or young.
What you want to know: You want to know whether she can keep playing sport, and whether this is dangerous.
Understanding: If the candidate uses the word murmur without explaining it, ask what that actually means.
If offered an echocardiogram: Ask whether it hurts and whether it involves radiation.
## actor_flags
Interrupt once, gently, if the candidate uses more than two technical words in a row.
If the candidate reassures without explaining why, say "but how do you know it is nothing serious?"
If the candidate explains clearly, become visibly calmer and thank them.
Do not accept "it is just an innocent murmur" without a reason for saying so.
Ask directly, near the end, whether she has to stop playing handball.
## mark_scheme
Opening and structure (6): Introduces self, confirms who is present and establishes what they already understand
Opening and structure (6): Asks what the mother is most worried about before explaining
Explaining the murmur (12): Explains a murmur as the sound of turbulent blood flow, in plain language
Explaining the murmur (12): Explains that many murmurs in a healthy child are innocent, and says what makes one innocent
Explaining the murmur (12): Avoids jargon, or explains any technical term immediately
Safety-netting (8): Names the symptoms that would need review — breathlessness on exertion, chest pain, blackouts
Safety-netting (8): Asks about family history of sudden death and explains why
Next steps (8): Explains that an echocardiogram is a painless scan using sound waves, with no radiation
Next steps (8): States clearly whether sport should continue, and on what basis
Closing (6): Checks understanding by asking the mother to say back what she will tell the family
Closing (6): Offers written information and a route back if anything changes
