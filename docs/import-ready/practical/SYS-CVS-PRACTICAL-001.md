Measuring and explaining a blood pressure
## subject

## candidate_instructions
Mrs Farida Salem, 47, has come to the clinic because a pharmacy machine gave her a high reading last week. Measure her blood pressure by auscultation, then explain to her what the two numbers mean and what you found. You have eight minutes.
## actor_opening
The machine at the pharmacy said my pressure was very high, and I have been worried ever since.
## actor_sections
Who you are: Farida Salem, 47, a schoolteacher. You are well and take no regular medicines.
If asked how the pharmacy reading was taken: Say you put your arm into the machine yourself while standing, and you had just walked up from the street.
If asked about symptoms: Say you have no headache, no chest pain, no breathlessness and no palpitations.
If asked about caffeine, smoking or exercise before today: Say you drank a coffee about ten minutes before coming in, and you climbed the stairs to the clinic.
If asked about family history: Say your mother took tablets for blood pressure from her fifties.
If asked whether you have had it checked before: Say a nurse checked it two years ago and told you it was normal, but did not give you a number.
Ideas and concerns: Say you are frightened that a high reading means you will have a stroke like your neighbour did.
What you want to know: Say you want to know what the two numbers actually mean.
## actor_flags
Roll your sleeve up only when the candidate asks you to, and if they leave a tight rolled sleeve above the cuff, do not point it out.
If the candidate applies the cuff loosely, say nothing about it; this is what the examiner is watching for.
If the candidate starts talking while inflating the cuff, answer them, so that the candidate must decide whether to stop you.
If asked to sit quietly for a few minutes before the reading, comply without comment.
If the candidate uses the words systolic and diastolic without explaining them, ask: what does that actually mean?
## mark_scheme
Preparation (6): Introduces self, confirms the patient's identity and gains consent
Preparation (6): Asks about caffeine, smoking and exertion in the last thirty minutes
Preparation (6): Rests the patient seated and supports the arm at heart level
Technique (12): Selects an appropriately sized cuff and applies it snugly to the bare upper arm
Technique (12): Palpates the radial or brachial pulse to estimate systolic pressure before auscultating
Technique (12): Inflates above the estimated systolic pressure, then deflates slowly and steadily
Technique (12): Identifies the first Korotkoff sound as systolic and the disappearance of sounds as diastolic
Interpretation (6): States the reading in full, including which arm was used
Interpretation (6): Recognises that a single raised reading does not by itself establish hypertension
Explanation (6): Explains that the upper number is the pressure while the heart ejects and the lower is the pressure between beats
Explanation (6): Addresses the patient's specific fear rather than reassuring in general terms

Measure an arterial pressure by auscultation with the technique that makes the number trustworthy, and explain what each of the two numbers is a pressure of.

## actor_opening

Onset: Say it began about two hours ago while you were carrying a heavy load upstairs.
Character: Say it is heavy and tight, like a band being pulled around you. Do not use the word sharp.
Radiation: Say it goes down the inside of your left arm and up into your jaw.
Associated symptoms: Say you were sweaty and felt sick with it, and you were more short of breath than the work should have made you.
Timing: Say it has lasted about twenty minutes at a time, eased a little when you sat down, then came back.
Exacerbating and relieving: Say walking uphill and carrying weight bring it on, and sitting still eases it. Say it is not worse when you breathe in and not worse when you press on your chest.
Severity: Say it is about seven out of ten at its worst.
Similar episodes: Only if asked, say that for the last two months you have had a milder version of this when you climb stairs, which stops within a few minutes of resting.
Past history: Say you have high blood pressure treated for six years and you were told your sugar was borderline last year.
Drug history: Say you take one tablet for the pressure but you do not know its name, and you took nothing today.
Family history: Say your father died suddenly at 58 and nobody explained why.
Smoking: Say you smoke twenty a day and have done for thirty years.
Ideas and concerns: Say you think it is the stomach, because your brother has acidity, but you are frightened it is the heart.
## actor_flags
Look uncomfortable and rub the centre of your chest with a closed fist when describing the pain.
Do not volunteer the two months of milder exertional pain unless the candidate asks about previous episodes.
Do not volunteer the family history unless the candidate asks about it directly.
If the candidate asks a leading question such as "it is sharp, isn't it?", say no and repeat that it is heavy and tight.
If the candidate asks whether you can walk to the toilet, say you would rather not because it starts the pain again.

Pain characterisation (14): Asks specifically about radiation
Pain characterisation (14): Establishes the relation to exertion and to rest
Pain characterisation (14): Asks what makes it worse, including breathing and chest wall pressure
Associated features (6): Asks about sweating, nausea and breathlessness
Associated features (6): Asks about previous similar episodes and elicits the two-month history
Risk assessment (8): Asks about smoking
Risk assessment (8): Asks about hypertension and diabetes
Risk assessment (8): Asks about premature cardiac death in the family
Reasoning and closing (6): States that the history describes myocardial ischaemia rather than a chest-wall or pleuritic cause
