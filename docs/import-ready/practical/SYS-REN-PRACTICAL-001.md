### The arrangement itself
Mr Adham Saber, 58, has been admitted with acute kidney injury after a period of hypotension in theatre. The registrar begins by explaining how renal blood supply is arranged.
Concept: CON-REN-D440DE4FAFC5DB
Difficulty: Easy
Q: How are the capillary beds of a single nephron arranged?
*= Two beds in series — a glomerular bed and a peritubular bed — connected by the efferent arteriole
Why: Correct. Each nephron has a glomerular capillary bed for filtration and a peritubular bed for reabsorption, one draining into the other through the efferent arteriole.
* One capillary bed, which both filters and reabsorbs
Why: A single bed could not hold the opposite pressures the two functions require.
* Two beds in parallel, each fed directly from an interlobular artery
Why: Parallel beds would receive blood at the same pressure, removing the pressure difference on which reabsorption depends.
* A capillary bed followed by a portal vein, as in the liver
Why: The efferent vessel is an arteriole, not a vein, which is what allows resistance to be regulated between the two beds.
Rationale: Two beds in series, with an arteriole between them. Every distinctive feature of renal physiology follows from that one arrangement.

### The pressure in the first bed
Concept: CON-REN-1A7B807ECBA748
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What is distinctive about the pressure in the glomerular capillaries?
*= They form a high-pressure bed, which is what drives filtration
Why: Correct. Glomerular capillary pressure is far higher than in a typical systemic capillary, and that hydrostatic pressure is the force driving filtration.
* They form a low-pressure bed, so that filtration is gentle
Why: A low pressure could not filter the volume the kidney filters each day.
* Their pressure equals that of a systemic capillary elsewhere
Why: It is markedly higher, which is precisely why the glomerulus filters and a muscle capillary does not.
* Their pressure varies with posture and is not regulated
Why: It is tightly autoregulated, which is what keeps filtration stable across a wide range of arterial pressures.
Rationale: A high-pressure bed is unusual in a capillary, and it exists for one purpose. The next question asks how the kidney achieves it.

### How the pressure is kept high
Concept: CON-REN-20C433D95CB7B3
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Which arteriolar arrangement maintains the high glomerular pressure?
*= A higher resistance in the efferent than in the afferent arteriole
Why: Correct. A relatively constricted vessel downstream holds pressure up within the bed it drains, in the same way that partly blocking the outflow of a tank raises the pressure inside it.
* A higher resistance in the afferent than in the efferent arteriole
Why: That would reduce the pressure reaching the glomerulus, which is what happens in afferent constriction and lowers filtration.
* Equal resistances in both, with pressure maintained by the renal artery alone
Why: Equal resistances would not sustain a pressure step across the bed.
* Absence of an efferent arteriole, so blood drains directly into venules
Why: The efferent arteriole is exactly the structure that makes the arrangement work.
Rationale: A resistance downstream of a bed raises the pressure within it. That is the whole mechanism, and it is why angiotensin II acting on the efferent arteriole defends filtration when perfusion falls.

### The pressure in the second bed
Concept: CON-REN-AFDA89F1A00D8C
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Why is a low pressure in the peritubular capillaries necessary?
*= Because reabsorption moves fluid from the interstitium into the blood, and a low capillary pressure permits that inward movement
Why: Correct. Having given up a fifth of its plasma at the glomerulus, blood arrives in the peritubular bed at low pressure and with a high oncotic pressure, and both favour reabsorption.
* Because a high pressure would rupture the delicate tubular epithelium
Why: Invents a mechanical hazard; the reason is the direction of fluid movement, not structural damage.
* Because the peritubular bed filters a second time at low pressure
Why: The peritubular bed reabsorbs; it does not filter again.
* Because low pressure slows the blood so that oxygen can be extracted
Why: Oxygen extraction occurs, but the pressure is what determines the direction of fluid movement.
Rationale: High pressure to filter, low pressure to reabsorb — two opposite requirements, met by putting an adjustable resistance between the two beds.

### Putting the series together
Concept: CON-REN-E89E76CF24E11F
Difficulty: Hard
Q: The peritubular capillaries are said to behave like the venous ends of systemic capillaries. Explain, and say what that predicts when renal perfusion falls.
*= They have a low hydrostatic and a high oncotic pressure, so net movement is into the capillary — and when perfusion falls, filtration fraction rises, oncotic pressure rises further, and reabsorption increases
Why: Correct. At the venous end of a systemic capillary, oncotic pressure exceeds hydrostatic pressure and fluid returns to the blood. The peritubular bed sits in that state along its whole length. When perfusion falls and a greater fraction of plasma is filtered, the protein left behind is more concentrated, raising oncotic pressure and driving still more reabsorption — which is why the kidney conserves sodium and water avidly in hypovolaemia.
* They have a high hydrostatic pressure like the arterial end, so fluid leaves them
Why: That would produce filtration in a bed whose function is reabsorption.
* They are identical to systemic capillaries in every respect
Why: Their distinctive feature is that the whole bed behaves like the venous end, rather than having an arterial and a venous end.
* Their behaviour is unchanged by renal perfusion, being fixed by tubular transport
Why: Tubular transport is active, but the Starling forces across the peritubular capillary vary directly with the filtration fraction upstream.
Rationale: The two beds are not independent — what happens in the first determines the forces acting in the second. That coupling is why hypovolaemia produces avid sodium retention without any hormone being required to explain it.
## debrief
Everything distinctive about the renal circulation follows from two capillary beds in series with an adjustable resistance between them. The first is held at high pressure to filter; the second sits at low pressure with concentrated protein and reabsorbs. Reducing perfusion raises the filtration fraction, which raises peritubular oncotic pressure, which increases reabsorption — the kidney's response to hypovolaemia written into its plumbing.

### What holds filtration steady
Mrs Soad Kamel, 71, has taken a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory for a week for back pain. She is mildly dehydrated after a diarrhoeal illness, and her creatinine has risen.
Concept: CON-REN-B7AC8C3EEC5F35
Difficulty: Easy
Q: Over what range of arterial pressures does renal autoregulation normally hold blood flow and filtration steady?
*= Between roughly 80 and 180 mmHg mean arterial pressure
Why: Correct. Within that range, autoregulation keeps renal blood flow and glomerular filtration relatively constant despite changes in perfusion pressure.
* Only between 40 and 70 mmHg
Why: Below about 80 mmHg autoregulation is exhausted and filtration falls with pressure.
* Across the whole physiological range without limit
Why: Autoregulation has limits at both ends, which is exactly why severe hypotension causes acute kidney injury.
* It does not autoregulate; filtration follows arterial pressure directly
Why: If that were so, filtration would swing with every change in posture or activity.
Rationale: Autoregulation is a range, not an absolute. Knowing where it fails is what predicts which patients will injure their kidneys when their pressure falls.

### Which vessel dilates
Concept: CON-REN-5F6FC1A9D0D6D6
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Which mediators dilate the afferent arteriole?
*= Bradykinin and the prostaglandins, including prostaglandin E and prostacyclin
Why: Correct. These are afferent vasodilators, and their action maintains glomerular perfusion when systemic pressure or volume falls.
* Angiotensin II and endothelin
Why: Both are vasoconstrictors; angiotensin II acts predominantly on the efferent arteriole.
* Aldosterone and antidiuretic hormone
Why: Both act on tubular transport rather than on arteriolar calibre.
* Noradrenaline and adrenaline
Why: Catecholamines constrict renal arterioles rather than dilating them.
Rationale: Naming the afferent dilators identifies exactly what a prostaglandin inhibitor removes, which is the mechanism of this patient's injury.

### What dilating it achieves
Concept: CON-REN-1F52ADA44439F1
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What does afferent arteriolar dilatation do to glomerular pressure and filtration?
*= It raises both, by allowing more of the arterial pressure to reach the glomerular capillaries
Why: Correct. Reducing resistance upstream of the bed raises the pressure within it, and filtration rises with it.
* It lowers both, by allowing blood to pass through more quickly
Why: Confuses flow velocity with pressure; reducing upstream resistance raises the downstream pressure.
* It raises flow but lowers filtration
Why: Filtration is driven by glomerular pressure, which rises together with flow when the afferent vessel dilates.
* It has no effect, since filtration depends only on the efferent arteriole
Why: Both arterioles influence glomerular pressure, in opposite directions.
Rationale: Upstream resistance and downstream resistance move glomerular pressure in opposite directions. Holding that pair straight is what makes every drug effect on the kidney predictable.

### What the drug removed
Concept: CON-REN-941A744DD6AE03
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: How does inhibiting prostaglandin synthesis reduce her filtration rate?
*= It removes the afferent vasodilatation that was compensating for her reduced perfusion, so glomerular pressure falls
Why: Correct. In a volume-depleted patient the afferent arteriole is held open by prostaglandins; inhibiting them removes that compensation and filtration falls.
* It constricts the efferent arteriole, raising glomerular pressure excessively
Why: Efferent constriction raises rather than lowers filtration, and is not the action of a prostaglandin inhibitor.
* It damages the tubular cells directly, and filtration falls as a consequence
Why: Direct tubular toxicity is a different mechanism from the haemodynamic one described here.
* It has no renal effect in a well patient, so she must have another cause
Why: The key point is that it has little effect in a well-perfused patient and a large effect in a volume-depleted one, which is exactly this patient.
Rationale: The drug is safe in a patient who does not need the compensation and dangerous in one who does. That conditional risk is why the volume state matters more than the drug itself.

### Predicting the second hit
Concept: CON-REN-856E2D6722DF4F
Difficulty: Hard
Q: She is also taking an ACE inhibitor. Explain what that adds, using the two arterioles.
*= Angiotensin II maintains efferent tone, so inhibiting it dilates the efferent arteriole and lowers glomerular pressure — combined with loss of afferent dilatation, filtration is attacked from both ends at once
Why: Correct. Angiotensin II preferentially constricts the efferent arteriole, defending glomerular pressure when perfusion falls. Removing it while also removing afferent prostaglandin dilatation abolishes both compensations, which is why this combination in a dehydrated patient is a recognised cause of acute kidney injury.
* The ACE inhibitor constricts the afferent arteriole, adding to the prostaglandin effect
Why: It does not constrict the afferent vessel; its renal action is on efferent tone.
* The two drugs oppose each other, so filtration is protected
Why: They act at opposite ends of the glomerulus but in the same direction on filtration — both reduce it.
* The ACE inhibitor has no glomerular effect once filtration has already fallen
Why: Its effect on efferent tone persists and compounds the problem.
Rationale: One glomerulus, two arterioles, two drugs, and both compensations removed. This is the clearest example in renal medicine of why a drug list must be read alongside the volume state.
## debrief
Filtration is defended by two opposite adjustments: prostaglandins hold the afferent arteriole open, angiotensin II holds the efferent arteriole shut. A dehydrated patient depends on both. Removing one is usually tolerated; removing both, in a patient whose autoregulation is already at its limit, is how an ordinary prescription becomes acute kidney injury.

### The pressure opposing filtration
Concept: CON-REN-04707764CDB752
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What is the approximate hydrostatic pressure in Bowman's capsule, and what does it do?
*= About 18 mmHg, opposing filtration
Why: Correct. The fluid already in Bowman's space exerts a hydrostatic pressure of roughly 18 mmHg, which acts against the filtering pressure in the capillary.
* About 60 mmHg, opposing filtration
Why: That is closer to glomerular capillary pressure, the force driving filtration rather than opposing it.
* About 18 mmHg, favouring filtration
Why: The right magnitude with the wrong sign; a pressure in the capsule pushes back against the capillary.
* Zero, since the capsule is freely drained by the tubule
Why: Drainage is not instantaneous, and the residual pressure is a real opposing force — which is why obstruction downstream raises it and stops filtration.
Explanation: Three forces act across the glomerular capillary: capillary hydrostatic pressure driving filtration, capsular hydrostatic pressure opposing it, and capillary oncotic pressure opposing it. Naming the sign of each is the whole of the calculation.

### The force that is absent
Concept: CON-REN-3DB73855CD2EFE
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Why is the oncotic pressure in Bowman's space normally zero?
*= Because almost no protein is filtered, so there is nothing in the filtrate to exert an oncotic pressure
Why: Correct. The filtration barrier retains plasma proteins, so the filtrate is essentially protein-free and contributes no oncotic force.
* Because the filtrate is immediately reabsorbed before protein can accumulate
Why: Reabsorption occurs downstream in the tubule, not within Bowman's space.
* Because the protein present is bound to the basement membrane
Why: Protein is largely prevented from crossing rather than bound after crossing.
* Because oncotic pressure requires red cells, which are absent
Why: Oncotic pressure is generated by protein, not by cells.
Explanation: A zero term simplifies the calculation and is itself a diagnostic statement — when protein appears in the urine, that term is no longer zero and the barrier has been damaged.

### What is done with the filtrate
Concept: CON-REN-12F95EB702AE3B
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What proportion of the glomerular filtrate is reabsorbed by the tubules?
*= At least 99%
Why: Correct. Of the large volume filtered each day, more than 99% is reabsorbed, leaving a small fraction as urine.
* About 50%
Why: Would produce an impossible urine output of many litres per hour.
* About 80%
Why: Still far too little reabsorption to be compatible with normal urine volumes.
* About 20%
Why: Would reverse the relationship between filtration and excretion entirely.
Explanation: Filtering a large volume and reclaiming almost all of it is a deliberate design: it allows precise regulation of what is kept, because everything is discarded first and selectively retrieved.

### Measuring the rate
Concept: CON-REN-8F865D6A0C16B4
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What makes inulin suitable for measuring glomerular filtration rate?
*= It is freely filtered and is neither reabsorbed nor secreted by the tubules
Why: Correct. Because the tubules neither add to it nor take it back, the quantity appearing in the urine equals the quantity filtered.
* It is freely filtered and completely reabsorbed
Why: Complete reabsorption would leave nothing in the urine to measure.
* It is secreted by the tubules in proportion to filtration
Why: Secretion would make urinary appearance exceed what was filtered.
* It is produced endogenously at a constant rate
Why: Inulin is not endogenous; that describes creatinine, which is why creatinine is convenient but less exact.
Explanation: A marker that is filtered and then left alone measures filtration exactly. Every practical alternative is a compromise on one of those two conditions.

### Proving the principle
Concept: CON-REN-9BDD70BB7A49F8
Difficulty: Hard
Q: At steady state, the quantity of inulin filtered each minute equals the quantity excreted. Why does that identity allow filtration rate to be calculated, and what would break it?
*= Because filtered load is the plasma concentration multiplied by the filtration rate, and excretion is the urine concentration multiplied by urine flow — equating them gives the rate, and any tubular reabsorption or secretion of the marker would break the identity
Why: Correct. The steady-state identity converts two easily measured quantities into the rate. It holds only because the tubule neither adds nor removes inulin; a marker that is secreted or reabsorbed makes the two sides unequal and the calculation invalid.
* Because inulin is metabolised at a constant rate, which cancels out
Why: Inulin is not metabolised, and metabolism would introduce exactly the error the marker is chosen to avoid.
* Because urine flow is constant, so concentration alone gives the rate
Why: Urine flow varies considerably, which is why it appears explicitly in the calculation.
* It does not allow calculation without also measuring renal blood flow
Why: Blood flow is needed for filtration fraction, not for the filtration rate itself.
Explanation: The clearance formula is a conservation statement — what goes in must come out when nothing is added or removed in between. Understanding it that way makes it derivable rather than memorised, and explains at once why creatinine slightly overestimates the rate.

### The formula
Concept: CON-REN-DB9326762E58CC
Difficulty: Easy
Q: How is the clearance of a substance X calculated?
*= Urine concentration of X multiplied by urine flow rate, divided by plasma concentration of X
Why: Correct. Clearance is the volume of plasma that would have to be completely cleared of the substance each minute to account for the amount appearing in the urine.
* Plasma concentration multiplied by urine flow, divided by urine concentration
Why: Inverts the ratio and would rise when clearance falls.
* Urine concentration divided by plasma concentration alone
Why: Omits urine flow, so a concentrated small volume and a dilute large volume would give the same answer.
* Filtration rate multiplied by plasma concentration
Why: That gives the filtered load, which equals excretion only for a substance neither reabsorbed nor secreted.
Explanation: Clearance is a volume per unit time, not an amount. Reading it as "the volume of plasma cleared each minute" is what makes the comparisons that follow interpretable.

### What a clearance value reveals
Concept: CON-REN-E4ECB5005A7A41
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A substance has a clearance lower than the glomerular filtration rate. What does that establish?
*= That it is reabsorbed by the tubules after being filtered
Why: Correct. Less appears in the urine than was filtered, so some has been taken back.
* That it is secreted by the tubules
Why: Secretion adds to what was filtered, giving a clearance above the filtration rate.
* That it is not filtered at all
Why: A substance that is not filtered and not secreted would have a clearance of zero, not merely a low one.
* That the filtration rate has been measured incorrectly
Why: The comparison is the intended use of clearance, not evidence of error.
Explanation: Compare a substance's clearance with the filtration rate and the tubule's handling of it is revealed: below means net reabsorption, above means net secretion, equal means neither.

### The endogenous marker
Concept: CON-REN-31C3C4C47EF721
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Where does creatinine come from, and why is that convenient?
*= It is produced endogenously from muscle creatine at a fairly constant rate, so no infusion is needed to measure its clearance
Why: Correct. Being generated continuously by muscle at a stable rate, its plasma level is steady enough to allow clearance to be measured without administering anything.
* It is produced by the liver in proportion to protein intake
Why: Urea is the product that varies with protein intake; creatinine derives from muscle.
* It must be infused intravenously, like inulin
Why: The absence of that requirement is exactly why creatinine is used in practice.
* It is produced by the kidney itself
Why: The kidney excretes it; muscle produces it.
Explanation: Convenience is the whole argument for creatinine. Its production is steady and endogenous, so a plasma sample and a urine collection are all that is needed.

### Why it is not exact
Concept: CON-REN-262E9A13219C74
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Creatinine clearance slightly overestimates the true filtration rate. Why?
*= Because a small amount of creatinine is secreted by the tubules in addition to being filtered
Why: Correct. Tubular secretion adds to the filtered load, so more appears in the urine than filtration alone would account for, and the calculated clearance exceeds the true rate.
* Because some creatinine is reabsorbed, reducing urinary appearance
Why: Reabsorption would cause underestimation, not overestimation.
* Because creatinine production varies through the day
Why: Production is comparatively steady; the systematic error comes from secretion.
* Because creatinine is metabolised in the tubular cells
Why: It is not metabolised there; it is secreted.
Explanation: Every practical marker fails one of the two conditions that made inulin exact. Knowing which condition creatinine fails tells you the direction of the error, which is more useful than knowing its size.

### The estimate at the bedside
Concept: CON-REN-7BA22E8596056E
Difficulty: Challenging
Q: The Cockcroft–Gault equation estimates creatinine clearance from age, weight, sex and plasma creatinine. Explain why each of those variables appears, and when the estimate misleads.
*= Because plasma creatinine depends on both excretion and production — production falls with age and is lower in women, and rises with muscle mass, which weight approximates — so the estimate misleads whenever muscle mass is atypical, as in the very frail, the amputee, or the heavily muscled
Why: Correct. The equation infers filtration from a plasma concentration, which requires an assumption about the rate at which creatinine is being produced. Age, sex and weight are proxies for muscle mass; when those proxies fail, so does the estimate.
* Because renal blood flow falls with age and rises with weight, which the equation corrects for
Why: The variables adjust for creatinine production, not for renal blood flow.
* Because the equation measures rather than estimates clearance, and needs the exact body size
Why: It is an estimate from a single plasma sample, not a measurement.
* Because creatinine binds to plasma proteins in proportion to body weight
Why: Creatinine is freely filtered and not significantly protein-bound.
Explanation: A plasma creatinine is a ratio of production to excretion. Every estimating equation is a guess at the numerator, which is why a frail elderly patient with a normal creatinine may have substantially reduced renal function.
