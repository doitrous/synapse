### Naming the inflammation
Mahmoud Selim, 26, a teacher, has three days of sore throat and has lost his voice. He is febrile, and there are tender lumps in his neck.
Concept: CON-RES-4D4B125FF50E60
Difficulty: Easy
Q: His hoarseness is attributed to acute laryngitis. What is that?
*= Acute catarrhal inflammation of the laryngeal mucosa
Why: Correct. Acute laryngitis is an acute catarrhal inflammation of the mucosa lining the larynx, and the swelling of the vocal folds is what alters the voice.
* Acute inflammation of the laryngeal cartilages
Why: Perichondritis of the laryngeal cartilages is a different and far less common condition, and it is not what produces simple hoarseness with a sore throat.
* Chronic fibrosis of the vocal folds
Why: A chronic process would not develop over three days, and fibrosis is a late change.
* Acute infection of the tracheal mucosa below the cords
Why: That is tracheitis. The site is what names the condition, and the hoarseness localises it to the larynx.
Rationale: The mucosa is the tissue involved and the larynx is the site. Because the vocal folds are mucosal structures, anything that swells that lining changes the voice — which is why hoarseness localises the inflammation for you.

### How the organism does its damage
Concept: CON-RES-E0B3E37B828F65
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What is the common mechanism by which respiratory pathogens injure the airway lining?
*= Attachment to the respiratory epithelium, followed by necrosis of those cells and an inflammatory response
Why: Correct. Adherence to the epithelium is the first step; cell death and the inflammatory reaction to it produce the symptoms and the signs.
* Direct entry into the bloodstream, with airway damage occurring only later
Why: Bacteraemia may follow, but the initial injury in a respiratory infection is at the epithelial surface.
* Production of a toxin that acts on the respiratory centre
Why: A few organisms produce systemic toxins, but that is not the common mechanism of local airway injury.
* Obstruction of the airway by the organisms themselves
Why: The organisms are microscopic and far too few in volume to obstruct; obstruction comes from the mucosal swelling and secretions they provoke.
Rationale: Attach, kill, inflame. That sequence explains why the epithelium is shed, why the mucociliary escalator fails during an infection, and why a secondary bacterial infection so often follows a viral one.

### When it looks worse than usual
Concept: CON-RES-A93F9D04787B4C
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: His pharynx is markedly red with a visible exudate. Which organisms are associated with this more severe appearance?
*= Beta-haemolytic streptococcus and adenovirus
Why: Correct. A more severe pharyngitis with marked hyperaemia and exudate is characteristically associated with beta-haemolytic streptococcal and adenoviral infection.
* Rhinovirus and coronavirus
Why: These are the usual causes of the common cold, which typically produces a milder pharyngitis without a marked exudate.
* Mycobacterium tuberculosis
Why: Tuberculosis of the pharynx is rare and presents as a chronic ulcerating lesion, not an acute exudative one.
* Candida albicans in an immunocompetent adult
Why: Candidal plaques occur in immunosuppression, after antibiotics or with inhaled steroids, and look white and adherent rather than as an acute exudative pharyngitis.
Rationale: An exudate raises the probability of streptococcal infection but does not establish it, since adenovirus produces the same appearance. That overlap is exactly why clinical appearance alone is a poor basis for deciding about antibiotics.

### Reading the glands in the neck
Concept: CON-RES-5BFE7F3DEA6061
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: In acute cervical lymphadenitis, what are the affected nodes like on examination?
*= Enlarged, separate, mobile, soft, and painful and tender
Why: Correct. Acutely inflamed nodes enlarge but remain discrete and mobile, and they are soft, painful and tender.
* Enlarged, matted together, hard and painless
Why: That is the description of malignant infiltration, and the contrast with it is what makes the acute description worth knowing.
* Enlarged, fixed to deep structures, rubbery and painless
Why: Fixation and a rubbery consistency without pain suggest lymphoma or metastatic disease rather than acute inflammation.
* Not palpable, since acute inflammation does not enlarge nodes
Why: Acute inflammation characteristically enlarges the draining nodes, which is why they are examined.
Rationale: Painful, soft, separate and mobile is the acute pattern; painless, hard, matted and fixed is the sinister one. The examination of a lump in the neck is largely a matter of placing it on that spectrum.

### The lesion that keeps coming back
Concept: CON-RES-7B27CD2545F4A5
Difficulty: Hard
Q: A child in the same clinic has had recurrent hoarseness and is found to have multiple warty laryngeal lesions. What are they, and what causes them?
*= Laryngeal papillomas — benign epithelial lesions caused by human papillomavirus, types 6 and 11
Why: Correct. Recurrent respiratory papillomatosis is caused by HPV types 6 and 11, produces benign epithelial papillomas of the larynx, and recurs after removal, which is what makes it so troublesome in children.
* Laryngeal carcinoma, which is the usual cause of hoarseness in a child
Why: Laryngeal carcinoma is a disease of adults with a smoking history; malignancy is not the usual cause of childhood hoarseness.
* Vocal nodules caused by voice overuse
Why: Vocal nodules do cause hoarseness in children who shout, and are a genuine differential — but they are smooth, paired and localised to the vocal folds, not multiple warty lesions.
* Tuberculous granulomas of the larynx
Why: Laryngeal tuberculosis is rare, occurs with pulmonary disease, and produces ulceration rather than papillomas.
Rationale: A benign lesion with a viral cause and a tendency to recur is a different clinical problem from either a nodule or a cancer. Naming the virus is what explains the recurrence and the need for repeated treatment.
## debrief
The case runs from a definition to a differential. Acute laryngitis is mucosal, which is why the voice changes; the pathogen attaches, kills epithelium and provokes inflammation; a marked exudate raises the odds of streptococcal or adenoviral infection without proving either; and the nodes are read on a spectrum from acute inflammation to malignancy. The last decision separates a recurrent viral papilloma from the two conditions students most often reach for.

### What fills the alveoli
Mr Sherif Zaki, 61, has four days of fever, cough and pleuritic pain. His chest radiograph shows dense consolidation of the right lower lobe.
Concept: CON-RES-A7AE91E130076E
Difficulty: Easy
Q: A biopsy of consolidated lung would show the alveolar spaces filled with what?
*= Acute inflammatory cells and red blood cells
Why: Correct. In the consolidated lung of acute bacterial pneumonia the alveolar spaces are packed with neutrophils and red cells within a fibrin exudate, which is what makes the lung solid.
* Air, with thickened alveolar walls
Why: Air-filled alveoli would be radiolucent; consolidation is by definition the replacement of alveolar air.
* Oedema fluid alone, with no cells
Why: A protein-poor transudate fills alveoli in cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, which is a different process from an inflammatory exudate.
* Fibrous tissue replacing the alveolar walls
Why: Fibrosis is a chronic outcome; over four days the process is exudative.
Rationale: Consolidation means alveolar air has been replaced by something dense. Naming what replaced it — cells and fibrin here, transudate in heart failure, blood in haemorrhage — is what turns a white lung on a film into a diagnosis.

### Naming the level of the airway
Concept: CON-RES-BECD91B06EA39D
Difficulty: Easy
Q: On a diagram of the distal airway, which structure lies immediately proximal to the respiratory bronchiole?
*= The terminal bronchiole
Why: Correct. The terminal bronchiole is the last purely conducting airway, and the respiratory bronchiole immediately distal to it is the first that participates in gas exchange.
* The alveolar duct
Why: The alveolar duct lies distal to the respiratory bronchiole, not proximal.
* The segmental bronchus
Why: Several generations too proximal; many divisions separate it from the respiratory bronchiole.
* The alveolar sac
Why: The alveolar sac is the most distal structure of all.
Rationale: Terminal bronchiole, respiratory bronchiole, alveolar duct, alveolar sac. That order defines where conduction ends and exchange begins, and it is the order every distal lung disease is described against.

### Where a single abscess appears
Concept: CON-RES-EA9A6089817998
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: He deteriorates and a single lung abscess forms. On which side would you expect it, and why?
*= The right, because the right main bronchus is wider, shorter and more vertical, so aspirated material passes into it preferentially
Why: Correct. A solitary abscess is more common on the right, and the geometry of the right main bronchus is the reason aspirated material lands there.
* The left, because the left lung has a cardiac notch that traps material
Why: The cardiac notch is a shape of the lung's anterior border and has no role in directing aspirated material.
* Either side equally, since aspiration is random
Why: The asymmetry of the main bronchi makes it distinctly non-random, which is the point being tested.
* The left, because the left main bronchus is shorter
Why: The right main bronchus is the shorter and more vertical of the two; this reverses the anatomy.
Rationale: One anatomical asymmetry explains aspiration pneumonia, aspirated foreign bodies and the side of a solitary abscess. It is a good example of a structural fact with a directly predictable clinical consequence.

### The muscle at the alveolar opening
Concept: CON-RES-223676347B292B
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: How is smooth muscle arranged at the level of the alveolar ducts?
*= It is limited to the openings of the alveoli, forming small knobs in the duct wall
Why: Correct. Smooth muscle persists only around the alveolar openings at this level, appearing as knob-like thickenings between adjacent alveoli.
* It forms a complete circular layer, as in the bronchioles
Why: A complete muscular layer belongs to the bronchioles; it becomes discontinuous by the alveolar duct.
* It is absent altogether at and beyond the alveolar duct
Why: Muscle is much reduced but not yet absent — it persists at the alveolar openings, which is the specific point here.
* It is replaced by cartilage supporting the duct
Why: Cartilage disappeared several generations earlier, at the transition from bronchus to bronchiole.
Rationale: Muscle thins out gradually rather than stopping abruptly, and the last of it sits at the alveolar mouths. That gradient is the structural counterpart of the loss of cartilage and epithelial height along the same route.

### Two patterns of emphysema
Concept: CON-RES-A6F61F4C0A2D64
Difficulty: Hard
Q: At post-mortem, two emphysematous lungs are compared. One is voluminous and pale and overlaps the heart; the other is less pale and obscures the heart less. Which pattern is which, and what does that reflect?
*= The voluminous pale lung is panacinar and the less affected one centriacinar — panacinar emphysema destroys the whole acinus uniformly, so the lungs are more expanded and paler
Why: Correct. Panacinar emphysema involves the entire acinus, producing voluminous pale lungs that overlap the heart anteriorly; centriacinar disease is patchier and centred on the respiratory bronchioles, so the gross change is less marked.
* The voluminous pale lung is centriacinar, because smoking causes the more severe destruction
Why: Smoking-related centriacinar emphysema is the commoner form and can be severe, but it is the panacinar pattern that produces the uniformly voluminous pale lung described.
* Both appearances are panacinar, differing only in duration
Why: The two patterns are defined by which part of the acinus is destroyed, not by how long the disease has been present.
* The appearance reflects the amount of anthracotic pigment rather than the pattern of destruction
Why: Pigment does darken a smoker's lung, and it is a genuine confounder at the bench — but the volume and the overlap of the heart follow the pattern of destruction.
Rationale: The two patterns differ in which part of the acinus is destroyed, and that difference is visible with the naked eye. It also predicts the distribution — centriacinar in the upper zones of smokers, panacinar in the lower zones and in alpha-1-antitrypsin deficiency.
## debrief
Consolidation is alveolar air replaced by something denser, and naming what replaced it is the diagnosis. The order of the distal airway locates every lesion beyond the bronchiole; the asymmetry of the main bronchi predicts which side an abscess appears; and the two patterns of emphysema are told apart at the bench by what they do to the volume and colour of the lung.

### The obstructed airway
A series of patients is discussed on a teaching round, each with inadequate oxygen reaching the tissues for a different reason.
Concept: CON-RES-0C581F605D7DB7
Difficulty: Easy
Q: A man is pulled from a canal and is deeply cyanosed. What has produced the cyanosis?
*= Asphyxia — obstruction of the airway or drowning prevents oxygen reaching the blood
Why: Correct. Asphyxia from airway obstruction or drowning stops gas exchange, arterial oxygen falls, and deoxygenated haemoglobin accumulates enough to appear as cyanosis.
* Anaemic hypoxia, because immersion destroys red cells
Why: Immersion does not destroy red cells in this way, and the failure here is of oxygenation rather than of carriage.
* Histotoxic hypoxia, because water blocks cellular respiration
Why: Histotoxic hypoxia is a failure of the tissue to use oxygen, caused by poisons such as cyanide, not by water.
* Stagnant hypoxia, because the circulation stops first
Why: The circulation may fail eventually, but the primary event in drowning is the exclusion of air from the lung.
Rationale: Cyanosis reports the amount of deoxygenated haemoglobin, and here the cause is simple exclusion of air. Establishing which step in the chain has failed is what the rest of the case rehearses.

### The heart that bypasses the lungs
Concept: CON-RES-723332CA1E127F
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A child is cyanosed with clear lungs and normal breathing. How can a cardiac defect produce this?
*= An intra-atrial or intraventricular defect allows blood to bypass the lungs, diluting the oxygenated blood in the systemic arteries
Why: Correct. A right-to-left shunt sends systemic venous blood directly into the systemic arterial circulation, diluting its oxygen content without any lung disease.
* The lungs are underperfused, so gas exchange is impaired
Why: Pulmonary blood flow may be reduced in some lesions, but the mechanism named here is dilution by shunted blood rather than impaired exchange.
* The heart cannot generate enough pressure to deliver oxygen
Why: A perfusion failure would give stagnant hypoxia with cold peripheries, not the picture described.
* The haemoglobin is abnormal and cannot bind oxygen
Why: That would be anaemic hypoxia from an abnormal haemoglobin, a different category with a normal heart.
Rationale: Blood that never passes a functioning alveolus cannot be oxygenated, however well the lungs work. Cyanosis with clear lungs and no distress is the clinical signature of that routing problem.

### The blocked vessel
Concept: CON-RES-654A12F4B21CC0
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A woman develops a cold, painful, dusky leg after an arterial thrombosis. What kind of hypoxia is present in that limb?
*= Localised stagnant hypoxia — flow to the part is obstructed
Why: Correct. Arterial or venous thrombosis or embolism obstructs flow to a region, producing a local failure of delivery with normal arterial oxygenation elsewhere.
* Hypoxic hypoxia confined to the leg
Why: Hypoxic hypoxia is a failure of oxygenation in the lung and is necessarily systemic.
* Anaemic hypoxia, because the clot consumes haemoglobin
Why: Clot formation does not meaningfully reduce circulating haemoglobin.
* Histotoxic hypoxia, because ischaemic cells cannot use oxygen
Why: The cells lose the ability to function because oxygen is not arriving; the defect is delivery, not utilisation.
Rationale: Stagnant hypoxia can be local or general — a thrombosed artery in one limb, or a failing circulation in the whole body. Recognising it as a delivery failure is what makes restoring flow the treatment.

### The blood that cannot carry
Concept: CON-RES-7B609D48BFC644
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: How can an abnormal form of haemoglobin cause hypoxia?
*= By reducing the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity, producing anaemic hypoxia despite normal lungs and circulation
Why: Correct. Abnormal haemoglobins cannot carry oxygen normally, so delivery falls even though oxygenation and perfusion are intact.
* By preventing the lungs from oxygenating blood at all
Why: The lungs oxygenate whatever haemoglobin is capable of being oxygenated; the defect is in the carrier.
* By obstructing capillaries mechanically
Why: Sickled cells can obstruct, and that is a real additional mechanism in one specific disease — but the category being asked about is the carriage failure itself.
* By raising the oxygen affinity so that no oxygen is loaded in the lung
Why: A high-affinity haemoglobin loads readily; its problem is releasing oxygen at the tissues.
Rationale: Anaemic hypoxia is defined by the carrier, whether the problem is too little haemoglobin or haemoglobin of the wrong kind. Arterial oxygen tension is normal in both, which is why an arterial gas can be reassuring and wrong.

### The cell that cannot use it
Concept: CON-RES-A0F793A4A37DC0
Difficulty: Challenging
Q: A patient with alcohol and opioid intoxication is described as having a component of histotoxic hypoxia. What is the proposed mechanism, and how does it differ from the other three categories?
*= They interfere with the cellular oxidative enzymes, leaving cytochrome in its oxidised state, so oxygen arrives but cannot be used — unlike the other three, in which oxygen fails to arrive
Why: Correct. Alcohol and narcotics can block dehydrogenase activity and leave cytochrome oxidised, impairing utilisation. Histotoxic hypoxia is distinguished by delivery being adequate while the tissue cannot use what it receives.
* They depress respiration, which is the whole of the mechanism
Why: Respiratory depression is real and important in this patient, but that produces hypoxic hypoxia; the question asks about the histotoxic component specifically.
* They reduce cardiac output, so delivery falls
Why: That would be stagnant hypoxia, another category with delivery again the problem.
* They displace oxygen from haemoglobin
Why: Displacement from haemoglobin describes carbon monoxide, and would be a carriage rather than a utilisation failure.
Rationale: Three of the four categories are failures to deliver oxygen; only histotoxic hypoxia is a failure to use it. That is why the venous oxygen content is characteristically high in histotoxic poisoning — the oxygen arrives and comes back unused.
## debrief
Four patients, four points in one chain: oxygenation in the lung, carriage in the blood, delivery by the circulation, and utilisation in the cell. Cyanosis appears only where enough deoxygenated haemoglobin accumulates, which is why it is absent in carbon-monoxide poisoning and in severe anaemia. Naming the step that failed is what selects the treatment.

### The first thing that must happen
The family from the flat with the faulty heater are brought to the emergency department.
Concept: CON-RES-71F421E3C883F6
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What is the first step in treating carbon-monoxide poisoning?
*= Terminating the exposure — removing the patient from the source
Why: Correct. Nothing else can work while the patient continues to inhale carbon monoxide, so ending the exposure precedes every other measure.
* Measuring the carboxyhaemoglobin level before doing anything
Why: The level guides later decisions, but taking a measurement while exposure continues delays the one action that stops further uptake.
* Giving a mucolytic to clear the airway
Why: Mucolytics address secretions and have no bearing on a gas bound to haemoglobin.
* Waiting for the cherry-red colour to resolve
Why: The colour is a sign, and an unreliable one; it is not a treatment endpoint.
Rationale: Stop the exposure, then treat what has been absorbed. It sounds obvious, and it is the step most often delayed while measurements are arranged.

### The measure for the most severe
Concept: CON-RES-D95BA1E8D505D9
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: In severe poisoning, what treatment is described alongside oxygen?
*= Exchange blood transfusion
Why: Correct. Exchange transfusion replaces carboxyhaemoglobin-laden red cells with unaffected ones, and is described for severe poisoning.
* Intravenous sodium bicarbonate to displace the carbon monoxide
Why: Bicarbonate alters pH; it does not displace carbon monoxide from haemoglobin.
* Venesection alone, without replacement
Why: Removing blood without replacing it lowers the oxygen-carrying capacity further in a patient who already cannot carry oxygen.
* A mucolytic to reduce secretion viscosity
Why: Belongs to airway secretion management and has no role in this poisoning.
Rationale: The logic follows the mechanism: the carrier is occupied, so treatment either displaces the carbon monoxide with oxygen or replaces the carrier. Everything else is supportive.

### The muscle that does most of the work
Concept: CON-RES-69811F39EAEB45
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: The patients are placed on high-flow oxygen and their breathing is observed. What does contraction of the diaphragm do?
*= It flattens the diaphragm and increases the longitudinal diameter of the thorax
Why: Correct. The dome descends and flattens on contraction, lengthening the thoracic cavity and drawing air in.
* It raises the diaphragm, reducing thoracic volume
Why: That is what happens when it relaxes, during expiration.
* It increases the transverse diameter by elevating the lower ribs only
Why: Diaphragmatic contraction does assist rib elevation, but its principal action is on the longitudinal diameter.
* It has no effect on thoracic volume, acting only to separate the cavities
Why: It does separate thorax from abdomen, but it is also the main muscle of quiet inspiration.
Rationale: The diaphragm lengthens the thorax; the intercostals widen it. Separating the two actions is what allows an abnormal breathing pattern to be described precisely.

### The muscles recruited when breathing is hard
Concept: CON-RES-62D30403C2A51D
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: One patient is using accessory muscles. What do the anterior serrati contribute during forced inspiration?
*= They lift multiple ribs, enlarging the thorax further
Why: Correct. The anterior serrati act on several ribs at once during forced inspiration, adding to the expansion achieved by the diaphragm and intercostals.
* They depress the ribs, assisting expiration
Why: Rib depression assists expiration and is the action of other muscles, not of the serrati in this role.
* They stabilise the scapula only, with no respiratory action
Why: Scapular stabilisation is their better-known action, which is why this is chosen — but with the shoulder girdle fixed they act on the ribs.
* They elevate the first rib alone
Why: Elevation of the first two ribs is the action of the scalenes; the serrati act on several ribs.
Rationale: Accessory muscles work by having their usual attachment fixed and acting on the other end. That is why a breathless patient braces the arms — it converts shoulder muscles into muscles of inspiration.

### Why posture changes the breathing
Concept: CON-RES-604B7BB05C7418
Difficulty: Hard
Q: One patient breathes more easily sitting upright than lying flat. What is the mechanical explanation?
*= Standing or sitting lets gravity draw the abdominal viscera downwards, so the diaphragm descends more freely
Why: Correct. Upright, the weight of the viscera falls away from the diaphragm, which can then descend further for the same effort — so vital capacity is greater and breathing easier.
* Lying flat increases lung compliance, making breathing harder
Why: An increase in compliance would make breathing easier, so this is internally inconsistent.
* Upright posture reduces airway resistance by straightening the trachea
Why: Tracheal geometry changes little with posture and is not the mechanism.
* Lying flat reduces the work of breathing, so upright posture should be worse
Why: Reverses the observation the question starts from.
Rationale: The diaphragm has an abdomen on the other side of it, and gravity determines how much that abdomen resists. It explains why breathless patients sit up, why obesity and ascites impair breathing, and why vital capacity is measured in a stated posture.
## debrief
Treatment follows mechanism: stop the exposure, then displace the carbon monoxide with oxygen or replace the carrier. The rest of the case is the mechanics of the breathing being supported — the diaphragm lengthening the thorax, the accessory muscles recruited when effort rises, and gravity determining how freely the diaphragm can descend.
