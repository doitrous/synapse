### The one that is not from the lungs
Three patients attend with cough. The first is a hypertensive man whose dry cough began some months after a change of medication.
Concept: CON-RES-AF0A37842A4B44
Difficulty: Easy
Q: Which causes of cough lie outside the lower airway?
*= Upper airway disease, reflux oesophagitis, and drugs such as ACE inhibitors
Why: Correct. Cough arises from the upper airway, from reflux irritating the larynx and from drugs, as well as from lower airway disease.
* None — a persistent cough always originates in the lungs
Why: Two of the commonest causes of chronic cough are extrapulmonary, which is why a normal chest examination does not end the assessment.
* Only anxiety, once the chest radiograph is normal
Why: A habit cough exists but is a diagnosis of exclusion, and a normal film excludes little.
* Only smoking, in an adult
Why: Smoking is a major cause but far from the only extrapulmonary or non-infective one.
Rationale: The drug history is part of the respiratory history. A dry cough in a treated hypertensive should raise the prescription before the pharmacy shelf.

### What treatment is aimed at
Concept: CON-RES-C386DA2E357D56
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What principle governs treating any of these three?
*= Treat the underlying cause
Why: Correct. Cough is a symptom; suppressing it without addressing the cause leaves the disease untreated and can conceal it.
* Suppress first, investigate only if suppression fails
Why: Reverses the order and risks abolishing a protective reflex in a patient whose cause is unknown.
* Use a combination preparation to cover all possibilities
Why: Combinations treat symptoms; covering several possibilities is not the same as identifying one.
* Reassure, since chronic cough is rarely serious
Why: Chronic cough may be the presenting feature of serious disease.
Rationale: The cough is the signal. Treating the signal is justified only alongside treating what is generating it.

### Matching preparation to cough
Concept: CON-RES-E54C71E3C8C556
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: The second patient has a productive cough after a chest infection. Which class is appropriate, and why not the other?
*= An expectorant or mucolytic, because a productive cough is clearing secretions — an antitussive would retain them
Why: Correct. Suppression suits a dry cough; a cough doing useful work should be made more effective, not abolished.
* An antitussive, because the aim is always to stop the cough
Why: Suppressing a productive cough retains the secretions the patient is trying to clear.
* Neither, since a productive cough always resolves without treatment
Why: Many do, but the question is which class fits if one is used.
* An antitussive combined with an expectorant, to cover both
Why: The two work against each other — one abolishes the cough the other is making more effective.
Rationale: Dry means suppress, productive means mobilise. Combining the two is the pharmacological equivalent of pressing the accelerator and the brake together.

### What the bottles are worth
Concept: CON-RES-2150FBD9FBEE6D
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: All three patients have tried combination cough mixtures. How should those be regarded?
*= As adjuncts, not as treatment of the cause
Why: Correct. Combination mixtures are presented as adjuncts; they may ease the symptom but do not address why the cough exists.
* As definitive treatment when the combination is broad enough
Why: No combination treats reflux, an ACE inhibitor or a resolving infection.
* As always harmful and never justified
Why: Overstates; the error is mistaking relief for cure.
* As interchangeable with a single-agent preparation
Why: Combining agents adds exposure and interaction without adding a treatment for the cause.
Rationale: An adjunct sits alongside treatment. A patient who has worked through three bottles has been treating a symptom whose cause was never established.

### Where a suppressant acts
Concept: CON-RES-9BCD8280370DF3
Difficulty: Hard
Q: The third patient has a dry cough disturbing sleep. If a central antitussive is used, where does it act and what is the consequence of that site?
*= It inhibits the medullary cough centre, abolishing the reflex itself — so it suppresses a protective mechanism regardless of the stimulus, and is inappropriate whenever secretions must be cleared
Why: Correct. Acting on the centre removes the reflex wherever the stimulus arises, which is powerful for a dry cough and dangerous for a productive one.
* It acts on the airway mucosa, reducing the stimulus reaching the centre
Why: That is the peripheral route used by local anaesthetic agents and demulcents.
* It thins secretions so less stimulus is generated
Why: Describes an expectorant, acting on secretions rather than the reflex.
* It relaxes bronchial smooth muscle, removing the mechanical trigger
Why: That is bronchodilatation, which relieves bronchoconstriction rather than suppressing the reflex.
Rationale: Knowing where a drug acts tells you when it is wrong. A central suppressant in a patient with secretions is the clearest example in this therapeutic area.
## debrief
Three coughs, one algorithm: find the cause, decide whether the cough is doing useful work, and only then choose a preparation. The drug history matters as much as the chest examination, and the site of action of a suppressant is what makes it inappropriate for the patient whose cough is clearing secretions.

### Adding water
Concept: CON-RES-5B33A2B1CB0210
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What do expectorants do?
*= Increase the water content of airway secretions, reducing stickiness so they are easier to cough up
Why: Correct. An expectorant makes secretions wetter and less adherent, so an existing cough clears them more effectively.
* Break the chemical bonds within mucus
Why: That is mucolysis, a chemical action rather than a change in water content.
* Suppress the cough once secretions are loosened
Why: Suppression is the opposite of what an expectorant is for.
* Reduce the volume of secretion produced
Why: They alter the character of secretions rather than their production.
Explanation: An expectorant depends on the patient still coughing. That dependency is why it must never be paired with a suppressant.

### Breaking bonds
Concept: CON-RES-DCD7BDC14AF7AE
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What do mucolytics do?
*= Break down and liquefy thick tenacious secretions, improving airflow
Why: Correct. They reduce viscosity so secretions can be cleared, improving flow through airways that were partly occluded.
* Suppress the reflex so secretions are not expelled
Why: The opposite intention.
* Reduce inflammation of the bronchial mucosa
Why: An anti-inflammatory action, which is not their mechanism.
* Dilate the bronchi directly
Why: Bronchodilators act on the wall; mucolytics act on the contents.
Explanation: Mucolytics change what is inside the airway, bronchodilators change the airway itself. Both improve airflow, and neither substitutes for the other.

### Naming the members
Concept: CON-RES-04CD9047E73472
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Which group are all mucolytics?
*= Bromhexine, acetylcysteine, inhaled dornase alfa and erdosteine
Why: Correct. These act to reduce the viscosity of secretions, by differing mechanisms.
* Codeine, dextromethorphan and pholcodine
Why: Central antitussives, suppressing the reflex.
* Salbutamol, salmeterol and ipratropium
Why: Bronchodilators, acting on airway smooth muscle.
* Beclometasone, budesonide and fluticasone
Why: Inhaled corticosteroids, acting on inflammation.
Explanation: Grouping drugs by what they do to the airway — thin the mucus, widen the tube, damp the inflammation, suppress the reflex — turns a long list of names into four short ones.

### Coating the surface
Concept: CON-RES-BEFE4500100131
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What are demulcents such as liquorice pastilles and syrups for?
*= Soothing the pharyngeal mucosa in pharyngitis and sore throat
Why: Correct. They coat and soothe the inflamed surface, reducing the local irritation that provokes coughing.
* Suppressing the medullary cough centre
Why: A central action; a demulcent works where it is applied.
* Liquefying secretions in the lower airway
Why: They act on the pharynx and do not reach the lower airway.
* Dilating the bronchi
Why: They have no action on airway smooth muscle.
Explanation: A demulcent acts where it touches. That is why it helps a sore throat and does nothing for a cough arising below the larynx.

### Numbing the receptors
Concept: CON-RES-3F6E8F02EBE7F3
Difficulty: Hard
Q: Benzonatate is described as a peripheral antitussive. Where does it act, and why might that be preferred to a central agent?
*= It anaesthetises the stretch receptors of the respiratory tract, lung tissue and pleura — acting at the start of the reflex arc rather than at the centre, so it does not carry the central effects of a medullary suppressant
Why: Correct. Acting on the receptors interrupts the reflex at its origin, which avoids the central nervous system effects that accompany medullary suppression.
* It inhibits the medullary cough centre, like other antitussives
Why: That is the central route, and the question turns on it being peripheral.
* It reduces the viscosity of secretions
Why: A mucolytic action, which benzonatate does not have.
* It blocks histamine receptors in the mucosa
Why: An antihistamine action, not the mechanism described.
Explanation: The cough reflex has a receptor, an afferent path, a centre and an efferent path, and a drug can be aimed at any of them. Naming the target is what predicts both the effect and the side effects.

### Where a bronchodilator acts
Concept: CON-RES-F399FE33B2F5C6
Difficulty: Easy
Q: A bronchodilator relaxes smooth muscle. How is that muscle arranged in the bronchioles, and why does the arrangement matter?
*= Circularly around the lumen, so relaxing it widens the airway directly
Why: Correct. A circular arrangement means the muscle's tone sets the radius, and radius is what airway resistance depends on most steeply.
* Longitudinally along the airway, so relaxing it lengthens rather than widens it
Why: Longitudinal muscle would alter length without much affecting calibre, and a drug acting on it would not relieve obstruction.
* Spirally, as in the intrapulmonary bronchi
Why: The spiral arrangement belongs to the bronchi; the bronchioles are circular, and it is the bronchioles that carry the variable resistance.
* Bronchioles have no smooth muscle, being held open by cartilage
Why: The reverse — cartilage has disappeared by this level, and it is precisely the muscle that remains.
Explanation: Circular muscle around a cartilage-free tube is why the bronchiole is the site of variable resistance, and why relaxing that muscle produces so large a fall in the work of breathing.

### The airway action
A ward round passes two patients receiving acetylcysteine and one child with a cyanotic heart lesion.
Concept: CON-RES-80300BAF6E1772
Difficulty: Easy
Q: How does acetylcysteine act on respiratory secretions?
*= It splits the disulfide bonds of mucoproteins, reducing viscosity and tenacity
Why: Correct. Breaking the disulfide cross-links between mucoprotein chains makes mucus less viscous and easier to clear.
* It increases the water content of the secretions
Why: That is an expectorant action.
* It reduces mucus production by goblet cells
Why: It acts on mucus already produced.
* It anaesthetises the airway so secretions are not felt
Why: A local anaesthetic action, leaving the secretions unchanged.
Rationale: The disulfide bond is the structural target, and naming it separates a mucolytic from an expectorant.

### The hepatic action
Concept: CON-RES-9DA5B3FB92BC7A
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Why is the same drug used in paracetamol poisoning?
*= It restores hepatic glutathione and binds the reactive hepatotoxic metabolite
Why: Correct. Paracetamol toxicity is mediated by a reactive metabolite that depletes glutathione; acetylcysteine replenishes glutathione and binds the metabolite.
* It binds paracetamol in the gut before absorption
Why: That is activated charcoal's role, and only early after ingestion.
* It blocks the enzyme that metabolises paracetamol
Why: It deals with the metabolite once formed rather than inhibiting its production.
* It thins hepatic secretions so the drug is excreted in bile
Why: Transfers the respiratory mechanism to the liver, where it does not apply.
Rationale: Two indications, two mechanisms, one molecule. The connection is chemical rather than clinical.

### The third property
Concept: CON-RES-6F7E169B8BE108
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Acetylcysteine is also an antioxidant. How does that fit with the other two actions?
*= All three follow from its free sulfhydryl group, which donates reducing equivalents — splitting disulfide bonds, supplying cysteine for glutathione, and scavenging oxidants
Why: Correct. One functional group accounts for the mucolytic, hepatoprotective and antioxidant effects.
* The antioxidant effect arises from a separate metabolite and is unrelated
Why: Misses the shared chemistry that makes one molecule serve three purposes.
* The antioxidant effect is the same action as mucolysis
Why: Related through the same group but not identical; mucolysis is a specific reduction of mucoprotein cross-links.
* The antioxidant effect appears only at doses far above clinical ones
Why: Invents a threshold and disconnects the property from the molecule's ordinary chemistry.
Rationale: One functional group, three consequences. That is the level of understanding at which a drug's uses become predictable rather than memorised.

### The severe end of a different poisoning
Concept: CON-RES-D95BA1E8D505D9
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: In severe carbon-monoxide poisoning, which measure is described alongside oxygen, and why does it work?
*= Exchange blood transfusion — it replaces the carboxyhaemoglobin-laden red cells with cells able to carry oxygen
Why: Correct. When the carrier itself is occupied, replacing the carrier is a logical treatment for severe poisoning.
* Intravenous bicarbonate, to displace carbon monoxide from haemoglobin
Why: Bicarbonate alters pH and does not displace carbon monoxide.
* Venesection alone, to remove the affected cells
Why: Removing blood without replacing it lowers carrying capacity further.
* A mucolytic, to reduce secretion viscosity
Why: Airway secretions are unrelated to a gas bound to haemoglobin.
Rationale: Treatment follows mechanism throughout: stop the exposure, displace what is bound, and replace the carrier when the burden is severe.

### The child who is blue with clear lungs
Concept: CON-RES-723332CA1E127F
Difficulty: Challenging
Q: The child is cyanosed with clear lungs and no respiratory distress. Explain how a cardiac defect produces this, and why the lungs remain clear.
*= An intra-atrial or intraventricular defect lets systemic venous blood bypass the lungs and dilute the oxygenated blood in the systemic arteries — gas exchange itself is normal, so there is no lung disease to cause distress or added sounds
Why: Correct. A right-to-left shunt delivers deoxygenated blood directly into the systemic circulation. The lungs oxygenate whatever reaches them perfectly well, so the chest is clear and the child is not distressed.
* Pulmonary blood flow is reduced, so gas exchange fails
Why: Flow may be altered, but the mechanism named is dilution by shunted blood rather than failure of exchange.
* The haemoglobin is abnormal and cannot bind oxygen
Why: That is a carriage failure with a structurally normal heart.
* The child is hypoventilating, and the lungs will become abnormal later
Why: Hypoventilation would produce distress and abnormal gases from a respiratory cause, which is what is absent here.
Rationale: Cyanosis with a clear chest and no distress is a routing problem until proved otherwise. Blood that never passes a functioning alveolus cannot be oxygenated, however well the lungs work.
## debrief
One molecule with a single reactive group explains a mucolytic, a hepatic antidote and an antioxidant. The last two decisions move to a different failure entirely — the carrier itself, and then blood that bypasses the lung altogether. Each treatment in the case follows directly from the step that failed.

### The lining over the turbinates
Concept: CON-RES-F818BF7BB20E31
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What epithelium covers the nasal conchae?
*= Pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium with goblet cells
Why: Correct. Respiratory epithelium covers the conchae, warming, humidifying and trapping particles from inspired air.
* Keratinised stratified squamous epithelium
Why: That lines the nasal vestibule at the entrance.
* Simple squamous epithelium
Why: Too thin for this exposure, and characteristic of the alveolus.
* Olfactory epithelium
Why: Confined to the roof and the adjacent superior concha and septum.
Explanation: The nose changes epithelium three times in a short distance, and each change follows a change in function.

### Why it blocks
Concept: CON-RES-1A28D007D56110
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What produces the nasal obstruction of an allergic reaction?
*= Congestion of the venous swell bodies in the mucosa, thickening it and narrowing the airway
Why: Correct. The nasal mucosa contains venous erectile tissue; engorgement swells the mucosa and narrows the passage.
* Contraction of smooth muscle in the nasal wall
Why: The nose has no bronchial-type muscular narrowing.
* Mucus accumulation alone, with an unchanged mucosa
Why: Secretion contributes to the symptom, but mucosal swelling narrows the passage.
* Collapse of the alar cartilages
Why: Alar collapse occurs on forceful inspiration in some people but is not the allergic mechanism.
Explanation: The obstruction is vascular, which is why a topical vasoconstrictor relieves it within minutes and an antihistamine does not.

### The lymphoid gateway
Concept: CON-RES-A811FF8C5B42AD
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What characterises the nasopharyngeal lamina propria?
*= Abundant lymphoid tissue, aggregated in the roof as the pharyngeal tonsil
Why: Correct. Lymphoid tissue is the defining feature, forming the adenoid in the roof of the nasopharynx.
* Dense collagen with almost no cells
Why: Understates a tissue defined by its lymphoid population.
* Skeletal muscle fibres within the lamina propria
Why: Muscle lies deep to the mucosa in the pharyngeal wall.
* Hyaline cartilage continuous with the septum
Why: Cartilage supports the nose and the auditory tube, not the nasopharyngeal lamina propria.
Explanation: The entrance to the airway is also an immunological checkpoint, which is why adenoidal enlargement obstructs the nasopharynx in children.

### The neuron that renews
Concept: CON-RES-CCE62F7217A235
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What is unusual about olfactory receptor neurons?
*= They are continuously replaced throughout life from basal stem cells
Why: Correct. They are among the few neurons that regenerate, replaced from a basal population within the epithelium.
* They never regenerate, so anosmia is always permanent
Why: The general rule for neurons, applied where the exception holds.
* The mature neurons divide to produce replacements
Why: The mature cells do not divide; a separate basal population supplies them.
* They regenerate only until puberty
Why: Turnover continues through life, though it becomes less efficient with age and injury.
Explanation: Recovery of smell after a viral illness depends on this population, which is also why the olfactory epithelium is studied as a model of neural regeneration.

### The route through the roof
Concept: CON-RES-74413C2E6FFA70
Difficulty: Hard
Q: Bundles of unmyelinated fibres pass upwards from the roof of the nasal cavity. Where do they go, and what makes that route clinically important?
*= Through the cribriform plate to the olfactory bulb — a direct communication between the nasal cavity and the cranial cavity, so a fracture there can leak cerebrospinal fluid into the nose and admit infection to the meninges
Why: Correct. The olfactory fibres traverse the perforated cribriform plate, and that perforated bone is the one place where nasal cavity and cranial cavity are separated by so little.
* They are trigeminal branches carrying general sensation
Why: General sensation is trigeminal, but those fibres do not cross the cribriform plate.
* They are postganglionic sympathetic fibres to the nasal vessels
Why: Autonomic fibres reach the nose by a different route.
* They are lymphatic channels draining to the cervical nodes
Why: Lymphatics are not nerve fibres and drain downwards.
Explanation: Anosmia after head injury, cerebrospinal fluid rhinorrhoea and meningitis following a nasal infection all follow from one perforated plate of bone.

### The wall in order
Concept: CON-RES-A570461528F6E3
Difficulty: Easy
Q: Name the layers of the tracheal wall from the lumen outwards.
*= Mucosa, submucosa, a cartilaginous and muscular layer, and adventitia
Why: Correct. That is the sequence, with the seromucous glands lying in the submucosa and the C-shaped cartilages with the trachealis beyond it.
* Mucosa, muscularis mucosae, submucosa and serosa
Why: The plan of the gut wall; the trachea has no serosa.
* Epithelium and adventitia only
Why: Omits the submucosa and its glands, which are prominent on the slide.
* Endothelium, media and adventitia
Why: The plan of a blood vessel.
Explanation: Naming the layers in order makes the slide readable, and it is the same sequence that thins progressively as the airways divide.

### The thin cell
Concept: CON-RES-E08739F0C0C229
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What is the morphology of the type I pneumocyte and why does it matter?
*= Extremely flattened and attenuated, minimising the diffusion distance for gas
Why: Correct. Its thin sheet-like cytoplasm keeps the blood-gas barrier as thin as possible, which is what makes diffusion efficient.
* Cuboidal with lamellar bodies for secretion
Why: That is the type II pneumocyte.
* Columnar and ciliated
Why: Ciliated columnar epithelium belongs to the conducting airways.
* Spherical and motile
Why: Describes the alveolar macrophage.
Explanation: Function dictates shape. A cell whose purpose is to be crossed by gas is built as thin as a cell can be, which is also why it is so easily injured.

### The lining before birth
Concept: CON-RES-C0E444E65F8201
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What lines the fetal alveolus, and how does it change towards term?
*= A cuboidal epithelium that flattens as the lung matures
Why: Correct. The fetal alveolar lining is cuboidal and becomes attenuated towards term, producing the thin cells needed for exchange.
* A stratified squamous lining replaced at birth
Why: Stratified squamous epithelium belongs to the upper airway.
* A ciliated lining that loses its cilia at birth
Why: Cilia end well proximal to the alveolus at any age.
* The same thin type I lining as an adult, from early gestation
Why: The thin lining develops late, which is part of why very preterm infants exchange gas poorly.
Explanation: Maturation of the lining and the arrival of surfactant both happen late, which is why gestational age predicts respiratory difficulty so strongly.

### What the preterm lung lacks
Concept: CON-RES-FB2D4322FCC2EB
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: How does prematurity cause respiratory distress syndrome?
*= Surfactant is deficient, so alveolar surface tension is unopposed, compliance falls and alveoli collapse at end-expiration
Why: Correct. Surfactant production rises late in gestation; without it the largest force opposing inflation acts unopposed.
* The airways are too narrow, making resistance the limiting problem
Why: Attributes a compliance problem to resistance.
* The respiratory muscles are too weak to generate a gradient
Why: The muscles are working hard, which is what the recession and grunting show.
* The alveoli have not formed, so there is no exchange surface
Why: Alveoli are present in reduced numbers; the defining problem is the surfactant lining them.
Explanation: One missing surface-active layer explains the grunting, the recession and the need for applied pressure.

### The film between the layers
Concept: CON-RES-B7C1C8079DFBE9
Difficulty: Hard
Q: What does the pleural fluid do, and what follows when it accumulates?
*= It lubricates the surfaces so they slide, while its surface tension holds them together — an accumulation abolishes that coupling, so the lung no longer follows the chest wall and collapses away from it
Why: Correct. The film both lubricates and couples; a large effusion separates the layers and compresses the lung.
* It lubricates only, so accumulation matters solely by volume
Why: Misses the coupling function, which is what makes the lung follow the chest wall at all.
* It holds the layers apart, and a reduction causes them to adhere
Why: Reverses the function.
* It nourishes the visceral pleura, which has no blood supply
Why: The visceral pleura is supplied by the bronchial circulation.
Explanation: Two functions from one thin film — sliding and sticking. That is why a small volume is essential and a large volume disabling.
