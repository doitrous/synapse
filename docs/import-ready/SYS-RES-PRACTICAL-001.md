### The starting point
Mr Hazem Sultan, 44, is being taught breathing exercises after abdominal surgery. The physiotherapist explains what happens each time he breathes in.
Concept: CON-RES-847612642A38F0
Difficulty: Easy
Q: Immediately before inspiration begins, what is the pressure inside the alveoli?
*= Equal to atmospheric pressure, and by convention assigned a value of zero
Why: Correct. With the airway open and no air moving, alveolar pressure has equilibrated with the atmosphere, and it is conventionally taken as the zero of the scale.
* Negative, because the chest is held expanded by the pleura
Why: Intrapleural pressure is negative between breaths, which is why this is chosen — but alveolar pressure is a different pressure and equals atmospheric.
* Positive, because the lungs are elastic and recoil inwards
Why: Elastic recoil is real, but it is balanced at the end of a quiet breath, so no pressure difference remains to drive flow.
* Undefined until air begins to move
Why: The pressure exists whether or not air is flowing; it is precisely because it equals atmospheric that no air moves.
Rationale: Zero is a reference point, not an absence of pressure. Every statement that follows about inspiration and expiration is a departure from this baseline.

### Which way the air goes
Concept: CON-RES-9206FAE9E0BDED
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What relationship between alveolar and atmospheric pressure moves air into the lungs?
*= Alveolar pressure below atmospheric — a negative gradient from atmosphere to alveoli
Why: Correct. Air flows down a pressure gradient, so it enters when alveolar pressure falls below atmospheric.
* Alveolar pressure above atmospheric
Why: That drives air out, not in — it is the expiratory condition.
* Equal alveolar and atmospheric pressures, with the diaphragm pushing air in
Why: With no gradient there is no flow. The diaphragm does not push air; it creates the gradient.
* A negative intrapleural pressure alone, regardless of alveolar pressure
Why: Intrapleural pressure is negative throughout quiet breathing, including at moments when no air is moving, so on its own it cannot explain flow.
Rationale: Flow follows the gradient between the atmosphere and the alveoli, and nothing else. Intrapleural pressure matters because of what it does to alveolar pressure, not directly.

### How the gradient is made
Concept: CON-RES-E81B4CC180E90F
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: During quiet inspiration, chest expansion lowers alveolar pressure by roughly how much?
*= To about −1 cm H₂O relative to atmosphere
Why: Correct. Quiet inspiration produces a small gradient of about 1 cm H₂O, which is enough to move a normal tidal volume.
* To about −10 cm H₂O
Why: An order too large for quiet breathing; pressures of that size belong to forced or obstructed breathing.
* To about −40 cm H₂O
Why: That approaches a maximal inspiratory effort against a closed airway, not a quiet breath.
* It does not fall; the diaphragm raises intrapleural pressure instead
Why: Reverses both changes. The diaphragm descending makes intrapleural pressure more negative, and alveolar pressure falls with it.
Rationale: Quiet breathing is a low-pressure event. Holding the scale — about 1 cm H₂O, not 10 or 40 — is what makes an abnormal effort recognisable as abnormal.

### The shape of the cycle
Concept: CON-RES-2B785411500F0D
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: How does alveolar pressure vary across one respiratory cycle?
*= Zero at the boundaries of the cycle, negative during inspiration, positive during expiration
Why: Correct. It returns to atmospheric whenever flow stops, and departs from it in opposite directions during the two phases of flow.
* Continuously negative, becoming more negative on inspiration
Why: Describes intrapleural pressure, which is negative throughout. Alveolar pressure crosses zero twice per cycle.
* Continuously positive, falling towards zero on inspiration
Why: Inverts the sign of inspiration; air could not enter against a positive alveolar pressure.
* Constant throughout, since the lungs are a closed system
Why: The lungs are open to the atmosphere, and a constant pressure would mean no flow at all.
Rationale: Two crossings of zero per cycle, one in each direction. Reading that pattern is what distinguishes alveolar from intrapleural pressure on a trace, which students routinely confuse.

### Doing the arithmetic of a quiet breath
Concept: CON-RES-96FDE2C1A0D74F
Difficulty: Hard
Q: A pressure difference of about 1 cm H₂O between atmosphere and alveoli moves what volume during quiet breathing, and what does that imply about the work of breathing?
*= About 500 mL — so a normal tidal breath is achieved by a very small pressure difference, and quiet breathing costs little work
Why: Correct. Roughly 1 cm H₂O moves a tidal volume of about 500 mL, which is why quiet breathing consumes only a small fraction of resting energy expenditure.
* About 500 mL, but at a high energy cost because the lungs are stiff
Why: The right volume with the wrong conclusion. A large volume for a small pressure means the system is compliant, not stiff.
* About 50 mL, so many breaths are needed to ventilate the lungs
Why: An order too small; 50 mL would barely exceed the dead space and could not sustain gas exchange.
* About 3000 mL, which is the vital capacity
Why: Confuses tidal volume with vital capacity, which requires a maximal effort and a far larger pressure.
Rationale: A small pressure moving a large volume is the definition of a compliant system. It is also why any disease that stiffens the lung or narrows the airway raises the work of breathing so steeply.
## debrief
The case builds the mechanics of one breath from a single reference point: alveolar pressure is zero when no air moves, falls about 1 cm H₂O to draw in 500 mL, and rises above atmospheric to push it out. Holding the size of those numbers is what makes an abnormal effort recognisable, and the volume-per-pressure relationship at the end is compliance, which the next case takes up.

### What sets the resistance
Nour Ibrahim, 19, has asthma. During an attack she is wheezing, using her accessory muscles, and finds breathing out harder than breathing in.
Concept: CON-RES-C3A645B7170C55
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What is the main determinant of airway resistance?
*= The radius of the airway
Why: Correct. Resistance depends very steeply on radius, so a small narrowing produces a large rise in resistance.
* The length of the airway
Why: Length does contribute, but it barely changes in disease, whereas radius changes dramatically.
* The viscosity of the air
Why: A determinant in principle, but essentially constant in a patient breathing air.
* The elastic recoil of the lung tissue
Why: Recoil governs the elastic work of breathing, which is a separate component from the frictional resistance to airflow.
Rationale: Radius dominates because resistance varies with a high power of it. That is why bronchoconstriction of a few millimetres transforms the work of breathing.

### What the narrowing costs her
Concept: CON-RES-CEC975EBFCD70E
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What does the airway narrowing of an asthma attack do to the work of breathing?
*= It raises the frictional work of both inspiration and expiration
Why: Correct. Narrowed airways increase the frictional resistance to airflow in both directions, so both phases cost more work.
* It raises the work of expiration only, since inspiration is passive
Why: Inspiration is the active phase in quiet breathing, not the passive one, and narrowed airways impede flow in both directions. Expiration is harder here, but that is not the same as inspiration being unaffected.
* It raises the elastic work, because the lungs become stiffer
Why: Confuses the two components of the work of breathing. Asthma is an airway problem, so it raises frictional rather than elastic work.
* It has no effect on work, only on the sensation of breathlessness
Why: The sensation follows from the increased work; it is not a substitute for it.
Rationale: The work of breathing has an elastic component and a frictional one. Naming which a disease attacks is what predicts the pattern the patient shows.

### Which component is being spent
Concept: CON-RES-C8EFB24BB6B270
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What is airway-resistance work spent on?
*= Overcoming the frictional resistance to airflow through the airways
Why: Correct. This component of the work of breathing is expended against friction as gas moves through the conducting airways.
* Stretching the elastic tissue of the lung and chest wall
Why: That is elastic work, the other component, and it is stored and returned rather than spent against friction.
* Overcoming the surface tension of the alveolar lining
Why: Surface tension opposes inflation and belongs to the elastic component.
* Moving the diaphragm against the abdominal contents
Why: A real load, but it forms part of the elastic and tissue work rather than airway resistance.
Rationale: Two components, two different diseases. A stiff lung raises elastic work; a narrow airway raises frictional work; and the treatments differ accordingly.

### The muscle that is narrowing them
Concept: CON-RES-F399FE33B2F5C6
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: How is the smooth muscle of the bronchioles arranged, and why does that matter here?
*= Circularly, so contraction narrows the lumen directly
Why: Correct. A circular arrangement means contraction reduces the radius, which is the variable resistance depends on most steeply.
* Longitudinally, so contraction shortens rather than narrows the airway
Why: Longitudinal muscle would shorten the tube without much affecting its calibre, and so would not produce the resistance change seen in asthma.
* Spirally, as in the intrapulmonary bronchi
Why: The spiral arrangement is found in the intrapulmonary bronchi. The bronchioles, where resistance is regulated, are circular.
* Bronchioles contain no smooth muscle, being held open by cartilage
Why: The reverse: cartilage disappears distally and it is precisely the bronchioles that are muscular and collapsible.
Rationale: Circular muscle around a cartilage-free tube is what makes the bronchiole the site of variable resistance — and the target of a bronchodilator.

### Why the trace lags the effort
Concept: CON-RES-9CD5A82E7529F8
Difficulty: Hard
Q: Plotting her lung volume against intrapleural pressure during breathing gives a loop rather than a single line. What does the loop represent?
*= Airway and tissue resistance, which make volume change lag behind pressure change during both inspiration and expiration
Why: Correct. Because flow must overcome resistance, volume does not follow pressure instantaneously, and the inspiratory and expiratory paths separate into a loop whose width reflects that resistance.
* Measurement error introduced by the patient breathing irregularly
Why: The loop is a reproducible physiological finding, not an artefact, and it widens predictably with airway narrowing.
* The difference between lung and chest-wall compliance
Why: Those combine into the compliance of the system, which sets the slope of the relationship rather than the separation of its two limbs.
* The volume of the anatomical dead space
Why: Dead space is a volume that takes no part in exchange; it does not produce hysteresis on a pressure-volume plot.
Rationale: The slope of the loop is compliance; its width is resistance. Reading the two separately from one plot is what turns a pressure-volume curve into a diagnosis.
## debrief
Asthma is a disease of radius. Circular muscle around a cartilage-free bronchiole narrows the lumen, resistance rises steeply because it depends so heavily on radius, and the frictional component of the work of breathing rises in both phases. On a pressure-volume plot that appears as a wider loop — the slope still reporting compliance, the width now reporting the resistance she is working against.

### Which way this gradient points
Mr Adel Gharib, 57, is asked to blow out as hard and fast as he can into a spirometer.
Concept: CON-RES-FB0423E46781C9
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What pressure relationship moves air out of the lungs?
*= Alveolar pressure above atmospheric — a positive gradient from alveoli to atmosphere
Why: Correct. Air leaves when alveolar pressure exceeds atmospheric, and the gradient's direction is what determines the direction of flow.
* Alveolar pressure below atmospheric
Why: That draws air in; it is the inspiratory condition.
* Equal pressures, with the elastic recoil of the lung squeezing air out
Why: Recoil is what raises alveolar pressure, but with no gradient there is no flow.
* A more negative intrapleural pressure than during inspiration
Why: Intrapleural pressure becomes less negative during expiration, and in a forced effort may become positive.
Rationale: Direction of flow follows direction of gradient, in expiration exactly as in inspiration. Only the sign changes.

### Who does the work now
Concept: CON-RES-58E46604D5D495
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Quiet expiration is passive. When do the expiratory muscles become active?
*= During exercise and during voluntary forced expiration
Why: Correct. Quiet expiration is driven by elastic recoil; the expiratory muscles are recruited when ventilation must rise or when expiration is deliberately forced.
* During quiet expiration, which is muscular throughout
Why: Quiet expiration is passive, driven by recoil of lung and chest wall.
* Only when the airway is obstructed
Why: Obstruction does recruit them, but so do exercise and voluntary effort in a healthy person.
* They are never active; expiration is always passive
Why: Would make a forced blow into a spirometer impossible.
Rationale: Expiration is passive until it is not. Recognising when the expiratory muscles are recruited is what makes accessory-muscle use at rest an abnormal sign.

### What the abdomen contributes
Concept: CON-RES-EEAE5822F0122C
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: How does contraction of the abdominal wall assist a forced expiration?
*= It raises intra-abdominal pressure, pushing the diaphragm upwards and reducing thoracic volume
Why: Correct. The abdominal muscles raise pressure inside the abdomen, which drives the relaxed diaphragm upwards and helps empty the lungs.
* It pulls the ribs upwards and outwards, expanding the chest
Why: That describes an inspiratory action, which would work against expiration.
* It stiffens the chest wall so that elastic recoil is more effective
Why: Invents a mechanism; the abdominal muscles act on the diaphragm through pressure, not by stiffening the thorax.
* It compresses the lower lobes directly through the chest wall
Why: The abdominal wall does not contact the lungs; the diaphragm lies between them.
Rationale: The diaphragm is a two-sided structure, and the abdomen is the other side. Anything that raises abdominal pressure assists expiration and opposes inspiration, which is why abdominal distension and recent abdominal surgery both impair breathing.

### The size of the expiratory pressure
Concept: CON-RES-06C38C46E91ECD
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: During quiet expiration, alveolar pressure rises to approximately what value?
*= About +1 cm H₂O
Why: Correct. Quiet expiration mirrors quiet inspiration in magnitude, with the sign reversed.
* About +10 cm H₂O
Why: An order too large for a quiet breath, though forced expiration can far exceed it.
* About +100 cm H₂O
Why: Approaches the pressure of a cough or a maximal Valsalva, not quiet breathing.
* It stays at zero, as air leaves by recoil alone
Why: Recoil is what raises the pressure; with no gradient no air would move.
Rationale: Quiet breathing is symmetrical and small: about −1 cm H₂O in, about +1 cm H₂O out. Any effort visibly larger than that is doing more work than a resting patient should need to.

### Naming the phases
Concept: CON-RES-566E60BD943370
Difficulty: Challenging
Q: A respiratory cycle is described as having three parts. Name them, and explain why the third matters when the rate rises.
*= Inspiration, expiration and an expiratory pause — and the pause is what shortens first as the rate rises, so a fast rate reduces the time available for the passive part of the cycle
Why: Correct. The cycle includes a pause after expiration, and it is that pause which is lost as respiratory rate increases, which is why a rapidly breathing patient with airway obstruction begins the next breath before the last has finished.
* Inspiration, expiration and an inspiratory pause, which shortens first
Why: The pause follows expiration rather than inspiration; the distinction matters because it is the expiratory time that becomes limiting in obstruction.
* Only inspiration and expiration; there is no pause in the cycle
Why: Omits the pause, and with it the explanation of why an obstructed patient becomes progressively hyperinflated as the rate rises.
* Inspiration, an inspiratory plateau and expiration, with the plateau shortening first
Why: Describes a ventilator waveform rather than the phases of a spontaneous respiratory cycle.
Rationale: The pause is the slack in the system. Losing it is what turns a fast respiratory rate in an obstructed patient into air trapping, because expiration is cut short by the arrival of the next breath.
## debrief
Expiration is the mirror of inspiration in direction and magnitude — about +1 cm H₂O against about −1 — and passive until exercise or effort recruits the abdominal wall to drive the diaphragm upwards. The final decision is the one with clinical consequences: the cycle has a pause, the pause disappears first as the rate rises, and that is how an obstructed patient traps air.

### Naming the property
A boy is born at 29 weeks. Within an hour he is grunting, drawing in between his ribs, and needing oxygen.
Concept: CON-RES-BB16D42253F081
Difficulty: Easy
Q: His lungs are described as poorly compliant. What is compliance?
*= The volume an elastic structure gains in response to a distending force
Why: Correct. Compliance is the change in volume produced by a change in distending pressure — how readily a structure expands.
* The resistance an elastic structure offers to expansion
Why: That is elastance, the reciprocal quantity. Confusing the two inverts every subsequent conclusion.
* The pressure required to hold a structure at a fixed volume
Why: A related measurement, but compliance is defined by the volume gained per unit pressure, not the pressure at a fixed volume.
* The speed with which a structure returns to its resting size
Why: Describes recoil over time rather than the volume-pressure relationship.
Rationale: Volume gained per unit pressure. A poorly compliant lung needs a large pressure for a small volume, which is exactly the work this baby is visibly doing.

### The cell that should have prepared for this
Concept: CON-RES-0A1B54247E50C8
Difficulty: Easy
Q: Which alveolar cell produces surfactant?
*= The type II pneumocyte
Why: Correct. Type II pneumocytes synthesise and secrete surfactant, and also serve as the progenitor cell for the alveolar lining.
* The type I pneumocyte
Why: Type I cells are the thin cells across which gas exchange occurs; they cover most of the surface but do not make surfactant.
* The alveolar macrophage
Why: Macrophages clear debris and organisms from the alveolus and have no secretory role of this kind.
* The bronchiolar club cell
Why: Club cells secrete into the bronchiolar lining and are not the source of alveolar surfactant.
Rationale: Two alveolar cell types with two jobs — one thin for exchange, one cuboidal for secretion and repair. Knowing which is which is what makes the pathology of prematurity predictable.

### What surfactant is opposing
Concept: CON-RES-364A753569C12F
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Which force is the major one opposing inflation of the lung?
*= Alveolar surface tension at the air-liquid interface
Why: Correct. Surface tension at the alveolar lining is the dominant force opposing inflation, exceeding the contribution of the tissue's own elasticity.
* The elastic recoil of the collagen and elastin in the lung tissue
Why: A genuine contributor, and the reason this is chosen — but the surface forces at the air-liquid interface are the larger part.
* The frictional resistance of the conducting airways
Why: Airway resistance opposes flow, not inflation, and belongs to the other component of the work of breathing.
* The weight of the chest wall resting on the lungs
Why: The chest wall's recoil acts outwards at resting volume, and its weight is not what a breath has to overcome.
Rationale: Most of the effort of inflating a lung is spent against surface tension, which is why a substance that lowers surface tension transforms the mechanics.

### Why prematurity does this
Concept: CON-RES-FB2D4322FCC2EB
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: How does prematurity produce respiratory distress syndrome?
*= Surfactant is deficient, so alveolar surface tension is unopposed and the lungs are stiff and prone to collapse
Why: Correct. Surfactant production rises late in gestation; without it, surface tension is unopposed, compliance falls and alveoli collapse at end-expiration.
* The airways are too narrow, so airway resistance is the limiting problem
Why: Attributes a compliance problem to resistance. The distress of respiratory distress syndrome is elastic work, not frictional.
* The respiratory muscles are too weak to generate a gradient
Why: The muscles are working hard, which is exactly what the grunting and recession show.
* The alveoli have not yet formed, so there is no surface for exchange
Why: Alveoli are present at 29 weeks in reduced numbers; the defining problem is the surfactant lining them, not their absence.
Rationale: A missing surface-active layer leaves the largest force opposing inflation unopposed. Everything the baby shows — grunting, recession, the need for pressure — follows from that single deficiency.

### Reading the bottom of the curve
Concept: CON-RES-D9AFAB78E7FD22
Difficulty: Hard
Q: On a pressure-volume curve of his lungs, the initial portion is almost flat. What is happening there?
*= The applied pressure is mainly opening collapsed alveoli, so volume changes little and compliance is low
Why: Correct. At low lung volumes much of the pressure is spent recruiting collapsed alveoli rather than expanding open ones, so the curve is flat and compliance is at its lowest.
* The lung has reached its maximum volume and cannot expand further
Why: Describes the flat portion at the *top* of the curve, where alveoli are already fully distended.
* Airway resistance is highest at low volumes, limiting flow
Why: Resistance does rise at low volumes, but a static pressure-volume curve is measured without flow, so resistance does not shape it.
* The measurement is unreliable below functional residual capacity
Why: The flat initial segment is a real physiological finding and the basis for the strategy of recruiting the lung with sustained pressure.
Rationale: Compliance is not one number; it varies along the curve. Low at the bottom where alveoli must be recruited, greatest in the middle, low again at the top where the lung is already full — which is why both under-inflation and over-inflation cost more work than breathing in the middle of the range.
## debrief
The case runs from a definition to a curve. Compliance is volume gained per unit pressure; the biggest force resisting that gain is alveolar surface tension; type II pneumocytes make the substance that lowers it; and prematurity arrives before they are ready. The flat foot of the pressure-volume curve is what that deficiency looks like when it is measured — pressure being spent opening alveoli rather than filling them.
