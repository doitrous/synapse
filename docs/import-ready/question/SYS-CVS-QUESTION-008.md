# Item

## title
What an electrocardiogram records

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Electrodes are placed on a patient's skin and a tracing is produced.

## question
What is being recorded?

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
The mechanical contraction of the cardiac chambers

## explanation_a
Contraction follows depolarisation but is not what the electrodes detect. The distinction matters: electrical activity can be present with no effective contraction at all, which is why a normal-looking trace never proves the heart is pumping.

## answer_b
The flow of blood through the heart and great vessels

## explanation_b
Flow is what echocardiography and Doppler assess. Students choose this by assuming the commonest cardiac investigation must measure the heart's principal job.

## answer_c
Changes in electrical potential generated by the heart, measured at the body surface

## explanation_c
An electrocardiogram is a body-surface recording of the potential changes the heart generates. Every feature of the trace is a voltage over time, which is why the questions it answers are about rhythm, conduction and the direction of depolarisation rather than about pumping.

## answer_d
The action potential of individual cardiac myocytes

## explanation_d
Single-cell action potentials are recorded with microelectrodes inside the cell. The surface trace is the summed effect of countless cells, and confusing the two makes the shape of the ECG impossible to interpret.

# Item

## title
Electrical activity without a pulse

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A monitor shows organised electrical complexes at a regular rate, but no pulse can be felt.

## question
What does this demonstrate about the electrocardiogram?

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
The trace must be an artefact, since organised electrical activity always produces contraction

## explanation_a
Dismissing the trace assumes electrical and mechanical activity are inseparable, which is the belief the situation disproves. Rejecting data that contradicts an assumption is the reasoning error the item targets.

## answer_b
The electrodes are recording skeletal muscle rather than cardiac activity

## explanation_b
Skeletal muscle does produce artefact, and it is worth recognising. But it appears as irregular noise rather than organised complexes at a regular rate.

## answer_c
The heart is contracting but the pulse is too weak to palpate at the wrist

## explanation_c
A weak pulse is a real possibility and is why other sites are checked. But it describes a measurement limitation rather than the principle at stake, which is that the trace reports only electrical events.

## answer_d
The trace reports depolarisation only, so it cannot confirm that contraction or output is occurring

## explanation_d
Depolarisation is the trigger for contraction, not the contraction itself. An organised trace with no output means the electrical sequence is intact while the mechanical response has failed. This is why the recording is never sufficient on its own and is always interpreted alongside a pulse or another measure of output.

# Item

## title
How the signal reaches the skin

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
The heart lies deep within the thorax, yet its electrical activity is recorded from electrodes on the limbs and chest wall.

## question
How does the signal get there?

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Along the cardiac nerves, which carry the potential change to the surface

## explanation_a
Nerves carry their own impulses; they do not relay the myocardium's potentials outwards. This is chosen by students looking for a dedicated conducting pathway when the mechanism needs none.

## answer_b
Body fluids conduct the potential changes to the skin, where they can be recorded

## explanation_b
The body is largely salt water, and an electrolyte solution conducts. The heart's depolarisation therefore creates potential differences throughout the surrounding tissue, extending to the skin. No special pathway is required — the whole body acts as the conductor, which is why electrode position determines what each lead sees.

## answer_c
The signal is transmitted mechanically through the chest wall and converted by the electrode

## explanation_c
Electrodes detect voltage, not movement. This confuses the ECG with a device that senses pulsation, such as a plethysmograph.

## answer_d
The pericardial fluid conducts the signal to the pleura and then to the skin

## explanation_d
Pericardial fluid does conduct, but so does every tissue between the heart and the skin; singling it out invents a specific route where the conduction is general.

# Item

## title
Why lead position changes the trace

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
The same cardiac depolarisation produces a tall positive deflection in one lead and a deep negative one in another, recorded simultaneously.

## question
How can one event give opposite deflections?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Each lead measures the potential difference along its own axis, so the same wave of depolarisation approaches one and recedes from another

## explanation_a
A lead is a pair of points with a direction between them. Depolarisation moving towards a lead's positive pole gives an upward deflection; the same wave is moving away from a lead pointing the other way, so that lead records a downward one. Twelve leads are twelve viewpoints on one event, which is why the pattern across them locates the event in space.

## answer_b
The leads are recording different beats, offset in time

## explanation_b
All twelve leads sample the same electrical activity simultaneously, which is what allows them to be compared. This removes the simultaneity that makes the comparison meaningful.

## answer_c
Some leads amplify and others invert the signal electronically

## explanation_c
The polarity is a consequence of where the electrodes sit relative to the heart, not of the machine's processing. This attributes an anatomical fact to the equipment.

## answer_d
Positive deflections come from atrial activity and negative ones from ventricular activity

## explanation_d
Both chambers can produce deflections in either direction, depending on the lead. This confuses which chamber is depolarising with which way the wave is travelling relative to a viewpoint.

## title
Electrodes and leads

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A standard twelve-lead electrocardiogram is recorded.

## question
How many electrodes are placed on the patient?

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Twelve — one for each lead

## explanation_a
This is the natural assumption from the name and the commonest error. It treats a lead as a physical object rather than as a viewpoint constructed from electrodes.

## answer_b
Six — all on the chest

## explanation_b
Six chest electrodes are placed, but the limb electrodes are omitted here. Without them there are no limb leads and no frontal-plane information at all.

## answer_c
Ten — one on each limb and six on the chest

## explanation_c
Four limb electrodes and six chest electrodes make ten. The twelve leads are derived from them: three bipolar limb leads, three augmented limb leads and six chest leads. Leads outnumber electrodes because each lead is a calculated view rather than a separate wire.

## answer_d
Fourteen — ten as above, plus four for the posterior leads

## explanation_d
Additional posterior or right-sided electrodes are used for specific purposes, but they are not part of the standard twelve-lead recording. This adds a real extension to the standard set and calls it standard.

# Item

## title
Twelve views from ten points

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Ten electrodes produce twelve leads. Six of those leads look at the heart in the frontal plane and six in the horizontal plane.

## question
How are twelve views obtained from ten points?

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Each electrode is sampled twice, once as positive and once as negative

## explanation_a
Sampling an electrode in both polarities would give inverted copies of the same view, not new ones. This produces redundancy rather than additional information.

## answer_b
Each lead is a comparison between points — some between two electrodes, some between one electrode and a computed reference — so combinations exceed the number of electrodes

## explanation_b
A view is defined by which points are compared, not by how many wires exist. Comparing pairs of limb electrodes gives the bipolar limb leads; comparing each limb electrode against an average of the others gives the augmented leads; comparing each chest electrode against a central reference gives the chest leads. Ten points yield far more than ten possible comparisons.

## answer_c
Two of the electrodes record two leads each because they sit over the heart

## explanation_c
Proximity to the heart affects how large a deflection an electrode sees, not how many leads it contributes to. This attaches lead count to anatomical position.

## answer_d
The six chest electrodes each generate two leads, one anterior and one posterior

## explanation_d
Each chest electrode contributes one lead in the standard set; posterior leads require additional electrodes. This invents a second view from a single point.

# Item

## title
A vector along a lead's axis

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A wave of depolarisation travels through the ventricles in a direction exactly aligned with the positive axis of one particular lead.

## question
What does that lead record?

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
No deflection, because the wave passes directly along the lead without crossing it

## explanation_a
This describes what happens when a vector is perpendicular to a lead, not aligned with it. The two cases are exact opposites, and confusing them inverts every axis determination that follows.

## answer_b
A biphasic deflection, equally positive and negative

## explanation_b
A biphasic deflection is what a perpendicular vector produces, since the wave approaches and recedes equally. Aligned travel gives approach only.

## answer_c
A small positive deflection, because the signal is spread along the whole axis

## explanation_c
Spreading along the axis is precisely what concentrates the signal in that lead's direction. This reverses the geometry.

## answer_d
Its maximal positive deflection

## explanation_d
A vector aligned with a lead's positive axis projects entirely onto it, so that lead records the largest upward deflection it can. Every other lead sees only a component. This is the anchor for reading axis: the lead with the tallest positive deflection is the one the vector points most nearly towards.

# Item

## title
Projecting a vector onto a lead

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A depolarisation vector points at an angle to a lead's positive axis — neither aligned with it nor perpendicular to it.

## question
What determines the size of the deflection that lead records?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The component of the vector lying along that lead's axis, which shrinks as the angle between them grows

## explanation_a
Each lead records the projection of the vector onto its own axis. At zero degrees the whole vector projects and the deflection is maximal; as the angle opens the projection shortens and the deflection falls; at ninety degrees nothing projects and the lead is isoelectric. Deflection size is a geometric consequence, which is what makes the twelve leads solvable as a system.

## answer_b
The distance between the heart and that lead's electrodes

## explanation_b
Distance does influence signal amplitude in general, which is why chest leads give larger deflections than limb leads. But it does not explain differences between leads at similar distances, and it is not what the vector model describes.

## answer_c
The duration of the depolarisation, since longer waves produce larger deflections

## explanation_c
Duration determines the width of a complex, not its height. This confuses the two axes of the trace.

## answer_d
The total mass of myocardium depolarising, which is the same for every lead

## explanation_d
Muscle mass does set the size of the vector itself — which is why hypertrophy produces larger complexes — but being the same for every lead, it cannot explain why leads differ. A constant cannot account for a variation.

# Item

## title
The isoelectric lead

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
In one limb lead the QRS complex has positive and negative areas that are almost exactly equal, so the net deflection is close to zero.

## question
What does that tell you about the mean QRS vector?

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
It is aligned with that lead's positive axis

## explanation_a
Alignment gives the maximal positive deflection, not a null one. This is the inversion of the correct relationship, and it is the reason isoelectric leads are so often misread.

## answer_b
It is close to perpendicular to that lead's axis

## explanation_b
A vector at right angles to a lead projects almost nothing onto it, so approach and recession cancel and the net deflection is near zero. That makes the isoelectric lead the most informative one on the trace: it fixes the vector to a line at ninety degrees to that lead, and the polarity in another lead resolves which of the two directions it points.

## answer_c
It is very small, so no lead records much

## explanation_c
A small vector would give small complexes everywhere, and the stem describes substantial positive and negative areas in this lead. The size is not the issue; the cancellation is.

## answer_d
The depolarisation is disorganised, as in fibrillation

## explanation_d
Disorganised activity produces no coherent QRS complex at all. The stem describes a well-formed complex whose areas balance, which is a statement about direction rather than about organisation.

## title
Equal deflections in two leads

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Lead I looks along the horizontal, at zero degrees. Lead II looks down and to the left, at sixty degrees. A depolarisation vector produces positive waves of equal size in both.

## question
Where must the vector point?

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
At zero degrees, along lead I

## explanation_a
At zero degrees the vector would project fully onto lead I and only partly onto lead II, giving unequal deflections. Students choose this by taking the first of the two named leads as the reference.

## answer_b
At sixty degrees, along lead II

## explanation_b
By the same reasoning as A but in the other direction: full projection onto lead II and a smaller one onto lead I. Equality requires the vector to favour neither.

## answer_c
At thirty degrees, midway between the two axes

## explanation_c
Equal projections require equal angles to the two axes. Midway between zero and sixty degrees is thirty, so a vector at plus thirty degrees makes the same angle with each and produces equal positive waves in both. Reading axis is this argument run repeatedly across the leads.

## answer_d
At ninety degrees, perpendicular to the average of the two

## explanation_d
At ninety degrees the vector would be well away from both axes and would give a much smaller deflection in lead I than in lead II. This picks a symmetrical-sounding angle without projecting onto either axis.

# Item

## title
Working out the axis from the trace

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
On a twelve-lead recording, the QRS complex is strongly positive in lead I and strongly negative in lead aVF. Lead aVR is close to isoelectric.

## question
Roughly where does the mean QRS vector point?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Up and to the left, at around minus sixty degrees — a left axis deviation

## explanation_a
Positive in lead I means the vector points leftwards; negative in aVF means it points upwards rather than downwards. Together those place it in the upper left quadrant. The near-isoelectric aVR, which looks up and to the right at minus one hundred and fifty degrees, puts the vector close to perpendicular to that — around minus sixty degrees. Three leads, each constraining the answer, converge on one direction.

## answer_b
Down and to the left, at around plus sixty degrees — a normal axis

## explanation_b
A vector at plus sixty degrees would be strongly positive in aVF, not negative. This is chosen by students who read lead I correctly and then assume the axis must be normal.

## answer_c

## title
Why a pause can provoke an arrhythmia

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
In a patient whose ventricular repolarisation is prolonged, runs of a rapid ventricular arrhythmia begin characteristically after a pause — for example, after the compensatory pause that follows a premature beat.

## question
Why does the pause matter?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The pause lengthens the following action potential further, widening the window for an early afterdepolarisation to reach threshold

## explanation_a
Action potential duration lengthens as rate falls, so the beat after a pause has the longest repolarisation of all. In a heart whose repolarisation is already prolonged, that beat offers the widest possible window for an early afterdepolarisation to develop and reach threshold. The pause is not incidental to the timing — it is what creates the opportunity.

## answer_b
The pause allows intracellular calcium to accumulate, producing a delayed afterdepolarisation

The pause reduces coronary perfusion, making the myocardium ischaemic

## answer_d
The pause allows the sinoatrial node to lose dominance, so an ectopic focus takes over

## explanation_d
Escape rhythms do emerge when the sinoatrial node is slow, but an escape beat is slow and orderly, not a rapid arrhythmia. This substitutes a mechanism of bradycardia for one of tachycardia.

## title
Delayed afterdepolarisations

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
An abnormal depolarisation occurs after repolarisation is complete, when the cell has returned to its resting potential. It reaches threshold and triggers an action potential.

## question
What underlies this, and what makes it more likely?

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Incomplete inactivation of fast sodium channels, made worse by bradycardia

## explanation_a
A persistent sodium current does prolong the action potential and favours the early type. This names a real current and attaches it to the wrong type and the wrong rate dependence.

## answer_b
Reduced extracellular potassium, which hyperpolarises the cell past threshold

## explanation_b
Hyperpolarisation moves the membrane further from threshold, not past it. The direction of the effect contradicts the outcome described.

## answer_c
Intracellular calcium overload, which drives an inward current after repolarisation and is made worse by fast rates and by catecholamines

## explanation_c
When the cell is loaded with calcium, its handling mechanisms release some of it after repolarisation, and extruding that calcium generates a net inward current that depolarises the cell again. Anything increasing calcium loading — a fast rate delivering more calcium per unit time, or catecholamines increasing entry — makes the disturbance larger and more likely to reach threshold. This is why the resulting arrhythmias appear on exertion rather than at rest.

## answer_d
Slowed conduction through partially depolarised tissue, allowing an impulse to re-enter

## explanation_d
Re-entry is a circuit phenomenon requiring a path of tissue, and it produces arrhythmia by a different route entirely. This substitutes the other major arrhythmia mechanism for triggered activity.

# Item

## title
Two triggered mechanisms, opposite rate dependence

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Two patients each have a ventricular arrhythmia arising from triggered activity. In the first, runs begin after pauses and are suppressed when the heart rate is increased. In the second, runs begin during exertion and settle at rest.

## question
What distinguishes the two mechanisms, and why do they respond to rate in opposite ways?

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Both are early afterdepolarisations; the difference is which chamber they arise in

## explanation_a
Chamber of origin does not determine rate dependence, and treating both as the same mechanism cannot explain opposite responses to the same intervention. The observation is precisely that they differ.

## answer_b
The first is early — it needs a long action potential, which pauses provide and fast rates abolish. The second is delayed — it needs calcium loading, which fast rates and catecholamines increase

## explanation_b
Each mechanism has a different requirement, and rate supplies one while removing the other. An early afterdepolarisation needs time during repolarisation, so slowing helps it and speeding up abolishes it. A delayed afterdepolarisation needs calcium accumulation, so speeding up feeds it and rest starves it. Their opposite behaviour follows from what each one needs, which is why the clinical pattern identifies the mechanism.

## answer_c
The first is triggered activity and the second is re-entry, which is always exertional

## explanation_c
The stem specifies triggered activity for both. Re-entrant arrhythmias also occur with exertion, so exertional onset alone cannot establish re-entry.

## answer_d
The first arises in Purkinje tissue and the second in working myocardium, which differ in refractory period

## explanation_d
Both mechanisms occur in both tissues. Refractory periods do differ between them, but that difference does not produce the opposite rate dependence described.
