# Item

## title
Chambers of the heart

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A heart is opened along its long axis in the dissecting room and the cavities within it are counted.

## question
How are the heart's chambers arranged?

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Two chambers: one receiving and one pumping

## explanation_a
A two-chambered heart is the fish plan, in which one atrium and one ventricle drive a single circuit. This is chosen by students reasoning from function alone — blood comes in, blood goes out — without accounting for the two separate circulations a mammal must serve.

## answer_b
Three chambers: two atria and one ventricle

## explanation_b
This is the amphibian arrangement, and it is the most instructive wrong answer: the single ventricle is exactly why oxygenated and deoxygenated blood mix in a frog. Students choose it when they remember that the atria are paired but have not fixed that the ventricles are too.

## answer_c
Four chambers: two atria and two ventricles

## explanation_c
Two atria receive, two ventricles pump, and the pairing is what keeps the pulmonary and systemic circulations separate. The interatrial and interventricular septa are not decoration — they are the walls that make two circuits out of one organ.

## answer_d
Four chambers: three atria and one ventricle

## explanation_d
No vertebrate heart has three atria. This catches students who have memorised "four chambers" as a number without attaching it to which classes of chamber make up the four.

# Item

## title
Where a ventricular septal defect usually sits

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
The interventricular septum has a large lower muscular part and a small upper membranous part. Ventricular septal defects are far more common in one of the two.

## question
Which part is usually involved, and why?

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The muscular part, because it is much the larger of the two and so offers more surface for a defect

## explanation_a
Area alone is the wrong argument. The muscular septum is indeed larger, and muscular defects do occur, but they are the minority. This is chosen by students reasoning statistically from size rather than from how the septum is built.

## answer_b
The membranous part, because it is formed last and by the fusion of several separate contributions

## explanation_b
The membranous septum is the final piece to close, and it is completed where the endocardial cushions, the muscular septum and the outflow-tract septum all meet. A closure that depends on three structures meeting is the one most likely to fail, which is why the membranous septum is the usual site.

## answer_c
The membranous part, because it is under the greatest mechanical stress during systole

## explanation_c
The conclusion is right and the reason is not, which makes this the strongest distractor. The membranous septum is not the part bearing peak wall stress — the thick muscular septum does the work of contraction. The defect is developmental in origin, not mechanical.

## answer_d
The muscular part, because it must thicken enormously after birth as left ventricular pressure rises

## explanation_d
Postnatal thickening of the muscular septum is real, but it is a normal adaptation, not a mechanism for making holes. Students choose this by reaching for a postnatal event when the explanation lies in fetal development.

## title
Cusps of the aortic valve

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
The aortic orifice lies at the upper end of the aortic vestibule, surrounded by a fibrous ring that carries the valve cusps.

## question
How many cusps does the aortic valve normally have?

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
One

## explanation_a
A single-cusp aortic valve is a rare severe congenital malformation, not the normal arrangement. Students pick it only when guessing.

## answer_b
Two

## explanation_b
A bicuspid aortic valve is the commonest congenital cardiac malformation and matters greatly — it stenoses and calcifies decades earlier than a normal valve. But it is an abnormality, and this is the trap: a student who has just learnt about bicuspid disease may reach for it as though it were the baseline.

## answer_c
Three

## explanation_c
Three semilunar cusps sit in a fibrous ring at the aortic orifice, each backed by a dilatation of the aortic root. Three is also the pulmonary valve's number, which is worth pairing: the two semilunar valves share a plan, while the two atrioventricular valves do not.

## answer_d
Four

## explanation_d
A quadricuspid aortic valve exists but is very rare. This catches students who assume the aortic valve must have one more cusp than the mitral, counting upwards rather than recalling the semilunar plan.

# Item

## title
What the fibrous ring at a valve orifice does

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Each of the four cardiac orifices is encircled by a ring of dense fibrous tissue to which the valve cusps are attached. Together these rings form the fibrous skeleton of the heart.

## question
What does that ring provide?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
A non-distensible anchorage that holds the orifice at a constant size while the surrounding muscle contracts

## explanation_a
The cusps can only meet if the ring they hang from does not widen. Dense collagen keeps the orifice dimension fixed through systole, so the free edges still reach one another when the ventricle changes shape around them. When disease dilates a ring, the cusps stop meeting and the valve leaks even though the cusps themselves are normal.

## answer_b
A contractile sphincter that actively narrows the orifice to help the cusps close

## explanation_b
The rings contain no muscle and do not contract. This is chosen by students who reason that closure must be actively driven, when in fact valve closure is entirely passive and pressure-driven.

## answer_c
An elastic ring that stretches during filling and recoils to snap the valve shut

## explanation_c
Elastic recoil is an appealing mechanism but the opposite of what the ring is for: the tissue is deliberately inelastic. Students choose this by transferring the elastic-artery model onto a structure whose whole purpose is to not stretch.

## answer_d
A conductive bridge carrying the impulse from the atria to the ventricles

## explanation_d
The fibrous skeleton does exactly the reverse — it electrically insulates atria from ventricles, so the only route through is the atrioventricular bundle. This is a productive error to name, because it inverts one of the fibrous skeleton's two jobs.

## title
Where the coronary arteries begin

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
The root of the ascending aorta carries three slight dilatations, one behind each cusp of the aortic valve. A diagram shows the root opened out with the three sinuses lettered.

## question
What arises from these sinuses?

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Nothing — they are pockets that catch blood to help the cusps close, and no vessel arises from them

## explanation_a
The pocket function is real: blood eddying into a sinus behind a cusp helps float it back across the orifice. The error is treating that as the whole story. This is the strongest distractor because its physiology is correct and its anatomy is incomplete.

## answer_b
The bronchial arteries, one from each of the three sinuses

## explanation_b
The bronchial arteries arise from the descending thoracic aorta, well downstream. Students choose this when they know that some small arteries leave the aorta early without fixing which ones or where.

## answer_c
A coronary artery from each of the three sinuses

## explanation_c
Only two of the three give rise to a coronary artery. This is the commonest error, made by students who assume a symmetrical arrangement — three cusps, three sinuses, therefore three vessels.

## answer_d
The right and left coronary arteries, from two of the three sinuses

## explanation_d
The right coronary artery arises from the right (anterior) aortic sinus and the left from the left (posterior) sinus, while the third gives rise to nothing and is called the non-coronary sinus for that reason. The coronaries are the first branches of the aorta, which is why the heart is perfused before anything else.

## title
Why one aortic sinus carries no vessel

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Of the three aortic sinuses, two give rise to coronary arteries and one does not.

## question
Which sinus gives rise to no coronary artery, and how is it named?

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The right sinus, called the non-coronary sinus

## explanation_a
The right sinus gives rise to the right coronary artery. This is a straight inversion, chosen by students who recall that one sinus is empty but not which.

## answer_b
The posterior sinus, called the non-coronary sinus

## explanation_b
The posterior aortic sinus gives rise to no vessel and takes its name from that absence. The naming is worth noticing: the right and left sinuses are named for the coronary arteries they supply, so the third had to be named for what it lacks.

## answer_c
The left sinus, called the accessory sinus

## explanation_c
The left sinus gives rise to the left coronary artery, and "accessory sinus" is not a term in use. This distractor pairs a wrong sinus with an invented name, and is chosen by students who half-recall the naming logic.

## answer_d
The posterior sinus, called the septal sinus

## explanation_d
The sinus is correct and the name is not. This is the closest distractor, and separating the two halves is the point: a student who knows which sinus is empty still has to know that its name records the absence rather than a position.

## subject

## status
Draft

## vignette
A diagram marks the anatomical projections of all four cardiac valves on the anterior chest wall. One projection lies behind the left margin of the sternum, opposite the third left intercostal space.

## question
Which valve is that?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Aortic

## explanation_a
The aortic valve projects behind the left sternal margin at about the level of the third left intercostal space, making it the highest of the two left-sided valves. Its position here, and not at the right second space where it is auscultated, is the whole reason auscultation areas are named separately from valve positions.

Pulmonary

## explanation_b
The pulmonary valve lies behind the medial end of the third left costal cartilage — slightly higher and more lateral than the aortic. This is the closest distractor, because the two semilunar valves sit close together, and a student who has fixed only "third costal region on the left" cannot separate them.

## explanation_c
The mitral valve lies lower, behind the left half of the sternum at the fourth costal cartilage. Students choose it by knowing it is a left-sided valve without holding the vertical order of the two.

## answer_d
Tricuspid

## explanation_d
The tricuspid valve projects behind the centre of the sternum, lower and more medial. Choosing it here confuses the right-sided atrioventricular valve with a left-sided semilunar one.

## subject

## vignette
A student marks the aortic valve's anatomical projection behind the left sternal margin, then places the stethoscope there expecting the aortic component of the second heart sound to be loudest.

## question
What has the student misunderstood?

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Nothing — the anatomical projection is where each valve is best heard

## explanation_a
This is the belief being tested, stated plainly. If it were true, the four auscultation areas would simply be the four projections and would need no separate names.

## answer_b
The aortic valve lies on the right of the sternum, so the projection was marked on the wrong side

## explanation_b
The projection was marked correctly: the aortic valve genuinely lies behind the left sternal margin. It is the listening point that is on the right. Students choose this by working backwards from the auscultation area to where the valve must be, which is exactly the inversion the concept warns against.

## answer_c
Sound travels with the blood, so the valve is heard downstream of where it sits

## explanation_c
Ejected blood enters the ascending aorta and runs up and to the right, carrying the sound with it to the right second intercostal space. The valve's own position and its listening point are two different facts, and each has to be learnt on its own terms.

## answer_d
The second heart sound is produced by the pulmonary valve alone, so the aortic valve has no listening point

## explanation_d
The second sound has both aortic and pulmonary components, and their separation in inspiration is a normal finding students are examined on. This distractor catches anyone who has attached the second sound to one valve only.

B

It pulls the cusps together to close the valve

## explanation_a
Valve closure is passive and pressure-driven — the cusps are pushed together by blood in the ventricle, not pulled. This is the intuitive answer and the one worth dismantling: if the muscles closed the valve, a valve would not close in a heart with damaged papillary muscles, and it does.

## explanation_d
The semilunar valves open when ventricular pressure exceeds arterial pressure, and no tension is transmitted through the fibrous skeleton to them. This catches students who look for a mechanical linkage between the two valve pairs where the link is purely one of pressure and timing.

## title
The moderator band and the anterior papillary muscle

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A muscular bundle crosses the cavity of the right ventricle from the interventricular septum to the base of the anterior papillary muscle, carrying part of the conducting system within it.

## question
What does this arrangement achieve?

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
It shortens the distance blood must travel from the tricuspid orifice to the pulmonary outflow

## explanation_a
The band crosses the cavity but does not channel blood, and the flow path is set by the chamber's shape rather than by a bundle running through it. This is chosen by students who see a structure crossing a cavity and assume it must direct flow.

## answer_b
It carries the right bundle branch directly to the anterior papillary muscle, so that muscle contracts early

## explanation_b
The moderator band conveys the right bundle branch across the ventricular cavity, delivering the impulse to the anterior papillary muscle without waiting for it to spread through the free wall. The largest papillary muscle therefore tightens its chordae before ventricular pressure rises, which is exactly the timing the tricuspid valve needs.

## answer_c
It prevents over-distension of the right ventricle by acting as a mechanical restraint

## explanation_c
The name invites this reading — a band that "moderates" distension — and it is the historical reason for the term. It is the strongest distractor precisely because the name encodes an obsolete idea; the band's real significance is conductive.

## answer_d
It anchors the tricuspid valve's septal cusp to the anterior wall

## explanation_d
The band runs to the papillary muscle, not to a cusp, and the septal cusp is served by its own small muscle and by direct septal chordae. Students choose this by assuming any structure reaching the valve apparatus must attach to a cusp.

## title
Great-vessel connections in transposition

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
An echocardiogram in a cyanosed neonate reports that the aorta arises from the right ventricle.

## question
Which vessel arises from the left ventricle in this arrangement, and what circulation results?

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
The aorta also arises from the left ventricle, so both ventricles feed the systemic circulation

## explanation_a
A single vessel cannot arise from both ventricles in transposition — that describes an overriding aorta, a different lesion. Students choose this by reasoning that the aorta must still serve the body somehow, without following where each ventricle's outflow actually goes.

## answer_b
The superior vena cava, so systemic venous blood is returned directly to the lungs

## explanation_b
The venae cavae are veins entering the right atrium and never arise from a ventricle. This catches students who search for any vessel that would restore a workable circuit rather than reading the ventriculo-arterial connections.

## answer_c
The pulmonary trunk, so the two circulations run in parallel rather than in series

## explanation_c
With the aorta on the right ventricle and the pulmonary trunk on the left, deoxygenated blood returns to the body and oxygenated blood returns to the lungs. The circuits become two closed loops side by side instead of one continuous circuit, which is why oxygenated blood never reaches the tissues.

## answer_d
The pulmonary trunk, so the circulations remain in series but with reversed pressures

## explanation_d
The vessel is right and the consequence is wrong, which makes this the strongest distractor. Reversed pressures would still deliver oxygenated blood to the body; what actually happens is that the two circuits stop connecting at all.

# Item

## title
Why a shunt keeps a transposed circulation alive

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
In transposition of the great arteries the systemic and pulmonary circulations run as two parallel loops. A neonate with transposition and no other defect is profoundly cyanosed within hours, while one who also has a large atrial septal defect is less so.

## question
Why does the additional defect make the difference?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
It lets the two parallel loops exchange blood, so some oxygenated blood crosses into the systemic circuit

## explanation_a
Two closed parallel loops can sustain no oxygen delivery at all, however well each one works. Survival depends entirely on a connection between them — an atrial or ventricular defect, or a patent ductus — through which oxygenated blood can cross into the systemic loop. The defect is not an additional problem; it is the only thing making the arrangement survivable.

## answer_b
It lowers pulmonary vascular resistance, allowing the lungs to oxygenate more blood

## explanation_b
The lungs in transposition are already receiving and oxygenating blood perfectly well — that blood simply returns to the lungs again. Raising pulmonary oxygenation cannot help when the oxygenated blood has no route to the body. This distractor catches students who treat cyanosis as always a problem of gas exchange.

## answer_c
It decompresses the right ventricle, which is now working against systemic pressure

## explanation_c
The right ventricle does face systemic pressure in transposition, and that matters in the long term. But decompressing it would not add one molecule of oxygen to systemic blood, and the infant's immediate problem is oxygen delivery, not ventricular load.

## answer_d
It allows the ductus arteriosus to close safely, removing an abnormal shunt

## explanation_d
This inverts the situation: the ductus is one of the connections keeping the child alive, and its closure worsens the cyanosis. Students choose this by applying the general rule that fetal shunts should close after birth, without asking what this particular circulation depends on.
