## title
Which volume remains in the lungs at the end of a normal, unforced expiration?

## question
Which volume remains in the lungs at the end of a normal, unforced expiration?

## subject
resp

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A subject breathes quietly into a spirometer. At the end of one ordinary expiration, with no
additional effort made, they pause. The question concerns the gas still present in the lungs at
that moment.

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Residual volume

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Residual volume is the gas that remains after a **maximal forced** expiration — the
volume that can never be voluntarily expelled, because the airways close and the chest wall
reaches its limit before the lungs are empty. The stem describes an ordinary, unforced
expiration, at the end of which the subject could still breathe out considerably more. Residual
volume is one *component* of what is left at that moment, not the whole of it.

## answer_b
Expiratory reserve volume

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Expiratory reserve volume is the volume that can *still be forced out* after a normal
expiration — so it describes gas the subject is capable of expelling, not the total remaining in
the chest. It is the other component of the answer. Naming it alone leaves out residual volume,
which is also still present.

## answer_c
Functional residual capacity

## explanation_c
Correct. **Functional residual capacity (FRC) is the volume of gas remaining in the lungs at the
end of a normal, quiet expiration** — the resting equilibrium volume of the respiratory system,
typically about 2.5 L in an adult.

The name is worth unpacking, because it encodes the definition. It is *residual* in that the gas
is left behind rather than exhaled; it is *functional* in that it is what the lung is actually
working with between breaths under ordinary conditions; and it is a *capacity* rather than a
volume, which in respiratory terminology signals that it is the sum of more than one volume:

**FRC = expiratory reserve volume + residual volume**

Both distractors above are those two components. At the end of quiet expiration, the subject
could still force out their expiratory reserve volume, and after that the residual volume would
remain regardless of effort. Together they are the FRC.

FRC exists because of a **mechanical balance**: at this point the inward elastic recoil of the
lungs exactly opposes the outward recoil of the chest wall, so the respiratory system sits at
rest with no muscle activity required. That is why quiet expiration is passive — the system is
simply returning to this equilibrium.

Its physiological importance is that it acts as a **buffer**. Because a substantial reservoir of
gas is always present, blood continues to be oxygenated during the expiratory pause, and arterial
oxygen and carbon dioxide do not swing with each breath. Without an FRC, gas exchange would be
intermittent and blood gases would oscillate with every respiratory cycle.

FRC also changes predictably in disease, which is what makes it examinable: it is **raised** in
emphysema, where loss of elastic recoil allows air trapping and hyperinflation, and **reduced** in
pulmonary fibrosis and in conditions that splint the chest or push the diaphragm up.

## answer_d
Vital capacity

## explanation_d
Incorrect, and it names very nearly the opposite quantity. Vital capacity is the largest volume
that can be *moved* — the volume expelled by a maximal expiration following a maximal
inspiration. It is gas that leaves the lungs, whereas the stem asks about gas that stays. This
option picks the student matching on the word "capacity" without checking whether the quantity
describes movement or residue.

## answer_e
Tidal volume

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Tidal volume is the volume moved in and out with each ordinary breath, about 500 mL.
It is the correct volume associated with quiet breathing, which is what makes it tempting here —
but it is the amount *exchanged* during the breath, not the amount remaining once the breath is
over. The stem specifies what is still in the lungs after expiration has finished.

QST-RES-FRC-FORMULA-001

## title
A subject has a residual volume of 1.2 L and an expiratory reserve volume of 1.3 L. What is their functional residual capacity?

## question
A subject has a residual volume of 1.2 L and an expiratory reserve volume of 1.3 L. What is their functional residual capacity?

## subject
resp

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
Lung volume measurements in a healthy adult give a tidal volume of 0.5 L, an inspiratory
reserve volume of 3.0 L, an expiratory reserve volume of 1.3 L and a residual volume of 1.2 L.

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
1.2 L

## explanation_a
Incorrect. 1.2 L is the residual volume alone. It is part of the functional residual capacity
but not the whole of it: at the end of a quiet expiration the subject has not yet performed the
forced expiration that would leave only residual volume behind, so their expiratory reserve
volume is still in the chest as well.

## answer_b
2.5 L

## explanation_b
Correct. **Functional residual capacity = expiratory reserve volume + residual volume**, so

**FRC = 1.3 L + 1.2 L = 2.5 L**

The reason those are the two terms follows directly from the definition. FRC is the gas remaining
after a *normal* expiration. From that point the subject could still forcibly exhale their
**expiratory reserve volume** (1.3 L), and once that was gone the **residual volume** (1.2 L)
would remain no matter how hard they tried. Everything still in the chest at the end of quiet
expiration therefore falls into one of those two categories.

The other two figures in the stem are deliberately not part of this sum. **Tidal volume** is
exchanged during the breath and has already left the lungs at end-expiration. **Inspiratory
reserve volume** is gas the subject could still breathe *in*, so it is not in the lungs at all.

It is worth holding the family of capacities together, since each is a sum of volumes and they
are examined interchangeably:

- **Inspiratory capacity** = tidal volume + inspiratory reserve volume = 0.5 + 3.0 = **3.5 L**
- **Vital capacity** = inspiratory reserve + tidal + expiratory reserve = 3.0 + 0.5 + 1.3 = **4.8 L**
- **Functional residual capacity** = expiratory reserve + residual = **2.5 L**
- **Total lung capacity** = vital capacity + residual volume = 4.8 + 1.2 = **6.0 L**

The general rule that prevents most errors here: a **volume** is a single measured compartment,
while a **capacity** is always the sum of two or more volumes. If a question asks for a capacity
and you have quoted a single volume, the answer is incomplete.

Note also that residual volume is one of the two terms in FRC, and residual volume cannot be
measured by spirometry — which is why FRC cannot be either, and why it requires an indirect
method such as helium dilution.

## answer_c
1.8 L

## explanation_c
Incorrect, and it is the figure obtained by adding tidal volume to residual volume
(0.5 + 1.2 = 1.7 L) or by similar mis-selection from the stem's four numbers. Tidal volume does
not belong in the FRC: it has already been exhaled by the end of a quiet expiration, which is
exactly the moment FRC describes. This option catches the student who has remembered that FRC is
a sum of two volumes but chosen the wrong pair.

## answer_d
4.3 L

## explanation_d
Incorrect. 4.3 L is tidal volume plus inspiratory reserve volume plus residual volume
(0.5 + 3.0 + 1.2). It wrongly includes the **inspiratory** reserve volume, which is gas the
subject has not yet inhaled — it is not in the lungs at end-expiration and cannot form part of
what remains there. This distractor separates students who are adding plausible-looking numbers
from those who are reasoning about which gas is physically present.

## answer_e
6.0 L

## explanation_e
Incorrect: 6.0 L is the **total lung capacity**, the sum of all four volumes in the stem. It is
the volume in the lungs after a maximal *inspiration*, not after a normal expiration — the
opposite extreme of the respiratory cycle. This option picks the student who has added
everything available rather than selecting the two components the question asks for.

## id
QST-RES-FRC-NOT-SPIROMETRY-001

## title
Why can functional residual capacity not be measured with a spirometer?

## question
Why can functional residual capacity not be measured with a spirometer?

## subject
resp

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A student records a full spirometry trace — quiet tidal breathing, a maximal inspiration and a
maximal forced expiration — and is then asked to read the functional residual capacity off the
trace. They are unable to, and are asked to explain why not.

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Functional residual capacity is too small for a spirometer to resolve accurately

## explanation_a
Incorrect. FRC is around 2.5 L, one of the larger quantities in respiratory physiology and far
above any resolution limit — a spirometer measures a 500 mL tidal volume without difficulty. The
obstacle is not precision but access: the problem is that part of the FRC never passes through
the instrument at all. This option assumes a technical limitation where the barrier is a matter
of principle.

## answer_b
Functional residual capacity varies with each breath, so no single value can be recorded

## explanation_b
Incorrect. FRC is in fact one of the more *stable* lung volumes, because it is the mechanical
equilibrium at which inward lung recoil balances outward chest wall recoil — the respiratory
system returns to it at the end of every quiet expiration. Breath-to-breath variability is not
what defeats the measurement. This option offers a plausible-sounding reason without engaging
with what a spirometer can and cannot see.

## answer_c
A spirometer measures only flow, not volume

## explanation_c
Incorrect on a point of fact. Spirometry measures volume directly, and derives flow from volume
change over time — which is how it produces vital capacity, tidal volume and timed values such
as FEV₁. If it could not measure volume, none of the volumes it routinely reports would be
obtainable. The limitation with FRC is specific to FRC, so an explanation that would rule out
every spirometric measurement cannot be the right one.

## answer_d
Forced expiration is uncomfortable, so subjects cannot cooperate reliably enough

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Effort and cooperation genuinely limit spirometry in practice, and a poorly performed
manoeuvre gives an unreliable vital capacity. But this is a limitation of *technique*, and it
would be overcome by a cooperative subject. FRC remains unmeasurable by spirometry even with
perfect cooperation and maximal effort, which shows the barrier lies elsewhere.

## answer_e
It contains the residual volume, which cannot be exhaled and so never enters the spirometer

## explanation_e
Correct. A spirometer can only measure gas that **moves through it**. It records volumes
inhaled and exhaled, so any gas that never leaves the lungs is invisible to it.

**FRC = expiratory reserve volume + residual volume.** The expiratory reserve volume can be
forced out and is therefore measurable. The **residual volume cannot be exhaled at all**, no
matter how hard the subject tries: at the end of a maximal forced expiration, small airways
close and the chest wall reaches the limit of its inward movement while gas is still trapped in
the alveoli. Because that gas never passes the mouth, the spirometer cannot see it — and since
residual volume is one of the two terms in FRC, FRC cannot be obtained either.

The same argument applies to **total lung capacity**, which also contains residual volume. This
gives a clean rule for spirometry: **any volume or capacity that includes the residual volume
cannot be measured by spirometry.** So vital capacity, tidal volume, inspiratory and expiratory
reserve volumes and inspiratory capacity are all obtainable; residual volume, FRC and total lung
capacity are not.

Those three require an **indirect** method that measures the gas still in the chest rather than
gas passing the mouth. The standard approach is **helium dilution**: the subject rebreathes from
a closed circuit containing a known volume and known concentration of helium, an inert gas that
is not absorbed across the alveolar membrane. The helium distributes through the lungs and
circuit and is diluted by the gas already present, and the resulting fall in helium
concentration gives the unknown lung volume. Nitrogen washout and body plethysmography measure
the same volumes by different principles.

This is a routine clinical distinction, not merely academic: FRC and total lung capacity are the
volumes that distinguish **hyperinflation** in emphysema, where they are raised by air trapping,
from a genuinely **restrictive** defect such as fibrosis, where they are reduced. Spirometry
alone cannot make that call, which is why full lung volume studies are requested.

QST-RES-CO-POISONING-MECHANISM-001

## title
In carbon monoxide poisoning, why is arterial PO₂ normal while oxygen delivery is severely impaired?

## question
In carbon monoxide poisoning, why is arterial PO₂ normal while oxygen delivery is severely impaired?

## subject
resp

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A man is brought in after several hours beside a faulty gas heater in a closed room. He is
confused. Arterial blood gas analysis shows a normal PO₂ and a normal calculated oxygen
saturation, yet he is profoundly tissue-hypoxic. His skin and mucous membranes are not blue.

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Carbon monoxide inhibits cytochrome oxidase, so tissues cannot use the oxygen delivered

## explanation_a
Incorrect, and it describes **histotoxic** hypoxia — the mechanism of **cyanide**, not carbon
monoxide. In histotoxic hypoxia oxygen reaches the tissues normally but cannot be used, so
venous oxygen content is characteristically *high*. Carbon monoxide acts earlier in the chain,
at haemoglobin itself, so the oxygen never arrives. The two are frequently confused because both
present with normal arterial PO₂ and without cyanosis; the distinguishing question is whether
the blood is carrying oxygen at all.

## answer_b
Carbon monoxide reduces alveolar oxygen tension, lowering the amount of oxygen available for uptake

## explanation_b
Incorrect, and it contradicts the stem. If alveolar and therefore arterial oxygen tension were
reduced, this would be **hypoxic** hypoxia and the measured PO₂ would be low — but the stem
states it is normal. Carbon monoxide does not displace oxygen from the alveolar gas in any
significant amount; it acts in the blood, on haemoglobin, after oxygen has diffused across
normally. This option picks the student who reaches for the commonest type of hypoxia without
checking it against the blood gas result.

## answer_c
Carbon monoxide destroys red cells, so total haemoglobin concentration falls

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Carbon monoxide causes no haemolysis, and the total haemoglobin concentration in
these patients is normal — a full blood count is unremarkable. The deficit is **functional**
rather than quantitative: the haemoglobin is present in normal amount but a proportion of it is
occupied and unavailable. This distinction also explains the absence of cyanosis, and it is why
the poisoning is invisible to the tests that would detect true anaemia.

## answer_d
Carbon monoxide occupies the oxygen-binding sites of haemoglobin, so a normal amount of haemoglobin carries far less oxygen

## explanation_d
Correct. Carbon monoxide binds to the same haem sites as oxygen, but with an affinity
**200–250 times greater**, forming **carboxyhaemoglobin**. Haemoglobin so occupied **cannot
carry oxygen**, because the binding site is taken.

This is why the blood gas is misleading. **PO₂ measures oxygen dissolved in plasma**, and
dissolved oxygen is entirely unaffected — gas exchange at the alveolus is normal, so arterial
PO₂ is normal. But almost all oxygen transport occurs *bound to haemoglobin*, and it is that
carriage which has been lost. **Oxygen content** is therefore severely reduced while **oxygen
tension** is normal. A calculated saturation, derived from PO₂, will also read normally and is
falsely reassuring; the diagnosis requires carboxyhaemoglobin to be measured directly, by
co-oximetry.

This makes carbon monoxide poisoning a form of **anaemic hypoxia** — a deficiency of haemoglobin
*capable of carrying oxygen* — even though the haemoglobin concentration is normal. The deficit
is functional, not quantitative.

A **second, compounding injury** makes it worse than simple occupancy would suggest.
Carboxyhaemoglobin **increases the oxygen affinity of the remaining normal haem sites**, shifting
the oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve to the **left**. So the haemoglobin that is still
functional binds oxygen more tightly and releases less of it in the tissues. The patient loses
carrying capacity *and* unloading — which is why symptoms are more severe than the
carboxyhaemoglobin percentage alone would predict.

**No cyanosis** occurs, and the reason is worth stating precisely: cyanosis requires
approximately 5 g/dL of *reduced* (deoxygenated) haemoglobin in capillary blood, and
carboxyhaemoglobin is not reduced haemoglobin — it is bright **cherry-red**. The patient is
therefore pink, or even unusually red, while being severely hypoxic. Absence of cyanosis is
never evidence against this diagnosis.

Treatment follows from the mechanism: **remove the source of exposure** and give **high-flow
oxygen**, which shortens the half-life of carboxyhaemoglobin by competing carbon monoxide off
the haem sites.

## answer_e
Carbon monoxide shifts the oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve to the right, so oxygen is released too readily in the lungs

## explanation_e
Incorrect, and the direction is inverted. Carboxyhaemoglobin shifts the curve to the **left**,
increasing the affinity of the remaining haem sites so that oxygen is held more tightly and
released *less* readily in the tissues. A right shift would ease unloading, which would be
mildly protective rather than harmful. The option is built from a real feature of carbon
monoxide poisoning with its sign reversed, and it catches the student who recalls that the curve
moves without recalling which way or what that implies.

QST-RES-ANAEMIC-HYPOXIA-PATTERN-001

## title
In a patient with severe iron deficiency anaemia, what is the pattern of arterial PO₂, oxygen saturation and oxygen content?

## question
In a patient with severe iron deficiency anaemia, what is the pattern of arterial PO₂, oxygen saturation and oxygen content?

## subject
resp

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A young woman with heavy menstrual losses has a haemoglobin concentration of 6 g/dL. She is
breathless on exertion and appears pale, but not blue. Her lungs are clinically normal and she
is breathing room air.

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
PO₂ reduced, saturation reduced, content reduced

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This is the pattern of **hypoxic (arterial) hypoxia** — the picture in respiratory
disease or at altitude, where oxygen fails to reach the blood properly, so tension, saturation
and content all fall together. The stem specifies clinically normal lungs and room air, so
oxygen is entering the blood normally. This option picks the student who treats every hypoxia as
a problem of oxygenation.

## answer_b
PO₂ normal, saturation normal, content reduced

## explanation_b
Correct. The defining feature of **anaemic hypoxia** is that oxygen *tension* and *saturation*
are normal while oxygen *content* is low — and separating those three quantities is the whole
point of the question.

**PO₂** is the partial pressure of oxygen **dissolved in plasma**. It is set by alveolar gas and
by diffusion across a healthy alveolar–capillary membrane. This patient's lungs are normal, so
oxygen crosses normally and **PO₂ is normal**.

**Saturation** is the *percentage* of available haemoglobin binding sites that are occupied by
oxygen. Her haemoglobin is reduced in amount but perfectly normal in function, so at a normal
PO₂ it is fully loaded: **saturation is normal**, and her pulse oximeter will read normally.

**Content** is the total oxygen carried per unit volume of blood, and it depends on how much
haemoglobin there is:

**O₂ content ≈ (1.34 × haemoglobin × saturation) + dissolved oxygen**

With haemoglobin at 6 g/dL instead of about 14, **content is roughly halved** even at 100%
saturation. The carriage capacity has gone, not the loading.

So anaemic hypoxia is a deficiency of **haemoglobin capable of carrying oxygen**. That phrasing
covers both the quantitative case here — too few haemoglobin molecules — and functional cases in
which the amount is normal but a proportion is unusable, such as **carbon monoxide poisoning**
and **methaemoglobinaemia**.

Two consequences matter clinically. **Pulse oximetry and PO₂ are both normal and both
reassuring, wrongly** — neither measures content, so the severity of an anaemic hypoxia is
invisible to them, and only haemoglobin concentration reveals it.

And **no cyanosis** occurs, for a reason that initially seems paradoxical. Cyanosis requires
about **5 g/dL of reduced (deoxygenated) haemoglobin** in capillary blood. If total haemoglobin
is only 6 g/dL, that threshold is barely reachable even were most of it desaturated. The patient
is **pale rather than blue**: severe anaemic hypoxia, by definition, cannot generate enough
reduced haemoglobin to look cyanosed. Absence of cyanosis therefore says nothing about whether
tissue oxygen delivery is adequate.

Supplemental oxygen helps only marginally here, because saturation is already near maximal and
only the small dissolved fraction can increase. The treatment is to restore haemoglobin.

## answer_c
PO₂ normal, saturation reduced, content reduced

## explanation_c
Incorrect, and it is the closest wrong answer — the error is in the saturation. Saturation is a
*proportion*, not an amount: it reports what fraction of the haemoglobin present is carrying
oxygen. This patient has less haemoglobin, but what she has is structurally normal and fully
loaded at a normal PO₂, so the fraction is normal. Expecting saturation to fall with
haemoglobin confuses a percentage with a total, and it is precisely why a normal oximetry
reading misleads clinicians in anaemia.

## answer_d
PO₂ normal, saturation normal, content normal

## explanation_d
Incorrect. If content were normal, oxygen delivery would be adequate and there would be no
hypoxia and no exertional breathlessness — yet the patient is symptomatic at a haemoglobin of
6 g/dL. Content depends directly on haemoglobin concentration, so it cannot be normal when
haemoglobin is less than half of normal. This option picks the student who has correctly
identified that the lungs are normal and concluded that oxygen transport must therefore be
normal too.

## answer_e
PO₂ normal, saturation normal, content reduced, with raised venous oxygen content

## explanation_e
Incorrect because of the final clause, which belongs to a different condition. The first three
values are right, but raised venous oxygen content is the signature of **histotoxic** hypoxia —
cyanide poisoning, where tissues cannot extract the oxygen delivered, so it returns to the
veins unused. In anaemic hypoxia the tissues are oxygen-starved and extract *more* than usual
from each unit of blood, so venous oxygen content and saturation are **reduced**. This
distractor separates students who have learned the arterial pattern from those who have also
worked out what happens on the venous side.
