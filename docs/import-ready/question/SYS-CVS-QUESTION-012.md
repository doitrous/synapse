## title
A patient's cardiac output falls by a quarter, yet mean arterial pressure is unchanged. What must have happened?

## question
A patient's cardiac output falls by a quarter, yet mean arterial pressure is unchanged. What must have happened?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
During a period of blood loss, serial measurements show cardiac output has fallen from 5.0 to
3.75 L/min. Mean arterial pressure, measured at the same moments, has not changed. The
student is asked to account for the discrepancy from first principles rather than by naming a
reflex.

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Heart rate must have risen enough to restore cardiac output

## explanation_a
Incorrect, and it contradicts the stem. Cardiac output is heart rate multiplied by stroke
volume, so if output has genuinely fallen to 3.75 L/min then whatever heart rate is doing, the
product has not been restored. A rising heart rate is indeed part of the real physiological
response, but it is an explanation of how output is *defended*, not of how pressure can hold
while output is already measurably lower. This option picks the student who answers the
question they expected rather than the one asked.

## answer_b
Total peripheral resistance must have risen by about a third

## explanation_b
Correct. Mean arterial pressure is the product of two terms and two only:

**MAP ≈ cardiac output × total peripheral resistance**

If MAP is fixed and cardiac output falls, resistance must rise by exactly the reciprocal
factor. Output has fallen to 0.75 of its starting value, so resistance must rise to 1/0.75 =
**1.33**, a rise of about a third. No other quantity in the equation can absorb the change,
which is why this is a matter of arithmetic rather than of naming a mechanism.

It is worth expanding the first term, because the full chain is what examiners test:

**MAP ≈ (heart rate × stroke volume) × total peripheral resistance**

That gives three independent handles on arterial pressure, and every cardiovascular reflex
and most antihypertensive drugs act through one of them.

Physiologically, the resistance rise here is sympathetically mediated arteriolar
vasoconstriction — the baroreceptor response to falling pressure. Arterioles are the
resistance vessels because resistance varies with the fourth power of radius, so modest
constriction produces a large change. This is precisely why blood pressure is a *poor* early
warning of haemorrhage: a patient can lose substantial volume, drop their cardiac output
significantly, and still present with a normal blood pressure because vasoconstriction has
compensated. Pressure is the product being defended, not the variable that reveals the loss.
When the compensation is finally exhausted, pressure falls steeply and late.

## answer_c
Total peripheral resistance must have fallen by about a third

## explanation_c
Incorrect — the right magnitude with the sign inverted, and a very common slip under time
pressure. If resistance *fell* while cardiac output was falling, both terms of MAP = CO × TPR
would be decreasing and mean arterial pressure would drop sharply rather than hold steady. The
direction follows from the equation without any physiology: to keep a product constant while
one factor falls, the other must rise.

## answer_d
Stroke volume must have risen to compensate for the fall in output

## explanation_d
Incorrect and internally contradictory. Stroke volume is a *component* of cardiac output, not
a counterweight to it — a rise in stroke volume, at any given heart rate, raises cardiac
output. It cannot explain how pressure is maintained *despite* output having fallen. This
option catches the student who is reaching for any variable that might have increased without
checking which side of the equation it sits on.

## answer_e
Blood viscosity must have risen enough to maintain arterial pressure

## explanation_e
Incorrect, though not absurd, and it is worth being precise about why. Viscosity genuinely
does contribute to resistance, so it is not irrelevant to MAP. But in acute haemorrhage
viscosity *falls* rather than rises, because interstitial fluid and any administered
crystalloid dilute the blood and drop the haematocrit. Even if it did rise, viscosity is a
minor determinant beside vessel radius, which resistance depends on to the fourth power. This
option picks the student who has learned the components of resistance but not their relative
weight.

QST-CVS-AFTERLOAD-ESV-001

## title
Aortic pressure is raised while end-diastolic volume and contractility are held constant. What happens to end-systolic volume and stroke volume?

## question
Aortic pressure is raised while end-diastolic volume and contractility are held constant. What happens to end-systolic volume and stroke volume?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
In an isolated heart preparation, aortic pressure is raised experimentally. End-diastolic
volume is clamped at 130 mL and contractility is unaltered. Successive pressure–volume loops
are recorded.

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
End-systolic volume falls and stroke volume rises

## explanation_a
Incorrect, and it inverts the mechanism. This is what would happen if **contractility** were
increased: the ventricle would empty further, end-systolic volume would fall and stroke volume
would rise. The stem explicitly holds contractility constant and raises afterload instead.
This option picks the student who has merged afterload and contractility into a single vague
notion of "the heart working harder", when they move end-systolic volume in opposite
directions.

## answer_b
Both end-systolic volume and stroke volume are unchanged

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This treats stroke volume as if it were fixed by end-diastolic volume alone. If
that were true, afterload would have no effect on ejection at all, and conditions such as
severe aortic stenosis or a hypertensive crisis could not reduce stroke volume — which they
plainly do. Preload sets where ejection *starts*; afterload determines how far it can
proceed.

## answer_c
End-systolic volume rises and stroke volume falls

## explanation_c
Correct. Afterload is **the load the ventricle must overcome in order to eject** — clinically,
aortic pressure for the left ventricle. Raising it makes ejection harder, and the effect lands
on the *end* of systole.

Follow it through the cycle. End-diastolic volume is clamped at 130 mL, so the ventricle
begins from the same filled volume. With aortic pressure higher, isovolumetric contraction
must continue longer to reach the new, higher opening threshold, so the aortic valve opens
**late**. Once it opens, the ventricle is ejecting against greater opposition and against a
shortened time, so it ejects **less** and stops sooner. It therefore ends systole with more
blood left inside: **end-systolic volume rises**.

Since **stroke volume = end-diastolic volume − end-systolic volume**, and end-diastolic volume
is fixed while end-systolic volume has risen, **stroke volume must fall**. Ejection fraction
falls with it.

The clean summary, and the reason this is examined so often, is that **preload mainly sets
end-diastolic volume and afterload mainly sets end-systolic volume**. On the pressure–volume
loop, raising afterload makes the loop taller and narrower: it reaches a higher pressure, but
its width — which *is* stroke volume — shrinks.

This is why afterload reduction helps a failing ventricle. A heart that cannot generate much
pressure is disproportionately penalised by a high load, so lowering arterial pressure with a
vasodilator increases stroke volume without any change in contractility. It is also why
untreated hypertension and aortic stenosis eventually cause concentric hypertrophy: the
ventricle thickens to generate the pressure its load demands, and that thicker wall is
stiffer, impairing filling in turn.

## answer_d
End-systolic volume rises and stroke volume is unchanged

## explanation_d
Incorrect, and it is the near miss worth reasoning against. The first half is right, but the
two halves cannot both be true when end-diastolic volume is clamped. Stroke volume is defined
as end-diastolic minus end-systolic volume; if the first is fixed and the second rises, the
difference *must* shrink. This option picks the student who has the afterload effect right but
has not connected it to the definition of stroke volume.

## answer_e
End-diastolic volume rises and stroke volume is maintained

## explanation_e
Incorrect as an answer to this question, though it describes something real. Over subsequent
beats an intact circulation *does* respond this way: the residual volume left behind adds to
the next filling, end-diastolic volume climbs, and the Frank–Starling mechanism recruits extra
force to defend stroke volume. But the stem clamps end-diastolic volume precisely to exclude
that compensation and isolate the immediate effect of afterload. This option catches the
student who has answered about the compensated steady state rather than the controlled
condition described.

## title
Which nerves carry afferent traffic from the carotid sinus and from the aortic arch?

## question
Which nerves carry afferent traffic from the carotid sinus and from the aortic arch?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A patient becomes bradycardic and faints when a tight collar presses on one side of the neck.
A student correctly identifies a baroreceptor reflex arising from the carotid sinus and is
then asked to trace the afferent pathway, and to say whether the aortic arch receptors travel
with it.

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Both sets travel in the vagus nerve

## explanation_a
Incorrect for the carotid sinus, though right for the aortic arch. This option picks the
student who knows the baroreflex is "vagally mediated" and applies that to the whole arc. The
vagus certainly carries the *efferent* parasympathetic limb that slows the heart, and it does
carry aortic arch afferents — but carotid sinus afferents run in the glossopharyngeal nerve.
Collapsing the two sites into one nerve loses the anatomical distinction the question is
about.

## answer_b
Both sets travel in the glossopharyngeal nerve

## explanation_b
Incorrect, and it makes the opposite error to option A. The glossopharyngeal nerve does carry
carotid sinus afferents, via the carotid sinus branch, but it does not reach the aortic arch —
it is a nerve of the pharynx and upper neck. Aortic arch afferents ascend with the vagus,
which passes through the thorax and is anatomically available to the arch in a way the
glossopharyngeal is not.

## answer_c
Carotid sinus in the vagus nerve; aortic arch in the glossopharyngeal nerve

## explanation_c
Incorrect — the two correct nerves, assigned to the wrong sites. This is the most frequent
error on this topic, and the way to avoid it is anatomical rather than mnemonic: ask which
nerve actually passes the receptor. The glossopharyngeal nerve is confined to the neck and
pharynx, so it can serve the carotid sinus but never the arch of the aorta; the vagus descends
through the thorax alongside the arch. Position decides it.

## answer_d
Both sets travel in the sympathetic cervical chain

## explanation_d
Incorrect, and it confuses the afferent and efferent halves of the reflex. The sympathetic
chain carries the *efferent* limb — the outflow that raises heart rate and constricts vessels
when pressure falls. Baroreceptor afferents are cranial nerve sensory fibres, not sympathetic.
A student holding this cannot explain how carotid sinus massage produces bradycardia, since
that requires a sensory signal reaching the medulla before any outflow changes.

## answer_e
Carotid sinus in the glossopharyngeal nerve; aortic arch in the vagus nerve

## explanation_e
Correct. The two baroreceptor sites report along **two different cranial nerves**, and the
split follows the anatomy.

**Carotid sinus** — a dilatation at the origin of the internal carotid artery — sends
afferents through the **carotid sinus nerve (the nerve of Hering)**, a branch of the
**glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX)**.

**Aortic arch** receptors send afferents through the **aortic nerve**, which joins the
**vagus nerve (CN X)**.

Both terminate in the **nucleus tractus solitarius** in the medulla, where the reflex is
integrated. The useful memory aid is positional rather than arbitrary: the glossopharyngeal
nerve does not descend into the thorax, so it cannot serve the arch; the vagus does, so it can.

The reflex itself is a negative feedback loop. A rise in arterial pressure stretches the
receptors, afferent firing increases, and the medulla responds by *inhibiting* sympathetic
outflow and *increasing* vagal outflow to the heart — heart rate and cardiac output fall and
the vessels dilate, so pressure returns toward normal. A fall in pressure reduces firing and
releases the opposite response, which is why sympathetic activity rises in haemorrhage.

The vignette is **carotid sinus syndrome**: a hypersensitive sinus in which external pressure
— a tight collar, or a hand on the neck — mimics a large pressure rise, triggering
inappropriate bradycardia and vasodilatation, and hence syncope. Note that this depends on the
*carotid* site and therefore on the glossopharyngeal afferent; it is not reproduced by
pressing on the chest.

Two further points are commonly examined. These receptors respond to the *rate* of change as
well as absolute pressure, so they are best suited to buffering acute, moment-to-moment
swings — such as standing up. And they **reset** over days to a sustained new pressure, which
is why the baroreflex does not correct chronic hypertension and why long-term pressure control
is renal rather than neural.

QST-CVS-KOROTKOFF-ABSENT-001

## title
Why are no Korotkoff sounds heard while cuff pressure is above systolic pressure?

## question
Why are no Korotkoff sounds heard while cuff pressure is above systolic pressure?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A student inflates a brachial cuff to 180 mmHg in a patient whose pressure proves to be
130/80 mmHg, then begins to deflate it slowly while listening over the brachial artery. For
the first part of the deflation nothing at all is audible.

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The cuff has occluded the artery completely, so there is no flow to make a sound

## explanation_a
Correct. Korotkoff sounds are produced by **turbulent flow**, and turbulence requires flow.
While cuff pressure exceeds systolic pressure the brachial artery is held shut through the
whole cardiac cycle: even at the peak of systole, arterial pressure never rises above the
pressure squeezing the vessel from outside. No blood passes the cuff, so there is nothing to
be turbulent and nothing to hear.

The rest of the measurement follows from the same principle. As the cuff is released to just
below systolic pressure, the artery is forced open for a brief moment at each systolic peak
and snaps shut again as pressure falls. Blood spurts through a narrow, partly collapsed
segment at high velocity — the flow is turbulent, and the first tapping sound appears. That
first sound therefore marks **systolic pressure**.

As deflation continues, the artery stays open for longer in each cycle and the sounds change
in character. When cuff pressure falls below **diastolic** pressure, the artery is open
throughout the entire cycle, flow becomes smooth and laminar again, and the sounds
**disappear**. That disappearance marks diastolic pressure.

So both endpoints share one explanation: silence at either extreme means **laminar flow or no
flow**, and sound in between means the vessel is being intermittently narrowed. Reasoning it
through this way makes the technique's failure modes predictable rather than arbitrary — a
cuff deflated too quickly skips past the first sound and underestimates systolic pressure, and
a cuff never inflated above systolic pressure has no silent period to start from.

## answer_b
The artery is fully open, so flow is laminar and silent

## explanation_b
Incorrect — this is the correct explanation for the *other* silence, at the end of the
measurement. Below diastolic pressure the artery is indeed open throughout the cycle and
laminar flow is silent. But above systolic pressure the artery is the opposite of open: it is
completely closed. Both states are silent, for opposite reasons, and telling them apart is the
point of this question. A student who cannot separate them has no principled way to identify
which endpoint they are hearing.

## answer_c
Cuff pressure has abolished the pulse pressure, so the artery cannot vibrate

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This sounds mechanistically plausible but misplaces the source of the sound.
Korotkoff sounds are not the vessel wall vibrating in response to pulse pressure; they are
generated by turbulence in the blood moving through a narrowed segment. Pulse pressure
upstream of the cuff is entirely unaffected by inflation — the heart is still ejecting
normally. What the cuff removes is *flow past the cuff*, not pulsation in the artery.

## answer_d
The cuff has compressed the brachial vein, so venous congestion damps the sound

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A cuff at 180 mmHg certainly occludes the vein, and venous congestion below the
cuff is real — but it is not why nothing is heard. Korotkoff sounds are arterial in origin, and
the stethoscope is placed over the brachial artery. This option picks the student looking for
an effect of the cuff on surrounding structures instead of asking the direct question: is
blood crossing the cuff at all?

## answer_e
Blood is bypassing the occlusion through collateral vessels, so flow is continuous and silent

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Collateral flow around the elbow does exist and is why a limb is not rendered
ischaemic by a few seconds of cuff inflation. But collaterals do not carry flow *through the
compressed brachial segment under the cuff*, which is where the stethoscope is listening, and
diverted collateral flow would not produce turbulence there. This option catches the student
who is explaining why the arm survives rather than why the segment is silent.

QST-CVS-VENOUS-RESERVOIR-001

## title
Which part of the circulation holds most of the blood volume at rest, and what property allows it?

## question
Which part of the circulation holds most of the blood volume at rest, and what property allows it?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A student is asked why a healthy adult can donate 450 mL of blood — nearly a tenth of blood
volume — and walk out of the clinic with an essentially unchanged arterial pressure and
cardiac output.

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
The systemic arteries, because their thick elastic walls store blood under high pressure

## explanation_a
Incorrect, and it confuses storing *pressure* with storing *volume*. The arteries do hold
blood under high pressure, and their elastic recoil maintains diastolic pressure and smooths
flow — but they contain only around 13% of blood volume. Their thick, stiff, muscular walls
are exactly what makes them poor reservoirs: being of low compliance, they cannot accept much
extra volume without pressure rising steeply. High pressure and large capacity are opposite
consequences of wall stiffness.

## answer_b
The capillaries, because their enormous total cross-sectional area gives the greatest capacity

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Capillaries do have by far the largest total cross-sectional area, which is why
flow velocity is lowest there and exchange has time to occur — but area is not volume. Each
capillary is extremely short and narrow, and together they hold only a few per cent of blood
volume at any moment. This option picks the student who has correctly learned the
cross-sectional area profile of the circulation and read a volume claim out of it.

## answer_c
The pulmonary circulation, because it receives the entire cardiac output

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The pulmonary circulation does receive the whole cardiac output — the two circuits
are in series, so it must — but throughput is not storage. It holds roughly 9% of blood
volume, and acts as a modest reserve that can shift blood to the left heart. Confusing the
flow through a compartment with the volume resident in it is the same error as option B in a
different place.

## answer_d
The systemic veins, because their high compliance lets them hold large volumes at low pressure

## explanation_d
Correct. The **systemic veins and venules hold roughly two-thirds of total blood volume** —
about **64%** — at rest, which is why they are called the **capacitance** or reservoir vessels.

The property that permits it is **compliance**, the volume a vessel accepts per unit rise in
pressure. Veins are approximately **24 times more compliant than arteries**: thin-walled, with
little elastic tissue and a collapsible, initially non-circular cross-section, so a large
volume can be added while venous pressure rises only a few mmHg. Central venous pressure sits
at just 0–2 mmHg despite the veins containing most of the blood in the body. An artery given
the same volume would show a steep pressure rise.

That reservoir answers the vignette. Losing 450 mL is drawn largely from a compartment whose
pressure barely changes, so venous return and therefore cardiac output are minimally
disturbed. More actively, **sympathetic venoconstriction** reduces venous compliance and
shifts stored blood centrally, raising mean systemic filling pressure and defending venous
return — an autotransfusion from the body's own reserve. This is why the veins are properly
thought of as an adjustable reservoir rather than passive tubing, and why venous tone is as
important to cardiac output as arterial tone is to pressure.

The same compliance explains two everyday observations. Venous pooling in the legs on standing
occurs because compliant veins distend readily under a gravitational column — hence the
importance of the muscle pump and venous valves. And drugs that dilate veins reduce preload
rather than afterload, which is the basis of using a nitrate to unload a congested ventricle.

## answer_e
The systemic veins, because their valves trap blood and prevent it returning to the heart

## explanation_e
Incorrect, although it names the right compartment — which is exactly what makes it worth
separating. Venous valves do not trap blood for storage; they ensure flow is **unidirectional
towards the heart**, and they are what makes the skeletal muscle pump effective. Reservoir
capacity comes from compliant walls, not from valves. The distinction is clinically real:
when valves become incompetent, as in varicose veins, blood pools and refluxes *abnormally* —
so trapping blood is a description of valve failure, not of normal reservoir function.
