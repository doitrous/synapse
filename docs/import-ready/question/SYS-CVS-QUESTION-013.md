## title
What prevents atrial depolarisation from spreading directly into ventricular muscle?

## question
What prevents atrial depolarisation from spreading directly into ventricular muscle?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A student is asked why an impulse arising in the atria cannot simply cross the
atrioventricular junction wherever atrial and ventricular muscle meet, and must instead pass
through a single narrow pathway.

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
The atrioventricular valves physically separate the two muscle masses

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The valves are flow structures — they open and close to direct blood — and they are
not an electrical barrier. Insulation is provided by the dense connective tissue of the fibrous
skeleton in which the valve rings are *set*, not by the cusps themselves. This option picks the
student who has noticed something anatomical between the chambers and named the most familiar
structure rather than the tissue that does the insulating.

## answer_b
Ventricular myocytes have a resting potential too negative to be excited by atrial current

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Working ventricular myocytes rest near −90 mV and are readily excited once
depolarising current from a neighbour brings them to a firing level near −65 mV — that is
exactly how activation spreads through the ventricle. Their resting potential is no obstacle at
all. This option invents an excitability barrier where the real barrier is structural.

## answer_c
The fibrous skeleton is non-conducting connective tissue, leaving the atrioventricular bundle as the only route

## explanation_c
Correct. The **cardiac fibrous skeleton** — the dense connective tissue rings surrounding the
four valve orifices and the tissue joining them — is **electrically non-conducting**. It forms a
complete insulating plate between atrial and ventricular myocardium, so no impulse can cross it
directly.

The single exception is the **atrioventricular bundle** (bundle of His), which passes from the
atrioventricular node *through* the fibrous skeleton to reach the interventricular septum. That
makes it the **only** electrical connection between the atria and the ventricles in a normal
heart.

Three consequences follow, and each is examined:

**The conduction sequence is obligatory.** Every atrial impulse must funnel through the
atrioventricular node and bundle. That funnel is what imposes the **atrioventricular delay** of
about 0.1 s, which lets atrial contraction finish before the ventricles begin to contract.

**The ventricles are protected in atrial arrhythmia.** In atrial fibrillation the atria are
depolarising chaotically at very high rates, yet the ventricles respond far more slowly. The
insulation plus the node's refractoriness filters the traffic; without it, atrial fibrillation
would drive the ventricles into fibrillation too.

**Block occurs at one identifiable place.** Because there is only one route, disease of that
route produces atrioventricular block — a prolonged PR interval, or P waves with no following
QRS. A single pathway is also why a pacing lead placed beyond it can restore ventricular
activation.

An **accessory pathway** — an abnormal strand of conducting muscle bridging the fibrous
skeleton — bypasses this arrangement, which is why it permits pre-excitation and re-entrant
tachycardia.

## answer_d
The atrioventricular node actively blocks retrograde and direct conduction

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The node slows conduction and, through its long refractory period, limits how fast
impulses can pass — but it is a **conducting** structure, not a blocking one, and it does not
prevent conduction elsewhere. It also conducts retrogradely under some conditions. This option
confuses the *gatekeeper* in the pathway with the *insulation* around it: the node regulates
traffic through the one open door, while the fibrous skeleton is the wall.

## answer_e
Purkinje fibres conduct so rapidly that atrial current is short-circuited away

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The Purkinje system does conduct rapidly, and that speed serves the synchronous
activation of the ventricles — but rapid conduction distributes an impulse, it cannot prevent one
from entering. Purkinje fibres also lie *downstream* of the fibrous skeleton, so they play no
part in separating atria from ventricles. This option repurposes a real property of the
conduction system to answer a question about insulation.

QST-CVS-AV-DELAY-PURPOSE-001

## title
What is achieved by the 0.1 second delay at the atrioventricular node?

## question
What is achieved by the 0.1 second delay at the atrioventricular node?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A student is asked why the atrioventricular node conducts more slowly than any other part of the
conduction system, and treats the slow conduction as a design weakness that the heart tolerates
rather than a function it needs.

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
It allows the coronary arteries to fill before the ventricles contract

## explanation_a
Incorrect, although it names a real dependency in the wrong place. Left coronary filling does
depend on the cardiac cycle, and it occurs mainly during **diastole**, when the myocardium is
relaxed and aortic pressure is maintained by elastic recoil. That has nothing to do with the
0.1 s spent in the atrioventricular node, which is far too brief to matter for perfusion. This
option picks the student who knows coronary filling is timing-dependent and attaches it to the
first timing figure they meet.

## answer_b
It slows the ventricular rate so that cardiac output is not excessive

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The node's **refractory period** does limit the maximum rate at which impulses can
reach the ventricles, and that protects them during atrial tachyarrhythmias — but that is rate
*filtering*, a different property from the fixed conduction delay on each normal beat. In sinus
rhythm the ventricular rate is set by the sinoatrial node, not restrained by the atrioventricular
node, and a normal cardiac output is not something the heart needs protecting from.

## answer_c
It gives the Purkinje system time to distribute the impulse evenly

## explanation_c
Incorrect, and the sequence is the wrong way round. Purkinje conduction happens *after* the
impulse has passed the node and the bundle, so a delay upstream cannot create time for it —
Purkinje fibres conduct very rapidly precisely so that ventricular activation is near-synchronous
once it begins. This option catches the student who has correctly learned that synchrony matters
and assumed the delay must be what buys it.

## answer_d
It prevents the atria and ventricles from contracting at the same moment, which would be fatal

## explanation_d
Incorrect as stated, and it is the closest wrong answer — worth arguing with carefully. The
delay does separate atrial from ventricular contraction, so the first clause is nearly right.
But the reason is not that simultaneous contraction would be *fatal* — it is that simultaneous
contraction would waste the atrial contribution to filling, since the atria would be pumping
against closed valves. The consequence is inefficiency, not immediate death; people in atrial
fibrillation lose the coordinated sequence entirely and survive. Overstating the stakes usually
signals a memorised phrase rather than the mechanism.

## answer_e
It allows atrial contraction to finish and complete ventricular filling before the ventricles contract

## explanation_e
Correct. The atrioventricular nodal delay of about **0.1 s** exists so that **atrial systole can
finish before ventricular systole begins**.

The mechanism is deliberate slowness. Node cells are small, have few gap junctions and a
less negative resting potential, so conduction through them is markedly slower than through
atrial muscle, the bundle or the Purkinje system. On the ECG this delay is the segment between
the **P wave** (atrial depolarisation) and the **QRS complex** (ventricular depolarisation) — most
of the PR interval.

What the pause buys is the **atrial contribution to ventricular filling**. Passive filling has
already delivered about 80% of the end-diastolic volume; atrial contraction supplies the final
20%, topping the ventricle up to roughly 130 mL. If the ventricle began to contract while the
atrium was still contracting, the atrioventricular valves would already be closing and that final
increment would be lost — the atrium would be pumping into a closed chamber and driving blood
back towards the veins.

So the delay converts two pumps into one **sequential** pump, and its length is a compromise:
long enough to complete filling, short enough not to waste diastole.

The clinical corollary is what happens when the timing is disturbed. If the delay lengthens
beyond normal, the PR interval prolongs — **first-degree atrioventricular block**. If conduction
fails intermittently, some P waves are not followed by a QRS at all. And when atrial contraction
becomes uncoordinated, as in atrial fibrillation, the top-up is lost regardless of nodal
conduction — which is why patients with a stiff ventricle, who depend on that final 20%,
deteriorate sharply when they fibrillate.

## title
By what cellular mechanism does vagal stimulation slow the sinoatrial node?

## question
By what cellular mechanism does vagal stimulation slow the sinoatrial node?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
Carotid sinus massage in a patient with a narrow-complex tachycardia produces an abrupt fall in
heart rate. The effect is abolished by atropine.

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Acetylcholine acts on nicotinic receptors, depolarising pacemaker cells to threshold more slowly

## explanation_a
Incorrect on the receptor. Cardiac muscarinic blockade with atropine abolishes the effect, as the
stem states, which identifies the receptor as **muscarinic** — nicotinic receptors are found at
the autonomic ganglion and the neuromuscular junction, not on sinoatrial pacemaker cells. This
option picks the student who knows acetylcholine is the transmitter and stops before asking which
of its two receptor families is involved.

## answer_b
Acetylcholine acts on muscarinic receptors, lowering cAMP and reducing the funny current

## explanation_b
Correct. Vagal fibres release **acetylcholine**, which acts on **muscarinic (M₂) receptors** on
sinoatrial cells. These are G-protein coupled and **inhibitory**: they reduce adenylyl cyclase
activity, so **intracellular cAMP falls**.

The rate consequence follows from what cAMP does in a pacemaker cell. Sinoatrial cells have no
stable resting potential — they depolarise spontaneously during phase 4, largely through the
**funny current (I_f)**, an inward current carried by HCN channels that is **directly gated by
cAMP**. Less cAMP means less I_f, so the slope of spontaneous depolarisation becomes shallower,
threshold is reached later, and each beat takes longer to initiate. Heart rate falls. Muscarinic
activation also opens potassium channels, hyperpolarising the cell so that it starts further from
threshold, which adds to the effect.

**Sympathetic stimulation is the exact mirror.** Noradrenaline on β₁ receptors, and circulating
adrenaline and noradrenaline reaching the node in the blood, **raise** cAMP, **increase** I_f,
steepen phase 4 and speed the node up. One second messenger, opposite directions — which is why
the two limbs of autonomic control can be learned as a single mechanism rather than two.

Two points make this clinically useful. The vagus exerts **continuous resting tone** on the node:
the intrinsic sinoatrial rate is around 100/min, and a normal resting rate near 70 reflects
ongoing vagal restraint — which is why atropine *raises* heart rate in a resting person, and why
the tachycardia in this patient responds to a manoeuvre that merely increases vagal output.
Second, this is the pharmacological target of **ivabradine**, which blocks I_f directly and slows
the node without affecting contractility.

## answer_c
Acetylcholine increases calcium entry through L-type channels, prolonging the plateau

## explanation_c
Incorrect, and it describes the wrong direction in the wrong cell type. L-type calcium current
does contribute to the *upstroke* in nodal cells and to the plateau in working myocytes, but
muscarinic activation **reduces** rather than increases calcium entry. Prolonging a plateau would
alter action potential duration, not the rate at which new action potentials are initiated —
which is what determines heart rate. This option confuses the shape of an action potential with
the frequency of them.

## answer_d
Acetylcholine blocks conduction through the atrioventricular node, reducing the ventricular rate

## explanation_d
Incorrect as an answer to this question, although it describes something real. Vagal activity does
slow atrioventricular nodal conduction, and that is genuinely how vagal manoeuvres terminate
some re-entrant tachycardias. But the stem asks specifically how the **sinoatrial node** is
slowed, which is a matter of pacemaker rate, not of conduction downstream. This option is a
correct fact about a different node, and it catches the student who answers from the clinical
scenario rather than the question.

## answer_e
Acetylcholine hyperpolarises pacemaker cells by inhibiting the sodium–potassium pump

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The sodium–potassium ATPase maintains the ionic gradients that underlie all cardiac
electrical activity, and **inhibiting** it — as digoxin does — tends to raise intracellular sodium
and calcium, which increases contractility rather than causing hyperpolarisation. Muscarinic
hyperpolarisation is achieved by **opening potassium channels**, letting potassium leave the cell.
This option correctly identifies that hyperpolarisation is involved but attributes it to the wrong
protein.

## title
Why does the impulse travel faster through the Purkinje system than through working myocardium?

## question
Why does the impulse travel faster through the Purkinje system than through working myocardium?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
Activation spreads through atrial and ventricular working myocardium at roughly 0.5 m/s. A
student is asked why the specialised conducting fibres are several times quicker, and answers
that they must be insulated like a myelinated nerve.

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Purkinje fibres are myelinated, so conduction is saltatory

## explanation_a
Incorrect, and it is the misconception stated in the vignette. Myelin is a property of
**peripheral and central nerve** axons, produced by Schwann cells or oligodendrocytes, and there
is none in the heart. Purkinje fibres are modified **cardiac muscle** cells, not neurones.
Saltatory conduction — jumping between nodes of Ranvier — therefore cannot occur. The analogy to
a fast nerve is natural but anatomically wrong, and it obscures the real reason.

## answer_b
Purkinje fibres have a more negative resting potential, so depolarisation is larger

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Resting potential affects the *amplitude and rate of rise* of an action potential, and
a very negative resting potential does favour a brisk upstroke — but it is not what makes
propagation from cell to cell fast. Working myocytes already rest near −90 mV and still conduct at
only about 0.5 m/s. This option picks the student who has correctly linked resting potential to
excitability and assumed excitability and conduction velocity are the same thing.

## answer_c
Purkinje fibres contain more mitochondria, so they repolarise more quickly

## explanation_c
Incorrect on both the fact and the logic. Purkinje fibres actually contain relatively **few**
myofibrils and are not distinguished by mitochondrial density, and in any case repolarisation
speed governs how soon a cell can fire *again* — its refractory period — not how fast an impulse
travels along a chain of cells. This option confuses the recovery of excitability with the
velocity of propagation.

## answer_d
Purkinje fibres are large in diameter and richly coupled by gap junctions, so local current spreads further ahead

## explanation_d
Correct. Cardiac conduction velocity depends on how easily depolarising current flows **from an
active cell into the cells ahead of it**, and two structural features govern that.

**Fibre diameter.** A wider fibre offers lower internal resistance to longitudinal current, just
as a thicker wire does. Purkinje fibres are the **largest cells in the heart**, so current spreads
further along the fibre before it dissipates, bringing more distant membrane to threshold sooner.

**Gap junction density.** Cardiac cells are joined end to end at intercalated discs containing
**gap junctions**, channels of connexin protein that let ions pass directly between cytoplasms.
Purkinje fibres are exceptionally richly coupled, so the resistance *between* cells is very low.
Working myocytes have fewer such connections and conduct at about **0.5 m/s**, while Purkinje
fibres reach several metres per second.

The functional point is what this arrangement achieves. Rapid Purkinje conduction delivers the
impulse to widely separated parts of both ventricles almost simultaneously, so the ventricle
contracts as a **coordinated unit** rather than as a slow wave crawling from one point. That
matters mechanically: a synchronised squeeze from apex to outflow generates pressure efficiently,
whereas sequential activation would let already-contracting regions push blood into
still-relaxed ones.

The **atrioventricular node** is the instructive contrast, since it is built the opposite way —
small cells with sparse gap junctions — and conducts slowly on purpose, producing the delay that
protects ventricular filling. The same two variables, set to opposite extremes, produce the
fastest and slowest conduction in the heart.

Note also that gap junctional coupling is what makes the myocardium a **functional syncytium**:
cell-to-cell current, not any nerve supply, is what activates each working myocyte from its
neighbour.

## answer_e
Purkinje fibres are directly innervated by sympathetic fibres that accelerate conduction

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Sympathetic activity does increase conduction velocity somewhat, and the heart is
richly innervated — but innervation is a *modulator* of an existing property, not the reason
Purkinje conduction is intrinsically fast. A denervated or transplanted heart still conducts
rapidly through its Purkinje system. This option also implies the impulse is carried *by* nerves,
which is the deeper error: within the heart, conduction is myocyte to myocyte through gap
junctions.

QST-CVS-AV-BLOCK-ECG-001

## title
An ECG shows P waves at a regular rate, some followed by a QRS complex and some not. Where is the abnormality?

## question
An ECG shows P waves at a regular rate, some followed by a QRS complex and some not. Where is the abnormality?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
An elderly patient reports dizziness. The ECG shows regular P waves at 80 per minute. Most are
followed by a QRS complex, but intermittently a P wave occurs with no QRS after it. The QRS
complexes that do appear are of normal duration.

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Conduction from the atria to the ventricles is impaired

## explanation_a
Correct. Regular P waves that are **not all followed by a QRS** localise the fault precisely: the
atria are depolarising normally, but some of those impulses are failing to reach the ventricles.
That is **atrioventricular block**.

The reasoning is worth making explicit, because the ECG lets each step be checked separately.

**The P waves are regular and normal in rate**, so the sinoatrial node is firing properly and
atrial depolarisation is intact — the problem is not one of impulse *generation*.

**Some P waves have no following QRS**, so impulses are being generated but not transmitted. Since
the **atrioventricular bundle is the only electrical route** through the insulating fibrous
skeleton, a failure of atrial-to-ventricular transmission must lie in that pathway — the
atrioventricular node or the bundle.

**The QRS complexes are of normal duration**, which indicates that once an impulse does get
through, ventricular activation proceeds normally through the Purkinje system. This points the
lesion at the node rather than lower in the bundle branches, where a wide QRS would be expected.

Atrioventricular block has two ECG signatures, and both follow from the same idea of a struggling
pathway: a **prolonged PR interval**, where conduction is delayed but always succeeds
(first-degree), and **P waves not followed by QRS**, where conduction intermittently or completely
fails (second- and third-degree). This patient has the second pattern.

The dizziness follows mechanically. Every dropped beat is a missed ventricular contraction, so
cardiac output falls intermittently and cerebral perfusion dips. If block becomes complete, the
ventricles are driven only by a slow subsidiary pacemaker below the block, and the resulting rate
may be too slow to maintain output — which is the indication for pacing.

## answer_b
The sinoatrial node is failing to fire regularly

## explanation_b
Incorrect, and the ECG excludes it directly. Sinoatrial failure would show as **absent or
irregularly timed P waves**, because the P wave *is* atrial depolarisation triggered by the node.
Here the P waves are present and regular at 80 per minute, which is evidence that the node is
working normally. This option picks the student who sees a bradycardic, symptomatic patient and
reaches for the pacemaker without reading which waves are missing.

## answer_c
The atria are depolarising chaotically without organised contraction

## explanation_c
Incorrect — this describes **atrial fibrillation**, which has a quite different signature: **no
discrete, consistently shaped P waves** at all, replaced by an irregular baseline, with an
irregularly irregular ventricular response. The stem specifies regular, identifiable P waves, so
atrial activity is organised. The distinguishing question is whether P waves are *absent*
(fibrillation) or *present but unaccompanied* (block).

## answer_d
Ventricular depolarisation is spreading abnormally through the ventricular muscle

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Abnormal spread through ventricular muscle — bundle branch block, or a ventricular
ectopic focus — produces a **wide, abnormally shaped QRS**, because activation bypasses or only
partly uses the fast Purkinje system. The stem states the QRS complexes are of normal duration, so
ventricular activation is proceeding normally when it happens. This option confuses a failure of
*transmission to* the ventricles with a failure of conduction *within* them.

## answer_e
An accessory pathway is conducting impulses around the atrioventricular node

## explanation_e
Incorrect, and it is close to the opposite of what is happening. An accessory pathway bridges the
fibrous skeleton and lets impulses reach the ventricles **early**, producing a *short* PR
interval, a slurred QRS upstroke and a predisposition to re-entrant tachycardia. This patient's
impulses are arriving **late or not at all**. The option catches a student who has recognised that
the abnormality concerns the atrioventricular junction but not which direction it fails in.

## title
A depolarisation vector points directly along the positive axis of lead II. What is recorded in lead II?

## question
A depolarisation vector points directly along the positive axis of lead II. What is recorded in lead II?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
During ventricular depolarisation the net electrical vector at one instant points at +60°, which
is exactly the orientation of the positive axis of lead II.

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
An isoelectric trace, because the vector is parallel to the lead and generates no deflection

## explanation_a
Incorrect, and it inverts the rule. A lead records **nothing** when the vector is
**perpendicular** to its axis, because there is then no component of the vector along the
direction the lead is measuring. A vector *parallel* to the lead axis gives the largest possible
signal. This option picks the student who remembers that some orientation produces an isoelectric
trace but has attached it to the wrong geometry — and it is the single most common error on
cardiac vectors.

## answer_b
A maximal negative deflection, because current flows towards the negative electrode

## explanation_b
Incorrect on the sign. The convention is fixed and worth stating precisely: a wave of
depolarisation moving **towards the positive electrode** of a lead produces an **upward
(positive)** deflection. The vector here points along the positive axis, so the deflection is
positive. A maximal *negative* deflection would require the vector to point at −120°, directly
away from lead II's positive pole. This option has the magnitude right and the direction reversed.

## answer_c
A maximal positive deflection, larger than in any other limb lead at that instant

## explanation_c
Correct. A lead records the **component of the cardiac vector projected onto that lead's axis**.
When the vector lies **exactly along the positive axis** of a lead, the whole of its magnitude
projects onto that axis, so the recorded deflection is the **maximum possible positive** value for
that lead — and, at that instant, larger than in any other limb lead, since every other lead sees
only a partial projection.

Two rules generate everything else on this topic:

**Direction sets the sign.** Depolarisation moving *towards* a lead's positive electrode gives an
**upward** deflection; moving *away* gives a **downward** one.

**Angle sets the size.** The deflection is proportional to the cosine of the angle between the
vector and the lead axis. So it is maximal at 0° (parallel), zero at 90° (**perpendicular**, an
isoelectric or biphasic complex), and maximally negative at 180°.

This is the reasoning behind the **12-lead ECG**, which uses ten electrodes — one on each limb and
six on the chest — to view one three-dimensional vector from many angles. No single lead can
locate a vector; a set of them can triangulate it.

Two standard applications follow. Because lead II's axis lies at **+60°** and the normal mean QRS
axis is roughly **−30° to +90°**, lead II normally shows a **tall positive QRS** — which is why it
is the conventional rhythm monitoring lead. And a vector at **+30°**, lying midway between lead I
at 0° and lead II at +60°, projects **equally** onto both and therefore produces **equal positive
deflections** in leads I and II. Comparing deflection sizes across leads is how the mean axis is
estimated, and how axis deviation is recognised.

## answer_d
A biphasic complex, because the vector crosses the lead axis during depolarisation

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A biphasic complex — roughly equal positive and negative components — arises when the
vector is **perpendicular** to the lead axis, so that it swings from one side of the axis to the
other and its projection changes sign. A vector aligned *with* the axis projects positively
throughout. This option describes a real ECG appearance but attaches it to the geometry that
actually produces the maximal positive deflection.

## answer_e
A deflection whose size depends on distance from the heart rather than on vector direction

## explanation_e
Incorrect as the determinant here. Electrode position and the conducting properties of the
intervening tissue do influence recorded amplitude, which is why body habitus, effusions and
emphysema all affect ECG voltages. But within the standard limb lead system the dominant
determinant of a deflection's size and sign is the **angle between the vector and the lead axis**.
This option replaces the variable the question is about with a secondary one.

## title
What distinguishes an afterdepolarisation from a normal pacemaker depolarisation?

## question
What distinguishes an afterdepolarisation from a normal pacemaker depolarisation?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A monitored patient has occasional extra beats. Each arises earlier than the next expected sinus
beat, and each is preceded by a normal action potential in the same tissue.

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
It arises spontaneously in the sinoatrial node rather than in the ventricles

## explanation_a
Incorrect, and it describes normal pacemaker activity rather than an abnormality.
Spontaneous depolarisation in the sinoatrial node is exactly what should happen — that is the
**automaticity** that makes it the pacemaker. An afterdepolarisation is defined by being
**triggered by a preceding action potential**, and it may occur in atrial, ventricular or
conducting tissue. This option inverts normal and abnormal.

## answer_b
It occurs only in tissue that has lost its resting membrane potential entirely

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Afterdepolarisations occur in cells that do have a resting potential and that have just
completed a normal action potential — indeed a preceding action potential is a **requirement**.
Complete loss of resting potential would render tissue inexcitable rather than prone to extra
beats. This option confuses a triggered abnormality with the depolarised, unexcitable state of
severely injured myocardium.

## answer_c
It is a conducted impulse arriving from the atria earlier than expected

## explanation_c
Incorrect, and it names the wrong origin. An impulse arriving early **from elsewhere** is a
conducted beat, and if it comes from the atria it is an atrial ectopic. An afterdepolarisation is
generated **locally, in the same cell**, by the depolarisation that preceded it — nothing has been
conducted in. The distinction matters because it separates a problem of abnormal *impulse
generation* from one of abnormal *conduction*.

## answer_d
It always occurs during the plateau phase and shortens the action potential

## explanation_d
Incorrect. One class of afterdepolarisation — the **early** afterdepolarisation — does arise
before repolarisation is complete, and it is associated with action potentials that are
**prolonged**, not shortened, which is why conditions that lengthen the QT interval predispose to
it. But afterdepolarisations do not *always* occur then: **delayed** afterdepolarisations arise
**after** repolarisation is complete. This option takes one subtype and its inverse effect and
generalises both.

## answer_e
It is an abnormal depolarisation triggered by the action potential that preceded it

## explanation_e
Correct. An **afterdepolarisation** is an abnormal depolarisation that is **triggered by a
preceding cardiac action potential** — which is precisely what the vignette describes, and what
separates it from every other source of an extra beat.

Two features define it. It is **abnormal**, unlike the spontaneous phase 4 depolarisation of a
pacemaker cell, which is a normal property. And it is **triggered**: it cannot occur on its own,
because it depends on the action potential immediately before it. That dependence is why this
mechanism is called *triggered activity* and is classified separately from enhanced automaticity
and from re-entry.

If an afterdepolarisation is large enough to **reach threshold**, it activates inward current and
initiates a full action potential. Because that beat arises earlier than the next expected sinus
beat, it appears as a **premature beat** — the extra beats in this patient. Repeated triggering
can sustain a tachycardia.

There are two subtypes, distinguished by *when* in the cycle they occur:

**Early afterdepolarisation** — arises **before repolarisation is complete**, during phase 2 or 3.
It is favoured by anything that **prolongs** the action potential and the QT interval, such as
bradycardia, hypokalaemia, or drugs that block potassium channels.

**Delayed afterdepolarisation** — arises **after repolarisation is complete**, during phase 4. It
is favoured by **intracellular calcium overload**, as in digoxin toxicity or high catecholamine
states, and tends to appear at faster rates.

The unifying idea worth carrying is that an extra beat can arise in three distinct ways — enhanced
automaticity, re-entry, or triggered activity of this kind — and that identifying which one is
operating is what makes treatment rational, since the provoking conditions differ.
