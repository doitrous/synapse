## title
A drug crosses the membrane via a carrier, down its concentration gradient, without ATP. Which process is this?

## question
A drug crosses the membrane via a carrier, down its concentration gradient, without ATP. Which process is this?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A drug's transfer across a cell membrane is abolished by a structurally similar compound applied at
the same time, is not affected by metabolic inhibitors that deplete cellular ATP, and never produces
a higher concentration inside the cell than outside.

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Simple diffusion

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Simple diffusion also proceeds down the gradient without ATP, so two of the three
observations fit — but it uses no carrier, and therefore cannot be **inhibited by a structurally
similar compound**. Competition for a binding site is the observation that excludes it: simple
diffusion has no site to compete for, and its rate depends on lipid solubility and the surface area
available, not on a saturable protein. This option is the trap for a student who stops at "no
energy required".

## answer_b
Primary active transport

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Primary active transport uses an energy-releasing pump, commonly an ATPase, to move
solute **against** its electrochemical gradient. Both features are contradicted by the stem: the
transfer is unaffected by ATP depletion, and it never concentrates the drug inside the cell. This
option picks the student who sees carrier-mediated competition and assumes any protein-mediated
transport must be active.

## answer_c
Pinocytosis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Pinocytosis engulfs material in a membrane vesicle, and it is a route for very large
molecules and particles rather than for small drug molecules. It shows no competition between
structurally similar small solutes and is not the mechanism being described. This option is chosen
by students listing membrane transport routes without matching each to the evidence given.

## answer_d
Facilitated diffusion

## explanation_d
Correct. **Facilitated diffusion requires a carrier but no energy, and proceeds down the
concentration gradient.** All three observations in the stem follow from that definition.

**A carrier is involved**, so the process is **substrate specific** — the carrier has a binding site
that recognises particular molecular shapes. That is why a **structurally similar compound competes**
and inhibits transfer, and it is the observation that separates facilitated diffusion from simple
diffusion.

**No energy is supplied**, so ATP depletion has no effect. The carrier does not pump; it provides a
pathway.

**Transport is down the gradient only.** Because the process is not energised, it can equalise
concentrations across the membrane but never reverse the gradient — which is exactly why the drug is
never concentrated inside the cell.

The mechanism is a **conformational change**: the solute binds the carrier on one face of the
membrane, the carrier changes shape so that the bound molecule is **exposed to the opposite side**,
and the solute is released. Nothing about that cycle requires energy when the movement is downhill.

The practical consequence, and the reason it is examined in pharmacology, is **saturability**.
Because there is a finite number of carriers, the rate reaches a **maximum** once they are all
occupied — carrier saturation imposes a ceiling on facilitated diffusion. Beyond that point,
increasing the concentration gradient does not increase the rate. Simple diffusion has no such
ceiling: its rate rises linearly with the gradient indefinitely.

So facilitated diffusion shares **saturability, specificity and competitive inhibition** with active
transport, and shares **downhill direction and energy independence** with simple diffusion. It sits
between the two, and questions on this topic are almost always answered by asking which of those
five properties has been observed.

## answer_e
Secondary active transport

## explanation_e
Incorrect, though it is the closest of the active mechanisms and worth distinguishing carefully.
Secondary active transport does not use ATP **directly** — it harnesses the electrochemical gradient
of another ion, usually sodium, which was itself established by an ATP-dependent pump. So it can
appear insensitive to short-term ATP depletion, and it is carrier-mediated and saturable. But its
purpose is to move a solute **against** its gradient, and it therefore **can** concentrate a drug
inside the cell. The stem states that no such accumulation occurs, which excludes it.

# Item

## id
QST-PHA-CARRIER-SATURATION-001

## title
Why does a carrier-mediated transport process have a maximum rate?

## question
Why does a carrier-mediated transport process have a maximum rate?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
As the concentration of a drug outside a cell is raised progressively, its rate of entry increases
at first and then stops increasing altogether, remaining constant despite further large increases in
external concentration.

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The concentration gradient is abolished once enough drug has entered the cell

## explanation_a
Incorrect. If the gradient were abolished, transport would stop, not continue at a constant maximal
rate — and the stem describes continued steady entry. The external concentration is also being
raised throughout, which *increases* the gradient rather than removing it. This option confuses
reaching equilibrium, where net movement ceases, with reaching a transport maximum, where movement
continues at a ceiling rate.

## answer_b
The number of carriers is finite, so beyond a certain concentration all binding sites are occupied

## explanation_b
Correct. **Carrier saturation imposes a maximum rate**, and the reason is simply that carriers are
**proteins present in finite number**.

Transport proceeds by a cycle: the solute **binds** the carrier, the carrier undergoes a
**conformational change** that exposes the bound molecule to the opposite side of the membrane, the
solute is **released**, and the carrier returns to its original conformation. Each carrier can
handle only one such cycle at a time.

At low concentrations, most carriers are unoccupied at any moment, so raising the concentration
recruits more of them and the rate rises. As concentration increases further, a progressively
larger fraction is occupied. Once **essentially all carriers are occupied at all times**, the
system is **saturated**: the rate is then limited by how fast the carriers can complete their
cycle, not by how much solute is available. Adding more solute cannot help, because there is
nothing free to bind it.

Three consequences follow, and all are examined in pharmacology:

**A transport maximum exists.** This is why the curve plateaus, and why carrier-mediated processes
are described by a maximum rate in a way simple diffusion is not — simple diffusion has no protein
to run out of, so its rate rises linearly and indefinitely with the gradient.

**Competition occurs.** Two substrates using the same carrier compete for the same finite sites, so
each reduces the transport of the other. This is the basis of a whole class of **drug
interactions**.

**Saturation can change kinetics.** When an elimination process that depends on a carrier or enzyme
becomes saturated, elimination stops being proportional to concentration and proceeds at a fixed
rate instead — the switch from **first-order to zero-order** kinetics. This is why a drug can
accumulate disproportionately once its clearance mechanism is saturated.

The same reasoning applies to **active tubular excretion** in the kidney, which is carrier-mediated
and therefore saturable — and consequently a site of competition and drug interaction.

## answer_c
The carriers become progressively denatured at high solute concentrations

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Carriers are not destroyed by carrying their substrate; saturation is a **reversible
occupancy** state, and the rate returns to normal when concentration falls. If denaturation were
occurring, the rate would **decline** rather than plateau, and the effect would not be readily
reversible. This option treats a kinetic ceiling as protein damage.

## answer_d
The membrane becomes electrically depolarised, opposing further entry

## explanation_d
Incorrect for this observation. Charge movement can oppose transport of an ion, and electrical
gradients genuinely matter for charged solutes — but depolarisation would produce a **progressive
slowing**, dependent on the charge carried, rather than a sharp ceiling common to all
carrier-mediated transport including that of uncharged solutes. Saturation is observed for neutral
substrates too, so the explanation cannot be electrical.

## answer_e
The drug's lipid solubility limits how much can cross per unit time

## explanation_e
Incorrect, and it names the determinant of the wrong process. Lipid solubility governs **simple
diffusion**, where the rate of transfer is proportional to how readily the substance dissolves in
the membrane lipid — and precisely because that route has no carrier, it shows **no maximum**. The
stem describes a plateau, which is the signature of a carrier. This option supplies the correct
variable for a mechanism that does not behave as described.

## title
What distinguishes primary from secondary active transport?

## question
What distinguishes primary from secondary active transport?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
Two carrier systems both move their substrate against its electrochemical gradient. One hydrolyses
ATP at the carrier protein itself. The other has no ATPase activity, but stops working when the
sodium gradient across the membrane is abolished.

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Primary transport moves ions and secondary transport moves organic molecules

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The distinction is about the **source of energy**, not the chemical class of the
substrate. Primary active transport handles ions in the familiar pumps, but the category is defined
by direct ATP hydrolysis rather than by what is carried; secondary systems also transport ions,
including sodium and hydrogen. This option substitutes a classification by cargo for one by
energetics.

## answer_b
Primary transport is saturable and secondary transport is not

## explanation_b
Incorrect. **Both** are carrier-mediated, so both are saturable, both are substrate specific, and
both are subject to competitive inhibition. Saturability distinguishes carrier-mediated transport
from **simple diffusion**, not primary from secondary active transport. This option applies a real
discriminator at the wrong level of the classification.

## answer_c
Primary transport moves solute down the gradient and secondary transport moves it against

## explanation_c
Incorrect. **Both** primary and secondary active transport move solute **against** its
electrochemical gradient — that is what makes each of them *active*. Movement down the gradient is
the defining feature of the passive processes, simple and facilitated diffusion. This option
confuses the active/passive division with the primary/secondary one.

## answer_d
Primary transport requires a carrier and secondary transport uses a channel

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Both use **carriers**. Channels are quite different structures — water-filled pores
through which solute flows down its gradient when the channel is open, as with leak channels or
ligand-gated channels — and they cannot transport against a gradient at all. Attributing a channel
to secondary active transport would make uphill movement impossible.

## answer_e
Primary transport hydrolyses ATP directly at the carrier; secondary transport uses an ion gradient established by a primary pump

## explanation_e
Correct. Both processes move solute **against** its electrochemical gradient, and both therefore
require energy. They differ in **where that energy comes from**.

**Primary active transport** uses an **energy-releasing membrane pump — commonly an ATPase — at the
carrier itself.** ATP is hydrolysed by the transport protein, and the released energy drives the
conformational change that carries solute uphill. The sodium–potassium ATPase is the standard
example: it hydrolyses ATP to move sodium out of the cell and potassium in, both against their
gradients.

**Secondary active transport** performs no ATP hydrolysis of its own. Instead it **couples** the
uphill movement of its substrate to the **downhill movement of an ion**, usually sodium, flowing
into the cell along the gradient that a primary pump has already created. The energy is therefore
still ultimately from ATP, but it has been **stored in an ion gradient** and is spent indirectly.
This is why the second system in the stem has no ATPase activity yet fails as soon as the sodium
gradient is abolished.

The dependency is the point worth carrying: **secondary active transport is parasitic on primary
active transport.** Inhibit the sodium–potassium pump and secondary systems fail too, after a delay,
as the sodium gradient runs down — even though they never used ATP directly.

Two clinically relevant consequences follow. Secondary systems can appear **insensitive to
short-term metabolic inhibition**, because the stored gradient persists briefly after ATP falls,
which is a common exam discriminator. And because both types are **carrier-mediated**, both are
**saturable and open to competition** — the basis of interactions at renal tubular transporters,
where active tubular excretion is saturable and drugs compete for the same carrier.

QST-PHA-LIPID-SOLUBILITY-DIFFUSION-001

## title
Two drugs of similar size differ tenfold in lipid solubility. How do their rates of simple diffusion compare?

## question
Two drugs of similar size differ tenfold in lipid solubility. How do their rates of simple diffusion compare?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
Two drugs of comparable molecular weight are applied to the same membrane at the same
concentration. Neither uses a carrier, and neither is ionised at the pH of the experiment. One is
ten times more lipid-soluble than the other.

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The more lipid-soluble drug diffuses about ten times faster, since rate is proportional to lipid solubility

## explanation_a
Correct. For simple diffusion, the **rate is proportional to lipid solubility** — so a tenfold
difference in lipid solubility produces roughly a tenfold difference in rate.

The reason is what the molecule has to traverse. A cell membrane is a **lipid bilayer**, and a drug
crossing it by simple diffusion must **dissolve into the lipid** on one side, move through it, and
leave on the other. How readily it dissolves in that lipid phase — its lipid solubility, expressed
as the **lipid/water partition coefficient** — therefore directly determines how much drug is
present in the membrane and available to move. Doubling the solubility doubles the concentration in
the lipid phase, and so doubles the flux.

The other determinants of simple diffusion follow the same logic: rate rises with the
**concentration gradient** and the **surface area** available, and falls with **membrane thickness**
and increasing **molecular size**. The stem controls for size, concentration and membrane, which is
why lipid solubility alone accounts for the difference.

Two points make this the most practically important idea in this section of the module.

**Ionisation dominates lipid solubility in practice.** An ionised drug is surrounded by water and is
effectively lipid-insoluble, so only the **non-ionised** fraction crosses membranes by simple
diffusion. Since the ionised fraction of a weak acid or base depends on the **pH** of the
compartment and the drug's **pKa**, the local pH controls the effective lipid solubility, and
therefore absorption. This is why the pH of the stomach and small intestine matters for oral
absorption, and why altering urinary pH changes renal drug excretion by trapping the ionised form
in the tubule.

**Simple diffusion has no ceiling.** Because no carrier is involved, there is nothing to saturate:
the rate rises with the gradient indefinitely, and there is no competition between structurally
similar drugs. That is the clean contrast with carrier-mediated transport.

## answer_b
Their rates are the same, since simple diffusion depends only on the concentration gradient

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The concentration gradient is one determinant, and the stem holds it equal — which is
precisely why it cannot explain a difference. Lipid solubility is an independent determinant,
because it fixes how much drug enters the lipid phase of the membrane in the first place. If only
the gradient mattered, every uncharged molecule of similar size would cross at the same rate, and
lipid-soluble anaesthetics would have no kinetic advantage over water-soluble compounds.

## answer_c
The more lipid-soluble drug diffuses faster, but the difference plateaus once the carriers are saturated

## explanation_c
Incorrect because it imports saturation into a process that has none. The stem states that neither
drug uses a carrier, and simple diffusion involves no transport protein — so there is nothing to
saturate and no plateau. Saturability, along with substrate specificity and competitive inhibition,
belongs to **carrier-mediated** transport. This option gets the direction right and attaches a
carrier-mediated property to a carrier-free mechanism.

## answer_d
The less lipid-soluble drug diffuses faster, because water-soluble drugs cross through aqueous pores

## explanation_d
Incorrect, and it inverts the relationship. Aqueous pores and channels do exist and do allow small
water-soluble molecules and ions to cross, so the mechanism named is real — but that is a separate
route, restricted to very small molecules, and it is not simple diffusion through the lipid
bilayer. For diffusion across the bilayer itself, lipid solubility helps rather than hinders. This
option picks the student who knows there is more than one route and applies the wrong one.

## answer_e
The difference cannot be predicted without knowing each drug's volume of distribution

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Volume of distribution is a **whole-body** parameter describing how widely a drug
distributes once in the circulation, and it is itself partly a *consequence* of lipid solubility —
lipid-soluble drugs tend to have a large Vd because they enter tissues readily. It is not needed to
predict the rate at which a molecule crosses a single membrane in vitro, which is what the stem
describes. This option confuses a pharmacokinetic descriptor with a physicochemical determinant.

QST-PHA-LIGAND-GATED-CHANNEL-001

## title
What opens a ligand-gated ion channel, and how does that differ from a leak channel?

## question
What opens a ligand-gated ion channel, and how does that differ from a leak channel?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
Two membrane channels are compared. The first passes ions continuously in the resting cell without
any stimulus. The second passes ions only when a specific extracellular molecule is present.

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
A change in membrane potential opens it; a leak channel opens in response to a ligand

## explanation_a
Incorrect, and the two mechanisms are transposed. Opening in response to a change in membrane
potential describes a **voltage-gated** channel — a third category, and the basis of the cardiac and
neuronal action potential. A **leak** channel is not gated by a ligand at all; it is open at rest.
This option names a real channel type and misassigns both halves of the comparison.

## answer_b
Mechanical stretch opens it; a leak channel is opened by phosphorylation

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Mechanically gated channels do exist — in baroreceptors and in hair cells, for instance —
and phosphorylation does modulate some channels, so neither term is invented. But the stem
specifies that the second channel responds to a **specific extracellular molecule**, which is a
ligand, and that the first is open **without any stimulus**, which is a leak channel. This option
supplies two real gating mechanisms that do not match the observations given.

## answer_c
Binding of a ligand to a specific receptor opens it; a leak channel is already open at rest

## explanation_c
Correct. The two channels differ in whether their opening requires a **signal**.

**Ligand-gated channels open when a ligand binds a specific receptor.** The receptor and the channel
are part of the same functional unit, so binding of the ligand produces a conformational change that
opens the pore directly. Because the receptor site is specific, only particular molecules will do
it — which is why these channels are pharmacological targets, and why an **agonist** opens the
channel while an **antagonist** occupies the site without opening it and blocks the agonist's
effect. The nicotinic acetylcholine receptor is the standard example: acetylcholine binding opens an
intrinsic cation channel.

**Ungated or leak channels** provide a membrane pathway that is **open at rest**, with no signal
required — although the open state of any individual channel may still **fluctuate**. Their function
is to set the resting permeability of the membrane, and the resting potassium leak is the main
determinant of the resting membrane potential in most cells.

The distinction that matters is therefore **regulated versus continuous** conductance. A leak
channel establishes the baseline; a gated channel is the switch that changes it in response to a
signal.

Two related points are examined alongside this. Ligand-gated channels produce a **fast** response
measured in milliseconds, because the channel and receptor are one structure and no intermediate
messenger is involved — the contrast with G-protein coupled receptors, which act through second
messengers over seconds, and with nuclear receptors, which alter gene transcription over hours. And
all channels, gated or not, allow ions to move **down** their electrochemical gradient only: a
channel is a pore, not a pump, so it can never transport against a gradient.

## answer_d
Both are opened by a ligand, but leak channels respond to intracellular ligands

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Intracellularly-gated channels do exist — those opened by cyclic nucleotides or by
calcium, for example — so the category is not fictitious. But a leak channel is defined by being
**ungated**: it requires no ligand from either side of the membrane. Making both channels
ligand-dependent removes the very distinction the stem sets up, since the first channel is described
as passing ions with no stimulus at all.

## answer_e
The ligand-gated channel actively pumps ions inward once the ligand binds

## explanation_e
Incorrect, and it confuses a channel with a pump. A channel is a **passive pore**: when it opens,
ions flow **down** their electrochemical gradient, and the channel adds no energy. Active pumping
against a gradient requires **primary active transport** with ATP hydrolysis at the carrier. This
option is worth separating because it is the error that makes the whole transport classification
collapse — if channels could pump, there would be no need for the active/passive distinction.

## learning_objective
Distinguish a ligand-gated channel, opened by ligand binding to a specific receptor, from an ungated
leak channel open at rest, and state that channels conduct only down the electrochemical gradient.

Brief: Three channels in one membrane — a leak channel drawn open with no stimulus, a ligand-gated
channel shown closed and then open with a ligand bound at its receptor site, and a voltage-gated
channel opening in response to a change in membrane potential — each labelled with its gating
stimulus and with ion flow drawn down the gradient in every case

the three gating types with flow always downhill fixes both the classification and the constraint
that channels are passive.

## author_notes
Graded Easy and written as a paired comparison so the two source concepts (ligand-gated channels,
leak channels) are assessed together rather than separately. Distractor A introduces voltage gating
as the third category students confuse with ligand gating; E tests the channel-versus-pump
distinction, which is the more fundamental error. The receptor-speed contrast is kept in the worked
explanation because it links this item to the pharmacodynamics section of the module.
