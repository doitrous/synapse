## title
Type I pneumocytes cover most of the alveolar surface, yet type II cells are more numerous. How is that possible, and what does each do?

## question
Type I pneumocytes cover most of the alveolar surface, yet type II cells are more numerous. How is that possible, and what does each do?

## subject
resp

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
Electron microscopy of an alveolus shows two epithelial cell types. One is extremely flattened,
with a thin rim of cytoplasm and a flat nucleus. The other is rounded, bulges into the alveolar
lumen, and contains conspicuous lamellar inclusion bodies.

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Type I cells are more numerous but individually small; type II cells secrete mucus

## explanation_a
Incorrect on both counts. Type I cells are the *less* numerous of the two yet cover the greater
area, which is the paradox the question turns on — so calling them more numerous inverts it. And
type II cells secrete **surfactant**, a phospholipid-rich material that lowers surface tension, not
mucus. Mucus is produced by **goblet cells**, which belong to the conducting airways and disappear
before the alveoli are reached. This option imports a conducting-airway cell into the alveolus.

## answer_b
Type II cells are flattened for gas exchange; type I cells are the progenitor population

## explanation_b
Incorrect — the two roles are transposed. The cell described as extremely flattened in the stem is
the **type I** pneumocyte, and flattening is precisely what suits it to gas exchange. The
**progenitor** function belongs to type II cells. This option is worth separating because getting
the numbering backwards makes every downstream inference wrong, including which cell repairs the
alveolus after injury.

## answer_c
Type I cells are few but extremely flattened, covering ~95% of the surface for gas exchange; type II cells are numerous, rounded, and secrete surfactant

## explanation_c
Correct. The apparent paradox dissolves once **cell number** is separated from **surface area
covered**.

**Type I pneumocytes** are the flattened cell in the stem: squamous, with a flat nucleus and very
little cytoplasm. They make up only about **40% of the alveolar epithelial cells by number**, but
because each is spread so thin and so wide, they cover roughly **95% of the alveolar surface**.
That extreme attenuation is their function — it minimises the diffusion distance across the
**blood–air barrier**, which comprises the type I cell, its fused basal lamina, and the capillary
endothelium. Since diffusion rate is inversely proportional to thickness, a thin cell is a fast
one. Type I cells are also **incapable of division**, which matters after injury.

**Type II pneumocytes** are the rounded cell bulging into the lumen, and the lamellar bodies are
the diagnostic feature — they are the storage form of **surfactant**. Type II cells are the
**more numerous** population, about **60% by number**, but occupy only about **5% of the surface**
because they are cuboidal rather than spread out. They have two jobs: they **secrete surfactant**,
which lowers alveolar surface tension, raises compliance and stabilises small alveoli against
large ones; and they serve as the **progenitor cell** of the alveolar epithelium, dividing after
damage and differentiating into type I cells.

The relationship between the two is what to carry forward: **type I cells do the gas exchange but
cannot replace themselves, so recovery from alveolar injury depends on type II cells.** It also
explains two clinical facts — that surfactant deficiency in the premature infant reflects immature
**type II** cells, and that type II hyperplasia is a characteristic response to diffuse alveolar
damage.

A third resident cell completes the picture: the **alveolar macrophage**, or dust cell, derived
from blood monocytes, which patrols the alveolar surface and phagocytoses inhaled particles.

## answer_d
Type I cells cover most of the surface and also produce surfactant; type II cells are phagocytic

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The first clause is half right — type I cells do cover most of the surface — but they do
not produce surfactant, which is the defining product of type II cells and the reason type II cells
contain lamellar bodies. Phagocytosis in the alveolus is performed by **alveolar macrophages**, not
by type II pneumocytes. This option gathers the correct facts about the alveolus and distributes
them among the wrong cells.

## answer_e
Both cell types exchange gas equally; the difference is that type II cells rest on a thicker basal lamina

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The two cells are functionally quite distinct, and the difference is in **cell shape and
secretory role**, not in the basal lamina beneath them. The basal lamina is in fact **fused** with
the capillary endothelial basal lamina at the thinnest parts of the barrier, which is what makes
diffusion efficient. A cuboidal, surfactant-filled type II cell is poorly suited to gas exchange
whatever it rests on. This option avoids the real distinction by inventing a structural difference
in the wrong layer.

QST-RES-DISTAL-AIRWAY-TRANSITION-001

## title
Moving distally along the bronchial tree, in what order do cartilage, goblet cells and cilia disappear?

## question
Moving distally along the bronchial tree, in what order do cartilage, goblet cells and cilia disappear?

## subject
resp

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A series of histological sections is taken at successive levels of the airway, from trachea to
alveolar duct, and a student is asked to place them in order using only the wall components
present in each.

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Cartilage disappears at the bronchioles, then goblet cells, and cilia last

## explanation_a
Correct. The bronchial tree changes progressively rather than abruptly, and the order in which
components are lost is the most reliable way to identify a level histologically.

**Cartilage** is present as C-shaped rings in the trachea and as irregular plates in the bronchi,
diminishing steadily and **disappearing at the bronchioles** — which is the defining criterion: a
bronchiole is an airway of about 1 mm or less with **no cartilage** in its wall. Its patency
therefore depends on the radial traction of surrounding lung tissue rather than on a skeleton,
which is why bronchioles collapse in expiration when that traction is lost, as in emphysema.

**Goblet cells** disappear next, and — the point most often examined — **goblet cells disappear
before cilia do**. That order is functionally sensible. Cilia beat to move the mucus blanket
proximally, so a short stretch of ciliated airway *beyond* the last mucus-producing cell is useful:
it can clear secretions swept up from further out. The reverse arrangement would leave mucus
produced with no means of moving it.

**Cilia** persist longest, thinning out through the respiratory bronchioles.

Three other components change in the same direction and are worth carrying together: the
**epithelium** becomes progressively thinner — pseudostratified ciliated columnar in the trachea,
simple columnar, then simple cuboidal in bronchioles, and finally simple squamous at the alveolus;
the **lamina propria** thins; and the **seromucous glands** decrease and disappear.

**Smooth muscle** is the exception to the pattern of simple decline. It does not disappear where the
others do — instead its **arrangement changes**: spiral in the intrapulmonary bronchi, **circular**
in the bronchioles, and reduced to rings at the alveolar openings in the alveolar ducts. Because
bronchioles have circular muscle and no cartilage, they are the airways in which constriction most
effectively narrows the lumen — which is precisely why the bronchiole is the site of airflow
obstruction in asthma.

## answer_b
Cilia disappear first, then goblet cells, and cartilage last

## explanation_b
Incorrect, and it reverses the sequence entirely. Cartilage is confined to the larger airways and
is the **first** of the three to go, at the bronchiole; cilia extend furthest distally and are the
**last**. As written, this option would place cartilage in the smallest airways and a
non-ciliated epithelium in the bronchi, which contradicts the definition of a bronchiole and the
existence of the mucociliary escalator.

## answer_c
Goblet cells disappear first, then cartilage, and cilia last

## explanation_c
Incorrect on the first step, and it is the near miss worth checking carefully. Cilia do indeed
persist longest, so the end of the sequence is right — but **cartilage disappears before goblet
cells**, not after. Cartilage is absent by definition from the bronchioles, whereas goblet cells
are still present in the largest bronchioles and disappear only beyond them. This option catches
the student who has learned the goblet-before-cilia rule and not fixed cartilage relative to it.

## answer_d
Cartilage disappears at the bronchioles, then cilia, and goblet cells last

## explanation_d
Incorrect, and it inverts the single detail this question is built around. **Goblet cells
disappear before cilia**, not after. If goblet cells persisted beyond the last ciliated cell, mucus
would be secreted into airways with no mechanism to clear it, and it would accumulate distally —
the opposite of how the mucociliary escalator works. This option gets cartilage right and then
transposes the pair that matters.

## answer_e
All three disappear together at the transition from bronchus to bronchiole

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The change along the airway is **gradual and staggered**, not a single step, which is
exactly why successive sections can be placed in order at all. If all three vanished at one point,
the bronchiolar and respiratory bronchiolar levels would be histologically indistinguishable. This
option picks the student who has learned the bronchus–bronchiole distinction as one boundary and
collapsed every change onto it.

## title
Why is tracheal cartilage C-shaped rather than a complete ring, and what closes the gap?

## question
Why is tracheal cartilage C-shaped rather than a complete ring, and what closes the gap?

## subject
resp

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A transverse section of the trachea shows a cartilage ring that is incomplete posteriorly. The
posterior deficiency is bridged by a band of tissue, and immediately behind it lies the
oesophagus.

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
The gap allows the trachea to be compressed during coughing; it is closed by dense fibrous tissue only

## explanation_a
Incorrect in its second half, which is the part that matters. The posterior gap does permit
narrowing of the tracheal lumen during a cough, and that narrowing raises expiratory air velocity —
so the first clause is defensible. But the gap is not bridged by fibrous tissue alone: it contains
**smooth muscle**, the trachealis, and it is active contraction of that muscle that produces the
narrowing. Describing the bridge as purely fibrous removes the mechanism that makes the first half
true.

## answer_b
The gap accommodates the recurrent laryngeal nerves as they ascend in the tracheal wall

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The recurrent laryngeal nerves ascend in the **tracheo-oesophageal groove**, alongside
the trachea rather than within the posterior gap of its cartilage, and they are not what the
deficiency exists for. Their course is clinically important — it is why they are at risk in
thyroid surgery — but it is a relation of the trachea, not the content of the cartilage gap. This
option names a true neighbouring structure and gives it the wrong role.

## answer_c
The cartilage is incomplete because it is a developmental remnant of the pharyngeal arches, and the gap is closed by respiratory epithelium

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The arrangement is functional, not a vestige, and the gap is not closed by epithelium —
epithelium lines the **lumen** as part of the mucosa and does not form part of the fibrocartilaginous
coat. This option confuses the layers of the tracheal wall: the deficiency lies in the
fibrocartilaginous coat and is bridged within that coat, deep to the mucosa and submucosa.

## answer_d
The gap lets the oesophagus expand during swallowing; it is closed by the trachealis smooth muscle

## explanation_d
Correct. The tracheal cartilages are **C-shaped**, deficient **posteriorly**, and the deficiency is
bridged by the **trachealis**, a band of **smooth muscle** running between the free ends of the
cartilage.

The arrangement solves two problems at once.

**It accommodates the oesophagus.** The oesophagus lies immediately **posterior** to the trachea. A
complete rigid cartilage ring would present an unyielding surface against which a swallowed bolus
would have to be forced. The soft posterior wall yields as the bolus passes, so swallowing and
breathing share the space without conflict — and the cartilage still keeps the airway open where
rigidity is needed, which is anteriorly and laterally.

**It permits active narrowing.** Contraction of the trachealis pulls the free ends of the cartilage
together and reduces the tracheal lumen. That is functionally significant during **coughing**: a
narrower lumen raises the velocity of expired air for the same flow, so secretions are cleared more
forcefully.

So the C-shape is a compromise between two competing requirements: **enough rigidity to prevent
collapse, enough compliance to allow the oesophagus to distend and the lumen to be actively
narrowed.**

This is worth contrasting with what happens further down. In the **intrapulmonary bronchi**,
cartilage becomes irregular plates and the smooth muscle is arranged **spirally**; in the
**bronchioles**, cartilage is absent altogether and the smooth muscle is **circular**, which is why
those airways are the effective site of bronchoconstriction. The same two components — cartilage
and smooth muscle — are simply reweighted as the airway becomes smaller.

The tracheal wall as a whole has four layers: **mucosa, submucosa, fibrocartilaginous coat**
(containing the cartilage and trachealis) **and adventitia**.

## answer_e
The gap allows the trachea to elongate during inspiration; it is closed by elastic cartilage

## explanation_e
Incorrect on both parts. The trachea does lengthen slightly with inspiration and with neck
extension, but that is permitted by the **fibroelastic tissue between successive cartilage rings**
— the vertical dimension — not by the posterior gap within each ring. And the bridge is smooth
muscle, not cartilage of any type; tracheal cartilage is **hyaline**, not elastic. This option
misplaces both the axis of movement and the tissue.

## title
The lung receives blood from two sources. What does the bronchial arterial supply do that the pulmonary arteries do not?

## question
The lung receives blood from two sources. What does the bronchial arterial supply do that the pulmonary arteries do not?

## subject
resp

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A patient with a pulmonary embolus occluding a segmental pulmonary artery is found to have no
infarction of the affected lung segment, and the tissue remains viable.

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The bronchial arteries carry deoxygenated blood to the alveoli for gas exchange

## explanation_a
Incorrect, and it describes the pulmonary arteries. The **pulmonary** arteries carry
**deoxygenated** blood from the right ventricle to the alveolar capillaries, where it is
oxygenated — that is the functional circulation. The **bronchial** arteries arise from the systemic
side and therefore carry **oxygenated** blood, and they supply lung tissue rather than participating
in gas exchange. This option transposes the two circulations.

## answer_b
The bronchial arteries deliver oxygenated systemic blood to nourish lung stroma, bronchial walls and visceral pleura

## explanation_b
Correct. The lung has **two** arterial supplies with quite different jobs, and confusing them makes
the vignette inexplicable.

**Pulmonary arteries — the functional circulation.** They carry **deoxygenated** blood from the
right ventricle to the alveolar capillaries for **gas exchange**. This is a high-flow, low-pressure
circuit receiving the entire cardiac output.

**Bronchial arteries — the nutritive circulation.** They arise from the **systemic** circulation
(from the descending thoracic aorta) and therefore carry **oxygenated** blood at systemic pressure.
They run along the **posterior** aspect of the principal bronchus and its divisions, as far as the
**respiratory bronchioles**, and they **nourish the lung's own tissues**: the bronchial tree walls,
the connective tissue stroma, and the **visceral pleura**. They carry only a small fraction of lung
blood flow.

The dual supply explains the vignette. If a pulmonary artery is occluded, the tissue it served
loses its gas-exchange perfusion, but the **bronchial arteries continue to nourish it**, so the
segment often remains viable and **pulmonary embolism frequently does not cause infarction**. That
is unusual — most organs have one arterial supply and infarct when it is blocked — and it is why
infarction after embolism tends to occur when the bronchial supply is also compromised, as in
pre-existing heart failure.

Two related points follow. **Where the two supplies meet, at the respiratory bronchiole, there are
anastomoses**, so a small amount of deoxygenated bronchial venous blood drains into the pulmonary
veins and reaches the left atrium unoxygenated — a normal physiological shunt contributing to the
small difference between alveolar and arterial oxygen tension. And the **visceral pleura is supplied
by the bronchial arteries** while the parietal pleura is supplied by systemic intercostal vessels —
relevant because the parietal pleura, unlike the visceral, has somatic sensory innervation and is
the source of pleuritic pain.

Note also that **alveolar walls contain no lymphatic vessels**; lymphatics begin at the level of
the interlobular septa and travel with the bronchial vessels.

## answer_c
The bronchial arteries provide the low-pressure high-flow circuit that receives the whole cardiac output

## explanation_c
Incorrect — that describes the pulmonary circulation. The **pulmonary** circuit necessarily receives
the entire cardiac output, because it lies in series with the systemic circulation, and it operates
at low pressure, which is why the right ventricle is thin-walled. The bronchial arteries are
**systemic** vessels carrying a small fraction of lung flow at **high** pressure. This option
attaches the defining haemodynamic features of one circulation to the other.

## answer_d
The bronchial arteries drain the lung of lymph, which the pulmonary vessels cannot do

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Arteries do not drain lymph — lymph is carried by a separate lymphatic system. It is
true that pulmonary lymphatics **travel alongside** the bronchial vessels and that **alveolar walls
themselves are devoid of lymphatics**, so the association is not accidental. But accompanying the
lymphatics is not the same as being them, and the bronchial arteries' role is nutritive. This
option confuses a spatial relationship with a function.

## answer_e
The bronchial arteries supply the parietal pleura and the diaphragm

## explanation_e
Incorrect on territory, and the distinction is clinically important. Bronchial arteries supply the
**visceral** pleura, which invests the lung itself. The **parietal** pleura is supplied by systemic
vessels of the chest wall — intercostal and internal thoracic branches — and the diaphragm by the
pericardiacophrenic and musculophrenic vessels. The split matters because the parietal pleura has
somatic sensory innervation and produces pleuritic pain, while the visceral pleura does not.

QST-RES-NASAL-CONCHAE-EPITHELIUM-001

## title
Which nasal concha is covered by olfactory rather than respiratory epithelium?

## question
Which nasal concha is covered by olfactory rather than respiratory epithelium?

## subject
resp

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
Biopsies are taken from the mucosa covering each of the three nasal conchae. Two show
pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium with goblet cells. The third shows a taller
pseudostratified epithelium without goblet cells, containing cells whose apical processes bear
non-motile cilia.

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The superior concha

## explanation_a
Correct. The **superior concha**, together with the adjacent roof of the nasal cavity and the upper
part of the nasal septum, is covered by **olfactory epithelium**. The **middle and inferior
conchae** carry ordinary **respiratory epithelium**.

The two epithelia are distinguishable on the features given in the stem.

**Respiratory epithelium** is pseudostratified ciliated columnar with **goblet cells**. Its cilia
are **motile** and beat in a coordinated fashion, propelling the mucus blanket — the mucociliary
escalator — towards the pharynx to be swallowed. Its job is **conditioning** inspired air:
warming, humidifying and trapping particles.

**Olfactory epithelium** is taller, lacks goblet cells, and contains three cell types: **olfactory
receptor neurones**, whose apical processes bear **non-motile** cilia carrying the odorant
receptors; **supporting (sustentacular) cells**; and **basal cells**. Its secretions come from
**Bowman's glands** in the lamina propria rather than from goblet cells. The non-motile cilia in the
stem are the diagnostic clue — they are sensory structures, not motile ones.

The **position** is functionally logical. The superior concha lies **high in the nasal cavity**,
out of the main airstream, so olfactory mucosa is protected from the bulk of inspired air and its
particulate load. It also explains why **sniffing** improves the perception of a faint smell:
sniffing directs more air up into that recess, which quiet breathing largely bypasses.

Two further points are commonly examined. The olfactory receptor neurone is a **bipolar neurone**
whose central process forms an **olfactory nerve fibre**, passing through the cribriform plate to
the olfactory bulb — so the olfactory epithelium contains neurones directly exposed to the
environment. Because of that exposure, they are unusual among neurones in being **capable of
regeneration** from the basal cell population. And fracture of the cribriform plate can shear these
fibres, causing anosmia and a potential route for cerebrospinal fluid leak.

## answer_b
The middle concha

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The middle concha is covered by **respiratory** epithelium, and it is clinically
prominent for a different reason: the middle meatus beneath it receives drainage from the maxillary,
frontal and anterior ethmoidal sinuses. This option picks the student who recalls that the middle
concha is important without checking what makes it so.

## answer_c
The inferior concha

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The inferior concha carries **respiratory** epithelium over an exceptionally rich venous
plexus — the nasal swell bodies — and it is the largest of the three, projecting into the main
airstream. That position is the point: it is built for **conditioning** air, and its vascular
mucosa is what congests in allergy or inflammation to restrict airflow. Olfactory mucosa in this
exposed position would be poorly protected.

## answer_d
All three conchae carry olfactory epithelium

## explanation_d
Incorrect, and the stem excludes it directly: two of the three biopsies show ciliated columnar
epithelium **with goblet cells**, which is respiratory, not olfactory. Olfactory mucosa occupies
only a small area of the nasal cavity — the superior concha, the roof and the upper septum — because
most of the nasal cavity is committed to conditioning inspired air rather than to smell.

## answer_e
None of the conchae; olfactory epithelium is confined to the nasal septum

## explanation_e
Incorrect, though it contains a partial truth. Olfactory epithelium **does** extend onto the
**upper** part of the nasal septum, so the septum is genuinely part of the olfactory area — but it
is not confined there, and the **superior concha** is the standard concha-based answer. This option
catches the student who has learned one component of the olfactory area and treated it as the
whole.

## title
At autopsy of a newborn, the lung is placed in water and sinks. What does this indicate?

## question
At autopsy of a newborn, the lung is placed in water and sinks. What does this indicate?

## subject
resp

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
An infant is found dead and it is not known whether the birth was live or stillborn. At autopsy the
lungs are firm and dark, and a portion placed in water sinks.

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
The lung is congested with blood, indicating heart failure before death

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Pulmonary congestion does increase lung density and can make lung tissue heavier — but
it is not what this test demonstrates, and a congested lung that had been **aerated** would still
usually float, because the trapped air in the alveoli dominates the overall density. The test
distinguishes **aerated from unaerated** tissue, not congested from uncongested. This option
substitutes a different cause of a heavy lung for the one being tested.

## answer_b
The lung is consolidated by pneumonia acquired before delivery

## explanation_b
Incorrect as the inference. Consolidation fills alveoli with exudate and does make affected lung
sink, so the physical reasoning is sound — but it is not the conclusion supported here, because an
**unaerated fetal lung sinks anyway**, whether or not it is infected. Congenital pneumonia would
have to be established by histology showing an inflammatory infiltrate, not by flotation. This
option offers a possible cause of a sinking lung without recognising that the normal fetal state
already produces the finding.

## answer_c
The lung has never been aerated, so respiration had not been established

## explanation_c
Correct. **Before birth the lung is firm and sinks in water.** After breathing begins, air enters
the alveoli, mean tissue density falls below that of water, and aerated lung **floats**.

The reason is straightforward. In fetal life the lung is not a gas-filled organ. Its potential
airspaces are filled with **fetal lung fluid** secreted by the respiratory epithelium, the alveoli
are unexpanded, and the tissue is therefore solid and relatively dense — glandular in appearance,
which is why the fetal lung is described as firm and liver-like. Its specific gravity exceeds that
of water, so it sinks.

With the **first effective breaths**, three things happen: fetal lung fluid is cleared, partly by
compression during delivery and largely by active reabsorption; **surfactant** from type II
pneumocytes lowers surface tension so the alveoli can be expanded and kept open; and air replaces
fluid. The resulting air-containing tissue is **less dense than water** and floats.

So flotation is a simple test of whether the lung has ever contained air, and this is its
**forensic** application: it contributes to determining whether an infant was **live-born and
breathed**, or **stillborn**. A sinking lung supports the latter.

The important limitations belong with the finding, because a single physical test cannot carry a
conclusion of that gravity. **Putrefactive gas** in a decomposing body can make an unaerated lung
float, giving a false positive for breathing. **Attempted resuscitation** can introduce air into the
lungs of a stillborn infant. Areas of **atelectasis** or **consolidation** can sink even in a lung
that has breathed, so sampling matters. The test is therefore corroborative and is interpreted with
histology — which shows expanded alveoli lined by flattened epithelium in a lung that has breathed —
and with the rest of the autopsy findings.

## answer_d
Surfactant was present, allowing the alveoli to collapse completely after death

## explanation_d
Incorrect, and it reverses surfactant's action. Surfactant **lowers** surface tension and thereby
**opposes** alveolar collapse — its deficiency is what causes the widespread collapse of neonatal
respiratory distress syndrome. Its presence therefore favours alveoli remaining open and the lung
floating. This option correctly identifies surfactant as relevant to alveolar expansion and then
inverts the direction of its effect.

## answer_e
The lung is normal for a term newborn, since neonatal lung is denser than adult lung

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A term newborn **who has breathed** has aerated lungs that float; there is no normal
state in which a lung that has established respiration sinks in water. The relevant distinction is
**before versus after the onset of breathing**, not newborn versus adult. This option treats the
finding as a normal developmental variant, which would remove any diagnostic meaning from the test.
