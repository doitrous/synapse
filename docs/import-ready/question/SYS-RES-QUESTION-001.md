## title
How does alveolar pressure change across one quiet respiratory cycle?

## question
How does alveolar pressure change across one quiet respiratory cycle?

## subject
resp

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A student is shown a trace of alveolar pressure recorded through one cycle of quiet breathing
and asked to label four points: immediately before inspiration begins, mid-inspiration, at
end-inspiration, and mid-expiration. They label the pre-inspiratory point −3 cmH₂O, having
recalled that pressure inside the chest is negative.

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Constantly negative throughout, at about −3 cmH₂O, becoming more negative on inspiration

## explanation_a
Incorrect, and it is the misconception stated in the vignette. −3 cmH₂O is **intrapleural**
pressure at the end of quiet expiration, not alveolar pressure. The two are different
measurements in different spaces, and intrapleural pressure genuinely is negative throughout
the cycle. Alveolar pressure is the pressure of gas *inside the alveoli*, which communicates
with the atmosphere through open airways — so it cannot stay negative, because air would
simply keep flowing in until the gradient was abolished.

## answer_b
Zero throughout, because the airways are open to the atmosphere

## explanation_b
Incorrect, and it over-applies a correct idea. Open airways mean alveolar pressure *returns*
to zero whenever flow stops, but not that it is always zero. If alveolar pressure were
permanently equal to atmospheric there would be no pressure gradient at any moment, and with
no gradient there is no airflow — the patient would not be breathing at all. A gradient must
exist during inspiration and expiration; it is only at the turning points that it vanishes.

## answer_c
Zero at the start and end of each phase, about −1 cmH₂O during inspiration, and about +1 cmH₂O during expiration

## explanation_c
Correct. Alveolar pressure **oscillates around atmospheric**, and the sign tells you which way
air is moving.

By convention all these pressures are quoted relative to atmospheric, so "zero" means equal to
atmosphere. Before inspiration begins, no air is flowing, the alveoli communicate freely with
the atmosphere through open airways, and alveolar pressure is therefore **zero**.

Inspiratory muscles then expand the thorax. The lungs are pulled outwards with it, alveolar
volume increases, and by Boyle's law the pressure of the gas inside falls — to about
**−1 cmH₂O**. Air now flows *inwards* down that gradient. As the lung fills, the gradient is
progressively abolished, and at **end-inspiration**, when flow stops, alveolar pressure is back
to **zero**.

Quiet expiration is passive. The inspiratory muscles relax and elastic recoil shrinks the
lungs, compressing the contained gas to about **+1 cmH₂O**. Air flows *outwards*, and at
end-expiration pressure returns once more to **zero**.

Two general points make this reconstructible rather than memorised. First, **alveolar pressure
is zero whenever there is no flow** and non-zero only while air is moving — so the two
zero-crossings are the turning points of the cycle. Second, the gradient is remarkably small:
roughly 1 cmH₂O moves about 500 mL of tidal volume, which is a measure of how little resistance
healthy airways offer.

Do not confuse this with **intrapleural** pressure, which is negative throughout — about −3
cmH₂O at end-expiration, falling to about −8 during inspiration — because it reflects the
opposing recoil of lung and chest wall rather than the movement of gas. Alveolar pressure
changes sign; intrapleural pressure does not.

## answer_d
About +1 cmH₂O during inspiration and −1 cmH₂O during expiration

## explanation_d
Incorrect: the correct magnitudes with the signs reversed. Air flows from higher to lower
pressure, so for air to enter the lungs alveolar pressure must be *below* atmospheric during
inspiration, not above it. As written, this option describes a lung that blows air out when it
should be drawing it in. It picks the student who has learned the ±1 cmH₂O figures without
attaching them to the direction of flow.

## answer_e
About −8 cmH₂O during inspiration, returning to −3 cmH₂O at end-expiration

## explanation_e
Incorrect, and it is the most instructive distractor because both numbers are real. −8 and −3
cmH₂O are the **intrapleural** pressures at peak inspiration and end-expiration respectively.
This option is the vignette's error carried through the whole cycle: the right values, from the
right textbook page, describing the wrong compartment. The way to keep the two apart is to ask
what is being measured — gas inside the alveoli, which must equalise with the atmosphere when
flow stops, or fluid pressure in the pleural space, which never does.

## title
Bronchial smooth muscle contraction halves airway radius. What happens to resistance through those airways?

## question
Bronchial smooth muscle contraction halves airway radius. What happens to resistance through those airways?

## subject
resp

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
During an acute asthma attack, bronchial smooth muscle contraction and mucosal oedema reduce
the effective radius of a population of airways to half its baseline value. Airway length and
gas viscosity are unchanged, and flow through them remains laminar.

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
It doubles

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Doubling is what would happen if resistance varied inversely with radius to the
first power — the intuitive assumption that halving a dimension doubles the difficulty. Airway
resistance does not behave that way. This option underestimates the effect by a factor of
eight, and a student who holds it cannot explain why asthma is dangerous: a mere doubling of
resistance would be a nuisance, not a cause of respiratory failure.

## answer_b
It quadruples

## explanation_b
Incorrect, and this is the commonest wrong answer because it borrows a real exponent from the
wrong law. Resistance would quadruple if it varied with the *square* of radius — the
relationship for cross-sectional **area**, which does fall fourfold when radius halves. But
resistance to laminar flow depends on the fourth power of radius, not on area alone, because
the velocity profile across the tube also changes. Confusing the area relationship with the
resistance relationship is the specific error here.

## answer_c
It rises eightfold

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Eightfold corresponds to the *third* power of radius, which governs no step in this
calculation. It tends to be chosen by students who recall that the exponent is "high" and
reconstruct it as a cube, or who conflate the radius relationship with a volume relationship.
The exponent is four.

## answer_d
It is unchanged, because total cross-sectional area is what determines resistance

## explanation_d
Incorrect, and it confuses two distinct facts about the airway tree. It is true that *total*
cross-sectional area increases enormously with each generation of branching, which is why the
terminal bronchioles contribute little to total resistance and why the medium-sized bronchi are
the main site of resistance. But that is a statement about how resistance is *distributed*
between airway generations. It does not mean the radius of an individual airway is irrelevant —
narrow any given airway and its own resistance rises steeply, which is exactly what happens in
asthma.

## answer_e
It rises sixteenfold

## explanation_e
Correct. For laminar flow through a tube, **Poiseuille's law** gives

**R = 8ηl / πr⁴**

so resistance is inversely proportional to the **fourth power of the radius**. Halving the
radius multiplies resistance by 1/(½)⁴ = **2⁴ = 16**.

This fourth-power relationship is the single most important quantitative idea in airway
mechanics, and it explains a great deal:

**Why asthma is dangerous.** A change in airway calibre that sounds modest produces a
disproportionate rise in the work of breathing. A 50% reduction in radius raises resistance
sixteenfold, so the respiratory muscles must generate far larger pressure swings for the same
airflow — which is why the accessory muscles are recruited and why the patient tires.

**Why bronchodilators work so well.** The relationship runs in reverse with equal force. A
relatively small increase in radius produces a large fall in resistance, so even partial
relaxation of bronchial smooth muscle gives marked symptomatic relief.

**Why radius dominates the other terms.** Length and viscosity enter the equation only to the
first power. Doubling airway length doubles resistance; halving radius multiplies it by
sixteen. Radius is therefore the variable the body regulates — through autonomic tone — and the
variable disease attacks.

Two related points follow from the same equation. Airway resistance falls during **inspiration**
because the increasingly negative intrapleural pressure distends the airways and radial traction
from the surrounding parenchyma holds them open; it rises during expiration as those forces
reverse, which is why expiratory wheeze predominates in obstructive disease. And
**parasympathetic (vagal) stimulation** causes bronchoconstriction and increases mucus
secretion, so it raises resistance, while sympathetic and circulating adrenergic activity
dilates the airways.

## title
Why does surfactant prevent small alveoli from emptying into large ones?

## question
Why does surfactant prevent small alveoli from emptying into large ones?

## subject
resp

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A student is asked why, given that the collapsing pressure of a bubble is inversely
proportional to its radius, the small alveoli of a healthy lung do not progressively empty
into the larger ones until the lung consists of a few enormous air spaces.

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Surfactant lowers surface tension equally in all alveoli, so the pressure difference between large and small is abolished

## explanation_a
Incorrect, and it is the near miss that most students settle on. A *uniform* reduction in
surface tension would lower the collapsing pressure everywhere but would not remove the
inequality: by Laplace's law, if tension is the same in a small and a large alveolus, the small
one still generates the higher pressure and still empties into its neighbour. Reducing tension
uniformly makes the lung easier to inflate, but only a tension that varies *with alveolar size*
can stabilise small alveoli against large ones. That variation is the whole point.

## answer_b
Surfactant molecules become more concentrated as an alveolus shrinks, so surface tension falls further in the smallest alveoli

## explanation_b
Correct. The stabilising mechanism depends on surfactant's effect being **size-dependent**, not
merely large.

Start from **Laplace's law** for a fluid-lined sphere: collapsing pressure **P = 2T / r**,
where T is surface tension and r the radius. With T fixed, a small alveolus generates a higher
collapsing pressure than a large one, so gas would flow from small to large and the lung would
progressively consolidate into a few big spaces. That is the paradox the stem describes.

Surfactant resolves it because it is a **surface-active film of fixed quantity spread over a
changing area**. As an alveolus deflates its surface area falls, the surfactant molecules are
packed closer together, and the film's ability to disrupt the hydrogen bonding between water
molecules rises — so **surface tension falls steeply in exactly the alveoli that are getting
smaller**. Conversely, when an alveolus inflates, the film is spread thin and surface tension
rises, resisting further expansion.

The result is that T is not a constant but falls as r falls, and the ratio 2T/r stays roughly
even across alveoli of different sizes. Small alveoli are stabilised and large ones are
restrained: the lung becomes **self-equalising** rather than self-consolidating.

Two consequences are heavily examined. **Surface tension is the major force opposing inflation**
— it accounts for roughly two-thirds of the lung's elastic recoil, with tissue elasticity
contributing the remainder — so surfactant markedly increases compliance and reduces the work
of breathing. And surfactant is produced by **type II pneumocytes**, appearing late in
gestation; its deficiency in the premature infant causes **respiratory distress syndrome**,
in which high surface tension produces stiff, poorly compliant lungs and widespread alveolar
collapse. A lecithin:sphingomyelin ratio above 2:1 in amniotic fluid indicates that production
has matured.

The same size-dependence explains **hysteresis**: at any given pressure, lung volume is greater
during deflation than during inflation, because the surfactant film is more densely packed on
the way down.

## answer_c
Surfactant increases the radius of small alveoli until all alveoli are the same size

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Surfactant does not change alveolar geometry — it alters the surface tension of the
fluid lining. Alveoli in a normal lung are *not* all the same size, and they do not need to be;
what matters is that the collapsing pressures are equalised despite the differences in radius.
This option picks the student who has understood that the problem is a difference between large
and small alveoli and assumed the solution must be to abolish the difference itself.

## answer_d
Interdependence of adjacent alveoli holds small alveoli open, and surfactant plays no part

## explanation_d
Incorrect in its second half, which is what makes it wrong. Structural interdependence is real:
alveoli share walls, and a collapsing alveolus is held open by radial traction from its
neighbours, which genuinely contributes to alveolar stability. But it supplements surfactant
rather than replacing it, and it cannot account for the observations that define surfactant's
role — the steep fall in tension with area, the hysteresis of the pressure–volume curve, or the
collapse seen when surfactant is absent in respiratory distress syndrome.

## answer_e
Surfactant raises alveolar pressure in small alveoli to match that in large ones

## explanation_e
Incorrect, and it reverses the mechanism. Surfactant does not generate pressure; it reduces the
tension that *creates* collapsing pressure. Nor is the goal to raise pressure in small alveoli —
their problem is that their collapsing pressure is already too *high*. The effect of surfactant
is to lower 2T/r in the small alveolus by lowering T, bringing it down to match its larger
neighbour. This option picks a student who has the direction of the imbalance backwards.

## title
In emphysema and in pulmonary fibrosis, which way does lung compliance move, and why?

## question
In emphysema and in pulmonary fibrosis, which way does lung compliance move, and why?

## subject
resp

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
Two patients are compared. One has emphysema with radiological destruction of alveolar walls;
the other has established pulmonary fibrosis with dense interstitial collagen deposition. Both
report breathlessness on exertion.

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Compliance is reduced in both, since both are chronic lung diseases that stiffen the lung

## explanation_a
Incorrect, and it treats "chronic lung disease" as a single mechanical category. The two
conditions damage opposite components of the lung's elastic behaviour: fibrosis *adds*
stiffening tissue, while emphysema *destroys* the elastic tissue that provides recoil. Grouping
them together loses the distinction between restrictive and obstructive physiology, which is
the basis of interpreting every spirometry result.

## answer_b
Compliance is increased in both, since both reduce the effective alveolar surface area

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Reduced surface area for gas exchange explains the hypoxaemia in both conditions,
but it is not what determines compliance. Compliance is a mechanical property — how much volume
the lung takes up per unit distending pressure — and it is set by the elastic properties of the
tissue and by surface tension, not by how much area is available for diffusion. This option
picks the student who has substituted a gas-exchange explanation for a mechanical one.

## answer_c
Compliance is reduced in emphysema because air trapping stiffens the lung, and increased in fibrosis

## explanation_c
Incorrect — the correct directions, applied to the wrong diseases. This is the most frequent
error on this topic. The reasoning behind it is usually that emphysematous lungs are
hyperinflated and therefore "full and tight", so they must be stiff. But hyperinflation is a
*consequence* of the loss of elastic recoil, not evidence of stiffness: the lung is
over-distended precisely because nothing is pulling it back. Fibrosis, conversely, produces
small stiff lungs, not compliant ones.

## answer_d
Compliance is increased in emphysema because elastic tissue is destroyed, and reduced in fibrosis because collagen stiffens the lung

## explanation_d
Correct. **Compliance is the volume change produced per unit change in distending pressure**,
ΔV/ΔP — a measure of how readily the lung distends. Normal combined lung–thorax compliance is
about **100 mL/cmH₂O**. The two diseases move it in opposite directions, and the reason lies in
what each does to the tissue.

**Emphysema — compliance increased.** Destruction of alveolar walls destroys the **elastic
fibres** that generate recoil. With less elastic tissue opposing expansion, a given distending
pressure produces a larger volume change, so compliance rises. The lung is *easier to inflate*
but harder to empty, because expiration depends on the very elastic recoil that has been lost.
Airways lose the radial traction that held them open and collapse during expiration, producing
air trapping, hyperinflation and a raised functional residual capacity. The clinical picture is
**obstructive**: expiration is prolonged and effort-dependent.

**Fibrosis — compliance reduced.** Deposition of collagen in the interstitium makes the lung
stiff. A larger distending pressure is needed for the same volume change, so compliance falls
and the work of breathing rises. Lung volumes are small and breathing tends to be rapid and
shallow. The picture is **restrictive**.

The general rule, and the way to avoid inverting it, is to ask **what has happened to the
tissue** rather than how the lung looks on a film: *add* stiff material and compliance falls;
*destroy* elastic material and compliance rises. High compliance is therefore not the same as
healthy lung function — the emphysematous lung is highly compliant and works very poorly,
because a lung needs recoil as well as distensibility.

It is worth keeping compliance and **elastance** distinct: elastance is elastic resistance to
expansion, the reciprocal notion, so it is *reduced* in emphysema and *raised* in fibrosis.
Compliance also depends on which structure is being described — lung alone, chest wall alone, or
the two combined — and the combined value is lower than either component, because the lungs
recoil inward while the thorax recoils outward.

## answer_e
Compliance is unchanged in both, because chest wall compliance compensates for the change in lung compliance

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The chest wall does contribute to combined respiratory compliance, and the combined
value is lower than either component alone because lung and thorax recoil in opposite
directions. But the chest wall does not adjust itself to offset a change in the lung — it has
no mechanism for doing so. A change in lung compliance therefore alters the combined value in
the same direction. This option catches the student who has met the combined-compliance concept
and turned it into a homeostatic mechanism it is not.

## title
At the same transpulmonary pressure, why does the lung hold a larger volume during deflation than during inflation?

## question
At the same transpulmonary pressure, why does the lung hold a larger volume during deflation than during inflation?

## subject
resp

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A static pressure–volume curve is recorded from an excised lung by inflating it in steps and
then deflating it in steps, pausing at each step until flow ceases. The inflation and deflation
limbs do not coincide: at any given pressure on the way down, the lung holds more volume than
it did at that pressure on the way up.

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The surfactant film is more densely packed during deflation, so surface tension is lower at any given volume

## explanation_a
Correct. This separation of the inflation and deflation limbs is **hysteresis**, and its main
cause is the behaviour of the surfactant film.

Surfactant is a fixed quantity of surface-active material spread over a changing surface area.
During **inflation** the alveolar surface is expanding, so the film is stretched thin, its
molecules are spread further apart, and **surface tension is relatively high** — the lung
resists expansion, and a given pressure achieves comparatively little volume. During
**deflation** the surface area is shrinking, the same molecules are compressed into a smaller
area, and **surface tension falls**. With less surface tension opposing it, the lung holds on to
more volume at any given pressure, so the deflation limb lies **above** the inflation limb.

Put as a slope, **compliance is greater on deflation than on inflation** — which is the same
statement about the same two curves.

Note that the stem describes a *static* measurement: each step is held until flow stops, so
airway resistance has been deliberately excluded. This matters, because there is a separate and
commonly confused phenomenon in which resistance *is* responsible — during ordinary rhythmic
breathing, airway and tissue resistance make volume lag behind pressure, so a **dynamic** loop
also encloses an area. The static hysteresis described here persists even with no flow at all,
which is precisely how surface forces are shown to be the cause.

Two further points connect this to the rest of respiratory mechanics. The same size-dependence
of surfactant that produces hysteresis is what stabilises small alveoli against large ones under
Laplace's law. And at **low lung volumes** the inflation limb is especially flat, because
pressure is being spent re-opening collapsed alveoli rather than expanding open ones — the
reason compliance is lowest at the extremes of inflation and greatest in the mid-range, and the
physiological argument for positive end-expiratory pressure keeping alveoli recruited.

## answer_b
Airway resistance opposes inflation more than deflation, so volume lags behind pressure

## explanation_b
Incorrect for the measurement described, and it is the distractor this question exists to
separate. Airway and tissue resistance do make volume lag pressure — but only when gas is
*flowing*, which produces hysteresis in a **dynamic** loop recorded during rhythmic breathing.
The stem specifies a static curve in which each step is held until flow ceases, so resistance
contributes nothing. That static hysteresis remains is the experimental evidence that surface
forces, not resistance, are responsible.

## answer_c
The chest wall recoils outward during deflation, holding the lung open

## explanation_c
Incorrect on two counts. The measurement is made on an **excised lung**, so there is no chest
wall involved at all. And even in an intact subject, transpulmonary pressure is defined across
the lung alone — the pressure difference between alveoli and pleural space — so chest wall
recoil is already excluded from the x-axis of this curve. This option picks the student
reaching for a familiar fact about combined lung–thorax mechanics without checking what is being
measured.

## answer_d
Alveoli rupture during inflation and reseal during deflation, trapping additional gas

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Nothing ruptures during a normal inflation, and hysteresis is fully reversible — the
same loop can be recorded repeatedly from the same lung, which would be impossible if each
inflation caused structural damage. Trapped gas would also raise the volume at *end*-deflation
rather than shifting the whole limb. This option treats a routine mechanical property as
evidence of injury.

## answer_e
Surfactant is secreted continuously during the manoeuvre, so more is present by the deflation limb

## explanation_e
Incorrect, and it is a plausible-sounding near miss. Hysteresis does depend on surfactant, but
on the **redistribution of a fixed amount** over a changing surface area, not on additional
secretion. Type II pneumocyte secretion is far too slow to alter surface tension within a single
inflation–deflation manoeuvre, and hysteresis is demonstrable in a lung whose secretory activity
has ceased. The variable that changes is molecular *packing density*, not quantity.
