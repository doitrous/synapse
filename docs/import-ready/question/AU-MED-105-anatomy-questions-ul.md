## title
Muscle attached to the greater tuberosity of the humerus

## question
Which muscle is attached to the greater tuberosity of the humerus?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Subscapularis muscle

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Subscapularis is the one rotator cuff muscle that inserts on the lesser tuberosity, not the greater.

## answer_b
Teres minor

## explanation_b
Correct. The greater tuberosity has three facets that take three of the four rotator cuff muscles in order from top to bottom — supraspinatus, infraspinatus and teres minor — so teres minor is a correct answer here. Subscapularis is the odd one out among the cuff, inserting on the lesser tuberosity instead. The intertubercular (bicipital) groove between the two tuberosities then takes the tendon of the long head of biceps, not a muscle insertion.

## answer_c
Serratus anterior

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Serratus anterior inserts on the costal surface of the scapula's medial border, nowhere near the humerus.

## answer_d
Pectoralis major

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Pectoralis major inserts on the lateral lip of the bicipital groove, not the greater tuberosity.

## explanation_b

True statement about the upper end of the humerus

## question
About the upper end of the humerus, which is true?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Subscapularis muscle attaches to the greater tuberosity

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Subscapularis attaches to the lesser, not the greater, tuberosity.

## answer_b
Teres major is attached to the floor of the bicipital groove

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Teres major attaches to the medial lip of the bicipital groove; latissimus dorsi occupies the floor.

## answer_c
The bicipital groove allows passage of the short head of biceps

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The bicipital groove carries the tendon of the long head of biceps, not the short head, which instead arises from the coracoid process as part of the conjoint tendon.

Correct. The head faces medially, superiorly and slightly posteriorly, matched against the shallow glenoid, which is what makes this joint so mobile but also so prone to dislocation. All three other options misattribute a structure to the wrong landmark of the upper humerus.

Nerve supply of trapezius

## question
The trapezius muscle is supplied by:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Cranial accessory nerve

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The cranial (accessory) root of the accessory nerve joins the vagus and supplies pharyngeal and laryngeal muscles, not trapezius.

## answer_b
Spinal accessory nerve

## explanation_b
Correct. Trapezius is supplied by the spinal root of the accessory nerve (cranial nerve XI), which descends through the posterior triangle of the neck to reach it, with proprioceptive fibres from C3 and C4 travelling alongside. This makes trapezius the one scapula-moving muscle supplied from outside the brachial plexus entirely — every other scapular muscle in this bank (rhomboids, levator scapulae, serratus anterior, the rotator cuff) takes a plexus branch instead. This is why testing trapezius (asking a patient to shrug against resistance) checks cranial nerve XI, not a plexus root or trunk.

Dorsal scapular nerve

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The dorsal scapular nerve supplies the rhomboids and levator scapulae, not trapezius.

## answer_d
Thoracodorsal nerve

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The thoracodorsal nerve supplies latissimus dorsi, not trapezius.

Muscle that rotates the scapula downward

## question
Which muscle can rotate the scapula downward?

## subject
msk

## status

## vignette

## answer_a
Rhomboideus minor

## explanation_a
Correct. Rhomboid minor, together with rhomboid major and levator scapulae, rotates the scapula so the glenoid cavity faces downward — the opposite action to trapezius and serratus anterior, which rotate it upward during abduction above 90 degrees. This makes the rhomboids and levator scapulae, all three supplied by the dorsal scapular nerve, the antagonists of the upward rotators during arm elevation.

## answer_d
Latissimus dorsi

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Latissimus dorsi acts on the humerus at the shoulder joint (extension, adduction, medial rotation), not on the scapula.

## title
Nerve injury causing winging of the scapula

## question
Winging of the scapula is due to injury of:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Axillary nerve

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Axillary nerve injury weakens deltoid and teres minor, producing a flattened shoulder contour, not winging.

## answer_b
Musculocutaneous nerve

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Musculocutaneous nerve injury weakens the arm's anterior flexor compartment (biceps, brachialis, coracobrachialis), unrelated to the scapula.

## answer_c
Dorsal scapular nerve

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Dorsal scapular nerve injury weakens the rhomboids and levator scapulae but does not produce the classic winging pattern.

## answer_d
Long thoracic nerve

## explanation_d
Correct. The long thoracic nerve (C5-C7) runs superficially on serratus anterior's surface, making it vulnerable to injury during mastectomy, axillary surgery or a penetrating stab wound to the chest wall. Its loss paralyses serratus anterior, which normally holds the scapula's medial border flush against the thoracic wall and rotates it upward during abduction; without it, the medial border lifts away from the ribs — winging — most visible when the patient pushes against a wall. This is one of the most frequently tested nerve-injury patterns in the upper limb because the mechanism (surgical or penetrating injury low in the axilla) is so clinically recognisable.
