## title
The general chemical formula of carbohydrate is

## question
The general chemical formula of carbohydrate is

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
(CH2O)n

## explanation_a
Correct. Carbohydrates as a class share the general empirical formula (CH2O)n, which reflects their composition of carbon together with hydrogen and oxygen in the same 2:1 ratio found in water — the origin of the older name "hydrates of carbon." Glucose (C6H12O6) is a direct example: writing it as (CH2O)6 shows the formula in action. Fructose and galactose, both also C6H12O6, fit the same (CH2O)6 pattern despite differing from glucose in structure.

## answer_b
(CH2O)2n

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Doubling the subscript this way does not correspond to how carbohydrate formulas are actually built; it would imply twice as many carbons as hydrogens/oxygens relative to the true 1:2:1 ratio, which does not match glucose or any other common sugar.

## answer_c
(CHO)n

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Dropping one hydrogen from the repeating unit removes the second hydrogen that pairs with oxygen in the water-like ratio, so this formula would not correctly describe glucose (C6H12O6) or other simple sugars.

## answer_d
CnH2nO

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This rearrangement changes where the oxygen sits in the formula and does not correctly reproduce the atom counts of an actual sugar like glucose; the standard, testable form keeps the repeating unit as (CH2O), not as a single terminal oxygen attached to a CnH2n chain.

## question
The only carbohydrate which is not having any chiral carbon atom is

## subject

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Glyceraldehyde is in fact the reference aldotriose used to define the whole D/L system, precisely because its single carbon-2 bears four different groups (H, OH, CHO, CH2OH) and is a true chiral centre — the opposite of what this question asks for.

## answer_b
Erythrose

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Erythrose is an aldotetrose with two chiral carbons (C2 and C3), giving it several stereoisomeric forms (including its epimer threose); it is not the achiral exception this question is looking for.

## answer_c
Dihydroxyacetone

## explanation_c
Correct. Dihydroxyacetone, the simplest possible ketose, has a central carbon bonded to two identical -CH2OH groups (plus the carbonyl oxygen), rather than four different groups. With no four-way asymmetry, that carbon is not a true chiral centre, so dihydroxyacetone is optically inactive and has no D/L or enantiomeric forms — the one exception among the carbohydrates.

Erythrose

## explanation_d
Incorrect, for the same reason as option B: this option repeats "Erythrose" (a duplication printed in the source itself), and erythrose has two chiral carbons, so it is not the achiral answer either way it is listed.

Which of the following is a keto-Hexose

## subject

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Dihydroxyacetone is a ketose, but it is a ketotriose (three carbons), the simplest possible ketose — not a six-carbon (hexose) sugar.

## answer_b
Erythrulose

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Erythrulose is a ketotetrose (four carbons); it fits the ketose half of "keto-hexose" but not the six-carbon requirement.

## answer_c
Glyceraldehyde

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Glyceraldehyde is an aldose, not a ketose (its carbonyl is a terminal aldehyde, not an internal ketone), and it is also a triose (three carbons) rather than a hexose — it fails both parts of "keto-hexose."

Fructose

## explanation_d
Correct. Fructose is a six-carbon sugar (a hexose) whose carbonyl group is an internal ketone rather than a terminal aldehyde, making it a ketose. Combining the two facts, fructose is the keto-hexose — the sweetest of the common monosaccharides and a component of sucrose.

## answer_e
Ribulose

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Ribulose is a ketopentose (five carbons, an intermediate of the pentose phosphate pathway), not a six-carbon hexose.

Glucose

## explanation_a

## answer_b
Galactose

With no free anomeric carbon left on either residue, sucrose has no reducing group and is a non-reducing sugar.

## answer_e
Maltose

## explanation_e
Incorrect as the "except" answer. Maltose is two glucose units joined by an alpha-1,4 bond that leaves one glucose's anomeric carbon free, so maltose is a reducing sugar.

## title
Which of the following best describes the bond between a ribose sugar and the non-sugar moiety in a nucleotide?

## question
Which of the following best describes the bond between a ribose sugar and the non-sugar moiety in a nucleotide?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
N-Glycosidic bond

## explanation_a
Correct. In a nucleotide, the nitrogenous base attaches to the pentose sugar (ribose or deoxyribose) through a bond from the base's nitrogen atom to the sugar's anomeric (C1') carbon — an N-glycosidic bond. This is distinct from the phosphate's attachment, which esterifies onto the pentose's 5' carbon rather than forming a glycosidic bond at all.

## answer_b
Peptide bond

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A peptide bond links amino acids together in a protein through a carbon-nitrogen linkage between backbone groups; it has no role in nucleotide structure, which is built from a base, a pentose sugar and phosphate rather than amino acids.

## answer_c
O-Glycosidic bond

## explanation_c
Incorrect. An O-glycosidic bond (through an oxygen atom) is how two monosaccharides join to form a disaccharide, such as the alpha-1,4 bond in maltose. The base-to-pentose linkage in a nucleotide instead goes through the base's nitrogen, making it an N-glycosidic bond, not an O-glycosidic one.

## answer_d
Phosphodiester bond

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A phosphodiester bond is what links one nucleotide to the next within a nucleic acid strand (joining the 3'-hydroxyl of one sugar to the 5'-phosphate of the next), not what attaches the base to the sugar within a single nucleotide.

Glycogen

## explanation_b
Correct. Starch

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Starch does contain a branched component (amylopectin, with alpha-1,6 branches), but it also contains a substantial linear component (amylose), and its overall branching frequency is lower than glycogen's — glycogen is the more highly branched homopolysaccharide of the two.

## question
Isomers differing as a result of variations in configuration of the -OH and -H on carbon atoms 2, 3 or 4 of glucose are known as

## subject
fnd

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Epimers

## explanation_a
Correct. Two sugars that differ in configuration at only one carbon — other than the anomeric carbon — are called epimers. Glucose and galactose (differing only at C4) and glucose and mannose (differing only at C2) are the classic epimer pairs, and the definition specifically covers non-anomeric carbons such as C2, C3 or C4.

## answer_b
Anomers

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Anomers differ specifically at the anomeric carbon (C1 in an aldose), the carbon created when the sugar cyclises — not at C2, C3 or C4, which is what this question specifies.

## answer_c
Aldo-Keto Isomer

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Aldose-ketose isomers differ in the type of carbonyl group they carry (an aldehyde versus a ketone), a functional-group difference rather than a difference in spatial configuration at one particular carbon such as C2, C3 or C4.

## answer_d
D-L Isomer

## explanation_d
Incorrect. D and L isomers (enantiomers) differ in configuration at the reference carbon that is farthest from the carbonyl group (for glucose, C5) — mirror images of each other at every chiral centre — not a difference confined to just one of C2, C3 or C4.

## title
Sucrose

## question
Sucrose:-

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Is a non-reducing sugar

## explanation_a
Correct. Sucrose is built from glucose and fructose joined through their anomeric carbons (C1 of glucose to C2 of fructose), which uses up both sugars' anomeric carbons in the glycosidic bond and leaves no free anomeric carbon able to open into the reactive aldehyde or ketone form needed to reduce a testing reagent. This is exactly why sucrose is the standard textbook example of a non-reducing sugar, unlike maltose or lactose, which each retain one free anomeric carbon. The absence of a free anomeric carbon is the same structural feature the next option's claim gets backwards.

## answer_b
Has a free anomeric carbon

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Sucrose's glycosidic bond specifically links the anomeric carbons of both glucose and fructose to each other, so neither sugar has a free anomeric carbon left — the opposite of what this option claims, and exactly why sucrose does not reduce.

## answer_c
Results from partial hydrolysis of carbohydrates

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Sucrose is a disaccharide built by joining two monosaccharides (glucose and fructose) together through a condensation reaction; it is not a partial-hydrolysis breakdown product of a larger carbohydrate.

## answer_d
None of the above

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Sucrose being a non-reducing sugar is a true statement, so an option denying all of the above cannot be correct when one of the preceding statements is in fact accurate.

## title
Glucose and fructose are isomers — what does this mean?

## question
Glucose and fructose are isomers. This means that:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
They differ in the configuration around carbon number 2

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Differing at C2 alone would describe an epimer relationship (as with glucose and mannose); glucose and fructose's difference is not confined to one carbon's configuration, since their carbonyl-group type differs entirely.

## answer_b
They differ in the configuration around carbon number 4

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Differing at C4 alone would describe an epimer relationship (as with glucose and galactose); glucose and fructose's relationship is a different kind of isomerism altogether, based on carbonyl-group type rather than a single carbon's configuration.

## answer_c
One is an aldose and the other a ketose

## explanation_c
Correct. Glucose and fructose share the same molecular formula (C6H12O6) but differ in the position and type of their carbonyl group: glucose's carbonyl is an aldehyde at C1, making it an aldohexose, while fructose's carbonyl is a ketone at C2, making it a ketohexose. This aldose/ketose functional-group difference is a distinct category of isomerism from epimers (one-carbon configurational difference), anomers (anomeric-carbon difference) or enantiomers (full mirror image), and it is exactly the same fact tested elsewhere by asking which sugar has six carbons and a keto group. Glucose and fructose being isomers means they share a formula, not that they share a carbonyl-group type.

## answer_d
They differ in the configuration around carbon number 5

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A difference at C5 specifically describes the D-/L- (enantiomer) relationship; glucose and fructose's relationship is instead an aldose-ketose difference, not a C5 configurational one.

## answer_e
They are mirror images of each other

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Being mirror images describes enantiomers (such as D-glucose and L-glucose), which differ at every chiral carbon while keeping the same carbonyl-group type; glucose and fructose are not mirror images of each other, since their carbonyl-group type itself differs.
