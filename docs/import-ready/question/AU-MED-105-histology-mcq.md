## explanation_d

## title
A young boy presented to the ENT clinic with a large perforated nasal septum following trauma. Despite his young age, the doctor told his parents that spontaneous healing would be very poor and that he needs a repair operation. Why did the doctor reach this conclusion?

## question
A young boy presented to the ENT clinic with a large perforated nasal septum following trauma. Despite his young age, the doctor told his parents that spontaneous healing would be very poor and that he needs a repair operation. Why did the doctor reach this conclusion?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette


## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Due to absence of the perichondrium in the nasal septum

## explanation_a
The nasal septum's hyaline cartilage does carry a perichondrium — it is the structure the cartilage actually depends on for any repair it can manage, not one that is absent here.

## answer_b
The chondrocytes are a static cell population

## explanation_b
Chondrocytes can still divide (interstitial growth); calling them a fixed, non-dividing population overstates the limitation and is not the reason given for poor healing.

## answer_c
The matrix here is poorly hydrated

## explanation_c
Cartilage matrix is in fact highly hydrated (proteoglycan-rich), and hydration is not what limits a reparative response reaching an injury.

## answer_d
Avascularity of the matrix

## explanation_d
Correct. Cartilage has no blood vessels, nerves or lymphatics of its own; it is nourished only by diffusion from the perichondrium's vessels. A perforation removes tissue faster than diffusion-fed repair can replace it, and no inflammatory or reparative cell population can be delivered by blood to the defect the way it would be to a vascularised tissue. This is exactly why the answer to 'will cartilage heal on its own' is almost always 'poorly, and worse with age', regardless of the patient's youth.

## correct_answer

## answer_a
Letter C

## explanation_a
Correct. The epimysium is the dense connective-tissue layer surrounding the whole muscle, carrying its major vessels and nerves — the outermost of the three named coverings (epimysium, perimysium, endomysium), and on this diagram it is labelled C. Losing sight of which of the three named layers is the outermost is the most common way this question is missed.

## answer_b
Letter A

## explanation_b
Letter A is not the outermost, whole-muscle covering on this diagram; it more plausibly labels the perimysium (around a fascicle) or endomysium (around a single fibre).

## answer_c
Letter D

## explanation_c
Letter D is not the epimysium on this labelled diagram.

## answer_d
Letter B

## explanation_d
Letter B is not the epimysium on this labelled diagram — it more plausibly labels one of the finer coverings around a fascicle or single fibre.
