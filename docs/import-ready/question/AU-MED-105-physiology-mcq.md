## title
Which of the following refers to rheobase?

## question
Which of the following refers to rheobase?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Alexandria content lane (W1-105-PHYS)

## vignette


## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Maximal time by weak stimulus which is required to give a response

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This describes a time value at a weak (sub-threshold) strength, not the current intensity that rheobase actually names, and confuses the axis being measured.

## answer_b
Maximum current intensity of unlimited duration to give a response

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Rheobase is the minimal, not the maximal, current that excites the tissue; a maximal current at unlimited duration is not a defined excitability parameter at all.

## answer_c
Minimal current intensity of unlimited duration to give a response

## explanation_c
Correct. Rheobase is defined as the minimal current strength that, given unlimited duration, is just able to excite a tissue to threshold. It is a pure measure of the tissue's excitability threshold, independent of any time term, which is what separates it from chronaxie. Rheobase and chronaxie together define a tissue's strength-duration curve, and rheobase is always the intensity axis reference point from which chronaxie's "twice rheobase" strength is measured.

## answer_d
Minimal time by strong stimulus which is required to give a response

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This describes utilisation time (the time needed at rheobase strength), or conflates it with a "strong stimulus" duration, rather than defining rheobase itself, which is a current intensity, not a time.

## question
Chronaxie is:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner

## vignette


## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Minimal time below which no current whatever its strength can excite the tissue

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This describes utilisation time at an unspecified strength, not chronaxie, which is anchored specifically to twice-rheobase current, not to "whatever strength".

## answer_b
Time needed by threshold current of unlimited duration to give a response.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Threshold current of unlimited duration is rheobase itself, and the time needed at that unlimited-duration rheobase strength is utilisation time, not chronaxie.

Minimal time needed by a current strength double rheobase to give a response.

Correct. Chronaxie is defined as the minimal duration of a stimulus at twice the rheobase strength that is needed to excite the tissue. It is a time-based measure of excitability that is read directly off the strength-duration curve at the point where current is fixed at twice rheobase, giving a practical, reproducible index for comparing the excitability of different tissues or nerve fibre types. A shorter chronaxie means a more excitable tissue, since less time is needed at that fixed, doubled current strength.

## answer_d
Threshold current of unlimited duration

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This restates rheobase's own definition (a current, at unlimited duration) rather than chronaxie, which is a time value measured at twice rheobase.

## title
The resting membrane potential of a nerve fibre is:

## question
The resting membrane potential of a nerve fibre is:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Alexandria content lane (W1-105-PHYS)

## vignette


## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Due to K+ diffusion from outside to inside the nerve fibre

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Potassium's resting diffusion runs from inside to outside the fibre, down its concentration gradient, not from outside to inside as stated, and this outward K+ leak (not an inward one) is what makes the interior negative.

## answer_b
Due to diffusion of intracellular protein to outside the nerve fibre

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Intracellular proteins are large, negatively charged anions that cannot cross the membrane at all; they contribute to the resting potential precisely by staying trapped inside, not by diffusing out.

## answer_c
Normally about -70 mV in small nerves

## explanation_c
Correct. The resting membrane potential of a typical small nerve fibre is normally around -70 mV, interior negative to exterior, set mainly by the membrane's much greater resting permeability to potassium than to sodium, which drives the potential close to the potassium equilibrium potential. This value is the stable baseline from which threshold depolarisation must occur to trigger an action potential. Small departures from this figure occur between fibre types, but -70 mV is the standard teaching value for a nerve fibre at rest.

## answer_d
Dependent on the permeability of calcium

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The resting membrane potential is set overwhelmingly by potassium (and, to a lesser extent, sodium and chloride) permeability, not by calcium permeability, which plays essentially no role in the fibre's resting state.

## title
The all-or-none rule means that:

## question
The all-or-none rule means that:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Alexandria content lane (W1-105-PHYS)

## vignette


## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
The action potential is graded

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The action potential is specifically not graded; grading is a property of the local response and of whole-muscle or whole-nerve-trunk recruitment, not of a single fibre's action potential.

## answer_b
The amplitude of the action potential is proportionate to the intensity of the stimulus

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This describes a graded, proportional response, which is the opposite of all-or-none behaviour; the action potential's amplitude does not scale with stimulus intensity above threshold.

## answer_c
All types of stimuli can trigger an action potential

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Only a stimulus that reaches threshold intensity triggers an action potential; sub-threshold stimuli of any kind produce no propagated response.

## answer_d
A threshold stimulus gives a maximal response

## explanation_d
Correct. The all-or-none rule states that once a stimulus reaches threshold, the excitable unit (a single nerve fibre, a single skeletal muscle fibre, or cardiac muscle acting as a syncytium) fires a full, maximal action potential; any stronger stimulus produces an identical-sized response, not a bigger one. A sub-threshold stimulus, by contrast, produces no propagated action potential at all, only a local, decaying response. This threshold-or-nothing behaviour at the single-fibre level is what allows a whole muscle or nerve trunk to still grade its overall response, by varying how many all-or-none units are recruited, not by varying each unit's own response size.

## title
The sinoatrial node (S.A.N.) is the normal cardiac pacemaker because:

## question
The sinoatrial node (S.A.N.) is the normal cardiac pacemaker because:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Alexandria content lane (W1-105-PHYS)

## vignette


## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The fastest impulse discharge.

## explanation_a
Correct. Every part of the heart's specialised conducting tissue can generate its own spontaneous rhythm, but the SA node's cells discharge spontaneously at the fastest intrinsic rate of all of them. Because its impulse always reaches and resets the slower latent pacemakers (the AV node, the Purkinje system) before they reach their own threshold, the SA node's own discharge rate is what sets the heart rate under normal conditions. If the SA node is damaged, a slower latent pacemaker such as the AV node takes over, but only at its own lower intrinsic rate.

## answer_b
Location in the atrium.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The SA node's location at the junction of the superior vena cava and right atrium is anatomical, not the reason it leads; a structure in that location with a slower discharge rate would not become the pacemaker.

## answer_c
Neural control.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Autonomic nerves modulate the SA node's rate but do not confer its pacemaker status, which is intrinsic to its own automaticity, present even in a denervated heart.

## answer_d
Relative position to the A.V.N.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Its position relative to the AV node explains impulse sequence, not why it wins the race to threshold; that is decided purely by which tissue's spontaneous discharge rate is fastest.

## title
The pacemaker potential instability is due primarily to:

## question
The pacemaker potential instability is due primarily to:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Alexandria content lane (W1-105-PHYS)

## vignette


## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Slow decrease in K+ permeability

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A slow decrease in K+ permeability is not the primary driver named for the prepotential; the initiating event is hyperpolarisation switching on the sodium-carrying funny current.

## answer_b
Slow increase in K permeability.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. An increase, not a decrease, in K+ permeability is what produces repolarisation and the hyperpolarisation that follows it, but the instability itself is driven onward by the funny current, not by K+ permeability continuing to rise.

## answer_c
Hyperpolarization stimulating voltage gated Na channels.

## explanation_c
Correct. At the end of repolarisation the pacemaker cell hyperpolarises to about -60 mV, and it is exactly this hyperpolarisation that activates the "funny current" (I_f), a slow inward sodium current through voltage-gated channels that begins carrying the membrane spontaneously back toward threshold. This spontaneous drift is the prepotential (diastolic depolarisation), and it is the reason the pacemaker potential never settles at a fixed resting value the way a working myocyte's membrane does. As the potential drifts further, transient and then L-type calcium channels take over to complete the depolarisation to firing level.

## answer_d
Rapid increase in Ca permeability.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Calcium permeability does rise, but only later in the prepotential (via T-type, then L-type channels); the primary trigger that starts the drift is the sodium-carrying funny current switched on by hyperpolarisation.

## title
The main cause of the plateau in the cardiac muscle action potential:

## question
The main cause of the plateau in the cardiac muscle action potential:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Alexandria content lane (W1-105-PHYS)

## vignette


## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Out flux of K in exchange of Na

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The plateau is not an exchange of K for Na; it is Ca2+ influx balanced by K+ efflux, with no direct ionic exchange with sodium involved in maintaining the plateau.

## answer_b
Outflux of K

## explanation_b
Incorrect. K+ efflux alone, without the balancing Ca2+ influx, would simply repolarise the membrane rather than hold it flat at a plateau; both currents together are required.

## answer_c
Influx of Ca in exchange to efflux of Na

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The plateau's calcium influx is balanced by potassium efflux, not by sodium efflux; sodium channels have already inactivated by this phase of the cardiac action potential.

## answer_d
The balance in influx of and out flux of positive ions along the membrane

## explanation_d
Correct. During the plateau, voltage-gated L-type calcium channels opened during the upstroke keep letting Ca2+ enter the cell, and this inward calcium current is nearly balanced by an outward efflux of K+ through voltage-gated potassium channels. Because these two large, opposite currents of positive charge are closely matched, the net current — and so the membrane potential — barely changes, producing the flat plateau instead of an immediate repolarisation. That calcium entry during the plateau is also what couples the action potential to contraction, via calcium-induced calcium release.

## title
Regarding the differences between cardiac muscle and pacemaker action potentials, which of the following is true:

## question
Regarding the differences between cardiac muscle and pacemaker action potentials, which of the following is true:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Alexandria content lane (W1-105-PHYS)

## vignette


## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Cardiac muscle action potential has lower magnitude than pacemaker

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The pacemaker action potential in fact has a lower magnitude and a slower, smaller upstroke than the fast-response cardiac muscle action potential, the reverse of what this option states.

## answer_b
Both have fast depolarization

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Only the working (fast-response) cardiac muscle has a fast, high-magnitude upstroke carried by fast Na+ channels; the pacemaker's upstroke is slow, carried by L-type Ca2+ channels because fast Na+ channels are inactivated at its less negative operating range.

## answer_c
Both have 2 parts repolarization

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The characteristic two-part (rapid then plateau) repolarisation pattern belongs to the fast-response cardiac muscle action potential; the pacemaker potential's own repolarisation is a single, simpler phase feeding directly into the next prepotential.

## answer_d
Pacemaker action potential is unstable while cardiac muscle potential is not.

## explanation_d
Correct. A pacemaker (slow-response) cell never settles at a fixed potential: its prepotential continuously drifts the membrane toward threshold between beats. A working atrial or ventricular myocyte (fast-response cell), in contrast, sits at a stable resting potential until an external stimulus arrives and does not spontaneously depolarise on its own. This difference in baseline stability, more than any single ionic detail, is the defining functional contrast the bank is testing between the two action potential types.

## question
Which of the following is true regarding conduction in unmyelinated nerve fibers:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Alexandria content lane (W1-105-PHYS)

## vignette


## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
1. It is in the form of saltatory conduction

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Saltatory conduction, where the impulse jumps from node to node, is the hallmark of myelinated, not unmyelinated, fibres, which have no insulated internodes to jump across.

## answer_b
2. It saves energy

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Continuous conduction is less, not more, energy-efficient than saltatory conduction, because every successive patch of membrane must be depolarised and then restored by the Na+/K+ pump along the whole excited length.

## answer_c
3. It is local unpropagated

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Unmyelinated conduction is propagated (it travels the length of the fibre); it is a genuine, if slow, propagated action potential, not a local, non-propagated response like the local excitatory state.

## answer_d
4. It is in the form of continuous conduction

## explanation_d
Correct. In an unmyelinated fibre, every patch of membrane along its length is excitable, so the depolarised patch sets up a local circuit with the immediately adjacent resting membrane, driving that neighbouring patch to threshold in turn; the process repeats continuously along the entire fibre. This continuous, point-to-point spread is what defines unmyelinated conduction, and it is comparatively slow and metabolically costly compared with the saltatory conduction of a myelinated fibre.
