QST-PHA-WEAK-BASE-ABSORPTION-001

## title
Why is a weak base drug poorly absorbed from the stomach?

## question
Why is a weak base drug poorly absorbed from the stomach?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A weak base drug with a pKa of 8 is given orally. Very little is absorbed while it remains in the
stomach at pH 2, but absorption proceeds readily once it reaches the small intestine.

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
The stomach lacks the carriers required to transport basic drugs

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Most drug absorption occurs by **simple diffusion** through the lipid membrane, which
requires no carrier at all — so the absence of a carrier cannot explain the difference. The
gastric mucosa is perfectly capable of absorbing lipid-soluble molecules; weak **acids** are
absorbed there for exactly that reason. This option invents a transport requirement where the
limiting factor is physicochemical.

## answer_b
Gastric acid destroys the drug before it can be absorbed

## explanation_b
Incorrect as the general explanation. Some drugs genuinely are degraded by gastric acid, and that
is a real reason for enteric coating — but it would render the drug inactive rather than merely
delay its absorption, and the stem states that absorption proceeds normally further along the
gut. A drug destroyed in the stomach does not reappear intact in the duodenum. This option
substitutes chemical degradation for a reversible ionisation effect.

## answer_c
A base accepts a proton, so in gastric acid it is almost entirely ionised and cannot cross the lipid membrane

## explanation_c
Correct. A **Brønsted–Lowry base accepts a proton**, and that single property determines where a
basic drug is absorbed.

In the **stomach** at pH 2, protons are abundant. The base accepts one, becoming **protonated and
therefore ionised** — it now carries a charge, is surrounded by water, and is effectively
lipid-insoluble. Since simple diffusion requires the drug to dissolve into the lipid bilayer,
the ionised form **cannot cross**, and absorption is negligible.

In the **small intestine** at a pH nearer 6–7, far fewer protons are available. Much more of the
drug remains **non-ionised**, lipid-soluble, and able to diffuse across the mucosa — so absorption
proceeds.

The general rule follows from the definitions and does not need separate memorising: **a weak base
is ionised in acid and non-ionised in alkali; a weak acid is the reverse.** For this drug with a
pKa of 8, the pH is six units below the pKa in the stomach, so the ionised form outnumbers the
non-ionised by roughly a millionfold — absorption there is effectively zero.

Two qualifications belong with this. First, the **small intestine dominates absorption for almost
all oral drugs regardless of pKa**, because its surface area is enormous by comparison; the pH
effect modifies an already lopsided contest rather than deciding it. Second, the same logic run
in reverse is **ion trapping**: a drug that becomes ionised on one side of a membrane is held
there, which is the basis of manipulating urinary pH to accelerate excretion in overdose.

## answer_d
Basic drugs are too large to cross the gastric mucosa

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Molecular size does affect diffusion, and very large molecules cross membranes poorly —
but "basic" says nothing about size. Weak bases span the same range of molecular weights as weak
acids, and the same molecule is absorbed readily a short distance further along the gut, which
size alone could never explain. This option attributes a pH-dependent effect to a fixed property
of the molecule.

## answer_e
The drug binds to gastric mucus, which prevents it reaching the epithelium

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Binding to luminal contents can reduce absorption for particular drugs, and it is a real
mechanism for some interactions with antacids and adsorbents — but it is not what governs weak base
absorption, and it would not resolve simply because the drug moved into the duodenum. The
determinant is the **ionisation state**, which changes with the surrounding pH.

## title
Gastric fluid has a pH of 2. How do its hydrogen- and hydroxide-ion concentrations compare?

## question
Gastric fluid has a pH of 2. How do its hydrogen- and hydroxide-ion concentrations compare?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A sample of gastric fluid is measured at 25°C and found to have a pH of 2.

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Hydrogen ion 10⁻² mol/L and hydroxide ion 10⁻² mol/L — equal, since the two are always balanced

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Equal hydrogen- and hydroxide-ion activities define a **neutral** solution, which at 25°C
means pH 7 — not pH 2. The two ions are **linked** by a constant product, which is quite different
from being equal: their product is fixed at about 10⁻¹⁴, so when one rises the other must fall. This
option confuses a fixed relationship with a fixed equality.

## answer_b
Hydrogen ion 10⁻² mol/L and hydroxide ion 10⁻⁷ mol/L

## explanation_b
Incorrect on the hydroxide value. 10⁻⁷ mol/L is the hydroxide concentration of a **neutral**
solution, and it cannot coexist with a hydrogen-ion concentration of 10⁻² mol/L — their product
would be 10⁻⁹, not 10⁻¹⁴. This option correctly reads the hydrogen ion from the pH and then leaves
the hydroxide at its neutral value instead of recalculating it.

## answer_c
Hydrogen ion 10⁻¹² mol/L and hydroxide ion 10⁻² mol/L

## explanation_c
Incorrect: the two values are transposed. A pH of 2 means the **hydrogen**-ion concentration is
10⁻² mol/L — pH is defined on hydrogen ion, not hydroxide. As written, this option describes a
strongly **alkaline** solution of pH 12, which is the opposite of gastric fluid. It picks the student
who has the ion product right and has attached each concentration to the wrong ion.

## answer_d
Hydrogen ion 10⁻² mol/L and hydroxide ion 10⁻¹² mol/L, so hydrogen ion greatly exceeds hydroxide

## explanation_d
Correct. **An acidic solution has a pH below 7, with hydrogen-ion concentration greater than
hydroxyl-ion concentration** — and this sample shows that in an extreme form.

The two values come from two relationships:

**From the pH.** pH is the negative logarithm of hydrogen-ion activity, so a pH of 2 gives
[H⁺] = 10⁻² mol/L.

**From the ion product of water.** In aqueous solution [H⁺] × [OH⁻] ≈ 10⁻¹⁴ at 25°C. So
[OH⁻] = 10⁻¹⁴ ÷ 10⁻² = **10⁻¹² mol/L**.

Hydrogen ion therefore exceeds hydroxide by a factor of **10¹⁰** — ten billion-fold. That is the
point worth carrying: because the scale is logarithmic, a pH only five units below neutral
represents an enormous disparity between the two ions, not a modest one.

It also shows why the ions cannot be considered separately. **Increasing hydrogen-ion concentration
decreases hydroxyl-ion concentration**, because their product is held near 10⁻¹⁴ — so specifying
either one fixes the other, and no aqueous solution can have both high.

The pharmacological relevance is the environment this creates. Gastric fluid at pH 2 is roughly six
units below the pKa of a typical weak base, so such drugs are almost completely ionised and poorly
absorbed there, while weak acids are largely non-ionised and can be absorbed from the stomach.

## answer_e
Hydrogen ion 10² mol/L and hydroxide ion 10⁻¹⁶ mol/L

## explanation_e
Incorrect: the sign of the exponent on hydrogen ion is wrong. Because pH is a **negative**
logarithm, a pH of 2 corresponds to 10⁻² mol/L, not 10² — and a concentration of 100 mol/L is
physically impossible for hydrogen ion in water. This option drops the negative sign that defines
the pH scale, which then propagates through the ion product to give an equally impossible hydroxide
value.

QST-PHA-URINARY-ALKALINISATION-001

## title
Why does alkalinising the urine accelerate the excretion of a weak acid drug in overdose?

## question
Why does alkalinising the urine accelerate the excretion of a weak acid drug in overdose?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A patient has taken an overdose of a weak acid drug with a pKa of 3.5. Sodium bicarbonate is given
to raise the urinary pH above 7, and the rate of drug elimination increases.

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Raising urinary pH increases glomerular filtration of the drug

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Glomerular filtration is a passive, non-selective process determined by molecular size
and plasma-protein binding, not by urinary pH — and the filtrate's pH is altered further along the
nephron, after filtration has already occurred. Alkalinisation acts on what happens to the drug
**after** it is filtered, not on how much enters the tubule. This option places the effect at the
wrong step of renal handling.

## answer_b
In alkaline urine the weak acid is almost entirely ionised, so it cannot be passively reabsorbed and is trapped in the tubule

## explanation_b
Correct. **A basic solution has pH above 7, with hydroxyl-ion concentration exceeding hydrogen-ion
concentration** — and it is that shortage of protons that traps the drug.

The mechanism has two steps.

**Ionisation.** A weak acid donates its proton more readily when protons are scarce. At a urinary pH
above 7, this drug sits more than three units above its pKa of 3.5, so it is **more than 99.9%
ionised**.

**Failure of reabsorption.** Passive tubular reabsorption occurs by **simple diffusion** back across
the tubular epithelium, and only the **non-ionised**, lipid-soluble form can cross. With almost none
of the drug in that form, reabsorption is effectively abolished. The drug remains in the tubular
fluid and is carried out in the urine — it is **ion trapped**.

Because reabsorption normally returns a substantial fraction of a filtered lipid-soluble drug to the
blood, removing that return markedly increases net excretion and shortens the drug's half-life.

The principle generalises symmetrically, and the direction is worth stating explicitly because it is
easy to reverse: **alkalinise the urine to trap and excrete a weak acid; acidify it to trap and
excrete a weak base.** In each case the aim is to push the drug into its **ionised** form in the
tubular fluid.

Two limits belong with it. The manoeuvre only helps drugs for which **renal excretion is a
significant elimination route** — it does nothing for a drug cleared mainly by hepatic metabolism.
And it depends on **passive reabsorption** being the step that is blocked, which is why it is
unrelated to the carrier-mediated active secretion that produces competitive interactions.

## answer_c
Bicarbonate binds the drug in the tubular fluid and carries it out

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Bicarbonate acts by **raising the pH** of the tubular fluid, not by forming a complex with
the drug. If it bound the drug directly, the effect would depend on the two molecules' affinity
rather than on the relationship between urinary pH and the drug's pKa — and the same result would
not be achievable with any other alkalinising agent, which it is. This option supplies a chemical
mechanism in place of the physicochemical one.

## answer_d
Alkaline urine increases active tubular secretion of the drug

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Active tubular secretion is **carrier-mediated**, and its rate depends on carrier
availability and competition from other substrates rather than on tubular pH. Ion trapping works on
the **passive reabsorption** limb instead — the drug is not secreted faster, it is simply prevented
from coming back. This option is worth separating because it confuses the two renal mechanisms that
drugs interact with, only one of which is pH-sensitive.

## answer_e
Ionised drug is more water-soluble, so it diffuses back into the blood more rapidly

## explanation_e
Incorrect, and the conclusion contradicts its own premise. Greater water solubility means **less**
lipid solubility, and diffusion back across the tubular epithelium requires the drug to cross lipid
membranes — so an ionised, water-soluble drug diffuses back **less** readily, not more. The first
clause is true and the inference is reversed. This is the trap for a student who associates "more
soluble" with "moves more easily" without asking soluble in what.

QST-PHA-IS-BLOOD-NEUTRAL-001

## title
Arterial blood has a pH of 7.4. Is it neutral, acidic or alkaline?

## question
Arterial blood has a pH of 7.4. Is it neutral, acidic or alkaline?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A student is told that arterial pH is normally 7.35–7.45 and concludes that blood is essentially
neutral, since 7.4 is close to 7.

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Slightly alkaline, because hydrogen-ion activity is below hydroxide-ion activity at pH 7.4

## explanation_a
Correct. **A neutral solution has pH 7 with equal hydrogen- and hydroxyl-ion concentrations** — that
equality is the definition, and any departure from it makes a solution acidic or alkaline regardless
of how close the pH number looks to 7.

At **pH 7.4**, hydrogen-ion activity is about 4 × 10⁻⁸ mol/L, which is **below** the 10⁻⁷ mol/L of a
neutral solution at 25°C. Hydroxide-ion activity is correspondingly **above** it. Since hydroxide
exceeds hydrogen ion, blood is **alkaline** — slightly, but genuinely.

Two points make this more than a technicality.

**The scale is logarithmic, so 0.4 of a unit is not a small difference.** Hydrogen-ion activity at
pH 7.4 is roughly **40% lower** than at pH 7.0. This is why the narrow clinical range 7.35–7.45
matters so much: a fall to 7.1, which sounds minor, is close to a doubling of hydrogen-ion
concentration.

**"Normal" and "neutral" are different claims.** Blood is normally alkaline relative to water, and
homeostasis defends that alkalinity. A patient at pH 7.0 is in severe acidosis even though the
solution would be chemically neutral — which is precisely why the terms **acidaemia** and
**alkalaemia** are defined against the normal range of 7.35–7.45, not against 7.

One further refinement: because the ionisation of water is temperature-dependent, the **neutral pH
at 37°C is nearer 6.8 than 7.0**, so blood at 7.4 is somewhat more alkaline relative to true
neutrality at body temperature than the 25°C comparison suggests.

## answer_b
Neutral, because 7.4 lies within the physiological normal range

## explanation_b
Incorrect, and it is the conflation the vignette invites. The normal range describes what is
**healthy**, whereas neutrality describes an **equality of ion activities** — two unrelated
standards. If falling inside a normal range made a solution neutral, then gastric fluid at its
normal pH of 2 would also have to be called neutral. Normality is a biological judgement;
neutrality is a chemical one.

## answer_c
Neutral, because the buffers in blood hold hydrogen and hydroxide ions equal

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Buffers do resist **changes** in pH, and the bicarbonate system is what keeps arterial pH
within its narrow range — but they hold the pH near **7.4**, not at the point of ion equality. A
buffer stabilises a solution wherever its own equilibrium sits, which here is on the alkaline side.
This option correctly identifies the mechanism of pH stability and wrongly assumes stability means
neutrality.

## answer_d
Slightly acidic, because pH 7.4 is below the neutral point of 7.4 for plasma

## explanation_d
Incorrect, and it is circular: it asserts that the neutral point *is* 7.4 and then calls 7.4 acidic
relative to it, which cannot both hold. Neutrality is not defined separately for each body fluid —
it is the pH at which hydrogen- and hydroxide-ion activities are equal in aqueous solution, about 7
at 25°C. This option invents a fluid-specific neutral point to accommodate the intuition that blood
must be neutral.

## answer_e
Cannot be determined without knowing the bicarbonate concentration

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Bicarbonate is essential for working out the **cause** of an acid–base disturbance and for
distinguishing metabolic from respiratory origins — but the question asked is simply whether the
solution is acidic, neutral or alkaline, and pH alone answers it. The pH already reports
hydrogen-ion activity, which is what the classification depends on. This option confuses the
diagnostic workup with the chemical description.

# Item

## id
QST-PHA-PH-POH-SUM-001

## title
A buffer solution has a pH of 5.5. What is its pOH, and which ion predominates?

## question
A buffer solution has a pH of 5.5. What is its pOH, and which ion predominates?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A pharmaceutical buffer solution is prepared and measured at 25°C, giving a pH of 5.5.

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
pOH 5.5, with the two ions equal

## explanation_a
Incorrect. pH and pOH are equal only in a **neutral** solution, where both are 7 at 25°C and the two
ion activities are equal. Here the pH is 5.5, so the two cannot be equal — pOH must be the
complement that brings the sum to 14. This option assumes pH and pOH always match, which would make
pOH a redundant quantity carrying no additional information.

## answer_b
pOH 8.5, with hydroxide ion predominating

## explanation_b
Incorrect in the second clause, which makes it the near miss. The arithmetic is right — 14 − 5.5
gives pOH 8.5 — but the interpretation is inverted. Because **pOH is also a negative logarithm**, a
**higher** pOH means **less** hydroxide. With pH 5.5 below 7 and pOH 8.5 above it, hydrogen ion is
the more abundant of the two. This option catches the student who completes the subtraction and then
reads the larger number as the larger concentration.

## answer_c
pOH 14, since pOH is measured independently of pH

## explanation_c
Incorrect. pOH is not independent of pH: in any dilute aqueous solution the two are tied by the ion
product of water, so **pH + pOH ≈ 14**. Specifying one determines the other, which is why either can
be measured and the other calculated. The value 14 is the **sum** of the two, not a value pOH itself
takes in this solution. This option mistakes the constant for one of its terms.

## answer_d
pOH 8.5, and the solution is alkaline because pOH exceeds 7

## explanation_d
Incorrect in its conclusion, and it applies the pH criterion to the wrong scale. The pOH value is
right, but a solution is alkaline when its **pH** is above 7 — or equivalently when its **pOH is
below 7**, since the two scales run in opposite directions. Here pOH 8.5 is above 7, which means the
solution is **acidic**. This option carries the "above 7 means alkaline" rule across from the pH
scale without inverting it.

## answer_e
pOH 8.5, with hydrogen ion predominating, so the solution is acidic

## explanation_e
Correct. **For aqueous solutions, pH plus pOH equals 14** — so

pOH = 14 − 5.5 = **8.5**

The reason the sum is constant is the **ion product of water**: [H⁺] × [OH⁻] ≈ 10⁻¹⁴ at 25°C. Taking
the negative logarithm of both sides converts that multiplication into an addition, which is exactly
the pH + pOH ≈ 14 relationship. So the constant sum is not an arbitrary rule but a restatement of
the ion product.

Interpreting it requires holding **both scales inverted**. Each is a negative logarithm, so a **low**
pH means abundant hydrogen ion and a **low** pOH means abundant hydroxide. Here:

- pH 5.5 is **below** 7, so [H⁺] is above its neutral value — 10⁻⁵·⁵ mol/L.
- pOH 8.5 is **above** 7, so [OH⁻] is below its neutral value — 10⁻⁸·⁵ mol/L.

**Hydrogen ion predominates**, by three orders of magnitude, and the solution is therefore
**acidic** — consistent with pH 5.5 lying below 7.

The safe method, and the one that avoids every distractor here, is to **convert to pH and judge
against 7**. Reasoning directly from pOH requires remembering that its scale runs opposite to pH,
which itself runs opposite to hydrogen-ion concentration — two inversions, and one of them is
usually dropped.

Buffer pH matters pharmaceutically because it fixes the **ionisation** of a dissolved drug, and
therefore both its solubility in the preparation and its ability to cross membranes on
administration.

## title
In acidosis, hydrogen-ion concentration doubles. What happens to hydroxide-ion concentration?

## question
In acidosis, hydrogen-ion concentration doubles. What happens to hydroxide-ion concentration?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A patient develops a metabolic acidosis in which the arterial hydrogen-ion concentration doubles.

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
It also doubles, since both ions rise together when a solution becomes more concentrated

## explanation_a
Incorrect, and it treats the two ions as varying together. In water they are **inversely** related:
their product is held near 10⁻¹⁴, so if one doubles the other must halve. Both ions do rise together
in one circumstance — when water's **ionisation increases with temperature** — but that is not what
happens when an acid load is added at constant temperature. This option confuses adding solute with
changing the ion product.

## answer_b
It is unchanged, since hydroxide is regulated separately by the kidney

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The kidney does regulate acid–base balance, principally by handling bicarbonate and
excreting hydrogen ion — but it does not hold hydroxide-ion concentration constant, and no
physiological mechanism could. The relationship between the two ions is a **chemical constraint** on
any aqueous solution, not a variable under homeostatic control. This option promotes a regulated
parameter (bicarbonate) into a fixed one (hydroxide).

## answer_c
It halves, because the product of the two ion concentrations is held near 10⁻¹⁴

## explanation_c
Correct. **In water solutions, increasing hydrogen-ion concentration decreases hydroxyl-ion
concentration while their product remains 10⁻¹⁴.** So if [H⁺] doubles, [OH⁻] must **halve** to keep
the product constant:

(2 × [H⁺]) × ([OH⁻] ÷ 2) = [H⁺] × [OH⁻] ≈ 10⁻¹⁴

The reason is that water **self-ionises** to a fixed extent at a given temperature. That equilibrium
fixes the product of the two ion activities, so they cannot vary independently — adding hydrogen ion
drives the equilibrium towards recombination, consuming hydroxide.

Two consequences are worth carrying.

**Either value determines the other**, which is why pH alone is sufficient to describe a solution's
acid–base status and why **pH + pOH ≈ 14**. Measuring hydroxide separately would add nothing.

**"More acidic" always means "less alkaline"** in the same solution — the two are not independent
axes. A solution cannot be simultaneously high in both ions.

In clinical terms, the doubling described corresponds to a fall of about **0.3 pH units**, since a
twofold change in hydrogen-ion concentration is log₁₀2 ≈ 0.3 on the pH scale. A patient moving from
pH 7.4 to about 7.1 has doubled their hydrogen-ion concentration — which is why an apparently small
pH change represents a serious disturbance, and why the normal range is defended so narrowly.

The one qualification is temperature: the ion product is approximately 10⁻¹⁴ **at 25°C** and rises
somewhat at body temperature, so the neutral point shifts. The reciprocal relationship itself holds
regardless.

## answer_d
It falls to zero, since the added acid neutralises all the hydroxide present

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Hydroxide concentration **falls but never reaches zero**, because the product of the two
must remain finite — a zero hydroxide concentration would require an infinite hydrogen-ion
concentration. Water continues to self-ionise no matter how acidic the solution becomes, so both
ions are always present. This option takes a reciprocal relationship and reads it as complete
consumption.

## answer_e
It halves, but only if the acidosis is respiratory rather than metabolic

## explanation_e
Incorrect in its qualification. The reciprocal relationship is a property of **aqueous chemistry**
and applies whatever raised the hydrogen-ion concentration — a retained carbon dioxide load, an
accumulated organic acid, or acid added in a beaker. The metabolic/respiratory distinction concerns
the **cause** of the disturbance and how bicarbonate and carbon dioxide behave, not whether the ion
product holds. This option attaches a clinical classification to a chemical constant.

## title
What role do ungated leak channels play in a resting cell?

## question
What role do ungated leak channels play in a resting cell?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A membrane channel is studied in a cell at rest. No ligand is applied and the membrane potential is
not changed, yet ions continue to pass through the channel, with individual channels opening and
closing unpredictably over time.

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
They open only when the cell is depolarised, terminating the action potential

## explanation_a
Incorrect, and it describes a **voltage-gated** channel. Channels that open on depolarisation —
including the potassium channels that repolarise an action potential — require a change in membrane
potential as their stimulus. The stem specifies that the membrane potential is **not** changed and
that ions pass anyway, which is the defining behaviour of an ungated channel. This option supplies
a gating stimulus the observation excludes.

## answer_b
They provide a pathway that is open at rest, setting the membrane's resting permeability

## explanation_b
Correct. **Ungated or leak channels provide a membrane pathway that is open at rest, although the
open state of individual channels may still fluctuate** — which is exactly the behaviour described.

Two features define them.

**No gating stimulus is required.** Unlike ligand-gated channels, which need a ligand bound to a
receptor, or voltage-gated channels, which need a change in membrane potential, leak channels are
**constitutively open**. Nothing has to happen for ions to pass.

**Individual channels still fluctuate.** "Open at rest" is a statement about the **population**, not
a guarantee that any one channel is permanently open. Single channels flicker between conformations;
what matters is that a substantial fraction is open at any moment, giving a steady net conductance.

Their function is to set the **resting permeability** of the membrane, and hence the **resting
membrane potential**. Potassium leak channels are the principal example: because the membrane is far
more permeable to potassium than to sodium at rest, potassium leaves the cell down its concentration
gradient until the developing electrical gradient opposes further loss. That balance is what holds
the resting potential near the potassium equilibrium potential — around −90 mV in a cardiac myocyte.

The contrast with gated channels is the useful organising idea: **leak channels establish the
baseline; gated channels are the switches that change it in response to a signal.** An action
potential is a transient departure from a resting state that leak channels maintain.

Two constraints apply to every channel, gated or not. Ions move **down** their electrochemical
gradient only — a channel is a pore, not a pump, so it cannot concentrate solute against a gradient.
And conduction through an open channel is **fast**, because no binding or conformational cycle is
needed for each ion, which is why channel-mediated flux is not saturable in the way
carrier-mediated transport is.

## answer_c
They actively extrude sodium to maintain the resting potential

## explanation_c
Incorrect, and it confuses a channel with a pump. Active extrusion of sodium against its gradient is
performed by the **sodium–potassium ATPase**, a primary active transporter that hydrolyses ATP. A
leak channel supplies no energy and can only allow passive flow down a gradient. Both contribute to
the resting potential, but by entirely different mechanisms — the pump builds the gradients, the
leak channels let the membrane respond to them.

## answer_d
They open in response to membrane stretch, allowing the cell to sense mechanical deformation

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Mechanically gated channels do exist — in baroreceptors and cochlear hair cells, for
example — and they are a genuine category. But they are **gated**: they require deformation as their
stimulus. The stem describes conduction with no stimulus applied at all, which excludes any gated
channel regardless of what the stimulus would be.

## answer_e
They allow drugs to cross the membrane by simple diffusion

## explanation_e
Incorrect on the route. Simple diffusion occurs **through the lipid bilayer itself**, and its rate
depends on the drug's lipid solubility — it does not use channels. Channels are aqueous pores that
carry small ions and a few very small water-soluble molecules, not a general route for drug entry.
This option conflates the two passive mechanisms, which is worth separating because they depend on
opposite properties: lipid solubility for diffusion, water solubility and small size for channels.

QST-PHA-FACILITATED-SPECIFICITY-001

## title
Two structurally similar drugs share one carrier. What is observed when they are given together, and why?

## question
Two structurally similar drugs share one carrier. What is observed when they are given together, and why?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
Drug A crosses a membrane by facilitated diffusion. When structurally similar drug B is added, the
transfer of drug A falls markedly. A structurally unrelated drug, given at the same concentration,
has no effect on drug A at all.

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Drug B has altered the concentration gradient of drug A

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Drug B is a different molecule and does not contribute to drug A's own concentration
gradient, which is what drives its facilitated diffusion. If gradient alteration were the mechanism,
the **structurally unrelated** drug would interfere just as much at the same concentration — and the
stem states it does not. That selective effect is the observation this option cannot explain.

## answer_b
Drug B has saturated the membrane's lipid, reducing space for drug A to dissolve

## explanation_b
Incorrect, and it describes a mechanism that would apply to **simple diffusion** through the
bilayer — where, in any case, the lipid does not become "full" in this way. It also fails the same
test as option A: dissolving in membrane lipid depends on lipid solubility, not on structural
resemblance to drug A, so an unrelated drug would compete equally. The selectivity points to a
binding site, not to bulk lipid.

## answer_c
Drug B has induced additional carriers, which now prefer drug B

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Carrier induction — an increase in the number of transporter proteins — is a slow process
requiring gene expression and protein synthesis, typically over days, not something observed on
adding a second drug acutely. And inducing **more** carriers would tend to increase total transport
capacity rather than reduce drug A's transfer. This option supplies a real pharmacological
phenomenon on the wrong timescale and with the wrong direction of effect.

## answer_d
Drug B has been actively transported, consuming the ATP that drug A required

## explanation_d
Incorrect on two counts. **Facilitated diffusion requires a carrier but no energy** — it uses no ATP,
so there is none for drug B to consume. And competition for a shared energy supply would again be
non-selective, affecting any ATP-dependent process rather than sparing a structurally unrelated
drug. This option imports an energy requirement into a passive mechanism.

## answer_e
Drug B competes for the same carrier binding site, because facilitated diffusion is substrate specific

## explanation_e
Correct. **Facilitated diffusion is substrate specific**, and that specificity is what makes the
observation selective.

The mechanism explains it directly. A carrier transports by binding its substrate at a specific
site, then undergoing a **conformational change** that exposes the bound molecule to the opposite
side of the membrane. That binding site has a defined shape, so it recognises molecules of
particular structure — which is why the carrier is **specific**.

A **structurally similar** molecule fits the same site and can occupy it. Since the carriers are
present in **finite number**, every carrier occupied by drug B is one unavailable to drug A, and drug
A's transfer falls. This is **competitive inhibition**. A **structurally unrelated** molecule does
not fit the site, cannot occupy it, and therefore has no effect — exactly as the stem describes. The
selectivity of the interference is the evidence that a specific binding site is involved.

Three properties travel together and all follow from the same binding step: **specificity**,
**saturability** (a transport maximum once all carriers are occupied), and **competitive
inhibition**. They are shared by facilitated diffusion and by active transport, since both are
carrier-mediated, and they are **absent** from simple diffusion, which has no protein and therefore
no site to compete for and no ceiling.

This is the basis of an important class of **drug interactions**, and it operates wherever a shared
carrier does — at intestinal absorption, at the blood–brain barrier, and at renal tubular transport,
where active secretion is saturable and a site of competition. The practical consequence is that one
drug can raise another's plasma concentration simply by occupying the transporter that would have
removed it.
