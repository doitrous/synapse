# Item

## id

## title
tRNA carries the highest percentage of modified nucleotides among RNA types

## question
The RNA with high percent of modified nucleotides:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
tRNA

## explanation_a
Correct. Transfer RNA carries by far the highest proportion of chemically modified nucleotides of any RNA type: bases such as dihydrouridine, pseudouridine, inosine and ribothymidine are added post-transcriptionally and concentrated in its D-arm, anticodon arm and TψC arm. These modifications stabilise tRNA's compact cloverleaf fold and fine-tune codon-anticodon recognition, which is why tRNA is singled out for this superlative among rRNA, mRNA and hnRNA.

## answer_b
rRNA

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Ribosomal RNA does carry some modified nucleotides (methylated riboses and pseudouridines added by snoRNA-guided enzymes), but its modification density is well below tRNA's — rRNA's defining features are its abundance (about 80% of cellular RNA) and its structural/catalytic role in the ribosome, not a high percentage of modified bases.

## answer_c
mRNA

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Messenger RNA is the least modified of the major RNA types at the nucleotide level (beyond its 5' cap and, in some transcripts, a small number of regulatory base modifications) — its defining property is sequence heterogeneity between different genes' transcripts, not a high modified-nucleotide percentage.

## answer_d
hnRNA

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Heterogeneous nuclear RNA is the unprocessed nuclear precursor to mature mRNA and shares mRNA's defining property of sequence heterogeneity, not tRNA's high modified-nucleotide content; hnRNA is trimmed down to mature mRNA by capping, polyadenylation and splicing, none of which chemically modifies internal bases the way tRNA-processing enzymes do.

# Item

## id

## title
mRNA is the most sequence-heterogeneous RNA in the cell

## question
The RNA in the cell with greatest sequence diversity "most Heterogenous type" is:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Messenger RNA

## explanation_a
Correct. mRNA is the most sequence-heterogeneous RNA species because every different protein-coding gene produces its own uniquely sequenced and uniquely sized transcript — thousands of distinct sequences coexist in one cell. This diversity is exactly why mRNA's unprocessed nuclear precursor was historically named "heterogeneous nuclear RNA" (hnRNA): the name records this same defining property.

## answer_b
Ribosomal RNA

## explanation_b
Incorrect. rRNA is the opposite of heterogeneous — a given species has only a handful of distinct rRNA molecules (28S, 18S, 5.8S, 5S in humans), each highly conserved in sequence across virtually all of a cell's ribosomes, making rRNA one of the least sequence-diverse RNA classes despite being the most abundant by mass.

## answer_c
Transfer RNA

## explanation_c
Incorrect. tRNA sequences are also comparatively uniform: there are only on the order of dozens of distinct tRNA species (roughly one set per amino acid, allowing for isoacceptors), all sharing the same conserved cloverleaf scaffold — far less sequence diversity than the thousands of distinct mRNA transcripts a cell expresses.

## answer_d
(a) & (c)

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This combines the correct answer (mRNA) with tRNA, which is not heterogeneous — tRNA sequences are conserved and limited in number. Since only mRNA fits the "greatest sequence diversity" description, a combined answer that also includes tRNA is wrong.

# Item

## id

## title
The one false statement about tRNA: it does show hydrogen bonding and base pairing

## question
Which of the following statements regarding tRNA is not correct:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Transfer RNA constitutes about 15% of cellular RNA.

## explanation_a
Incorrect as an answer to "not correct" — this statement is actually true. tRNA makes up roughly 10-15% of total cellular RNA, well behind rRNA's dominant ~80% share but ahead of mRNA's small single-digit percentage, so it does not qualify as the false statement the question asks for.

## answer_b
All tRNA molecules are approximately the same shape & size, about 75 nucleotides.

## explanation_b
Incorrect as an answer to "not correct" — this statement is true. Despite carrying dozens of different anticodon sequences for different amino acids, every tRNA molecule folds into the same general cloverleaf secondary structure and L-shaped tertiary structure, and all are close to 75-90 nucleotides long, so this uniform size and shape claim is accurate rather than false.

## answer_c
There are at least 20 different species of tRNA, one for each amino acid.

## explanation_c
Incorrect as an answer to "not correct" — this statement is true. A cell needs at least one tRNA species per standard amino acid (about 20), each charged by its own dedicated aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase, so this is an accurate description rather than the false statement being asked for.

## answer_d
Transfer RNA molecule shows no hydrogen bonds or base pairing.

## explanation_d
Correct — this is the false statement. tRNA is full of intramolecular hydrogen bonding and base pairing: its single strand folds back on itself to form the stems of the cloverleaf's acceptor, D, anticodon and TψC arms, all held together by Watson-Crick base pairing, and its anticodon also hydrogen-bonds to the mRNA codon during translation. Claiming tRNA shows "no" hydrogen bonds or base pairing directly contradicts its defining cloverleaf structure.

# Item

## title
A human cell needs at least about 20 tRNA species, one per amino acid

## question
The least number of tRNA in human cells is

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
10

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Ten tRNA species could not cover all 20 standard amino acids one-for-one; this undercounts the minimum a cell needs to translate every codon into its correct amino acid.

## answer_b
20

## explanation_b
Correct. There are 20 standard amino acids used in protein synthesis, and a cell needs a minimum of one dedicated tRNA species per amino acid — each charged by its own specific aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase — so 20 is the theoretical floor. In practice most cells have somewhat more than 20 species because several amino acids are served by more than one isoacceptor tRNA, but 20 is the minimum the question is asking for.

## answer_c
50

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Fifty overstates the strict minimum; while the actual number of tRNA species/isoacceptors in a real cell is often higher than 20, "50" is not the minimum figure taught for this fact and does not correspond to any standard count referenced in this topic.

## answer_d
30

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Thirty is not the number tied to any standard teaching point here; the minimum is anchored to the 20 standard amino acids, not to 30.

## answer_e
61

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Sixty-one is the number of sense codons in the genetic code (64 total codons minus 3 stop codons), not the minimum number of tRNA species — wobble pairing lets a single tRNA species read more than one codon, which is precisely why the cell does not need 61 separate tRNA species despite there being 61 sense codons.

# Item

## id

## title
RNA is alkali-labile, not alkali-resistant, because of its 2'-OH group

## question
All the following are true about RNA Except

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Present mainly in Cytosol

## explanation_a
Incorrect as an answer to "Except" — this statement is true. Most cellular RNA (chiefly rRNA and mature mRNA) is found in the cytosol, where translation occurs, in contrast to DNA which stays confined to the nucleus (and mitochondria), so this is an accurate statement, not the exception being asked for.

## answer_b
Shorter than DNA

## explanation_b
Incorrect as an answer to "Except" — this statement is true. Individual RNA molecules (even the longest mRNAs) are dramatically shorter than genomic DNA molecules, which can span from millions to hundreds of millions of base pairs, so this comparison is accurate rather than the false statement being asked for.

## answer_c
Resist alkali

## explanation_c
Correct — this is the false statement. RNA is alkali-labile, not alkali-resistant: its ribose sugar carries a 2'-hydroxyl (2'-OH) group that DNA's deoxyribose lacks, and under alkaline conditions this 2'-OH attacks the adjacent phosphodiester bond intramolecularly, cleaving the RNA backbone. DNA, lacking the 2'-OH group, is the one that actually resists alkaline hydrolysis, which is exactly why alkaline conditions are used in molecular biology to selectively degrade RNA while leaving DNA intact.

## answer_d
Contain uracil

## explanation_d
Incorrect as an answer to "Except" — this statement is true. RNA uses uracil in place of DNA's thymine as the pyrimidine base that pairs with adenine, so this is an accurate statement about RNA, not the exception.

## answer_e
May form intra-strand Hydrogen bonds

## explanation_e
Incorrect as an answer to "Except" — this statement is true. Although RNA is single-stranded, it routinely folds back on itself to form local intra-strand hydrogen-bonded secondary structure — exactly as seen in tRNA's cloverleaf stems and rRNA's stem-loops — so this is accurate rather than the false statement being asked for.

# Item

## id

## title
mRNA is the one coding RNA among rRNA, tRNA, snRNA and lncRNA

## question
All the following are non-coding RNA Except

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
rRNA

## explanation_a
Incorrect as an answer to "Except" — rRNA is indeed non-coding. It is never translated itself; instead it acts directly as a structural and catalytic component of the ribosome (including the peptidyl transferase activity), so it correctly belongs on the non-coding side and is not the exception.

## answer_b
tRNA

## explanation_b
Incorrect as an answer to "Except" — tRNA is indeed non-coding. It is never translated itself; instead it acts directly as the adaptor molecule that carries an amino acid and reads the mRNA codon, so it correctly belongs on the non-coding side and is not the exception.

## answer_c
snRNA

## explanation_c
Incorrect as an answer to "Except" — small nuclear RNA is indeed non-coding. It is never translated; instead it acts directly as part of the spliceosome, guiding intron removal during pre-mRNA splicing, so it correctly belongs on the non-coding side and is not the exception.

## answer_d
lncRNA

## explanation_d
Incorrect as an answer to "Except" — long non-coding RNA is, by definition and by name, non-coding. It acts directly as an RNA molecule (commonly in gene-expression regulation, such as X-inactivation or chromatin remodelling) rather than being translated into protein, so it correctly belongs on the non-coding side and is not the exception.

## answer_e
mRNA

## explanation_e
Correct — this is the exception. Messenger RNA is the one RNA type in this list that is coding: it is the direct template read by ribosomes and translated into a protein's amino acid sequence. Every other RNA listed here (rRNA, tRNA, snRNA, lncRNA) acts directly as an RNA molecule — structurally, catalytically or regulatorily — without ever being translated, which is exactly what "non-coding" means.
