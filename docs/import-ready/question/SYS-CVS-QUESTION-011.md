## title
At a resting heart rate of 75 beats/min, how long does one complete cardiac cycle last?

## question
At a resting heart rate of 75 beats/min, how long does one complete cardiac cycle last?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A first-year student is asked to work out cardiac timings from a resting pulse rather than
recall them. The pulse is regular at 75 beats per minute. On the board the demonstrator has
written three figures from the same textbook page — 0.1 s, 0.3 s and 0.7 s — without saying
what any of them refers to.

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
0.3 s

## explanation_a
Incorrect. 0.3 s is the duration of *ventricular systole* at this rate, not of the whole
cycle. This is the commonest error here: taking the phase a student has revised hardest and
reporting it as the cycle. Systole is one part of the cycle; the cycle also contains the
0.5 s of ventricular diastole that follows it.

## answer_b
0.5 s

## explanation_b
Incorrect. 0.5 s is the duration of ventricular *diastole* at 75 beats/min — the remainder
of the cycle once the 0.3 s of systole is subtracted. It is also the figure a student gets
by dividing 60 by 120, so it catches anyone who has substituted a remembered "normal" rate
of 120 instead of using the 75 given in the stem.

## answer_c
0.7 s

## explanation_c
Incorrect, and this option exists to catch a specific misreading. 0.7 s is the duration of
atrial *diastole*. Textbooks present the atrial figures — atrial systole 0.1 s, atrial
diastole 0.7 s — immediately beside the cycle length, and 0.7 is close enough to 0.8 to look
right. Note that the atrial pair sums to 0.8 s, which is the actual answer: the atrial
figures are a breakdown *of* the cycle, not alternatives to it.

## answer_d
0.8 s

## explanation_d
Correct. One cardiac cycle is simply the reciprocal of heart rate: 60 s ÷ 75 beats = 0.8 s
per beat. That is worth deriving rather than memorising, because the same division works at
any rate — at 60 beats/min the cycle is 1.0 s, at 100 it is 0.6 s.

The 0.8 s then divides into **ventricular systole, about 0.3 s**, and **ventricular
diastole, about 0.5 s**. On the atrial side the same 0.8 s divides into atrial systole of
about 0.1 s and atrial diastole of about 0.7 s. Every figure in the stem belongs to this one
breakdown, which is exactly why they are confusable — each is a *part* of the cycle, and
only the reciprocal of the rate gives the whole.

The clinically important consequence is that diastole is the longer half at rest and it is
the part that shortens first when rate rises. Coronary filling of the left ventricle happens
almost entirely during diastole, so tachycardia compromises myocardial perfusion at the same
time as it raises myocardial demand — the mechanism behind rate-related angina.

## answer_e
1.0 s

## explanation_e
Incorrect. 1.0 s is the cycle length at 60 beats/min. It picks the student who rounded the
rate to a convenient 60 to make the arithmetic easy, or who recalled the "one second per
beat" figure that textbooks often use for a worked illustration. The stem gives 75, and the
whole point of the calculation is that it must follow the rate actually measured.

QST-CVS-ATRIAL-SYSTOLE-TIMING-001

## title
Where in the ventricular cycle does atrial systole fall?

## question
Where in the ventricular cycle does atrial systole fall?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A student is shown a simultaneous ECG and ventricular pressure trace and asked to mark the
moment the atria contract. They correctly identify the P wave, then place atrial contraction
after the QRS complex, reasoning that the atria must empty into a ventricle that has already
begun to relax.

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
During ventricular systole, so that both chambers contract together

## explanation_a
Incorrect, and this is the most consequential misconception about the cycle. If the atria
contracted while the ventricles were contracting, they would be pumping against closed
atrioventricular valves and would drive blood backwards into the great veins. The atria and
ventricles are deliberately *not* synchronous: the fibrous skeleton insulates them
electrically, and the atrioventricular node imposes a delay precisely so that atrial
contraction finishes before ventricular contraction starts.

## answer_b
During early ventricular diastole, driving the rapid filling phase

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This is the error made in the stem, and it inverts cause and effect. The rapid
filling phase happens *passively*, in early diastole, driven by the pressure gradient that
exists the moment the mitral valve opens — the atria are not contracting then. Atrial
systole comes later, after that passive rush has already delivered most of the filling
volume. A student holding this view will also overestimate how much of filling the atria
supply.

## answer_c
In the final part of ventricular diastole, about the last 0.1 s

## explanation_c
Correct. Atrial systole is a **late-diastolic event**. It occupies roughly the final 0.1 s
of ventricular diastole, immediately before the ventricles begin to contract, and it is the
last thing that happens before the mitral and tricuspid valves shut.

The order matters and follows directly from the conduction sequence. The sinoatrial node
fires, the atria depolarise — the **P wave** — and the atria contract. The impulse is then
held up at the atrioventricular node for about 0.1 s. Only after that delay do the
ventricles depolarise (**QRS**) and contract. So on a trace, atrial contraction sits
*between* the P wave and the QRS, not after the QRS.

That delay is the functional point of the atrioventricular node. It gives atrial contraction
time to complete its top-up of ventricular filling before the ventricle starts to build
pressure. Two clinical consequences follow. First, in atrial fibrillation there is no
organised atrial contraction and no P wave, so this late-diastolic top-up is lost — which is
why patients dependent on it, such as those with a stiff, poorly compliant ventricle,
deteriorate when they fibrillate. Second, at very high heart rates it is diastole that
shortens, so the interval available for filling — including this final atrial contribution —
is squeezed.

## answer_d
Simultaneously with the rapid ejection phase

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Rapid ejection is well into ventricular systole, with the semilunar valves open
and the atrioventricular valves firmly shut. Atrial contraction at that moment could not
contribute to ventricular filling at all. This option picks the student who has learned that
the atria act as reservoirs during ventricular systole — which is true, they *fill*
passively then — and has confused passive reservoir filling with active atrial contraction.

## answer_e
During isovolumetric contraction, while all four valves are closed

## explanation_e
Incorrect, and it is a near miss rather than a wild guess. Isovolumetric contraction
directly *follows* atrial systole, so the two are adjacent — but by the time it begins the
atrioventricular valves have already closed, which is what makes the phase isovolumetric.
Atrial contraction against a closed mitral valve would add no volume. This option catches
the student who has the sequence right but is off by one phase.

## title
During isovolumetric relaxation, which valves are open and what happens to ventricular volume?

## question
During isovolumetric relaxation, which valves are open and what happens to ventricular volume?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
On a pressure–volume loop, a student is asked to identify the segment in which ventricular
pressure falls steeply while the loop moves straight down as a vertical line, with no
horizontal movement at all.

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The mitral valve is open; volume rises as filling begins

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This is the phase that comes *next*. The mitral valve opens only once ventricular
pressure has fallen below atrial pressure, and the moment it does, filling begins and volume
rises — which ends the isovolumetric period by definition. This option picks the student who
knows relaxation leads to filling and merges the two events, missing the interval in between
during which pressure has fallen but no valve has yet opened.

## answer_b
No valves are open; volume stays constant at its lowest value of the cycle

## explanation_b
Correct. "Isovolumetric" means exactly what it says: **volume does not change**, and it
cannot change because **all four valves are shut**.

The sequence is worth following carefully. At the end of ejection the ventricle begins to
relax and its pressure falls. As soon as ventricular pressure drops below aortic pressure,
the aortic valve snaps shut — this is the **second heart sound**, and on the aortic trace the
small backflow just before closure produces the **dicrotic notch**. But the mitral valve is
still closed, because ventricular pressure, although falling, is at that instant still
*higher* than left atrial pressure. So for a brief interval the ventricle is a sealed
chamber: pressure plummets, and volume is fixed.

That fixed volume is the **end-systolic volume**, roughly 60 mL, and it is the smallest the
ventricle holds at any point in the cycle. On the pressure–volume loop this phase is
therefore a **vertical line** on the left-hand side — pressure changing, volume not — which
is what the stem describes.

The interval ends the moment ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure and the mitral
valve opens, beginning rapid filling. The general principle to carry forward is that a
chamber can only change volume if a valve is open; whenever every valve is shut, the
ventricle can change its pressure but never its contents. The same logic defines
isovolumetric *contraction* at the other end of systole.

## answer_c
The aortic valve is still open; volume continues to fall

## explanation_c
Incorrect. If the aortic valve were open, blood would still be leaving the ventricle and
volume would be falling — which would make the phase anything but isovolumetric. Aortic
valve closure is the event that *starts* isovolumetric relaxation. This option catches the
student who thinks of relaxation as a gradual tailing-off of ejection rather than as a
distinct sealed interval that begins after the valve has shut.

## answer_d
All four valves are open; volume is constant

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The second half is right but the first half is impossible: there is no phase of
the cardiac cycle in which all four valves are open simultaneously. An atrioventricular valve
and the semilunar valve on the same side are never open together, because that would let
blood flow straight from atrium to artery. This option rewards a student who has learned the
words "volume is constant" without asking what would have to be true of the valves for that
to happen.

## answer_e
No valves are open; volume stays constant at its highest value of the cycle

## explanation_e
Incorrect, and this is the precise distractor for this question. The valve state is right —
all closed — but the volume named is wrong. Constant volume with all valves shut also
describes isovolumetric *contraction*, and in that phase the trapped volume is the
**end-diastolic volume**, about 130 mL, the largest of the cycle. This option picks the
student who has correctly identified an isovolumetric phase but not worked out *which* one:
contraction traps a full ventricle, relaxation traps an empty one.

## title
How high must left ventricular pressure rise before the aortic valve opens, and what sets that threshold?

## question
How high must left ventricular pressure rise before the aortic valve opens, and what sets that threshold?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A student is asked why the left ventricle spends the early part of systole building pressure
without ejecting any blood. They answer that the ventricle must generate the full systolic
pressure of 120 mmHg before the aortic valve can open.

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
About 80 mmHg, because it must exceed aortic diastolic pressure

## explanation_a
Correct. A valve opens on a **pressure gradient**, not at a fixed number, and the number
that matters is the pressure on the far side of it *at that moment*.

During isovolumetric contraction the left ventricle starts from an end-diastolic pressure of
only about 8–9 mmHg and pressure climbs steeply with all valves shut. On the other side of
the aortic valve sits the aorta, which has been held at its **diastolic** pressure of about
**80 mmHg** by the elastic recoil of the great arteries. The valve therefore opens the
instant ventricular pressure passes roughly 80 mmHg — and ejection begins.

Only *after* the valve is open does ventricular pressure continue rising to its peak of
about 120 mmHg, carrying aortic pressure up with it during rapid ejection. So 120 mmHg is
the pressure reached *during* ejection, not the threshold required to start it. That
distinction is the whole content of this question.

The right ventricle follows the same rule against a much lower load: pulmonary artery
diastolic pressure is only about 10 mmHg, so the pulmonary valve opens at around 10 mmHg and
right ventricular pressure peaks near 25 mmHg. Same mechanism, one-eighth the threshold —
which is why the right ventricle is a thin-walled chamber and the left is not.

This also gives the clean definition of afterload: the pressure the ventricle must overcome
before it can eject. Raise aortic diastolic pressure and the ventricle must generate more
pressure, for longer, before any blood leaves — the reason isovolumetric contraction
lengthens and stroke volume falls in severe hypertension or aortic stenosis.

## answer_b
About 120 mmHg, because it must exceed peak aortic systolic pressure

## explanation_b
Incorrect, and this is the misconception stated in the vignette. It confuses the pressure
reached during ejection with the pressure needed to begin it. 120 mmHg is the *peak* the
ventricle and aorta reach together once blood is already flowing. If the ventricle really had
to exceed 120 mmHg before the valve opened, it could never generate the peak in the first
place, because that peak is produced by ejection itself. The valve opens against aortic
*diastolic* pressure, which is the lowest — not the highest — pressure the aorta sees.

## answer_c
About 8–9 mmHg, because it must exceed left atrial pressure

## explanation_c
Incorrect. 8–9 mmHg is the left ventricular end-diastolic pressure — the pressure the
ventricle *starts* isovolumetric contraction from, not the threshold it is climbing towards.
Exceeding left atrial pressure is what closes the **mitral** valve at the beginning of
systole; it has nothing to do with opening the aortic valve at the other end of the chamber.
This option picks the student who has grabbed the correct figure from the wrong end of the
phase.

## answer_d
About 10 mmHg, because it must exceed pulmonary artery diastolic pressure

## explanation_d
Incorrect for the left ventricle, though the reasoning is right for the wrong chamber. About
10 mmHg is the threshold for the **pulmonary** valve, opened by the *right* ventricle
against pulmonary artery diastolic pressure. The stem asks about the aortic valve. This
option catches the student who has understood the gradient principle correctly but attached
the systemic and pulmonary figures to the wrong sides — a genuine and common mix-up, and the
reason the two circuits should always be learned as a pair with their pressures attached.

## answer_e
About 25 mmHg, because it must exceed mean pulmonary artery pressure

## explanation_e
Incorrect on two counts. 25 mmHg is the *peak systolic* pressure of the right ventricle, not
a mean pulmonary pressure, and neither figure has any bearing on when the aortic valve
opens. This option picks a student who is pattern-matching to a remembered right-heart
number without asking which chamber and which valve the question concerns.

QST-CVS-FILLING-CONTRIBUTIONS-001

## title
At rest, how is ventricular filling divided between passive filling and atrial contraction?

## question
At rest, how is ventricular filling divided between passive filling and atrial contraction?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A patient with a stiff, poorly compliant left ventricle develops atrial fibrillation and
deteriorates immediately, becoming breathless at rest. A student is asked how much of normal
resting ventricular filling depends on organised atrial contraction, and therefore how much
this patient has just lost.

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
About 50% passive, 50% by atrial contraction

## explanation_a
Incorrect, and it substantially overstates the atria. This option treats the atria as
co-equal pumps rather than as the reservoirs and primers they actually are. If half of
filling depended on atrial contraction, atrial fibrillation would be catastrophic in every
patient rather than well tolerated in most. It is picked by students who reason from the
anatomy — four chambers, two of them atria — instead of from the pressure gradients that
actually move the blood.

## answer_b
About 70% passive, 30% by atrial contraction

## explanation_b
Incorrect, though this figure appears in some question banks — including, inconsistently, the
source paper this item was drawn from, which gives both 20% and 30% for the atrial share in
different questions. The value taught in this module and used across the library is **20%**.
Flagging the conflict matters more than the eight-point difference: a student who has met
30% has not made a reasoning error, but should know which figure their own department
examines.

## answer_c
About 20% passive, 80% by atrial contraction

## explanation_c
Incorrect — this is the correct pair of numbers with the two contributions swapped, and it is
the most instructive wrong answer here. It describes a heart in which the atria do most of
the work of filling, which is the opposite of the truth at rest. The test of whether the
figures are the right way round is atrial fibrillation itself: if the atria supplied 80% of
filling, losing organised atrial contraction would be immediately fatal, and it plainly is
not.

## answer_d
About 100% passive, with atrial contraction contributing nothing at rest

## explanation_d
Incorrect, but it is the closest wrong answer and the one worth arguing with. Passive filling
does the great majority of the work at rest, so a student who says "essentially all of it"
has the right emphasis. But the atrial contribution is not zero — it is around 20%, enough
that its loss is clinically obvious in a poorly compliant ventricle, exactly as in the
vignette. "Small" and "absent" are different claims, and this patient's deterioration is the
evidence.

## answer_e
About 80% passive, 20% by atrial contraction

## explanation_e
Correct. At rest, roughly **80% of ventricular filling is passive** and complete before the
atria contract at all; **atrial contraction supplies the remaining 20%**.

The mechanism follows the pressure gradient. During ventricular systole the atria simply
collect venous return while the atrioventricular valves are shut, so atrial pressure climbs.
When the ventricle relaxes and its pressure falls below atrial pressure, the mitral valve
opens and blood rushes in down a gradient that has been building throughout systole — the
**rapid filling phase**, which alone accounts for the bulk of the volume. A slower phase of
reduced filling follows. Only at the very end, in late diastole, do the atria contract and
top the ventricle up to its end-diastolic volume of about 130 mL.

So the atria are best understood as **primers, not pumps**. This is why atrial fibrillation
is tolerated at all: lose the top-up and a normal ventricle still fills to about 80% of
usual, and the resulting fall in stroke volume is modest.

Two situations change that, and both appear in exams. The first is the vignette: when the
ventricle is **stiff and poorly compliant**, passive filling is impaired, the atrial top-up
becomes proportionately far more important, and losing it precipitates immediate
decompensation. The second is **exercise** — diastole shortens as heart rate rises, leaving
less time for passive filling, so the atrial contribution rises above its resting 20%. In
both cases the principle is the same: the less time or gradient available for passive
filling, the more the atrial kick matters.

## title
Why does the jugular venous pulse give a reading of right atrial pressure?

## question
Why does the jugular venous pulse give a reading of right atrial pressure?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette
A patient with a failing right ventricle is examined semi-recumbent at about 45°. The
internal jugular venous pulsation is visible high in the neck and shows a clear multiphasic
waveform that is not palpable. A student is asked what makes this an index of pressure inside
the heart rather than a local feature of the neck veins.

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Competent valves in the internal jugular vein sample and hold right atrial pressure

## explanation_a
Incorrect, and it gets the anatomy exactly backwards. Valves are what would *prevent*
transmission: a competent valve between the heart and the neck would block retrograde
pressure waves and make the jugular pulse useless as a gauge of atrial pressure. The
observation depends on the *absence* of valves along that path, not their presence. This
option picks the student who knows veins characteristically contain valves and applies the
generalisation without asking what it would imply here.

## answer_b
The jugular veins lie close to the chest, so they transmit left atrial pressure

## explanation_b
Incorrect, and it names the wrong chamber. The internal jugular vein drains via the
brachiocephalic vein and superior vena cava into the **right** atrium; there is no venous
path from the left atrium to the neck, since left atrial blood has come from the lungs and
leaves through the left ventricle. Left atrial pressure is estimated quite differently, from
pulmonary venous pressures. Mistaking which atrium the neck veins report makes every
subsequent interpretation of a raised JVP wrong.

## answer_c
The high compliance of veins allows them to distend in proportion to arterial pressure

## explanation_c
Incorrect, though it contains a true statement doing the wrong job. Veins are indeed highly
compliant, and that compliance is why a modest pressure change produces a *visible* change in
filling — so it explains why the sign can be seen, not why it reports right atrial pressure.
Compliance without an open, valveless connection to the atrium would show only local
distension. The option also smuggles in "arterial pressure": the jugular venous pulse is
venous, which is precisely why it is non-palpable and multiphasic rather than a single sharp
arterial upstroke.

## answer_d
There are no valves between the right atrium and the venae cavae, so atrial pressure waves travel back into the jugular veins

## explanation_d
Correct. The internal jugular vein, brachiocephalic vein, superior vena cava and right atrium
form a **continuous, valveless column of blood**. Because nothing interrupts that column,
pressure changes generated inside the right atrium are transmitted *retrogradely* into the
neck, where they can be seen. The jugular pulsation is therefore a direct window onto right
atrial pressure — in effect a manometer that needs no instrument.

Two things follow, and both are examined. First, the individual waves of the jugular trace
are simply right atrial events read at a distance: the **a wave** is atrial systole, the **c
wave** the bulge of the tricuspid cusps into the atrium during ventricular contraction, and
the **v wave** the atrial filling that accumulates against a closed tricuspid valve during
ventricular systole. Any deflection seen in the neck has a cause inside the atrium.

Second, because this is a fluid column open to the atmosphere at its top, **vertical height
matters**. Venous pressure falls with height above the right atrium, and neck veins collapse
where their internal pressure drops below atmospheric. The height of the visible pulsation
above the sternal angle is therefore the measurement, which is why the patient is positioned
semi-recumbent at about 45°: sit them fully upright and a normal column collapses out of
view; lie them flat and even a normal column fills the whole neck. In this patient a failing
right ventricle cannot clear its venous return, right atrial pressure rises, and the column
stands abnormally high — a raised JVP.

The distinction from the carotid pulse falls out of the same reasoning: the jugular pulse is
a low-pressure venous column, so it is non-palpable, multiphasic, and alters with posture and
respiration.

## answer_e
The tricuspid valve is normally incompetent, allowing ventricular pressure into the neck veins

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A normal tricuspid valve is competent, and no ventricular pressure reaches the
neck in health. This option confuses the normal mechanism with a pathological one: in
significant tricuspid *regurgitation* right ventricular systolic pressure is indeed
transmitted to the atrium and produces a giant v wave in the neck. But that is a specific
abnormality superimposed on the normal valveless atrium-to-jugular connection, not the reason
the JVP works in the first place.
