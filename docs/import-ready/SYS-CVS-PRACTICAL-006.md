### What the machine is recording
Concept: CON-CVS-CEF955ABD51569
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What does an electrocardiogram record?
*= The electrical activity of the heart, recorded from the body surface
Why: Correct. The ECG is a record of cardiac electrical activity detected at the skin, not of the heart's mechanical performance.
* The mechanical contraction of the heart chambers
Why: The commonest misconception, and a clinically dangerous one: electrical activity can be present with no effective contraction at all.
* The pressure generated inside the ventricles during systole
Why: Pressure is measured by catheterisation. Nothing in the trace is a pressure.
* The flow of blood through the coronary arteries
Why: Coronary flow is inferred from the trace only indirectly, through the changes ischaemia produces. It is not what is recorded.
Explanation: The distinction between electrical and mechanical is the foundation of everything that follows: a trace can look organised while the heart is not pumping, which is why a pulse is checked and not just the monitor.

### How many electrodes, and where
Concept: CON-CVS-11B3FD36CE3B72
Difficulty: Easy
Q: A standard 12-lead ECG is being recorded. How many electrodes are placed, and where?
*= Ten — one on each limb and six on the chest
Why: Correct. Ten electrodes generate twelve leads, because a lead is a view constructed from electrodes rather than a wire in its own right.
* Twelve — one for each of the twelve leads
Why: The natural assumption from the name, and the reason this is the single commonest error about the ECG. Leads are views, not electrodes.
* Six — one on each limb and two on the chest
Why: Too few to generate the six chest views, each of which needs its own electrode.
* Fifteen — ten standard plus five posterior
Why: Additional electrodes are used for posterior or right-sided views, but that is an extended recording, not the standard twelve.
Explanation: Twelve leads from ten electrodes is the point: six limb leads are derived by combining the limb electrodes, and each chest lead has an electrode of its own. Knowing this is what makes a right-sided or posterior recording an obvious extension rather than a new technique.

### How the signal gets to the skin
Concept: CON-CVS-D9A834BAEAFF68
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Why can electrodes on the skin detect events happening in the heart?
*= The body conducts, so currents generated by the heart spread through it and reach the surface
Why: Correct. The tissues act as a volume conductor, so a fraction of the current generated by cardiac depolarisation reaches the skin and can be recorded.
* The electrodes detect the heart's magnetic field rather than a current
Why: A magnetic field does exist and can be measured, but by magnetocardiography, not by an ECG electrode.
* Nerves carry the signal from the heart to the skin electrodes
Why: Cardiac nerves carry autonomic traffic to the heart; they do not relay the myocardial signal outwards to the skin.
* The signal is transmitted mechanically as a pressure wave through the chest wall
Why: A mechanical wave is what a phonocardiogram or a palpating hand detects, not what an electrode records.
Explanation: The whole body is a conducting medium. That is why electrode position matters, why a limb lead recorded at the wrist and at the shoulder give near-identical traces, and why anything that changes what lies between heart and electrode — fluid, air, fat — changes the amplitude.

### Which lead sees the most
Concept: CON-CVS-6CDC248C167C59
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A wave of depolarisation travels exactly along the axis of one lead, towards its positive electrode. What does that lead record?
*= Its maximal positive deflection
Why: Correct. A vector aligned with a lead's positive axis projects onto it completely, giving the largest positive wave that vector can produce in that lead.
* A biphasic wave, equally positive and negative
Why: That is what a lead perpendicular to the vector records, because the projection onto its axis is close to zero.
* Its maximal negative deflection
Why: The correct magnitude with the sign reversed: this is what a vector travelling directly away from the positive electrode produces.
* A flat line, because the current is parallel to the lead
Why: Reverses the geometry. A parallel vector gives the largest signal; a perpendicular one gives the smallest.
Explanation: Each lead records the projection of the depolarisation vector onto its own axis. Alignment gives the biggest deflection, opposition the biggest negative one, and perpendicularity gives nearly nothing — which is the whole basis of working out an axis.

### When the P waves are not there
Concept: CON-CVS-236CE7171C7289
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A trace shows an irregularly irregular rhythm with no discrete P waves. What does the absence of P waves indicate?
*= There is no single organised atrial depolarisation, as in atrial fibrillation
Why: Correct. A P wave is the record of one coordinated atrial depolarisation. Chaotic atrial activity produces no discrete P wave.
* The atria are not contracting at all and are electrically silent
Why: The atria are electrically very active in fibrillation; the activity is disorganised rather than absent, which is a different statement.
* The sinoatrial node has stopped and a ventricular escape rhythm has taken over
Why: That would give a slow, regular rhythm with broad complexes, not an irregularly irregular one.
* The P waves are present but hidden inside the QRS complexes
Why: This does happen in some rhythms, and looking for buried P waves is a good habit — but it would not produce an irregularly irregular ventricular response.
Explanation: A discrete P wave is evidence of organised atrial depolarisation. Its absence, with an irregularly irregular rhythm, is the recognition pattern for atrial fibrillation, and it also explains the lost atrial contribution to ventricular filling.

### Narrowing without occlusion
Concept: CON-CVS-2480077C89FDC3
Difficulty: Easy
Q: An exercise test reproduces ST depression at a heart rate of 130, which resolves within two minutes of rest. What does that indicate?
*= Coronary narrowing, limiting flow only when demand rises
Why: Correct. A stenosis permits adequate resting flow but cannot meet the increased demand of exercise, so ischaemia appears with exertion and resolves with rest.
* Complete coronary occlusion with established infarction
Why: Occlusion produces persistent changes at rest and does not resolve within two minutes of stopping.
* Normal coronary arteries with a physiological response to exercise
Why: ST depression is not a normal exercise response. This is the misconception that any change with exertion is physiological.
* Coronary artery spasm, which characteristically occurs during exertion
Why: Spasm exists but characteristically occurs at rest and unpredictably, rather than reproducibly at a given workload.
Explanation: Reproducible ischaemia at a threshold workload is the signature of a fixed stenosis: supply is adequate until demand exceeds what the narrowed vessel can deliver.

### From occlusion to dead muscle
Concept: CON-CVS-1A060C49C5C0C1
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A different patient has ST elevation persisting for three hours with a rising troponin. What has occurred?
*= Occlusion of a coronary artery with infarction of the muscle it supplied
Why: Correct. Sustained occlusion removes supply entirely, and the dependent myocardium dies — which is what the rising troponin reports.
* Severe narrowing without occlusion, producing prolonged ischaemia only
Why: Narrowing alone gives transient ischaemia related to demand. It does not produce hours of persistent elevation with cell death.
* A normal variant of ST elevation seen in young men
Why: Benign ST elevation is a genuine pattern and worth knowing, but it is stable and is not accompanied by a rising troponin.
* Pericarditis, which also elevates the ST segments
Why: A real differential, and the reason the distribution matters: pericarditis gives widespread rather than territorial change, and does not usually produce this troponin pattern.
Explanation: Ischaemia is reversible and demand-related; infarction is the consequence of occlusion. The trace, the timing and the troponin are read together, because none of the three alone separates them.

### Which wall, which vessel
Concept: CON-CVS-63D0C4219C40EA
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: ST elevation appears in leads I, aVL, V5 and V6. Which vessel is most likely occluded?
*= The circumflex artery, which runs in the coronary sulcus and supplies the lateral wall
Why: Correct. Those leads face the lateral wall of the left ventricle, which is circumflex territory.
* The anterior interventricular artery, in the anterior interventricular groove
Why: Its territory is the anterior wall and septum, giving changes in V1 to V4.
* The right coronary artery, in the right coronary sulcus
Why: Supplies the inferior wall in most hearts, giving changes in II, III and aVF.
* The right marginal artery, along the acute margin
Why: Supplies the right ventricular free wall, which the standard left-sided leads face poorly.
Explanation: The reading runs lead, wall, groove, vessel. Lateral leads face the lateral wall, and the vessel that travels around to the lateral wall is the circumflex in its sulcus.

### The territory the standard leads miss
Concept: CON-CVS-44B74F73B50D20
Difficulty: Hard
Q: A patient with inferior ST elevation becomes profoundly hypotensive. The team wants to assess the right ventricle. Why do the standard leads serve them poorly, and which vessel supplies that chamber?
*= The standard leads face the left ventricle, so right-sided leads are added; the right ventricle is supplied largely by the right marginal artery running along the acute margin
Why: Correct. The right ventricle is not well seen by the conventional twelve leads, and the right marginal branch of the right coronary artery is its principal supply.
* The standard leads face the right ventricle already, so no additional leads are needed
Why: Inverts the geometry. The chest leads are placed over the left side of the praecordium and face the left ventricle.
* The right ventricle is supplied by the circumflex artery, which the lateral leads already assess
Why: Places a left-sided vessel on the right side of the heart, and would make the lateral leads sufficient when they are not.
* The right ventricle has no separate arterial supply, being perfused from the left ventricular cavity
Why: Invents a mechanism. Perfusion from the cavity is a feature of some vessels in some circumstances, not the supply of a whole chamber.
Explanation: The twelve standard leads are a left-ventricular examination. Recognising an inferior infarct with hypotension as a reason to look right — and knowing which artery runs along the acute margin — is what turns a rule into a decision.

### Who supplies the back
Concept: CON-CVS-69303DBED9D05C
Difficulty: Hard
Q: The angiogram is reported as showing a balanced circulation. What does that mean for predicting territory from the ECG?
*= Both coronary arteries contribute to the posterior territory, so an inferior or posterior pattern does not identify a single culprit vessel with confidence
Why: Correct. In a balanced pattern neither artery dominates the posterior supply, so territory-to-vessel inference is less certain than it is in a dominant circulation.
* The right coronary artery supplies the whole posterior territory, as it does in every heart
Why: Describes right dominance, which is the commonest pattern but not universal — and not the pattern reported here.
* The two arteries are of equal calibre, which makes the ECG territories more reliable
Why: Dominance is about territory supplied, not calibre, and a balanced pattern makes inference less reliable rather than more.
* Territory cannot be assessed on ECG at all when dominance is balanced
Why: Overcorrects. The wall involved is still identifiable; it is the vessel that becomes ambiguous.
Explanation: The ECG identifies a wall. Which vessel supplies that wall depends on the pattern of dominance, which is why the same inferior trace can implicate different arteries in different people.

### What block looks like
Concept: CON-CVS-FAC483F74DFA04
Difficulty: Easy
Q: Which pair of findings on a trace indicates impaired atrioventricular conduction?
*= A prolonged PR interval, or P waves that are not followed by a QRS complex
Why: Correct. Delay lengthens the PR interval; failure means an atrial impulse does not reach the ventricles at all.
* A widened QRS complex, or a tall R wave in V5
Why: Both are ventricular findings. A wide QRS reflects slow conduction within the ventricles, not between atria and ventricles.
* Absent P waves, or an irregularly irregular rhythm
Why: Describes atrial fibrillation, a disorder of atrial activation rather than of the atrioventricular junction.
* ST elevation, or T wave inversion in the inferior leads
Why: Repolarisation changes point at ischaemia. An inferior infarct can cause block, but the ST change is not how block presents on the trace.
Explanation: Atrioventricular conduction is assessed by the relationship between P waves and QRS complexes, and by nothing else on the trace. The PR interval measures the delay; a non-conducted P wave shows the delay has become a failure.

### How long the gate holds
Concept: CON-CVS-0C1DCBDF079969
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: How long is the normal delay at the atrioventricular node, and how does it appear on the trace?
*= About a tenth of a second, contributing most of the PR interval
Why: Correct. The nodal delay is of the order of 0.1 seconds and is the largest single component of the interval between atrial and ventricular activation.
* About a hundredth of a second, too brief to affect the PR interval
Why: Ten times too short, and would leave the PR interval unexplained.
* About half a second, which is why the PR interval is the longest interval on the trace
Why: Ten times too long. At a normal rate a delay of half a second would occupy most of the cycle.
* There is no delay; the PR interval reflects atrial conduction alone
Why: Atrial conduction contributes, but the deliberate hold at the node is the dominant component.
Explanation: The PR interval is mostly the nodal delay made visible. That is why a lengthening PR interval is read as a statement about the node rather than about the atria.

### Why the delay exists
Concept: CON-CVS-3FEBA4CA87A0BD
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What is the purpose of the atrioventricular delay?
*= It allows the atria to complete their contribution to ventricular filling before the ventricles contract
Why: Correct. The hold separates atrial from ventricular systole in time, so the atrial contribution to filling is not wasted against a contracting ventricle.
* It protects the ventricles from every atrial impulse, whatever the atrial rate
Why: Rate-limiting protection is a genuine and useful property of the node, but it is a consequence of its refractoriness rather than the purpose of the delay in a normal beat.
* It gives the coronary arteries time to fill before systole begins
Why: Coronary filling occurs in diastole, but it is not what the atrioventricular delay is timed for.
* It allows the ventricular myocytes to repolarise fully after the preceding beat
Why: Repolarisation is complete well before the next atrial impulse arrives at a normal rate.
Explanation: The delay buys coordination. Once you see it as protected filling time, both the consequence of losing it and the cost of a very fast atrial rate follow.

### Naming the pattern
Concept: CON-CVS-F48CFC6D7458A2
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A trace shows regular P waves at 90 per minute and regular QRS complexes at 40 per minute, with no consistent relationship between them. What is this?
*= Complete atrioventricular block, with the ventricles driven by an independent escape rhythm
Why: Correct. Independent atrial and ventricular rates mean no impulse is crossing from one to the other, and the ventricles are being driven by a subsidiary pacemaker.
* Sinus bradycardia at 40 per minute
Why: Reads only the ventricular rate. The atria are going at 90, so the sinoatrial node is not slow.
* Second-degree block, in which some impulses conduct
Why: In second-degree block a consistent relationship persists for the conducted beats. Here there is none.
* Atrial fibrillation with complete block
Why: Would require the absence of discrete P waves. This trace has regular, uniform P waves.
Explanation: Reading the atrial rate before the ventricular rate is what distinguishes complete block from bradycardia. Two independent, regular rates is the pattern to recognise.

### Why there is no way round
Concept: CON-CVS-A6482415169BD3
Difficulty: Hard
Q: With no impulse crossing the junction, why can the atrial activity not simply spread into the ventricular muscle directly?
*= The fibrous skeleton insulates atrial from ventricular myocardium, so the conducting system is the only route
Why: Correct. The fibrous rings do not conduct, which leaves the atrioventricular bundle as the sole electrical connection between the chambers.
* Ventricular myocytes are refractory whenever the atria depolarise
Why: Refractoriness is intermittent and rate-dependent; it could not account for a permanent absence of conduction at a ventricular rate of 40.
* The atrioventricular valves physically separate the chambers during systole
Why: A mechanical separation during part of the cycle, not a permanent electrical one, and conduction does not depend on valve position.
* Atrial and ventricular myocytes lack the gap junctions needed to pass current
Why: Both have gap junctions in abundance — that is how cell-to-cell activation works within each chamber. The barrier is the fibrous tissue between them.
Explanation: One insulating plate with one hole in it. That single arrangement explains why atrioventricular block is possible at all, why the ventricles need an escape rhythm when it occurs, and why the level of the lesion determines how reliable that escape will be.

### Why the neck reports the atrium
Concept: CON-CVS-BB7AC16AB9B338
Difficulty: Easy
Q: A venous pressure trace recorded in the neck shows the same waveform as the right atrial pressure. Why?
*= There are no valves between the right atrium and the venae cavae, so atrial pressure waves pass straight up the jugular veins
Why: Correct. The absence of valves makes the jugular vein a continuous column with the right atrium, so its pressure waves are transmitted unmodified.
* The jugular veins generate their own pressure waves in time with the heart
Why: Veins do not generate pulsations of their own; what is seen is transmitted from the atrium.
* The carotid artery transmits its pulsation to the adjacent vein
Why: Arterial transmission is a real artefact at the bedside, and distinguishing the two pulsations is a core skill — but it does not reproduce the atrial waveform.
* Valves in the jugular vein open in systole and close in diastole, generating the waves
Why: Invents a valve mechanism. It is precisely the absence of valves that produces the sign.
Explanation: The jugular vein is a manometer for the right atrium because nothing intervenes. Everything that raises right atrial pressure — a filling pericardium, a failing right ventricle, an obstructed tricuspid valve — appears in the neck.

### The silence above systolic
Concept: CON-CVS-BE94FBA0B205EF
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: While a blood pressure cuff is inflated above systolic pressure, no sounds are heard. Why?
*= The artery is completely occluded, so there is no flow to become turbulent
Why: Correct. Korotkoff sounds are produced by turbulent flow through a partly compressed artery; with no flow at all there is nothing to make a sound.
* The artery is fully open, so flow is laminar and silent
Why: Describes the silence *below* diastolic pressure, at the other end of the measurement, where the cuff no longer compresses the vessel.
* The stethoscope is above the level of the cuff and cannot detect the sounds
Why: A placement error would produce silence throughout, not silence confined to pressures above systolic.
* The pressure in the cuff damps the sounds without stopping the flow
Why: Would predict quiet sounds rather than none, and misses that occlusion is complete at these pressures.
Explanation: There are two silences in a blood pressure measurement and they mean opposite things: no flow above systolic, and smooth flow below diastolic. Sound appears only in between, where flow is intermittent and turbulent.

### The cuff that lies
Concept: CON-CVS-85F9DE6C65F2F7
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A cuff is applied loosely. What effect does that have on the reading?
*= It gives a falsely high reading, because more cuff pressure is needed to compress the artery
Why: Correct. A loose cuff transmits its pressure to the limb inefficiently, so a higher cuff pressure is required to occlude the artery and the recorded pressure overstates the true one.
* It gives a falsely low reading, because the cuff compresses less tissue
Why: Reasons from the amount of tissue compressed rather than from the pressure actually transmitted to the artery.
* It has no effect provided the cuff is the correct width for the arm
Why: Width and fit are separate variables, and a correctly sized cuff applied loosely still misreads.
* It affects the diastolic reading only, leaving systolic accurate
Why: Both readings are affected, because both depend on the cuff pressure at which the artery's state changes.
Explanation: The measurement assumes the cuff pressure reaches the artery. Anything that breaks that assumption — a loose cuff, a cuff too narrow for the arm — shifts the reading upwards, and it is a technique error rather than a finding.

### The phase with nothing moving
Concept: CON-CVS-C5B68CCC8E4F3E
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A ventricular pressure trace shows pressure falling steeply while the volume trace is flat. Which phase is this, and what is the state of the valves?
*= Isovolumetric relaxation — all four valves are closed and ventricular volume is constant
Why: Correct. After the semilunar valves close and before the atrioventricular valves open, no blood enters or leaves, so pressure falls at constant volume.
* Isovolumetric contraction, with all valves closed and pressure rising
Why: The right description of a closed-valve phase but the wrong one: contraction shows pressure rising, and the trace here shows it falling.
* Rapid ventricular filling, with the atrioventricular valves open
Why: Volume would be rising steeply, not flat, once the atrioventricular valves open.
* Ejection, with the semilunar valves open
Why: Volume falls steeply during ejection; a flat volume trace excludes it.
Explanation: A flat volume with a changing pressure means every valve is shut. Whether it is contraction or relaxation is then read off the direction of the pressure change, which is why the two traces are always read together.

### What has to be exceeded
Concept: CON-CVS-974D60394FF346
Difficulty: Challenging
Q: During isovolumetric contraction, what determines the moment each semilunar valve opens?
*= Each opens when ventricular pressure exceeds the pressure in the artery beyond it, so the pulmonary valve opens first and at a much lower pressure than the aortic
Why: Correct. Opening is set by the pressure the ventricle must exceed: pulmonary arterial pressure is far lower than aortic, so the right ventricle reaches its opening pressure earlier and at a lower value.
* Both open simultaneously, when ventricular pressure exceeds atrial pressure
Why: Uses the wrong comparison entirely. Exceeding atrial pressure is what closes the atrioventricular valves at the start of systole.
* The aortic valve opens first, because the left ventricle generates more pressure
Why: The left ventricle does generate more pressure, which is exactly why this is chosen — but it has a much higher pressure to overcome, so it takes longer to get there.
* Both open when the ventricles reach a fixed threshold pressure set by the valve cusps
Why: Treats opening as a property of the valve rather than of the pressure difference across it. Cusps have no threshold of their own.
Explanation: A valve opens when the pressure difference across it reverses, and nothing else. Because the two ventricles face very different downstream pressures, the same contraction opens the two semilunar valves at different times — which is the physiological basis of the split second heart sound's counterpart at the start of systole.
