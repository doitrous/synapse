### Two actions in one molecule
Miss Mona Adly, 28, develops facial swelling, stridor and hypotension minutes after a wasp sting.
Concept: CON-REN-EC021DCE205941
Difficulty: Easy
Q: Which two receptor actions make adrenaline suited to this emergency?
*= Beta-2-mediated bronchodilatation together with alpha-mediated vasoconstriction of the mucosa
Why: Correct. The beta-2 action opens the airway while the alpha action reduces mucosal oedema and supports blood pressure — two problems addressed by one molecule.
* Beta-2 bronchodilatation together with alpha-mediated vasodilatation
Why: Alpha stimulation constricts rather than dilates; vasodilatation would worsen the hypotension.
* Beta-1 inotropy together with beta-2 vasoconstriction
Why: Beta-2 stimulation dilates vessels in muscle; the vasoconstriction is alpha-mediated.
* Alpha-mediated bronchodilatation with beta blockade of the mucosa
Why: Bronchial smooth muscle relaxation is beta-2, and adrenaline blocks nothing.
Rationale: Anaphylaxis threatens the airway and the circulation at once. Adrenaline is first-line because a single agent acts on both, which no other single drug does.

### Opposing histamine without blocking it
Concept: CON-REN-613F9151E4D17D
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Adrenaline is described as a physiological antagonist of histamine. What does that mean?
*= It produces the opposite effects to histamine by acting on different receptors, rather than blocking histamine receptors
Why: Correct. Histamine causes bronchoconstriction and vasodilatation; adrenaline causes bronchodilatation and vasoconstriction through its own receptors, opposing the effects without competing at the histamine receptor.
* It blocks histamine H1 receptors competitively
Why: That is a pharmacological antagonist — an antihistamine — which acts at the same receptor.
* It prevents histamine release from mast cells
Why: Mast-cell stabilisation is a different mechanism and not the meaning of physiological antagonism.
* It accelerates the metabolism of circulating histamine
Why: Increased clearance is neither its mechanism nor fast enough for an emergency.
Rationale: The distinction matters clinically: an antihistamine competes for a receptor already occupied, while adrenaline opposes the consequences whatever the histamine is doing. That is why an antihistamine is an adjunct and adrenaline the treatment.

### Why it cannot be swallowed
Concept: CON-REN-56CB82125AB641
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Why is adrenaline given by injection rather than by mouth, and what limits subcutaneous absorption?
*= It is inactivated before reaching the circulation when swallowed, and subcutaneous absorption is slow because its own vasoconstriction reduces local blood flow
Why: Correct. It is orally ineffective, and given subcutaneously the alpha-mediated vasoconstriction it produces slows its own uptake — which is why the intramuscular route is preferred in anaphylaxis.
* It is destroyed by gastric acid but well absorbed subcutaneously
Why: The subcutaneous route is specifically slow, for the reason above.
* It is too large a molecule to cross the intestinal mucosa
Why: It is a small molecule; the barrier is metabolic rather than one of size.
* It is well absorbed orally but has too short a half-life to be useful
Why: Oral ineffectiveness is the stated limitation, not half-life.
Rationale: A drug that constricts the vessels around it slows its own absorption. That single fact explains why the intramuscular route into a well-perfused muscle is chosen in an emergency.

### Where it is used systemically
Concept: CON-REN-295382C3A2AB49
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: In which situations is systemic adrenaline used?
*= Cardiac arrest, severe bronchial asthma, hypoglycaemic states and anaphylaxis
Why: Correct. These are the listed systemic indications, each exploiting a different combination of its actions.
* Hypertensive emergency and stable angina
Why: Both are contraindications rather than indications; adrenaline raises pressure and myocardial oxygen demand.
* Chronic heart failure and atrial fibrillation
Why: Chronic use has no place, and it is arrhythmogenic.
* Routine local anaesthesia without a vasoconstrictor indication
Why: Its local use is precisely as a vasoconstrictor added to local anaesthetic, which is a different setting from systemic administration.
Rationale: The indications are a list only until you see the mechanism behind each: restart a heart, open an airway, raise glucose, reverse anaphylaxis. Each is one of its actions used deliberately.

### What can go wrong
Concept: CON-REN-4CF248F99F1F87
Difficulty: Hard
Q: What adverse effects follow from adrenaline's own pharmacology, and which patients are most at risk?
*= Hypertension with the risk of haemorrhage, and ischaemic gangrene where alpha-mediated vasoconstriction is intense — most dangerous in patients with existing hypertension, ischaemic heart disease or arrhythmia
Why: Correct. Every adverse effect is an exaggeration of an intended one: alpha constriction raises pressure to the point of haemorrhage and can render an end-artery territory ischaemic, particularly in digits. Pre-existing vascular or ischaemic disease amplifies the risk.
* Hypotension and bradycardia from excessive beta-2 stimulation
Why: Beta-2 vasodilatation exists but does not dominate; adrenaline raises rate and pressure.
* Renal failure from direct tubular toxicity
Why: Its renal effects are haemodynamic rather than directly toxic.
* Respiratory depression from a central action
Why: It does not depress respiration; it is used to relieve bronchoconstriction.
Rationale: Adverse effects that are simply the intended effects taken too far are predictable from the receptor map. That is why the same drug is life-saving in a young person with anaphylaxis and hazardous in an older person with coronary disease — and why the dose and route differ accordingly.
## debrief
Anaphylaxis threatens airway and circulation simultaneously, and adrenaline is the only single agent that addresses both — beta-2 to open the airway, alpha to support the pressure and reduce mucosal oedema. It opposes histamine physiologically rather than by blockade, which is why it works whatever the histamine is doing. And its adverse effects are the same actions carried too far.

### The cardiac actions
An infusion is being titrated in the intensive care unit, and the effects are followed on the monitor.
Concept: CON-REN-E518604E062D82
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What does beta-1 stimulation by adrenaline do to the heart?
*= Increases contractility, rate, conduction velocity and automaticity
Why: Correct. Beta-1 stimulation raises force, rate and conduction speed, and increases automaticity — which is also why it is arrhythmogenic.
* Increases contractility while slowing the rate
Why: Both rise together under beta-1 stimulation.
* Slows conduction through the atrioventricular node
Why: It accelerates conduction; slowing is a vagal or beta-blocking effect.
* Reduces automaticity, stabilising the rhythm
Why: Increased automaticity is what makes it pro-arrhythmic.
Rationale: Four cardiac effects from one receptor, all in the same direction. The last of them, automaticity, is why the monitor is watched as closely as the blood pressure.

### The two vascular beds
Concept: CON-REN-25D5D0C2107B9D
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: How does adrenaline act on different vascular beds?
*= It constricts skin and mucosal vessels while dilating coronary and skeletal muscle vessels
Why: Correct. Alpha receptors predominate in skin and mucosa, beta-2 in coronary and skeletal muscle beds, so the same drug constricts some regions and dilates others.
* It constricts every vascular bed uniformly
Why: Uniform constriction would raise diastolic pressure markedly, which is not what adrenaline does.
* It dilates every vascular bed uniformly
Why: That would cause hypotension rather than the observed pressor effect.
* It constricts coronary vessels and dilates the skin
Why: Reverses both; coronary dilatation accompanies increased cardiac work.
Rationale: Receptor distribution, not the drug, decides the direction in each bed. That is why blood is redistributed towards muscle and heart and away from skin — the pattern of a fight-or-flight response.

### The shape of the pressure response
Concept: CON-REN-754AD98D67E114
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What happens to systolic, diastolic and pulse pressure during an adrenaline infusion?
*= Systolic pressure and pulse pressure rise, while diastolic pressure changes little or falls
Why: Correct. Increased cardiac output raises systolic pressure, while beta-2 dilatation in muscle offsets alpha constriction elsewhere, so diastolic pressure is little changed and the pulse pressure widens.
* Systolic and diastolic pressure rise equally, leaving pulse pressure unchanged
Why: That is the pattern of a pure alpha agonist, not of adrenaline.
* Diastolic pressure rises while systolic falls
Why: Would require a fall in cardiac output, which adrenaline increases.
* Both fall, because beta-2 dilatation predominates
Why: Beta-2 dilatation offsets but does not overwhelm the cardiac and alpha effects.
Rationale: The widened pulse pressure is the fingerprint of a drug with both cardiac and mixed vascular actions. A pure alpha agonist raises both pressures; adrenaline does not.

### Unmasking the other receptor
Concept: CON-REN-C5AE06E7A77A50
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: After alpha blockade, an adrenaline infusion lowers rather than raises the blood pressure. Why?
*= Because blocking the alpha-mediated vasoconstriction leaves the beta-2 vasodilatation unopposed, reversing the pressor response
Why: Correct. Adrenaline acts at both receptor families; removing the constricting arm leaves only the dilating one, so pressure falls — the classic adrenaline reversal.
* Because alpha blockade prevents adrenaline binding to any receptor
Why: Beta receptors remain fully available, which is exactly why the response reverses rather than disappearing.
* Because alpha blockade converts adrenaline into a beta antagonist
Why: The drug is unchanged; only the receptors available to it have changed.
* Because alpha blockade reduces cardiac output to the point of hypotension
Why: Beta-1 effects are intact, so output is maintained or raised.
Rationale: Adrenaline reversal is the clearest demonstration that a drug's net effect is the sum of what its receptors do. Change which receptors are available and the same molecule produces the opposite result.

### Who should not receive it
Concept: CON-REN-F0E1C5811243DA
Difficulty: Challenging
Q: In which patients is systemic adrenaline contraindicated or used with caution, and how does each caution follow from its actions?
*= In hypertension, ischaemic heart disease and arrhythmia — because it raises pressure further, increases myocardial oxygen demand while the supply is limited, and increases automaticity in a heart already prone to arrhythmia
Why: Correct. Each caution maps onto one of its actions: the alpha pressor effect in hypertension, the beta-1 increase in rate and contractility raising oxygen demand in ischaemia, and increased automaticity in arrhythmia.
* In asthma and anaphylaxis, where beta-2 stimulation is harmful
Why: Both are indications; beta-2 stimulation is what relieves them.
* In hypoglycaemia, because it lowers blood glucose further
Why: It raises glucose through glycogenolysis, which is why hypoglycaemia is an indication.
* In young patients, in whom the beta effects predominate dangerously
Why: Risk rises with cardiovascular disease rather than falling with age.
Rationale: Every contraindication is an indication read backwards. Once the receptor actions are held, the list needs no separate memorisation — and in anaphylaxis the balance still favours giving it, because the alternative is worse.
## debrief
Adrenaline's net effect is the sum of its receptors: beta-1 on the heart, alpha in skin and mucosa, beta-2 in muscle and coronary vessels. That mixture produces a rising systolic pressure with a widened pulse pressure, blood redistributed towards muscle and heart, and a list of cautions that is simply the same actions in patients who cannot tolerate them.

### The local action
Concept: CON-REN-35CDE4B8A1A693
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What does adrenaline applied locally to skin or mucous membrane produce?
*= Alpha-mediated vasoconstriction
Why: Correct. Alpha receptors predominate in these beds, so local application blanches the tissue by constricting its vessels.
* Beta-2-mediated vasodilatation with local warmth
Why: Beta-2 dilatation occurs in muscle and coronary beds, not in skin and mucosa.
* Local anaesthesia by blocking sodium channels
Why: That is the action of the local anaesthetic it is combined with, not of adrenaline.
* Histamine release producing a local weal
Why: It opposes histamine's effects rather than releasing it.
Explanation: One receptor family, one predictable local effect. Everything adrenaline is used for locally follows from constricting the vessels where it is applied.

### What that is used for
Concept: CON-REN-16DB42731765A0
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What are the local uses of adrenaline?
*= Prolonging local anaesthesia, controlling epistaxis, and decongesting mucous membranes
Why: Correct. Vasoconstriction keeps the anaesthetic in place, stops bleeding from a mucosal vessel, and shrinks a congested mucosa — three uses of one action.
* Prolonging anaesthesia by blocking nerve conduction directly
Why: It does not block conduction; it slows removal of the agent that does.
* Reducing swelling by an anti-inflammatory action
Why: It has no anti-inflammatory action in the steroidal sense; the decongestion is vascular.
* Sterilising the mucosa before a procedure
Why: It has no antimicrobial action.
Explanation: Keeping a drug where it was put, stopping bleeding, and shrinking a mucosa are three consequences of the same constriction — which is also why it is avoided where an end-artery supplies the tissue.

### The metabolic effects
Concept: CON-REN-3F808243D3CF49
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What are adrenaline's metabolic effects?
*= Increased glycogenolysis with rises in glucose, lactate and free fatty acids, and an increase in calorigenesis
Why: Correct. It mobilises stored fuel — glycogen to glucose, triglyceride to free fatty acids — and raises metabolic heat production.
* Increased glycogen synthesis with a fall in blood glucose
Why: The opposite: it breaks glycogen down, which is why it is used in hypoglycaemia.
* Increased protein synthesis with a fall in free fatty acids
Why: It mobilises fat rather than sparing it.
* No metabolic effects; its actions are purely cardiovascular
Why: Its metabolic actions are prominent and are one reason it is used in hypoglycaemia.
Explanation: A fight-or-flight hormone makes fuel available and raises heat production. The rise in lactate and free fatty acids is why a metabolic acidosis can accompany a large infusion.

### The smooth muscle it relaxes
Concept: CON-REN-55F2C6FC41F59E
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What does adrenaline do to the gastrointestinal and bladder walls, and to their sphincters?
*= It relaxes the walls while contracting the sphincters
Why: Correct. The wall musculature relaxes and the sphincters contract, which is why urinary retention can accompany sympathomimetic use.
* It contracts the walls and relaxes the sphincters
Why: That is the parasympathetic pattern, which promotes emptying.
* It relaxes both wall and sphincter
Why: Sphincteric contraction is the alpha-mediated component and is what opposes emptying.
* It has no effect on visceral smooth muscle
Why: The effects are well described and clinically relevant.
Explanation: Wall relaxed, sphincter shut is a general sympathetic pattern for hollow viscera — the opposite of the parasympathetic arrangement, and the reason emptying is deferred during a stress response.

### Where the uric acid comes from
Concept: CON-REN-90F0EB9115E7CA
Difficulty: Hard
Q: A patient's plasma urate is measured. Where does most circulating uric acid originate, and what does that imply about diet?
*= Absorbed dietary purines are largely oxidised to uric acid in the liver, but endogenous purine turnover contributes substantially — so dietary restriction alone lowers urate only modestly
Why: Correct. The liver oxidises absorbed purines to uric acid, and this dietary contribution adds to a continuous endogenous supply from nucleic acid turnover. Because much of the load is endogenous, diet alone rarely normalises a raised urate.
* All circulating uric acid derives from dietary purines, so diet alone controls it
Why: Endogenous turnover is a major and unavoidable source, which is why drug treatment is often required.
* Uric acid is produced by the kidney and diet is irrelevant
Why: The kidney excretes urate rather than producing it.
* Uric acid derives from pyrimidine rather than purine breakdown
Why: Pyrimidine catabolism yields soluble products; urate is the end-product of purine catabolism.
Explanation: Two sources feed one pool, and only one of them can be reduced by eating differently. That is why dietary advice in gout is worth giving and rarely sufficient on its own.

### Recognising the attack
Mr Salah Wagdy, 54, wakes at night with a red, hot, exquisitely painful first metatarsophalangeal joint.
Concept: CON-REN-7A52C1FD960B35
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What is the typical presentation of acute gout?
*= Rapid onset of joint pain with warmth, swelling and redness
Why: Correct. An acute attack develops over hours, characteristically at night, with a joint that is hot, swollen, red and extremely tender.
* Gradual onset of stiffness over weeks, worse in the mornings
Why: That pattern suggests an inflammatory arthropathy such as rheumatoid arthritis rather than crystal disease.
* Painless swelling of several joints symmetrically
Why: Acute gout is characteristically painful and usually monoarticular at onset.
* Joint pain relieved by rest and worsened only by exertion
Why: That is a mechanical pattern, as in osteoarthritis.
Rationale: Speed and severity are the discriminators. A joint that becomes agonising over hours is crystal arthritis or septic arthritis until proved otherwise — which is why aspiration matters.

### The chronic form
Concept: CON-REN-31708150F8B722
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What characterises chronic gout?
*= Deposition of urate tophi in soft tissues and joints, with joint damage over time
Why: Correct. Persistent hyperuricaemia leads to tophaceous deposits in soft tissue and joints, and to progressive destruction.
* Complete resolution between attacks with no long-term consequence
Why: Attacks do resolve, but untreated hyperuricaemia produces cumulative deposition and damage.
* Symmetrical erosion of the small joints of the hand only
Why: That distribution suggests rheumatoid disease.
* Deposition of calcium pyrophosphate in cartilage
Why: That is pseudogout, a different crystal.
Rationale: Acute attacks are the visible events; the tophi and the joint damage accumulate between them. That is the argument for treating the urate rather than only the attacks.

### Treating the attack
Concept: CON-REN-691A0268DB4342
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Which agents reduce the inflammation of an acute attack?
*= Colchicine, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and corticosteroids
Why: Correct. All three reduce the inflammatory response to the crystals; the choice between them depends on comorbidity, particularly renal function and gastric risk.
* Allopurinol, which lowers urate rapidly
Why: Allopurinol lowers urate over weeks and does not treat the acute inflammation; starting it during an attack can prolong it.
* Antibiotics, since the joint is infected
Why: Gout is not an infection, although septic arthritis is the differential that must be excluded.
* Aspirin at low dose, which promotes urate excretion
Why: Low-dose salicylate reduces urate excretion and can worsen gout.
Rationale: The attack and the urate are two different targets. Treating the inflammation now and the urate later is the sequence, and confusing the two is the commonest management error.

### Lowering the urate
Concept: CON-REN-E5BAEF03791C8F
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: How does allopurinol lower plasma urate?
*= By blocking the conversion of purines to uric acid
Why: Correct. It inhibits xanthine oxidase, so hypoxanthine and xanthine are excreted instead of being oxidised to the far less soluble uric acid.
* By increasing urate excretion in the urine
Why: That is the action of a uricosuric agent, which works by a different route.
* By dissolving urate crystals already deposited in the joint
Why: Lowering the plasma level allows deposits to mobilise slowly; the drug does not dissolve them directly.
* By reducing the inflammatory response to crystals
Why: That is the action of colchicine and anti-inflammatory drugs.
Rationale: Block production or increase excretion — two strategies for one problem. Knowing which a drug uses predicts both its interactions and whom it suits.

### Why that molecule
Concept: CON-REN-B3AEE6F22A046A
Difficulty: Hard
Q: Allopurinol resembles hypoxanthine structurally. Why does that matter, and what second consequence follows?
*= Because the resemblance lets it occupy xanthine oxidase in place of the natural substrate — and because it also reacts with PRPP, it depletes the PRPP pool and reduces de-novo purine synthesis as well
Why: Correct. Structural similarity to hypoxanthine is what allows it to inhibit the enzyme. Separately, its reaction with PRPP lowers the pool available for de-novo synthesis, so urate production falls by a second route.
* Because the resemblance allows it to be incorporated into DNA in place of a purine
Why: It is not incorporated into nucleic acid; it acts on the enzyme and on the PRPP pool.
* Because it is converted to uric acid itself, saturating excretion
Why: That would raise rather than lower the urate load.
* The resemblance is incidental and has no bearing on its action
Why: Substrate similarity is precisely the basis of the inhibition.
Rationale: A drug shaped like a substrate inhibits the enzyme that handles it — the general principle behind many antimetabolites. The PRPP effect is a second, less obvious route to the same end, and it is why the drug lowers production more than enzyme inhibition alone would predict.
## debrief
Gout has two clocks: the attack that develops over hours and the urate load that accumulates over years. They need different treatments given at different times, and starting the urate-lowering drug during an attack is the classic error. Allopurinol works because it looks like hypoxanthine — which both inhibits the oxidase and, through PRPP, reduces synthesis upstream.
