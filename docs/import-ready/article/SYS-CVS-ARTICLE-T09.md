## summary
Cheap, immediate and available everywhere — and it records only a few seconds. Most ECG errors are errors about time: reading one trace as though it described a whole illness.
## sections
### Principle
The electrocardiogram is a body-surface recording of the electrical potentials generated by the heart, displayed as voltage against time across twelve derived views.

Its physical basis — leads as viewpoints, deflections as projections of a cardiac vector — is established in the cardiac electrical activity article. This article covers its use as an investigation: what it can answer, what it cannot, and how it fails.
### Purpose and indications
Chest pain; palpitation; syncope or presyncope; breathlessness; suspected arrhythmia; suspected electrolyte disturbance; suspected drug toxicity; preoperative assessment in defined circumstances; and baseline assessment in hypertension, heart failure and valve disease.

In suspected acute coronary syndrome it is recorded within minutes of first contact, because the finding it may show determines immediate treatment.
### How it is performed
Ten electrodes — one on each limb, six on the chest — generate twelve leads. Correct limb electrode placement matters: reversal produces changes that mimic pathology, most recognisably a globally inverted lead I.

Chest electrode positions are anatomical, and misplacement alters R wave progression and can imitate or conceal infarction. Additional leads are recorded for specific questions: right-sided leads where inferior infarction raises the possibility of right ventricular involvement, and posterior leads where posterior infarction is suspected.

Standard calibration and paper speed are used, and both are checked before measuring intervals, since either being non-standard changes every derived value.
### Normal findings
Sinus rhythm at a normal rate, with a P wave preceding every QRS complex and a constant PR interval. A narrow QRS complex, a frontal axis within the normal range, orderly R wave progression across the chest leads, an isoelectric ST segment and upright T waves in most leads, and a rate-corrected QT interval within normal limits.

Normal variants are common and are frequently over-called: sinus arrhythmia in the young, isolated T-wave inversion in leads III and V1, early repolarisation, and the voltage criteria for hypertrophy met by thin young men without disease.
### Abnormal findings and interpretation
A systematic sequence prevents the commonest failure, which is fixating on an obvious abnormality and missing the rest: rate; rhythm; axis; the P wave; the PR interval; the QRS complex — width, voltage, Q waves and R wave progression; the ST segment; the T wave; and the QT interval.

Two limitations govern how any finding is treated.

**The trace records a few seconds.** Intermittent arrhythmia and evolving ischaemia are invisible between episodes, so a normal trace in a patient with intermittent symptoms is the expected finding rather than a reassuring one. Serial recording, continuous monitoring or extended ambulatory recording extend the observation window.

**Many abnormalities are chronic.** Bundle branch block, Q waves and T-wave inversion may be long-standing, and what matters is whether they are new. **A previous trace is one of the most valuable investigations available**, and obtaining one changes the interpretation more often than any additional test.
### Limitations and pitfalls
Low sensitivity for coronary disease at rest: a normal ECG does not exclude stable angina or an acute coronary syndrome.

Non-specific ST and T-wave changes are common and frequently over-interpreted.

It reports electrical activity only, so it cannot confirm that contraction or output is occurring — the reason a monitor showing organised complexes never substitutes for feeling a pulse.

Computer-generated interpretations are an aid and are frequently wrong, particularly for rhythm; they are checked rather than accepted.
### Contraindications
None. The investigation is non-invasive and carries no physical risk.

The hazards are interpretive: false reassurance from a normal trace, and unnecessary downstream investigation prompted by an over-read non-specific abnormality.

## hold_these
The ECG records a few seconds, so a normal trace between episodes excludes very little.
Obtaining a previous trace is itself an investigation, and often the most useful one.
The trace reports depolarisation only, and cannot confirm that the heart is producing output.
## lose_the_mark
Excluding an acute coronary syndrome or an arrhythmia on a single normal trace.
Acting on an abnormality without asking whether it is new.
Accepting the machine's interpretation, particularly of rhythm, without reading the trace.

## summary
The structural test for almost every cardiac question — and one whose numbers are calculated rather than measured. Knowing which figures are derived is what stops a report being over-read.
## sections
### Principle
Echocardiography uses ultrasound to image cardiac structure and to measure blood flow. Transthoracic imaging is performed through the chest wall; transoesophageal imaging places the probe in the oesophagus, immediately behind the heart.
### Purpose and indications
Suspected or known heart failure; murmurs and valve disease; suspected endocarditis; chest pain with suspected structural cause; suspected pericardial effusion or tamponade; assessment after myocardial infarction; cardiomyopathy; congenital heart disease; a source of embolism; and assessment before cardiac surgery or intervention.

Transoesophageal imaging is indicated for questions concerning posterior structures — the left atrium and its appendage, the mitral valve, the interatrial septum — for prosthetic valves, for suspected endocarditis where the transthoracic study is negative or inadequate, for aortic pathology, and to exclude atrial thrombus before cardioversion or intervention.
### How it is performed
Images are acquired through acoustic windows: parasternal, apical, subcostal and suprasternal. Ultrasound does not cross bone or air, so the ribs and the lungs constrain where the heart can be seen from.

Several modalities are combined. Two-dimensional imaging shows structure and motion; M-mode gives high temporal resolution along one line; Doppler measures the velocity of blood, displayed as colour flow or as spectral traces; and tissue Doppler and strain imaging assess myocardial motion and deformation.

Transoesophageal study requires fasting, topical anaesthesia and sedation, and carries a small risk of oesophageal injury.
### Normal findings
Chambers of normal dimension with normal wall thickness; uniform wall motion throughout; a normal ejection fraction; thin, freely mobile valve leaflets that coapt without a significant gradient or more than trivial regurgitation; a normal pattern of diastolic filling; no pericardial fluid beyond a physiological trace; and an inferior vena cava that collapses normally with inspiration.

Trivial regurgitation across the tricuspid, pulmonary and mitral valves is a normal finding and does not indicate disease.
### Abnormal findings and interpretation
Echocardiography answers structural and functional questions: chamber dimensions, wall thickness, systolic function and ejection fraction, regional wall motion, valve morphology and severity of stenosis or regurgitation, diastolic function and estimated filling pressures, pericardial fluid and tamponade physiology, estimated pulmonary artery pressure, and intracardiac masses or thrombus.

Two properties of the numbers matter more than the numbers themselves.

**Gradients are derived, not measured.** Doppler records velocity, and pressure gradient is calculated from it through a relationship in which velocity is squared. Small errors in velocity therefore produce large errors in gradient, and velocity is underestimated whenever the ultrasound beam is not aligned with the direction of flow. An under-aligned study understates severity, which matters most in exactly the lesions where severity determines intervention.

**The study is window-dependent and operator-dependent.** A poor acoustic window limits what can be excluded, and a negative study in a poor window is not a negative result. Reports state image quality for this reason, and that line should be read.
### Limitations and pitfalls
Poor windows in obesity, chronic lung disease and chest deformity. Posterior structures and prosthetic valves are seen poorly from the front. Estimated pulmonary pressures depend on assumptions and on an adequate regurgitant signal. Right ventricular volumes are poorly assessed, which is why cardiac MRI is preferred where they drive decisions.

Echocardiography does not visualise the coronary arteries.
### Contraindications
Transthoracic study has no contraindications and no physical risk.

Transoesophageal study is contraindicated where the oesophagus cannot safely be intubated — known stricture, recent oesophageal or gastric surgery, active upper gastrointestinal bleeding, or unstable cervical spine injury — and requires fasting, consent and sedation. It carries a small risk of oesophageal trauma and the risks of sedation.

## hold_these
Doppler measures velocity; gradients are calculated from it and are sensitive to beam alignment.
A poor acoustic window limits what a negative study can exclude.
The transoesophageal probe is used for posterior structures because the oesophagus lies behind the left atrium.
## lose_the_mark
Treating a reported gradient as a direct measurement.
Excluding endocarditis or a posterior structural abnormality on a transthoracic study alone.
Ignoring the image quality statement in the report.

## summary
Cardiac-specific does not mean coronary-specific. Troponin tells you a myocyte was injured; every clinically useful thing after that comes from the pattern over time and the context.
## sections
### Principle
Cardiac biomarkers are circulating molecules released by the heart in response to injury or to wall stress, used to detect and quantify cardiac pathology.

The two in routine use are **cardiac troponin**, released on myocyte injury, and the **natriuretic peptides**, released in response to wall stretch.
### Purpose and indications
**Troponin**: suspected acute coronary syndrome; assessment of myocardial injury in other acute illness; and prognostic assessment in defined circumstances such as pulmonary embolism.

**Natriuretic peptides**: diagnosis of heart failure in a breathless patient where the clinical picture is uncertain, and prognostic assessment in established heart failure.
### How it is performed
A venous sample, with the timing determined by the assay in use. Contemporary high-sensitivity troponin assays have validated protocols specifying when the first and subsequent samples are taken, and the interpretation depends on following the protocol for that assay — values from different assays are not interchangeable.
### Normal findings
Troponin below the upper reference limit for that assay, with no significant change between serial samples. Reference limits are assay-specific and are often sex-specific.

Natriuretic peptide below the exclusion threshold for the clinical setting, which differs between acute and non-acute presentations. High-sensitivity assays detect troponin in most healthy people, so a detectable value is not itself abnormal — what matters is whether it exceeds the reference limit and whether it is changing.
### Abnormal findings and interpretation
**Troponin.**

The molecule is specific to cardiac myocytes but not to any mechanism of injuring them. Sepsis, tachyarrhythmia, pulmonary embolism, myocarditis, heart failure, renal impairment, and severe hypertension all raise it. **Cardiac-specific means anatomically specific, not coronary-specific**, and that distinction is where most misinterpretation begins.

The distinction that matters clinically is between **myocardial injury** — a raised troponin from any cause — and **myocardial infarction**, which requires that rise together with evidence of ischaemia. And within infarction, between a **type 1** event from plaque disruption and a **type 2** event from supply–demand mismatch, because their treatment differs entirely.

**The pattern over time is the diagnostic feature, not the single value.** A rise and fall indicates acute injury; a stable elevation suggests a chronic cause such as renal impairment or structural heart disease.

High-sensitivity assays detect smaller quantities of injury. That improves early rule-out, which is their main clinical value, and simultaneously means more results are abnormal and a smaller proportion of those abnormalities are infarcts.

**Natriuretic peptides.**

Most useful for their negative predictive value: a low value in an untreated breathless patient makes heart failure unlikely. A raised value has many other causes — age, renal impairment, atrial fibrillation, pulmonary embolism, sepsis — and obesity lowers values, which can produce a falsely reassuring result.
### Limitations and pitfalls
Troponin cannot distinguish the mechanism of injury and cannot be interpreted without the clinical context and the ECG. It rises only after necrosis has begun, so an early value does not exclude an evolving infarct — which is why treatment of ST-elevation infarction never waits for it.

Natriuretic peptide thresholds differ between assays and between acute and non-acute presentations, and are modified by age, renal function and body habitus.

Older markers such as creatine kinase-MB have been superseded for diagnosis by troponin.
### Contraindications
None beyond those of venepuncture.

The risk is interpretive: a raised troponin attributed to a coronary cause without evidence of ischaemia leads to anticoagulation and catheterisation in a patient whose problem lies elsewhere.

## hold_these
Cardiac-specific means the tissue, not the mechanism — troponin reports injury of any cause.
The rise and fall over time is the diagnostic feature, not a single value.
Natriuretic peptides are most useful for ruling heart failure out.
## lose_the_mark
Diagnosing infarction from one raised troponin without evidence of ischaemia.
Waiting for troponin before reperfusing an ST-elevation infarct.
Comparing values from different assays as though they were equivalent.

## summary
A test that only means something once you have decided how likely the diagnosis was. Order it in the wrong patient and a positive result is more likely to be false than true.
## sections
### Principle
Stress testing provokes a supply–demand mismatch in order to expose coronary disease that is silent at rest, and detects the resulting ischaemia by ECG change, by wall motion abnormality, or by a perfusion defect.

Stress may be induced by **exercise**, which is physiological and yields functional information, or **pharmacologically**, where the patient cannot exercise.
### Purpose and indications
Assessment of chest pain of intermediate pre-test probability; assessment of the functional significance of a known stenosis; risk stratification after an acute coronary syndrome; assessment of exercise capacity and symptoms; and evaluation of exercise-induced arrhythmia.

**Pre-test probability determines whether the test is worth doing.** In a patient at very low probability a positive result is more likely to be false than true; in a patient at very high probability a negative result will not change management. The test is most informative in the intermediate range, which is where it belongs.
### How it is performed
**Exercise**, on a treadmill or bicycle, with continuous ECG, blood pressure and symptom monitoring, following a standardised protocol to a target workload or a defined endpoint.

**Pharmacological stress** where exercise is not possible: agents that increase demand simulate exercise, and vasodilator agents create heterogeneity of flow by dilating normal vessels more than diseased ones. The two act differently and the choice depends on the imaging modality and the patient.

**Imaging** may be added — stress echocardiography or myocardial perfusion imaging — which raises accuracy over ECG alone and localises the affected territory.

Contraindications and stopping criteria are defined in the guideline in force and are followed rather than judged individually.
### Normal findings
Achievement of the target workload without chest pain, without ischaemic ST-segment change, and with an appropriate rise in heart rate and blood pressure. On imaging, uniform perfusion and normal wall motion at rest and at peak stress.

A normal result carries a good prognosis, and that reassurance is strongest when a high workload was achieved. A test terminated early for non-cardiac reasons is not a normal test — it is an incomplete one, and the report states the workload for that reason.
### Abnormal findings and interpretation
Several independent pieces of information come from one test, and reading only the ST segments discards most of them.

**ST-segment change** during or after stress suggests ischaemia. Exercise ECG alone has modest sensitivity and specificity, which is why imaging is added where accuracy matters.

**Symptoms** reproduced during the test, and the workload at which they appear.

**Exercise capacity**, which carries prognostic weight independent of whether ischaemia is demonstrated. A patient who exercises well has a good prognosis even with some ischaemia; one who cannot has a poor prognosis even with a negative test.

**Haemodynamic response**: a failure of blood pressure to rise, or a fall, is an important abnormal finding.

**Imaging findings**: the territory involved, and the extent of the ischaemic burden, which is what informs the decision about revascularisation.

**A negative test excludes flow-limiting disease under the conditions achieved.** A submaximal test excludes very little, and the report states the workload reached for that reason.
### Limitations and pitfalls
Exercise ECG is uninterpretable where the resting trace is abnormal — left bundle branch block, paced rhythm, pre-excitation, significant resting ST change — and imaging is required instead.

Many patients cannot exercise adequately because of musculoskeletal, respiratory or vascular limitation.

False positives are more frequent in women and in the presence of certain drugs and electrolyte abnormalities.

A functional test reports whether a lesion limits flow, not whether plaque is present. It does not exclude non-obstructive atherosclerosis, and it is that plaque which may rupture.
### Contraindications
Absolute and relative contraindications are defined in the guideline in force and are followed rather than judged individually. They include acute coronary syndrome, decompensated heart failure, uncontrolled arrhythmia, severe symptomatic aortic stenosis, acute aortic dissection and acute myocarditis or pericarditis.

Pharmacological agents carry their own contraindications, including bronchospasm with some vasodilators. Exercise testing carries a small risk of arrhythmia and infarction, which is why it is supervised with resuscitation facilities available.

## hold_these
The test is most informative at intermediate pre-test probability; at the extremes it changes nothing.
Exercise capacity is prognostically informative independent of the ischaemia result.
A negative test excludes flow-limiting disease under the conditions achieved, not coronary atherosclerosis.
## lose_the_mark
Ordering the test in a very low- or very high-probability patient and treating the result as decisive.
Reading only the ST segments and discarding the capacity and haemodynamic data.
Performing an exercise ECG where the resting trace makes ST analysis uninterpretable.

## summary
Two interventions doing two different jobs. Compressions keep the myocardium alive and responsive; the shock restores the rhythm — and every pause in compressions makes the shock less likely to work.
## sections
### Principle
Cardiopulmonary resuscitation is the combination of chest compressions and ventilation used to maintain circulation and oxygenation during cardiac arrest, until an organised rhythm and spontaneous circulation can be restored.

**The full resuscitation algorithm — the sequence, timings, compression parameters, ventilation, drugs and energy levels — belongs to SYS-MUL and to the resuscitation guideline in force.** This article covers the cardiovascular principles that make the algorithm intelligible; it does not reproduce it, and no parameter should be taken from memory when the current guideline is available.
### Purpose and indications
Cardiac arrest: an unresponsive patient who is not breathing normally and has no pulse.

Recognition is clinical and immediate. **Agonal gasping is common in the first minutes and is frequently mistaken for breathing**, which delays recognition and costs perfusion time.
### How it is performed
Chest compressions are delivered over the lower half of the sternum, compressing the heart between the sternum and the vertebral column — the anatomical relationship that makes external compression possible at all.

Ventilation is provided alongside compressions, and defibrillation is delivered as soon as a shockable rhythm is identified.

Rate, depth, compression-to-ventilation ratio, recoil, hand position and energy levels are all specified in the resuscitation guideline in force and are followed as written.
### Normal findings
Effective resuscitation is judged by physiological signs rather than by appearance: a palpable pulse with compressions, and where monitoring is available an end-tidal carbon dioxide value consistent with adequate pulmonary blood flow.

Return of spontaneous circulation is recognised by a sustained palpable pulse, a rise in end-tidal carbon dioxide, and an organised rhythm on the monitor. A monitor showing organised complexes without a pulse is not a return of circulation.
### Abnormal findings and interpretation
Understanding two mechanisms explains most of what the algorithm asks for.

**Compressions maintain circulation.** They generate a modest but essential cardiac output, perfusing brain and myocardium. Coronary perfusion pressure builds over successive compressions and **falls immediately when they stop**, taking several compressions to rebuild. This is why interruptions are minimised and why the pause around a shock is kept as short as possible.

**Defibrillation restores rhythm.** A shock depolarises the myocardium simultaneously, terminating the disorganised activity and allowing an intrinsic pacemaker to resume. It does not start a heart; it stops a chaotic rhythm so that an organised one can return — which is why it works in ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia, and not in asystole.

The two are complementary. Compressions preserve a myocardium capable of responding to a shock; the shock provides the rhythm that compressions cannot.

**Rhythms are divided into shockable and non-shockable**, because that single classification determines the most important treatment decision. Reversible causes are sought and corrected throughout rather than after.
### Limitations and pitfalls
Compressions generate only a fraction of normal cardiac output. Survival falls steeply with time from arrest to defibrillation, so the quality and continuity of compressions and the speed of defibrillation dominate outcome.

Resuscitation is not appropriate in every circumstance, and decisions about attempting it — including advance decisions and treatment escalation planning — are made in accordance with local law and policy.
### Contraindications
Resuscitation is not attempted where a valid advance decision refusing it exists, or where death is established.

Decisions about attempting resuscitation, and treatment escalation planning, are made in accordance with local law and policy, and this is a matter for the treating team and the patient rather than for a textbook rule.

Rib and sternal fractures are common and are an accepted consequence of effective compressions rather than a reason to compress less firmly.

## hold_these
Compressions maintain perfusion; defibrillation restores rhythm. Neither substitutes for the other.
Coronary perfusion pressure falls immediately when compressions stop and takes several compressions to rebuild.
Agonal gasping is not breathing and does not exclude cardiac arrest.
## lose_the_mark
Delaying recognition because the patient appears to be making respiratory effort.
Interrupting compressions for procedures that could wait.

## summary
The same machine doing two different things. Whether the shock is timed to the QRS depends on whether there is a QRS to time it to — and getting that wrong can create the rhythm you were trying to treat.
## sections
### Principle
Delivery of an electrical current across the heart to terminate an arrhythmia by depolarising the myocardium simultaneously, allowing an organised rhythm to resume.

**Cardioversion** is synchronised to the R wave and is used for organised arrhythmias. **Defibrillation** is unsynchronised and is used for ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia.
### Purpose and indications
**Emergency, unsynchronised**: ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia.

**Emergency, synchronised**: an organised tachyarrhythmia with haemodynamic compromise — shock, syncope, ischaemia or heart failure attributable to the rhythm.

**Elective, synchronised**: restoration of sinus rhythm in atrial fibrillation or flutter, and in stable supraventricular or ventricular tachycardia where drug therapy has failed or is inappropriate.
### How it is performed
Pads are positioned to direct current across the myocardium, and the synchronisation mode is selected deliberately.

Elective cardioversion requires fasting, sedation or anaesthesia, and — for atrial fibrillation beyond a defined short duration — either a period of anticoagulation beforehand or exclusion of atrial thrombus by transoesophageal echocardiography, because restoring atrial contraction can dislodge thrombus that has formed during the arrhythmia. Anticoagulation continues afterwards.

Energy levels, waveform, pad position and sedation follow the guideline in force; none is reproduced here.
### Normal findings
A successful procedure restores an organised rhythm with a pulse, and in elective cardioversion for atrial fibrillation, sinus rhythm with visible P waves.

Immediately after a shock, brief periods of bradycardia, junctional rhythm or a short pause are common and usually self-terminating as the sinus node recovers. Transient ST-segment change and a small troponin rise may follow and do not by themselves indicate myocardial injury of clinical consequence.
### Abnormal findings and interpretation
**Why synchronisation matters.** During repolarisation the myocardium is electrically heterogeneous — some regions recovered, some not. A shock delivered in that window, on the T wave, can initiate ventricular fibrillation. Synchronising to the R wave places the shock safely away from it.

**Why defibrillation is unsynchronised.** In ventricular fibrillation there is no organised QRS complex to synchronise to. A device left in synchronised mode will search for an R wave, fail to find one, and **not discharge** — a delay that costs perfusion time in the situation where time matters most.

Success is judged by restoration of an organised rhythm with a pulse, and failure prompts reassessment of the rhythm, the pad position, the energy and any reversible contributor.
### Limitations and pitfalls
Cardioversion terminates an arrhythmia; it does not prevent recurrence, which is why the underlying substrate and any precipitant are addressed.

It is unlikely to succeed, or to be sustained, where a precipitant persists — electrolyte disturbance, thyrotoxicosis, sepsis, ischaemia — or where the atria are markedly dilated and the arrhythmia long-standing.

Restoring sinus rhythm does not remove the need for anticoagulation, because recurrence is frequently silent.
### Contraindications
Elective cardioversion is deferred where anticoagulation requirements for the duration of the arrhythmia have not been met and atrial thrombus has not been excluded, and where digoxin toxicity or significant electrolyte disturbance is present.

There are no contraindications to emergency defibrillation in a patient in cardiac arrest.

Complications include induced arrhythmia from an inappropriately timed shock, thromboembolism, skin burns, myocardial injury with repeated high-energy shocks, and the risks of sedation.

## hold_these
Cardioversion is synchronised to the R wave; defibrillation is unsynchronised because there is no R wave to find.
A device left in synchronised mode will not discharge in ventricular fibrillation.
Restoring sinus rhythm does not remove the need for anticoagulation.
## lose_the_mark
Attempting synchronised cardioversion in ventricular fibrillation.
Cardioverting atrial fibrillation of uncertain duration without addressing the embolic risk.
Treating the arrhythmia and leaving the precipitant uncorrected.

## summary
A device with two jobs — deliver a stimulus that works, and stay out of the way when the heart manages on its own. Nearly every malfunction is a failure of one or the other.
## sections
### Principle
Cardiac pacing delivers electrical stimuli to initiate myocardial depolarisation where the heart's own impulse formation or conduction is inadequate.

Pacing may be **temporary** — transcutaneous, or transvenous — or **permanent**, by an implanted device.
### Purpose and indications
Symptomatic bradycardia from sinus node disease or atrioventricular block; block at a level whose escape rhythm cannot be relied upon, even without symptoms; and bradycardia complicating other treatment where that treatment must continue.

Related device therapies extend beyond bradycardia: cardiac resynchronisation for selected patients with heart failure and conduction delay, and implantable defibrillators for arrhythmic risk. Both are considered against the criteria in the guideline in force.

**Reversible causes are excluded before committing a patient to a permanent device** — drugs, electrolyte disturbance, ischaemia and hypothyroidism among them.
### How it is performed
**Transcutaneous pacing** through external pads is immediate and is a bridging measure. It is uncomfortable and requires analgesia and sedation in a conscious patient.

**Transvenous pacing** places a lead through a central vein into the right ventricle, and sometimes the right atrium.

**Permanent pacing** implants a generator subcutaneously with leads placed transvenously. Devices are described by a standard code specifying the chamber paced, the chamber sensed and the response to sensing.

Thresholds are tested at implantation and at follow-up, and device settings are adjusted at each review.
### Normal findings
On a paced trace, each pacing spike is followed immediately by a paced complex — capture — and no spike appears when the patient's own rhythm is adequate, because those beats are sensed and pacing is inhibited.

Ventricular paced complexes are broad, since activation begins outside the conducting system and spreads through working myocardium. A normally functioning device may show no spikes at all in a patient whose intrinsic rate exceeds the programmed rate; absence of pacing is not absence of function.
### Abnormal findings and interpretation
Two functions must both work, and understanding them explains almost every malfunction.

**Capture** is the delivery of a stimulus sufficient to depolarise the myocardium. On the trace it appears as a pacing spike followed by a paced complex. **Failure to capture** shows spikes with no complex following, and its causes include lead displacement, a rising threshold, electrolyte disturbance, ischaemia at the lead tip, and battery depletion.

**Sensing** is detection of the heart's own beats, so that the device does not pace when it need not. **Failure to sense** shows pacing spikes falling inappropriately soon after a patient's own complexes, which risks a stimulus landing on the T wave. **Oversensing** is the opposite — the device mistakes other signals for cardiac activity and withholds pacing when it is needed, which in a pacing-dependent patient causes symptoms.

Distinguishing these three patterns is the whole of basic pacemaker trace interpretation, and calling any of them "pacemaker failure" loses the information the trace is offering.
### Limitations and pitfalls
Temporary transvenous pacing carries the risks of central venous access and lead displacement, and is a bridge rather than a solution.

Right ventricular pacing produces a non-physiological activation sequence and, when a high proportion of beats are paced, can impair ventricular function over time — one reason device programming aims to minimise unnecessary ventricular pacing.

Devices require lifelong follow-up, generator replacement, and precautions around certain electromagnetic environments and imaging.
### Contraindications
Permanent implantation is deferred where the bradycardia has a reversible cause not yet corrected, and where active infection is present, since a device implanted into a bacteraemic patient becomes infected.

Complications at implantation include pneumothorax, cardiac perforation and tamponade, lead displacement, haematoma and infection. Later complications include device infection, which usually requires complete system removal, lead fracture, venous stenosis, and pacing-induced ventricular dysfunction.

## hold_these
Capture and sensing are separate functions, and each fails in its own recognisable way.
Exclude reversible causes of bradycardia before implanting a permanent device.
Pacing for sinus node disease relieves symptoms; it has not been shown to prolong life.
## lose_the_mark
Describing every pacing abnormality as "pacemaker failure" rather than identifying capture or sensing failure.
Pacing a patient whose bradycardia is drug-induced without first stopping the drug.
Overlooking device infection as the explanation for an unwell patient with an implanted system.

## summary
A silhouette of the lumen, not a picture of the artery. Plaque that grew outwards is invisible on it — which is why "normal coronary arteries" and "no coronary disease" are not the same statement.
## sections
### Principle
Cardiac catheterisation is the introduction of catheters into the heart and great vessels to measure pressures and oxygen saturations and to inject contrast. Coronary angiography is the imaging of the coronary arteries by contrast injection under fluoroscopy.

The same access allows therapeutic intervention: percutaneous coronary intervention, valve intervention and structural procedures.
### Purpose and indications
**Diagnostic**: acute coronary syndromes where an invasive strategy is planned; stable angina where revascularisation is being considered or non-invasive testing is inconclusive; assessment before valve or structural surgery; investigation of cardiomyopathy of uncertain cause; and haemodynamic assessment of valve disease, shunts and pulmonary hypertension where non-invasive data are inadequate.

**Therapeutic**: percutaneous coronary intervention, valvuloplasty and transcatheter valve implantation, device closure of septal defects, and ablation.

The decision to catheterise should follow from the question being asked and from whether the answer would change management.
### How it is performed
Arterial access is obtained, most often radial, which carries lower bleeding and access-site complication rates than femoral access.

Catheters are advanced under fluoroscopic guidance and contrast is injected into each coronary ostium, with images acquired in multiple projections because a stenosis may be foreshortened or hidden in any single view.

Right heart catheterisation, where indicated, measures right-sided and pulmonary pressures and cardiac output.

Where the physiological significance of an intermediate stenosis is uncertain, pressure-wire measurement across the lesion assesses whether it actually limits flow.
### Normal findings
Smooth, evenly tapering coronary arteries filling promptly with contrast, without luminal irregularity, filling defect or delay. Normal chamber pressures and oxygen saturations where measured, with no step-up to indicate a shunt.

A normal angiogram excludes obstructive coronary disease. It does not exclude atherosclerosis, coronary spasm or microvascular disease, and reporting it as "normal coronary arteries" is a statement about the lumen only.
### Abnormal findings and interpretation
**Angiography is a luminogram.** It shows the silhouette of the contrast-filled lumen and nothing about the vessel wall.

Atherosclerotic plaque frequently expands outwards before it encroaches inwards, so a vessel may contain substantial plaque with a preserved lumen and a normal-looking angiogram. **A normal angiogram excludes obstructive coronary disease, not coronary atherosclerosis** — and the plaques that rupture are frequently those producing modest narrowing.

The angiographic severity of a stenosis corresponds only loosely to its physiological significance, which is why pressure-wire assessment exists. Anatomical severity and flow limitation are different measurements, and treating a lesion because it looks tight is a recognised source of unnecessary intervention.

Intravascular imaging visualises the wall directly where plaque characterisation or stent optimisation is needed.
### Limitations and pitfalls
It does not image the vessel wall, and it does not assess the microcirculation — so a patient with ischaemia and unobstructed arteries has a diagnosis still to be made rather than an absence of one.

It is invasive, requires contrast and ionising radiation, and provides an anatomical rather than a functional answer unless physiological assessment is added.
### Contraindications
Relative contraindications include uncorrected severe coagulopathy, active infection, decompensated heart failure that could be stabilised first, severe uncorrected electrolyte disturbance, and contrast hypersensitivity or severe renal impairment where the risk is not justified by the expected benefit.

Complications: access-site bleeding, haematoma, pseudoaneurysm and limb ischaemia; contrast-induced kidney injury and hypersensitivity; cumulative radiation exposure to patient and operator; and the cardiac risks of arrhythmia, coronary dissection or perforation, stroke and infarction.

## hold_these
Angiography shows the lumen; outward-remodelled plaque is invisible on it.
Anatomical severity corresponds only loosely to physiological significance.
Unobstructed coronary arteries with chest pain and a raised troponin require an explanation.
## lose_the_mark
Telling a patient with a normal angiogram that they have no coronary disease.
Treating an intermediate stenosis because it looks tight, without physiological assessment.
Requesting the procedure where the result would not change management, and accepting its contrast and radiation cost for nothing.
