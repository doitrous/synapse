## summary
A working set of cell-biology facts the Foundation module examines repeatedly: where cardiolipin sits in the mitochondrion, what autophagy actually degrades, what the nucleosome's core is made of, where the restriction checkpoint sits in the cell cycle, how the stages of mitosis are told apart, and where crossing-over happens in meiosis. Each is a small, precise fact that a vaguer, 'organelles in general' revision pass will not catch.

## sections
### Definition
The Foundation-module Cell chapter treats the cell as a system of organelles with specific, testable locations and jobs (the mitochondrion, the lysosome-dependent autophagy pathway, the nucleus and its chromatin), and a cell cycle divided into checkpoints and stages each defined by a distinct, nameable event. Mitosis produces two genetically identical daughter cells; meiosis produces four genetically distinct haploid gametes, and the two processes are examined for exactly where they differ.

### Mechanism
Cardiolipin is a phospholipid unique to mitochondria, concentrated in the inner membrane where it stabilises the respiratory-chain complexes; the smooth outer membrane, by contrast, is defined by its porins.

Autophagy is the lysosome-dependent pathway by which a cell encloses its own worn-out or damaged organelles in a membrane-bound vacuole and digests them, recycling the components — distinct from phagocytosis, which brings in material from outside the cell.

The nucleosome packages chromatin around a central core of eight histone molecules (two each of H2A, H2B, H3 and H4), with roughly two turns of DNA wound around that octamer and linker DNA running to the next nucleosome.

The restriction checkpoint is the G1 'go/no-go' decision point: once a cell passes it, having checked its size, nutrient supply and DNA integrity, it is committed to complete the cycle even without further external growth signals.

Mitosis proceeds through stages each defined by one observable event — prophase by chromosome condensation and nuclear-envelope breakdown, metaphase by alignment on the equatorial plate, anaphase by centromere splitting and sister-chromatid separation, telophase by decondensation, nuclear-envelope reformation and cytokinesis.

In meiosis, crossing-over — the exchange of segments between non-sister chromatids of paired (synapsed) homologous chromosomes — takes place in prophase I, generating genetic recombination before the first meiotic division separates the homologues.

### Key determinants
What distinguishes each fact from its nearest confusable neighbour is the point worth holding: cardiolipin (inner membrane) against the outer membrane's porins; autophagy (the cell's own organelles) against phagocytosis (material from outside); the nucleosome's histone core against the DNA wound around it; the restriction checkpoint's G1 location against the G2/M and spindle-assembly checkpoints; each mitotic stage's single defining event against the others; and meiotic prophase I's crossing-over against the equational, non-recombining division of meiosis II.

### Clinical significance
These are not merely descriptive facts. A cell's checkpoint controls are what fail in unregulated proliferation; autophagy failure contributes to the accumulation of damaged organelles seen in some degenerative processes; and the fidelity of crossing-over and chromosome segregation in meiosis is what a karyotype abnormality or a numerical chromosomal disorder ultimately traces back to.

## hold_these
Cardiolipin marks the inner mitochondrial membrane, not the outer one.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing autophagy (the cell digesting its own organelles) with phagocytosis (material taken in from outside).
Placing the restriction checkpoint in S phase or at G2/M instead of G1.
Naming the wrong mitotic stage for a diagrammed event by focusing on 'is the chromosome condensed' rather than the specific event shown (envelope breakdown vs centromere division vs cytokinesis).

## summary
Two facts the Foundation exam tests at named anatomical sites rather than as general classification: the specialised sensory neuroepithelium lining the organ of Corti, and the undifferentiated mesenchymal cells that dominate the pulp of a growing tooth. Both are the kind of site-specific detail a generic 'types of epithelium' or 'types of connective tissue cells' revision pass skips past.

## sections
### Definition
Epithelium and connective tissue are usually taught as general classification systems (simple vs stratified; fixed vs free cells). This article covers two named exceptions the Foundation papers test directly: what specifically lines the organ of Corti, and what specifically fills the pulp of a growing tooth.

### Mechanism
The organ of Corti, the cochlea's hearing receptor, is lined by neuroepithelium — a specialised sensory epithelium whose hair cells are secondary sensory receptors in synaptic contact with afferent nerve fibres, the same epithelial category as the vestibular maculae and cristae. It is not a conventional glandular or squamous epithelium.

The pulp of a growing tooth, unlike mature loose connective tissue, is dominated by undifferentiated mesenchymal cells — cells that retain the capacity to differentiate (most notably into odontoblasts) as the tooth continues to form.

### Key determinants
What separates 'neuroepithelium' from an ordinary sensory-adjacent epithelium is its synaptic function, not merely its location near a nerve. What separates the growing pulp's undifferentiated mesenchymal cells from a mature connective tissue's fibroblast population is the tissue's developmental stage — the pulp is still actively forming the tooth, not yet settled into mature connective tissue.

### Clinical significance
Recognising neuroepithelium at a named site (organ of Corti, vestibular sense organs, olfactory epithelium) is the histological basis for understanding sensorineural hearing and balance pathology. Recognising the pulp's mesenchymal reserve is the histological basis for the tooth's continued growth and repair capacity before it is fully formed.

## summary
Four skin facts the Foundation module tests at the level of a specific stratum or a specific cell, not at the level of 'the skin has layers': which epidermal stratum actually builds the waterproof barrier, which cell is lost in vitiligo, how the sebaceous gland's germinal layer looks under the microscope, and what makes eccrine sweat gland secretion distinct from apocrine secretion.

## sections
### Definition
The epidermis is organised into strata (basale, spinosum, granulosum, lucidum in thick skin, and corneum), each with a distinct role; its resident cell population includes keratinocytes, melanocytes, Langerhans cells and Merkel cells; and its appendages include sebaceous and eccrine (sweat) glands, each with its own secretory mechanism.

### Mechanism
The waterproof barrier of the skin is built by an upper epidermal stratum, where keratohyalin-derived, lipid-rich content is released between the cells to form an impermeable layer — the deeper strata are concerned with proliferation and structural support, not with waterproofing.

Vitiligo is loss of epidermal melanocytes: an acquired, typically autoimmune, destruction of the pigment-producing cell itself, leaving sharply demarcated, milky-white depigmented patches — not a defect of a cell that is still present but inactive.

A sebaceous gland's acinus renews itself from a peripheral, single layer of flattened germinal (basal) cells resting on the basement membrane; these divide and push inward, becoming the polyhedral, fat-filled cells at the centre of the acinus, which are eventually destroyed to release sebum (holocrine secretion).

Eccrine sweat glands secrete a thin, watery, electrolyte-rich fluid by a merocrine mechanism (the secretory cell stays intact) — distinct in both product and mechanism from the viscous, characteristically odorous secretion of apocrine glands.

### Key determinants
The barrier-forming stratum is defined by its granule content being extruded between cells, not simply by being the outermost dead layer. Vitiligo's white patches are a melanocyte-loss finding, distinguishing it from the other named epidermal cells (Merkel, Langerhans, keratinocyte). The sebaceous gland's germinal layer is defined by being flattened and not yet lipid-laden, in contrast to the differentiated cells it produces. Eccrine secretion's watery, electrolyte-rich, merocrine character is what separates it from apocrine secretion's viscosity, odour and cell-loss mechanism.

### Clinical significance
Loss of the epidermal barrier (whatever the cause) produces transepidermal water loss and increases infection risk; vitiligo is a common, visible, immune-mediated skin condition students are expected to link to melanocyte biology; sebaceous-gland and sweat-gland physiology underlie acne, hidradenitis and thermoregulatory sweating disorders respectively.

## published_sections

## hold_these
The waterproof barrier is built by a specific upper epidermal stratum's secreted lipid content, not simply by 'being on the outside'.
Vitiligo is melanocyte loss — the pigment cell itself is gone, not merely switched off.
The sebaceous gland's germinal cells are flattened and basal; the fat-filled cells sit more centrally.
Eccrine secretion is watery and merocrine; apocrine secretion is viscous, odorous and involves loss of apical cytoplasm.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming the thickest or most superficial dead layer is automatically the barrier-forming one.
Attributing vitiligo's depigmentation to Merkel cells, Langerhans cells or keratinocytes instead of melanocytes.
Describing the sebaceous gland's germinal cells as already fat-filled.
Attributing eccrine gland characteristics (viscosity, odour) that actually belong to the apocrine gland.
