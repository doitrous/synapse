## summary
The pacemaker fails and the same diseased atrium starts firing fast. Treating either half makes the other worse, which is why this syndrome needs both problems held in view at once.
## sections
### Definition
Sinus node disease is inappropriate sinus bradycardia, sinus pause or arrest, sinoatrial exit block, or chronotropic incompetence — failure of the rate to rise appropriately with exertion — caused by dysfunction of the sinus node and the surrounding atrium.

Where the same disease also generates atrial tachyarrhythmias, the combination is called tachycardia–bradycardia syndrome.
### Epidemiology
It is predominantly a disease of older people and a leading indication for permanent pacing worldwide. Incidence rises steeply with age.
### Aetiology and risk factors
**Intrinsic**: age-related fibrosis of the node and atrium, which is the commonest cause; ischaemia, since the sinus nodal artery arises from the right coronary artery in most hearts; infiltrative disease such as amyloidosis and sarcoidosis; myocarditis; cardiac surgery; and inherited channelopathies in the young.

**Extrinsic** causes are important because they are reversible: rate-slowing drugs, hypothyroidism, electrolyte disturbance, raised intracranial pressure, obstructive sleep apnoea, and high vagal tone — which in a trained athlete is physiological rather than diseased.
### Pathophysiology
Fibrosis and ion channel dysfunction slow spontaneous depolarisation in the nodal cells and impair the exit of the impulse into the atrium. The rate falls, pauses appear, and the rate fails to rise with demand.

The same diseased atrium provides the substrate for atrial fibrillation, flutter and atrial tachycardia — which is why slow and fast rhythms alternate in one patient.

That alternation creates a therapeutic conflict at the centre of this condition: drugs that control the tachycardia worsen the bradycardia, and there is often no drug regimen that manages both.
### Clinical picture
Symptoms arise from cerebral hypoperfusion during bradycardia or pauses: dizziness, presyncope, syncope, confusion in older patients, and falls. Chronotropic incompetence presents as exertional fatigue and breathlessness rather than as dizziness.

Palpitation accompanies the tachycardic phases. Many patients are asymptomatic and are identified incidentally.

Symptoms are typically intermittent, so an ECG taken between episodes is frequently normal — which is the central diagnostic difficulty.
### Investigation
The diagnosis requires correlation of symptoms with the rhythm at the time, and that is what most of the investigation is for.

A 12-lead ECG may show sinus bradycardia, pauses or exit block. Ambulatory monitoring over 24 hours or longer, patient-activated event recorders, and implantable loop recorders for infrequent symptoms all extend the recording window until an episode is captured.

Exercise testing assesses chronotropic competence. Bloods exclude reversible causes: thyroid function, electrolytes, and a review of the drug chart, which is the single highest-yield step. Echocardiography assesses structure. Electrophysiological study is occasionally used where non-invasive assessment is inconclusive.
### Management
The first step is to remove reversible causes. Stopping a contributing drug or correcting hypothyroidism resolves a proportion of cases entirely, and pacing a patient whose bradycardia was drug-induced commits them to a device they did not need.

Where symptoms are attributable to the bradycardia and no reversible cause is found, permanent pacing is the definitive treatment. Pacing relieves symptoms; it has not been shown to prolong life in this condition, which is a useful thing to be honest with patients about.

In tachycardia–bradycardia syndrome, pacing often has to be established before rate-controlling therapy for the tachycardia can be used safely — the pacemaker makes the drug possible rather than replacing it.

Anticoagulation follows the assessment of embolic risk where atrial fibrillation is present, decided on the same grounds as in atrial fibrillation generally.

Pacing indications, modes and thresholds follow the guideline in force; none is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Syncope and injury from falls, embolic stroke where atrial fibrillation coexists, and the complications of pacing where a device is implanted.

Prognosis is generally determined by comorbidity rather than by the sinus node disease itself, which is why the honest account of pacing is that it treats symptoms.
### Classification
The condition is described by its manifestations — bradycardia, pauses, exit block, chronotropic incompetence, and the tachycardia–bradycardia combination — rather than by a staging system, because each manifestation carries its own management implication.
## hold_these
Review the drug chart before diagnosing intrinsic sinus node disease; drug-induced bradycardia is common and reversible.
Symptoms must be correlated with the rhythm at the time — a normal ECG between episodes proves nothing.
Pacing relieves symptoms in this condition; it has not been shown to prolong life.
## lose_the_mark
Pacing a number rather than a symptom. Asymptomatic bradycardia in a fit person is usually normal.
Treating the tachycardic phase without recognising that the same drug will worsen the bradycardic phase.
Diagnosing sinus node disease in a trained athlete with a low resting rate and no symptoms.

## summary
Not how much of the conduction is failing, but where. A narrow escape at fifty and a broad escape at thirty are both called complete block, and only one of them is safe.
## sections
### Definition
Atrioventricular block is impaired conduction of the atrial impulse to the ventricles through the atrioventricular conduction system. It is graded by degree — first, second and third — and, more importantly for prognosis, by the anatomical level at which it occurs.

The definition of the block itself and its ECG appearances are established in the conduction system article; this article addresses the level, the causes and the management.
### Epidemiology
Acquired atrioventricular block is chiefly a disease of older people, from progressive fibrosis of the conduction system. It also complicates acute myocardial infarction — particularly inferior infarction, where the nodal arteries usually arise from the right coronary artery — and cardiac surgery.

Congenital complete heart block is rare and is associated with maternal autoantibodies.
### Aetiology and risk factors
**Degenerative** fibrosis of the conduction system is the commonest cause in older patients.

**Ischaemic**: inferior infarction typically causes block at nodal level, often transient and vagally mediated, with a reliable junctional escape. Anterior infarction causing block implies extensive septal necrosis involving the bundle branches, and carries a much worse prognosis.

**Drug-induced** block from rate-slowing agents is common and reversible, which is why the drug chart is reviewed first.

**Others**: infiltrative disease, particularly sarcoidosis and amyloidosis, where block may be the presenting feature in a young patient; myocarditis, including Lyme carditis and Chagas disease; endocarditis with aortic root abscess; cardiac surgery and catheter ablation; and hyperkalaemia.
### Pathophysiology
Conduction from atrium to ventricle passes through a single narrow route, so a small lesion at one point can interrupt it entirely.

**Nodal block** occurs within the atrioventricular node. Nodal tissue conducts decrementally, so block here is often progressive rather than sudden, is frequently vagally mediated and reversible, and leaves a junctional escape — relatively fast, narrow-complex and responsive to autonomic influence. It improves with exertion or atropine.

**Infranodal block** occurs in the bundle of His or the bundle branches. These tissues do not conduct decrementally, so conduction fails abruptly and unpredictably. The escape arises from ventricular tissue: slow, broad-complex, unreliable and unresponsive to atropine.

That distinction, rather than the numerical degree, drives management. A patient in complete block with a narrow escape at fifty is in a very different position from one with a broad escape in the thirties.
### Clinical picture
First-degree block is asymptomatic and found incidentally.

Second- and third-degree block cause symptoms from bradycardia and from pauses: fatigue, exertional breathlessness, dizziness, presyncope and syncope. Stokes–Adams attacks are abrupt syncopal episodes from transient ventricular standstill, with rapid recovery and characteristic flushing afterwards, and are a warning of unreliable escape.

Signs in complete block: bradycardia, a variable-intensity first heart sound because atrial and ventricular contraction bear no fixed relation, and intermittent cannon waves in the jugular venous pulse when the atrium contracts against a closed tricuspid valve.
### Investigation
A 12-lead ECG establishes the degree. The features that indicate the level are what the trace is then read for: the width of the QRS complex, the rate of the escape rhythm, and whether the block is preceded by progressive PR lengthening.

Ambulatory or inpatient monitoring is used where block is intermittent and symptoms have not been correlated with the rhythm.

Investigation of the cause: review of the drug chart, electrolytes including potassium, thyroid function, troponin and coronary assessment where ischaemia is suspected, echocardiography, and — in a young patient or where infiltration is suspected — cardiac MRI and screening for sarcoidosis, Lyme disease and Chagas disease according to exposure.

Electrophysiological study localises the block directly where the level remains uncertain and the decision depends on it.
### Management
Treat the reversible causes first: stop the contributing drug, correct hyperkalaemia, treat the ischaemia, treat the infection.

Symptomatic bradycardia is supported acutely by the measures in the resuscitation guideline in force, escalating to temporary pacing where the escape is unreliable or the patient is compromised.

Permanent pacing is the definitive treatment where block is symptomatic, or where the level is such that the escape cannot be relied on even if the patient is currently asymptomatic. This is where the level matters most: infranodal block is an indication for pacing on prognostic grounds, whereas nodal block in an asymptomatic patient often is not.

Block complicating inferior infarction is frequently transient and resolves with reperfusion. Block complicating anterior infarction usually reflects extensive necrosis and rarely resolves.

Every drug, dose, pacing indication and mode follows the guideline in force where the patient is treated; none is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Syncope and injury, ventricular standstill and sudden death where the escape fails, bradycardia-related heart failure, and pause-dependent polymorphic ventricular tachycardia where the QT is also prolonged.

Prognosis is excellent once appropriate pacing is established. Untreated infranodal block carries a substantial risk of sudden death, which is what justifies pacing before symptoms in that group.
### Classification
Grading by degree describes what the ECG shows. Classifying by level — nodal or infranodal — describes what will happen if conduction fails completely, and it is the classification that determines management.
## hold_these
The level of block matters more than its degree; infranodal block leaves an unreliable escape.
A narrow-complex escape at a reasonable rate indicates nodal block; a broad-complex escape in the thirties indicates infranodal block.
Block after inferior infarction is often transient; block after anterior infarction implies extensive septal necrosis.
## lose_the_mark
Grading block by degree alone and treating all complete block as equivalent.
Pacing before reviewing the drug chart and the potassium.
Reassuring a patient with asymptomatic infranodal block, where pacing may be indicated on prognostic grounds.

## summary
The commonest sustained arrhythmia, and the one where the symptom and the danger are unrelated. Whether the patient feels it has almost nothing to do with whether it will cause a stroke.
## sections
### Definition
Atrial fibrillation is a supraventricular tachyarrhythmia with uncoordinated atrial electrical activity and consequently ineffective atrial contraction. On the ECG there are no discrete, consistently shaped P waves, and the ventricular response is irregularly irregular.
### Epidemiology
Atrial fibrillation is the commonest sustained arrhythmia in clinical practice. Its prevalence rises steeply with age and it is a major cause of stroke, heart failure and hospital admission.

Locally, rheumatic mitral valve disease remains a more prominent cause than in Western populations, which shifts the age distribution downwards and changes the embolic risk assessment, since valvular atrial fibrillation is treated differently.
### Aetiology and risk factors
**Cardiac**: hypertension — the commonest association — ischaemic heart disease, heart failure, valvular disease and particularly mitral stenosis, cardiomyopathy, congenital heart disease, pericarditis and cardiac surgery.

**Non-cardiac**: thyrotoxicosis, alcohol, sepsis, pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, obstructive sleep apnoea, obesity, diabetes, chronic kidney disease and electrolyte disturbance.

Any acute illness can precipitate it, which is why a first episode prompts a search for the precipitant rather than an assumption of chronic disease.
### Pathophysiology
Atrial fibrillation requires a trigger and a substrate. The triggers are commonly ectopic foci in the muscular sleeves around the pulmonary vein ostia — which is the basis of ablation strategies. The substrate is an atrium made electrically heterogeneous by stretch, fibrosis and inflammation, able to sustain multiple simultaneous re-entrant wavelets.

Three consequences follow.

**No coordinated atrial contraction.** The atrial contribution to ventricular filling is lost, which matters little in a normal ventricle at rest and greatly in a stiff one or at speed.

**Irregular ventricular response.** Impulses bombard the atrioventricular node continually and unpredictably, and the node conducts only those finding it recovered. The node acts as an irregular filter, and the ventricular rhythm is the record of what it let through.

**Stasis and thrombus.** Blood stagnates in the left atrium, particularly in its appendage, and thrombus forms and embolises. This is the mechanism that dominates management, and it is largely independent of symptoms.

Fibrillation itself remodels the atrium electrically and structurally, so episodes become longer and more frequent over time.
### Clinical picture
Palpitation, breathlessness, fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance, chest discomfort, dizziness and syncope. A substantial proportion of patients are entirely asymptomatic, and in some the first manifestation is a stroke.

Signs: an irregularly irregular pulse, and a pulse deficit — an apical rate exceeding the radial rate — because weak beats after short filling intervals do not generate a palpable pulse. Look for the cause and the consequences: hypertension, murmurs of mitral disease, thyroid signs, and evidence of heart failure.
### Investigation
The 12-lead ECG confirms the diagnosis: absent discrete P waves, an irregular baseline and an irregularly irregular ventricular response.

Where episodes are paroxysmal, ambulatory monitoring or a longer-term recorder is needed to capture them.

Bloods: thyroid function, full blood count, renal function and electrolytes, and glucose. Echocardiography assesses left atrial size, valvular disease — mitral stenosis in particular — and ventricular function, and contributes to both the aetiology and the anticoagulation decision.

Where an acute precipitant is suspected, investigate for it: infection, pulmonary embolism, alcohol, thyrotoxicosis.
### Management
Management has three separate strands, and the commonest error is to let one substitute for another.

**Anticoagulation to prevent stroke** is decided by formal assessment of embolic risk against bleeding risk, using the scores specified in the guideline in force. This decision is independent of symptoms and independent of whether sinus rhythm is restored — because recurrence is often silent.

**Rate control** accepts the rhythm and controls the ventricular response, and is an entirely legitimate long-term strategy for many patients.

**Rhythm control** attempts to restore and maintain sinus rhythm, by cardioversion, antiarrhythmic drug therapy or catheter ablation. It is favoured where symptoms persist despite rate control, in younger patients, in first presentations, and where the arrhythmia is driving heart failure.

Cardioversion carries an embolic risk if the arrhythmia has been present beyond a defined short duration, so anticoagulation or exclusion of atrial thrombus by transoesophageal echocardiography precedes it, and anticoagulation continues afterwards.

Treat the underlying cause and the precipitant. Weight, alcohol, sleep apnoea and blood pressure are modifiable and materially affect recurrence.

Every score, threshold, agent, dose and duration follows the atrial fibrillation guideline in force where the patient is treated. None is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Ischaemic stroke and systemic embolism; heart failure, both from loss of atrial contraction and from tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy where the rate is persistently high; and reduced quality of life.

Prognosis depends on the underlying cardiac disease and on whether anticoagulation is appropriately given. Anticoagulation substantially reduces stroke risk, and failure to prescribe it in an eligible patient is among the most consequential omissions in general medicine.
### Classification
Atrial fibrillation is described as first-diagnosed, paroxysmal, persistent, long-standing persistent or permanent, according to duration and to whether rhythm control is still being pursued. The pattern guides the rhythm strategy — it does not determine the embolic risk, which is assessed from clinical risk factors regardless of the pattern.
## hold_these
Embolic risk is assessed from clinical risk factors, not from symptoms or from the pattern of the arrhythmia.
Restoring sinus rhythm does not remove the need for anticoagulation, because recurrence is often silent.
A pulse deficit occurs because weak beats after short filling intervals produce no palpable pulse.
## lose_the_mark
Withholding anticoagulation because the patient feels well or because sinus rhythm has been restored.
Treating a first episode without looking for the precipitant — sepsis, thyrotoxicosis, alcohol, pulmonary embolism.
Cardioverting without addressing the embolic risk of doing so.

## summary
A circuit, not a fast pacemaker. It starts and stops in a single beat because a circle either exists or it does not — and blocking the node either breaks it or reveals what it was hiding.
## sections
### Definition
Supraventricular tachycardia is a rapid regular rhythm arising above the ventricles, typically narrow-complex. In common use the term refers to the paroxysmal re-entrant tachycardias — atrioventricular nodal re-entrant tachycardia and atrioventricular re-entrant tachycardia — rather than to atrial fibrillation or flutter.
### Epidemiology
Paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia is common, presents at any age including childhood, and is more frequent in women. Most patients have structurally normal hearts, which is why the prognosis is generally good and the burden is one of symptoms.
### Aetiology and risk factors
Atrioventricular nodal re-entrant tachycardia arises from dual pathway physiology within the node, a normal anatomical variant present in a substantial minority of people.

Atrioventricular re-entrant tachycardia requires an accessory pathway — a congenital muscular connection between atrium and ventricle that bypasses the node.

Episodes are precipitated by a premature beat, and by caffeine, alcohol, stimulants, thyrotoxicosis and emotional or physical stress.
### Pathophysiology
Re-entry requires two connected pathways with different conduction velocities and different refractory periods, and unidirectional block in one. A premature impulse blocks in the fast pathway, travels down the slow one, and returns up the fast pathway now recovered — then circulates.

In **nodal re-entrant tachycardia** the circuit is small, confined to the node and the tissue immediately around it. Atria and ventricles are activated almost simultaneously, so the retrograde P wave is buried in or just after the QRS complex.

In **accessory pathway re-entrant tachycardia** the circuit is larger: down the node, through the ventricle, back up the accessory pathway to the atrium. Because atrial activation occurs later, the retrograde P wave appears after the QRS in the ST segment.

Both circuits pass through the atrioventricular node, which is why blocking the node terminates both.

An accessory pathway may also conduct anterogradely in sinus rhythm, producing pre-excitation — a short PR interval and a delta wave. A pathway that conducts only retrogradely is concealed, and the resting ECG is normal.
### Clinical picture
Abrupt onset and abrupt offset are characteristic, and patients often describe the beginning and end to the second — which distinguishes it from sinus tachycardia, which accelerates and decelerates gradually.

Palpitation, often rapid and regular; chest discomfort, breathlessness, anxiety, dizziness and occasionally syncope. Polyuria after an episode is a recognised feature, from natriuretic peptide release during the tachycardia.

Between episodes, examination and often the ECG are entirely normal. In pre-excitation the resting ECG shows the delta wave.
### Investigation
The ECG during tachycardia is the key recording, and obtaining one before terminating the episode is worth the effort — it is what allows the mechanism to be identified later.

Look for the rate, the regularity, the QRS width and the position of the retrograde P wave relative to the QRS.

A resting ECG between episodes is examined for pre-excitation. Where episodes are infrequent, ambulatory or event monitoring captures them. Bloods include thyroid function. Echocardiography looks for structural disease, particularly where an accessory pathway is present, since some are associated with congenital abnormalities.

Electrophysiological study defines the mechanism precisely and is combined with ablation.
### Management
**Acute termination** exploits the fact that the circuit passes through the node. Vagal manoeuvres are attempted first, and transient pharmacological nodal block follows where they fail. Either way the manoeuvre is informative: if the tachycardia terminates, the node was part of the circuit; if it merely slows and reveals the underlying atrial activity, it was not — so a manoeuvre that fails to terminate has still narrowed the differential.

Where the patient is haemodynamically compromised, electrical cardioversion is used rather than drug therapy, on the timescale set by the resuscitation guideline in force.

**One critical exception.** In an irregular broad-complex tachycardia suggesting atrial fibrillation with pre-excitation, blocking the atrioventricular node is dangerous. The node is the only structure filtering the atrial rate; remove it and conduction is diverted down the accessory pathway, which has no comparable rate-limiting property, and the rate can accelerate to ventricular fibrillation. This presentation requires a different approach entirely.

**Long-term**: avoidance of precipitants; drug prophylaxis for infrequent episodes; and catheter ablation, which is curative in the great majority and is first-line for many patients, particularly where a pathway conducts anterogradely.

Every drug, dose, manoeuvre technique and cardioversion threshold follows the guideline in force; none is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Prognosis is excellent in the ordinary case, with a structurally normal heart and a curable arrhythmia.

The exception is pre-excitation with a rapidly conducting accessory pathway, where atrial fibrillation can degenerate to ventricular fibrillation and cause sudden death. This is uncommon, and it is the reason anterogradely conducting pathways are assessed and often ablated rather than observed.
### Classification
Supraventricular tachycardias are classified by the circuit — within the node, using an accessory pathway, or within atrial tissue as in atrial tachycardia and flutter. The classification matters because it determines whether nodal block will terminate the rhythm or merely unmask it.
## hold_these
Abrupt onset and abrupt offset indicate a re-entrant circuit; sinus tachycardia speeds up and slows down gradually.
Transient nodal block is diagnostic whether or not it terminates the tachycardia.
In pre-excited atrial fibrillation, blocking the node removes the filter that is protecting the patient.
## lose_the_mark
Blocking the atrioventricular node in an irregular broad-complex tachycardia.
Terminating the episode without first recording a 12-lead ECG during it.
Assuming a normal resting ECG excludes an accessory pathway — a concealed pathway conducts only retrogradely.

## summary
A regular broad-complex tachycardia is ventricular tachycardia until proven otherwise — and a patient who looks well is not evidence against it.
## sections
### Definition
Ventricular tachycardia is a tachycardia arising below the bundle of His, producing broad QRS complexes at a rate above the normal ventricular escape.

It is described as **non-sustained** where it terminates spontaneously within a short period, and **sustained** where it persists or causes haemodynamic compromise. It is **monomorphic** where all complexes have the same shape, indicating a single fixed circuit or focus, and **polymorphic** where the shape varies.
### Epidemiology
Ventricular tachycardia occurs predominantly in hearts with structural disease, most often ischaemic with previous infarction. It is a major mechanism of sudden cardiac death.

In an undifferentiated regular broad-complex tachycardia in an adult, ventricular tachycardia is substantially more likely than supraventricular tachycardia with aberrancy — and more likely still where there is known structural heart disease.
### Aetiology and risk factors
**Structural**: previous myocardial infarction, with re-entry around scar, is the commonest substrate. Also cardiomyopathy of any type, myocarditis, sarcoidosis, arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy and congenital heart disease after repair.

**Functional and metabolic**: acute ischaemia, electrolyte disturbance particularly potassium and magnesium, acidosis, hypoxia and drug toxicity.

**Inherited arrhythmia syndromes** in structurally normal hearts: long QT syndrome, Brugada syndrome and catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia.

**Idiopathic** ventricular tachycardia occurs in structurally normal hearts and carries a much better prognosis.
### Pathophysiology
The dominant mechanism in structural disease is **re-entry** around a region of scar. Surviving myocyte bundles within fibrous tissue conduct slowly and have heterogeneous refractoriness, providing exactly the conditions a circuit requires. This is why the commonest substrate is an old infarct, and why the tachycardia is typically monomorphic — one fixed circuit produces one repeated shape.

**Triggered activity** and **abnormal automaticity** account for the remainder, and dominate in acute ischaemia, electrolyte disturbance and drug toxicity.

Because activation begins outside the conducting system and spreads slowly through working myocardium, the QRS complex is broad. Two consequences follow for the patient: filling is compromised by the rate and by the loss of atrial contribution, and the disorganised activation sequence contracts the ventricle inefficiently. Both reduce output, and the rhythm may degenerate into ventricular fibrillation.
### Clinical picture
Palpitation, chest pain, breathlessness, dizziness, syncope, or cardiac arrest.

Haemodynamic tolerance varies enormously and depends on rate, ventricular function and duration. A patient with good ventricular function may be alert and normotensive in sustained ventricular tachycardia — which is precisely why clinical stability must never be used as an argument for a supraventricular origin.

Signs suggesting ventricular origin: cannon waves in the jugular venous pulse and variable intensity of the first heart sound, both consequences of atrioventricular dissociation.
### Investigation
A 12-lead ECG during the tachycardia, recorded before termination wherever the patient's condition permits, is the single most valuable investigation.

Features favouring a ventricular origin: **atrioventricular dissociation**, which is the most reliable and is demonstrated by P waves marching independently through the tachycardia, by **capture beats** where a sinus impulse conducts through and produces a normal narrow complex, and by **fusion beats** where sinus and ventricular activation combine. Also very broad complexes, extreme axis deviation, concordance across the chest leads, and a QRS morphology that does not fit either bundle branch block pattern.

After the event: assess the substrate. Echocardiography, coronary assessment, cardiac MRI for scar and infiltration, electrolytes, and — where the heart is structurally normal — assessment for an inherited arrhythmia syndrome, including family history and family screening.
### Management
**Acute.** A patient with haemodynamic compromise requires immediate electrical cardioversion. A pulseless patient is in cardiac arrest and is managed on the resuscitation pathway.

Where the patient is stable, pharmacological termination or elective cardioversion is used, following the guideline in force. Correct the reversible contributors at the same time: potassium, magnesium, ischaemia, hypoxia, acidosis and any offending drug.

The safest default in an undifferentiated regular broad-complex tachycardia is to treat it as ventricular tachycardia. Treatment appropriate to a supraventricular origin, given to a patient in ventricular tachycardia, can precipitate collapse.

**Long-term.** Treat the substrate — revascularisation, heart failure therapy, immunosuppression for sarcoidosis. Assess the risk of sudden death and consider an implantable defibrillator, which treats the arrhythmia rather than preventing it. Antiarrhythmic drug therapy and catheter ablation reduce the burden of episodes and of device shocks.

Every drug, dose, energy level and device criterion follows the guideline in force; none is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Degeneration to ventricular fibrillation and sudden death; syncope and injury; and tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy where episodes are frequent and prolonged.

Prognosis is determined chiefly by the underlying structural disease and by ventricular function. Idiopathic ventricular tachycardia in a structurally normal heart carries a good prognosis; ventricular tachycardia in a scarred ventricle with poor function does not.
### Classification
Classification by duration and by morphology carries the clinical information: sustained or non-sustained determines urgency, and monomorphic or polymorphic points to the mechanism — a fixed circuit against a repolarisation abnormality or acute ischaemia.
## hold_these
A regular broad-complex tachycardia is ventricular tachycardia until proven otherwise.
Haemodynamic stability does not indicate a supraventricular origin.
Atrioventricular dissociation, capture beats and fusion beats establish a ventricular origin.
## lose_the_mark
Treating a broad-complex tachycardia as supraventricular because the patient looks well.
Terminating the rhythm without a 12-lead ECG when the patient's condition allowed one.
Treating the episode without correcting potassium, magnesium and ischaemia.

## summary
Not a fast rhythm. No rhythm and no output — a cardiac arrest that happens to have electrical activity on the monitor.
## sections
### Definition
Ventricular fibrillation is chaotic, disorganised ventricular electrical activity with no coordinated contraction and therefore no cardiac output. On the ECG there are no identifiable QRS complexes, only irregular undulation of varying amplitude and frequency.

It is a cardiac arrest rhythm. A patient in ventricular fibrillation is pulseless by definition.
### Epidemiology
Ventricular fibrillation is a leading mechanism of sudden cardiac death, and the commonest initial rhythm in witnessed out-of-hospital cardiac arrest of cardiac cause. It is the commonest cause of death in the first hours after myocardial infarction, frequently before the patient reaches hospital.

Survival falls steeply with every minute between arrest and defibrillation, which is the single most important determinant of outcome.
### Aetiology and risk factors
**Ischaemic**: acute myocardial infarction and acute ischaemia are the commonest causes. Previous infarction with scar provides a chronic substrate.

**Structural**: cardiomyopathies of all types, particularly hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy; severe valvular disease; myocarditis.

**Electrical, in structurally normal hearts**: long QT syndrome, Brugada syndrome, catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia, and pre-excited atrial fibrillation conducting rapidly down an accessory pathway.

**Metabolic and toxic**: severe electrolyte disturbance particularly of potassium and magnesium, hypoxia, acidosis, hypothermia, drug toxicity and electrocution.

Most episodes are preceded by ventricular tachycardia that degenerates.
### Pathophysiology
Multiple wandering re-entrant wavefronts propagate simultaneously through ventricular myocardium made heterogeneous in refractoriness and conduction velocity — by ischaemia, scar, electrolyte disturbance or a repolarisation abnormality.

No region is depolarised in a coordinated sequence, so no effective contraction occurs. Output falls to zero at once. Coronary perfusion ceases, the myocardium becomes progressively more ischaemic, the fibrillation waveform coarsens then becomes finer, and eventually electrical activity ceases altogether.

That deterioration is why the passage of time matters so much: the same rhythm becomes progressively less likely to respond as the myocardium it is occurring in becomes more ischaemic.
### Clinical picture
Sudden collapse with loss of consciousness within seconds. No pulse, no respiratory effort or agonal gasps only, and unresponsiveness.

Agonal breathing is common in the first minutes and is frequently mistaken for breathing, which delays recognition. It is not effective respiration and does not exclude cardiac arrest.
### Investigation
Recognition is clinical and immediate — unresponsive, not breathing normally, no pulse — and treatment begins before any investigation.

Rhythm identification from the monitor or defibrillator determines whether the rhythm is shockable. Investigation of the cause happens after resuscitation, or alongside it where a reversible cause is suspected: coronary assessment for ischaemia, electrolytes, arterial blood gas, echocardiography, and assessment for inherited arrhythmia syndromes and family screening where the heart is structurally normal.
### Management
Ventricular fibrillation is managed on the cardiac arrest pathway, and the sequence, timing, energy levels and drugs all follow the resuscitation guideline in force where the patient is treated. None is reproduced here, and using a remembered sequence rather than the current one is unsafe.

The principles that do not change: it is a shockable rhythm, and defibrillation is the definitive treatment; chest compressions maintain some circulation and myocardial perfusion between shocks and improve the probability that a shock succeeds; the interval from arrest to defibrillation is the dominant determinant of survival; and reversible causes are sought and corrected throughout rather than after.

After return of circulation, post-arrest care — including treatment of the underlying cause, particularly reperfusion where the cause is a coronary occlusion — determines neurological outcome as much as the resuscitation itself.

Secondary prevention with an implantable defibrillator is considered in survivors whose arrest was not due to a fully reversible cause.

Resuscitation as a whole belongs to SYS-MUL; this article states the mechanism and the principles and cross-references the resuscitation guideline for everything procedural.
### Complications and prognosis
Death, and in survivors hypoxic brain injury, which is the principal determinant of quality of survival.

Outcome depends overwhelmingly on the interval to defibrillation, on whether the arrest was witnessed, on whether bystander resuscitation was given, and on the underlying cause. Arrest from a reversible cause carries a far better prognosis than arrest from advanced structural disease.
### Classification
Ventricular fibrillation is one of the two shockable cardiac arrest rhythms, alongside pulseless ventricular tachycardia. That classification exists because it determines the single most important treatment decision.
## hold_these
Ventricular fibrillation is cardiac arrest — there is no output and the patient is pulseless.
The interval from arrest to defibrillation is the dominant determinant of survival.
Agonal gasping is common and is not breathing; it does not exclude cardiac arrest.
## lose_the_mark
Treating it as a fast rhythm requiring rate control.
Delaying defibrillation to obtain a 12-lead ECG or intravenous access.
Mistaking agonal breathing for effective respiration and delaying recognition.

## summary
An interval on the ECG that measures how long the heart stays vulnerable. Most cases are drugs and electrolytes rather than genetics — and the arrhythmia it causes gets worse if you slow the heart.
## sections
### Definition
The QT interval measures the duration of ventricular depolarisation and repolarisation. Prolongation, corrected for heart rate, indicates delayed repolarisation and predisposes to torsades de pointes — a polymorphic ventricular tachycardia occurring on that background, with complexes that appear to twist around the baseline.

Long QT syndromes are the inherited disorders producing this; acquired QT prolongation has the same consequence and is far commoner.
### Epidemiology
Congenital long QT syndrome is uncommon but an important cause of syncope and sudden death in young people, including sudden death in infancy and in the young athlete.

Acquired QT prolongation is common in hospital practice, particularly in older patients on several contributing drugs with concurrent electrolyte disturbance. Most torsades encountered clinically is acquired.
### Aetiology and risk factors
**Congenital**: mutations in genes encoding cardiac ion channels, principally potassium and sodium channels. Several subtypes exist with characteristic triggers — exertion and particularly swimming in one, auditory startle in another, rest or sleep in a third — which is why the circumstances of a syncopal episode carry diagnostic weight.

**Acquired**, which is commoner and additive:
- drugs that block repolarising potassium current, across several therapeutic classes;
- electrolyte disturbance — hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia and hypocalcaemia;
- bradycardia and pauses, including complete heart block;
- acute myocardial ischaemia, myocarditis and takotsubo syndrome;
- hypothermia, starvation and anorexia nervosa;
- hypothyroidism.

These causes compound. A patient on one contributing drug who becomes hypokalaemic and bradycardic has three additive causes, and correcting the electrolytes may matter as much as stopping the drug.
### Pathophysiology
Delayed repolarisation lengthens the action potential and widens the window during which an early afterdepolarisation can arise and reach threshold. Repolarisation is also heterogeneous across the ventricular wall, so a triggered beat falling on partially repolarised tissue meets regions in different states of recovery — the conditions for re-entry.

The result is polymorphic ventricular tachycardia. It is characteristically **pause-dependent**: action potential duration lengthens as rate falls, so the beat following a pause has the longest repolarisation and the widest vulnerable window. A short–long–short sequence — a premature beat, its compensatory pause, then the next beat — is the classic initiating pattern.

Torsades is often self-terminating, producing syncope, but it may degenerate into ventricular fibrillation.
### Clinical picture
Syncope, presyncope and palpitation, occurring in circumstances characteristic of the subtype in congenital disease. Sudden cardiac death may be the first event.

A family history of sudden death, of unexplained drowning, or of infant death is significant and should be sought explicitly.

In acquired cases the presentation is often syncope or a monitored arrhythmia in a patient already in hospital on contributing drugs.

Torsades causing sustained arrhythmia presents as collapse or arrest.
### Investigation
The 12-lead ECG with the corrected QT interval measured is the primary investigation. Measurement technique matters and the correction formula used affects the value, particularly at extremes of heart rate.

The ECG may also show characteristic T-wave morphology in some congenital subtypes, and T-wave alternans, which indicates high risk.

Where the resting interval is borderline, exercise testing with assessment of QT behaviour during recovery, and ambulatory monitoring, add information.

Investigate the acquired causes in every case: review the drug chart in full including recently started and over-the-counter agents, measure potassium, magnesium and calcium, and assess thyroid function.

Genetic testing and cascade family screening are offered where congenital long QT syndrome is diagnosed or suspected.
### Management
**Acute torsades** is managed on the guideline in force. Two principles are worth holding because they invert ordinary instinct: the arrhythmia is pause-dependent, so treatments that slow the rate can make it worse, and increasing the rate can suppress it; and magnesium is used regardless of the serum level. Withdrawal of every contributing drug and correction of every contributing electrolyte happen immediately and simultaneously.

Where the patient is pulseless, this is cardiac arrest and is managed on the resuscitation pathway.

**Acquired QT prolongation** is treated by removing the causes. Most cases resolve completely when this is done thoroughly.

**Congenital long QT syndrome** is managed with avoidance of QT-prolonging drugs and of subtype-specific triggers, drug prophylaxis, and an implantable defibrillator where risk is high. Family screening is part of management, since relatives may be affected and asymptomatic.

Every agent, dose, correction formula, interval threshold and device criterion follows the guideline in force; none is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Syncope with injury, torsades degenerating to ventricular fibrillation, and sudden cardiac death.

Acquired cases have an excellent prognosis once the causes are removed. Congenital long QT syndrome, identified and treated, also carries a good prognosis — which is why recognising it in a young person with syncope, and screening the family, is the intervention that matters.
### Classification
Congenital and acquired are separated because the management differs entirely: one is treated by removing causes, the other by lifelong risk management and family screening. The distinction is not always clean, since some patients with acquired torsades carry a genetic predisposition unmasked by the drug.
## hold_these
Most QT prolongation is acquired, and its causes are additive — drug, electrolyte and bradycardia together.
Torsades is pause-dependent, so slowing the heart worsens it and increasing the rate suppresses it.
A family history of sudden death, drowning or infant death is significant and must be asked for.
## lose_the_mark
Stopping the offending drug and not correcting potassium and magnesium.
Managing torsades with the reflexes that apply to monomorphic ventricular tachycardia; the pause dependence inverts them.
Dismissing exertional or startle-induced syncope in a young person without measuring the QT interval.
