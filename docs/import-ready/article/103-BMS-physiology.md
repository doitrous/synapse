## summary
An action potential is a series of rapid changes in membrane potential that follow an adequate stimulus. Two voltage-gated channels produce all of it: a sodium channel with an activation gate outside and an inactivation gate inside, and a potassium channel with one gate and no inactivation. Depolarisation is a regenerative sodium influx that carries the membrane from −90 mV through the −65 mV firing level to an overshoot of +35 mV. What the sodium channels do next sets the two refractory periods, which is why the ionic basis and the refractory periods are one story rather than two.

## sections
### Definition
An action potential is a series of rapid changes in the membrane potential that follow stimulation of a nerve fibre by an adequate stimulus. It is recorded with two microelectrodes, and it is preceded by a latent period — the interval between applying the stimulus and the start of the response, which is the time the impulse takes to travel from the stimulating electrode to the recording one.

The wave has three phases. Depolarisation carries the potential from the resting level to an overshoot. Repolarisation returns it, rapidly for the first 70 per cent and slowly for the remaining 30 per cent. Hyperpolarisation then takes it slightly past the resting level before it settles. The sharp rise and rapid fall together are the spike, which lasts about 2 msec; the hyperpolarisation lasts 35 to 40 msec.

### Mechanism
The main features of the action potential are determined by the voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels.

The voltage-gated sodium channel has two gates: an activation gate near the outer surface and an inactivation gate on the inner surface. At the resting membrane potential the activation gate is closed while the inactivation gate is open. Opening the outer gate makes the channel activated; closing the inner gate makes it inactivated. Those are two different ways of being shut, and confusing them is the commonest error in this topic. The voltage-gated potassium channel is simpler: one gate, on the inner surface, closed at rest, and no inactivation gate at all.

Depolarisation happens in two steps and is a positive-feedback, regenerative process. In the slow phase, a stimulus decreases the membrane potential from −90 mV toward the firing level; some sodium activation gates open, sodium enters down its electro-concentration gradient, and that inflow depolarises the membrane further, which opens more channels, until −65 mV is reached. This is the first 25 mV of depolarisation. In the rapid phase, at the firing level all the voltage-gated sodium channels open, the potential difference falls to zero and then reverses, so the inside becomes positive relative to the outside, to an overshoot of +35 mV. The amplitude of the action potential is therefore 125 mV. Sodium channels then inactivate rapidly and stay inactivated for a few milliseconds before returning to the resting state.

Repolarisation follows from two things happening together: sodium inactivation stops the influx and terminates depolarisation, while the potassium gates — which open later than the sodium gates, more slowly, and for longer — carry potassium out and complete the return. Slow closure of those potassium channels then overshoots into hyperpolarisation, and the leak potassium channels bring the membrane back to the resting level. The Na⁺–K⁺ pump restores the concentration gradients afterwards, pumping three sodium out for every two potassium in; it does not produce the spike.

### Key determinants
During the initial depolarisation up to the firing level excitability is increased. Through the rest of the action potential the neurone is refractory, and there are two refractory periods.

The absolute refractory period is the period during which another action potential cannot be produced, whatever the strength of the stimulus. It runs from the firing level to the early part of repolarisation. Its cause is that all the voltage-gated sodium channels have opened and then been rapidly inactivated by the inner gate — an inactivated channel must return to the resting state before it can open again, and no stimulus strength substitutes for that.

The relative refractory period is the period during which another action potential can be produced by a stronger stimulus above the threshold. It begins at the end of the absolute refractory period and ends when the membrane potential returns to its resting level. It has two causes rather than one: some sodium channels have returned to the resting state and are available for activation, and the potassium channels opened during repolarisation are still carrying potassium outwards, which opposes sodium moving in.

Refractoriness is not a defect. It protects the nerve from extremely rapid repetitive stimulation, and it ensures one-way forward propagation of the impulse — the segment an action potential has just left cannot be re-excited by it.

Excitability itself turns on sodium permeability. Anything that increases it, such as veratridine or a low extracellular calcium, makes the fibre depolarise more readily; anything that decreases it stabilises the membrane, which is what a high extracellular calcium and a local anaesthetic such as cocaine do. A fall in extracellular sodium reduces the size of the action potential but has little effect on the resting potential, and blocking the sodium channels with tetrodotoxin abolishes the action potential altogether. Potassium works on the resting potential instead: a rise in extracellular potassium depolarises the membrane and raises excitability, a fall hyperpolarises it and lowers excitability.

### Clinical significance
Local anaesthetics are membrane stabilisers. They reduce sodium permeability so the membrane depolarises slowly and cannot reach the firing level, and the book gives cocaine as its example. Hypercalcaemia acts the same way and hypocalcaemia the opposite way, which is why calcium disorders present with changes in nerve and muscle excitability.

Potassium disorders act on the resting potential. In familial periodic paralysis the extracellular potassium concentration is decreased, nerve excitability is greatly reduced, no nerve impulses are produced and the person becomes paralysed; the condition is treated by intravenous administration of potassium.

Tetrodotoxin blocks the sodium channel outright, and no action potential can be elicited at all. It is worth holding as the cleanest demonstration that the upstroke is a sodium current and nothing else.

### Common misconceptions
Three keep costing marks. The first is describing depolarisation as sodium being pumped in — nothing is pumped during the spike, and the pump only restores the gradients afterwards. The second is treating "closed" and "inactivated" as one channel state, which makes the absolute refractory period impossible to explain. The third is giving only the sodium half of the reason for the relative refractory period and leaving out the outward potassium current that is still flowing.

## summary
Tension is the force a muscle develops when it contracts, and it is produced by cross-bridges cycling through four steps: bind, bend, detach, reset. Calcium starts the cycle by uncovering the active site on actin; ATP is what ends each turn of it, because a cross-bridge cannot let go of actin until a fresh ATP replaces the ADP and phosphate on the myosin head. That is why a muscle without ATP goes stiff rather than limp.

## sections
### Definition
Excitation–contraction coupling is the process by which an action potential of a muscle fibre initiates the contractile process. Tension is the developed force when a muscle contracts.

The action potential comes first. A skeletal muscle fibre has a resting membrane potential of about −90 mV, its action potential lasts 2 to 4 msec, it is conducted along the fibre at about 5 m/sec, and it precedes the contraction by about 2 msec. Because the electrical event finishes before the mechanical one begins, the fibre has already recovered its excitability by the time it starts to shorten, which is why skeletal muscle can be tetanised and cardiac muscle cannot.

### Mechanism
Coupling runs in four stages, and the third is the one this article is named for.

Calcium is released first. The action potential propagates into the T-tubule, where a voltage-sensitive dihydropyridine receptor opens the ryanodine calcium channel on the terminal cisternae of the sarcoplasmic reticulum, and calcium flows out into the cytoplasm.

The muscle proteins are then activated. Calcium binds troponin C on actin; troponin changes conformation; tropomyosin moves away from the position in which it covers the myosin-binding site; and the uncovered site on actin combines with the myosin cross-bridges.

Tension is then generated by the cycling of the cross-bridges, in four steps. Binding of actin and myosin occurs spontaneously once calcium has bound troponin C and tropomyosin has moved off the active site. The cross-bridge then bends and the actin filament slides across the myosin filament; the energy used to phosphorylate the cross-bridge and generate tension comes from hydrolysis of ATP by ATPase into ADP and inorganic phosphate. The cross-bridge then detaches from actin, and for that to happen the ADP and phosphate must be removed and a new molecule of ATP put in their place — it is the new ATP that reduces the cross-bridge's affinity for the active site. Finally the cross-bridge returns to its original upright position to take part in another cycle. Cycling continues as long as calcium is attached to troponin C and ATP is available, and the force developed by the bending of the cross-bridge is transmitted through the actin filament to the Z disc, then through the sarcolemma and the tendinous insertions of the muscle to the bones.

Relaxation is the fourth stage. Calcium is removed from the cytoplasm by a calcium pump on the sarcoplasmic reticulum membrane; troponin returns to its original conformation, tropomyosin moves back to cover the binding site on actin, and cross-bridge cycling stops.

### Key determinants
Two things keep the cycle turning, and either one failing stops it. Calcium must remain bound to troponin C, which is a matter of how fast the sarcoplasmic reticulum pump clears the cytoplasm. ATP must be available, which is a matter of supply rather than of signalling.

A third determinant is geometric. The number of cross-bridges able to act at once depends on the overlap between the thick and thin filaments, so the tension a fibre can develop varies with its initial length — the length–tension relationship, which the book plots as the maximal active tension developed by isometric contraction against increasing initial fibre length.

Note what is not a determinant: the filaments themselves never change length. Contraction shortens the sarcomere because the filaments slide past one another.

### Clinical significance
If no ATP is available, the thick and thin filaments cannot be separated, and the muscle is in contracture. The same mechanism explains rigor mortis: several hours after death all the muscles of the body go into a state of contracture and become rigid even without action potentials, caused by the loss of ATP needed to separate the actin and myosin filaments.

This is worth holding because it inverts the intuition students bring. ATP is spent to break the actin–myosin bond, not to make it, so a muscle that runs out of energy locks rather than relaxes.

### Common misconceptions
Two recur. The first is that the actin and myosin filaments shorten during contraction — they do not, and the live library record on band changes is explicit that the sarcomere shortens without filament shortening. The second is that ATP powers contraction alone, so a muscle without ATP should go floppy; the book puts ATP at the detachment step, and contracture is the consequence.

## summary
Smooth muscle contracts on its own. It does so rhythmically, or as a maintained partial contraction called tone, and it keeps doing so when isolated with no nerve supply at all. Everything that acts on it therefore modifies an activity that is already running: stretch, local chemical conditions, cold, circulating and locally released humoral agents, and the dual autonomic supply, which changes the pattern and the sensitivity rather than switching the muscle on.

## sections
### Definition
Smooth muscle is characterised by its tendency to give rise to spontaneous contractions, which may occur in a rhythmic form or in a tetanic form — muscle tone, a maintained partial contraction. These occur even in isolated smooth muscle when there is no nerve supply.

Its membrane potential is unstable, averaging about −50 to −60 mV at relative rest, with waves superimposed on it. Those waves are not action potentials and cannot themselves cause contraction, but when a wave reaches about −35 mV an action potential is elicited and spreads over the muscle.

### Mechanism
The contractile machinery is regulated on the myosin side rather than the actin side, which is the structural reason smooth muscle behaves so differently from skeletal muscle.

Depolarisation opens voltage-gated calcium channels and calcium flows in down its electrochemical gradient; the calcium that enters may release further calcium from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Hormones and neurotransmitters can also open ligand-gated calcium channels in the membrane or release calcium from the sarcoplasmic reticulum through IP₃-gated channels, so a contraction can begin without any action potential at all.

Calcium then binds calmodulin. The calcium–calmodulin complex activates myosin light-chain kinase, which phosphorylates the regulatory light chain on the myosin head, and that produces ATP hydrolysis and cross-bridge cycling. The phosphorylated cross-bridges keep cycling until myosin light-chain phosphatase dephosphorylates them, so the amount of active myosin at any moment is set by the relative activity of the kinase and the phosphatase.

Relaxation follows a fall in intracellular calcium, produced by a pump that moves calcium into the extracellular fluid and a pump that moves it into the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Both are slow compared with the fast sarcoplasmic reticulum pump of skeletal muscle, which is why a smooth muscle contraction lasts longer.

Dephosphorylated cross-bridges do not all let go. Those that remain attached to actin are latch bridges, and because they do not cycle, or cycle very slowly, they hold tone for a long time on very little ATP. Smooth muscle uses less ATP than skeletal muscle and is fatigue resistant.

### Key determinants
Five groups of factors modify contraction, and the exam asks for them as a list.

Stretch is the first. Visceral smooth muscle responds to stretch by contraction, which lets a hollow organ contract automatically and evacuate its contents when it is distended.

Local factors are the second, and they act in both directions. Acids, excess carbon dioxide and lack of oxygen relax smooth muscle. Alkalis and excess potassium contract it.

Cold is the third: the book states that cold increases the contraction of smooth muscles.

Humoral factors are the fourth. A ligand binds its membrane receptor, and excitatory receptors increase cytoplasmic calcium and cause contraction while inhibitory receptors decrease cytoplasmic calcium and cause relaxation — mediated by opening potassium channels, which inhibits calcium influx, or by increasing the active transport of calcium out of the cytoplasm.

The nerve supply is the fifth. Smooth muscle has a dual supply from the two divisions of the autonomic nervous system, and the nerve supply does not initiate activity in the muscle; it modifies it, by affecting the spontaneous activity and the sensitivity to chemical agents.

### Clinical significance
Two everyday consequences follow directly.

The stretch response is why a distended hollow organ empties itself. A full bladder, a loaded rectum and a distended segment of gut all contract in response to their own filling, without waiting for a signal from outside.

Plasticity is the counterweight. Smooth muscle that is stretched first exerts increased tension, but if it is held stretched the tension gradually decreases, because the myosin cross-bridges readjust their position on the thin filaments. That is why urine can accumulate in the urinary bladder without much rise of intravesical pressure — the same organ both contracts when stretched quickly and accommodates when stretched slowly.

### Common misconceptions
The commonest is answering the exam question about factors with the coupling mechanism. Calcium, calmodulin and myosin light-chain kinase explain how a contraction is produced; they do not answer what modifies it, and a script that gives them earns none of the marks for the factors.

The second is reasoning from skeletal muscle and concluding that denervated smooth muscle is silent. It is not: cutting the nerve changes the pattern and the sensitivity, and the spontaneous activity continues.

## summary
Every substance that crosses the cell membrane does so by one of four routes: simple diffusion down its own gradient, facilitated diffusion down its gradient but through a carrier, active transport against its gradient using ATP, or direct cell-to-cell passage through a gap junction. Osmosis is diffusion applied to water, and it is what tonicity describes. The Na+-K+ pump is the worked example of active transport the book returns to throughout the chapter, because nearly everything that follows — the resting potential, the action potential, secondary active transport of glucose — depends on the gradients this one pump builds and maintains. This whole heading is excluded from the 2025-2026 final theoretical exam, but the department still teaches it, and every later mechanism in this module assumes a student already has it.

## sections
### Definition
Diffusion is the continual movement of molecules from a region of higher concentration to one of lower concentration. Simple diffusion does this passively, without a carrier, either through the lipid bilayer — lipid-soluble molecules such as oxygen and nitrogen, water itself, and small uncharged lipid-insoluble molecules such as urea — or through protein channels, which may be permanently open (non-gated) or gated by a voltage change (as in Na+ channels) or by a ligand (as in the acetylcholine receptor). Facilitated diffusion is also passive and follows the concentration gradient, but a large molecule such as glucose or an amino acid needs a specific carrier protein to cross at all; the carrier is specific to its substrate, competes between similar substrates, saturates at a maximum rate as concentration rises, and is more temperature-sensitive than simple diffusion. Osmosis is the diffusion of water across a membrane permeable to water but not to the solute, from high water concentration to low. Osmotic pressure is the pressure needed to stop it, set by the number of particles per unit volume rather than their mass; the osmolarity of intracellular and extracellular fluid is each about 300 mOsm/L, of which about 280 mOsm/L of plasma comes from Na+, Cl− and HCO3−. Tonicity compares a solution's osmolality with plasma's: isotonic solutions match it (300 mOsm/L — 0.9% NaCl or 5% glucose are the book's examples), hypertonic solutions exceed it, and hypotonic solutions fall short.

### Mechanism
Active transport moves a substance against its electrochemical gradient, and it always needs two things: a specific carrier protein and energy from ATP, supplied by the carrier's own ATPase activity. By direction, a uniport carries one substance one way (the Ca++ pump); a symport carries two substances the same way at once (Na+ and glucose from the intestinal lumen); an antiport carries one substance one way and another the opposite way (the Na+-K+ pump). Primary active transport spends ATP directly, and the book's worked example is the Na+-K+ pump: an alpha subunit carries the Na+ and K+ binding sites — three Na+ sites on the inner surface, two K+ sites on the outer — and an ATP-binding site, while a beta subunit supplies the ATPase that splits ATP into ADP, phosphate and energy. The pump moves three Na+ out for every two K+ it moves in, is electrogenic, maintains the high extracellular Na+ and high intracellular K+ that every excitable cell in this module depends on, and helps control cell volume. Secondary active transport spends no ATP of its own: it rides a gradient a primary pump has already built, exactly as glucose is carried into an intestinal cell alongside Na+ that the Na+-K+ pump has already pumped out. Vesicular transport is named in the tree but the book itself defers it entirely to the histology course in module 101, printing only "(Please refer to histology in module 101)" with no content of its own here.

### Key determinants
Cells signal each other by three routes. Gap junctions let a chemical substance pass directly from one cell to the next: hexagonal protein units on each membrane form a connexon, and two connexons lined up across the intercellular space form a channel that lets ions and molecules up to about 1000 molecular weight through without ever touching the extracellular fluid, which is what lets electrical activity propagate rapidly from cell to cell; the channel's own diameter is regulated by intracellular Ca++, pH, hormones and drugs. Neural communication releases a neurotransmitter across the synaptic cleft to a contiguous cell. Hormonal communication has three forms: endocrine, reaching distant cells by the bloodstream; paracrine, reaching neighbouring cells by diffusion through interstitial fluid; and autocrine, acting back on the very cell that released it. In every case, the messenger binds a receptor on the membrane, in the cytoplasm, or in the nucleus, and acts through one of three routes: opening a channel, raising cyclic AMP, or raising free cytoplasmic Ca++.

### Clinical significance
The mechanisms in this heading are the ones the rest of the module is built on rather than a source of stand-alone clinical vignettes in this book: the Na+-K+ pump's gradients set up the resting potential the next heading depends on, and the gap junction is the same structure that lets a wave of depolarisation spread through cardiac muscle and through single-unit smooth muscle later in the module. Osmotic and tonicity vocabulary is the language used whenever an intravenous fluid is described as isotonic, hypertonic or hypotonic, which the book states without a worked clinical example on this page.

### Common misconceptions
Two recur. The first is calling facilitated diffusion "active" because it uses a carrier protein; it does not use ATP and does not move a substance against its gradient, so it stays passive despite needing help to cross. The second is treating the Na+-K+ pump as the cause of the resting membrane potential; the book is explicit later in the chapter that diffusion is the main determinant and the pump's own contribution is only about −4 mV — its job here is building the gradients that diffusion then runs down, not generating the potential itself.

## summary
A neuron is a soma, dendrites and an axon ending in synaptic knobs, and it is one of the two most excitable cell types in the body because of the membrane potential across it. Excitability is the ability to respond to a stimulus, and an electrical stimulus — preferred experimentally because it is controllable, measurable and non-damaging — is only effective if it has enough strength, enough duration, and a fast enough rate of rise. The strength-duration curve plots the trade-off between the first two, and gives two of the module's most quoted numbers: rheobase, the minimum intensity that can ever excite the nerve, and chronaxie, the duration a stimulus at twice that intensity needs.

## sections
### Definition
The nervous system is a network of neurons specialised for the rapid transfer and integration of information; an activated neuron generates and conducts electrical signals that affect other neurons, muscles or glands. The typical spinal motor neuron has three parts. The soma is the cell body and acts as the processing centre. Dendrites project from the soma and receive signals from other neurons. The axon, or nerve fibre, starts at the axon hillock and ends in synaptic knobs that contact another cell — muscle, gland or neuron — at a synapse; the presynaptic neuron is the one whose axon forms the synapse, and the postsynaptic neuron is the one it contacts. An axon may be only millimetres long if its target is nearby, as within the central nervous system, or many centimetres long if its target is distant, as a skeletal muscle fibre.

### Mechanism
Membrane potential is the electrical potential difference between the inner and outer surfaces of the membrane of every cell in the body, and it is the basis of excitability — a living cell's ability to respond to a change in its environment, a stimulus. Stimuli can be electrical, mechanical, chemical or thermal, but the electrical stimulus is preferred experimentally because it resembles the body's own natural stimuli, can be controlled, can be measured accurately, and leaves the tissue undamaged; nerve and muscle cells are the most excitable cells in the body. Three factors decide whether an electrical stimulus is effective: its strength must reach a minimum value; its duration must be maintained long enough; and its rate of rise matters independently of both — a rapidly rising stimulus that reaches threshold gives a response, while a slowly rising stimulus to the same threshold value gives none at all, a phenomenon the book names nerve accommodation and develops fully in a later section.

### Key determinants
The strength-duration curve shows the inverse relationship between a stimulus's intensity and the duration it must be applied to produce a response: within limits, a stronger stimulus needs less time, but an extremely brief stimulus will not excite the nerve no matter how intense it is. Rheobase is the threshold stimulus — the minimum intensity that can excite the nerve, applied for as long as needed. A subthreshold stimulus, below rheobase, produces only a local response, not a propagated impulse. Utilization time is the duration a stimulus at exactly rheobase intensity needs. Chronaxie is the duration a stimulus at twice rheobase intensity needs, and it is used as an index of excitability — a more excitable fibre has a shorter chronaxie.

### Clinical significance
Chronaxie is the figure clinicians reach for when comparing how excitable different tissues are, since it reduces the whole strength-duration relationship to one number; the book does not give a worked clinical example on this page, but the concept is the same one nerve-conduction and denervation studies elsewhere in the module depend on — a denervated muscle fibre's own excitability properties change measurably, which is exactly the kind of shift a chronaxie or rheobase measurement would pick up.

### Common misconceptions
Two recur. The first is treating rheobase and chronaxie as interchangeable: rheobase is an intensity, the minimum current that can ever excite the nerve regardless of duration; chronaxie is a duration, the time a stronger current — twice rheobase — needs. The second is assuming a stimulus that eventually reaches threshold must excite the nerve; the book's third factor, rate of rise, overrides that assumption whenever the rise is slow enough for accommodation to catch up with it.

## sections
### Definition
The resting membrane potential (RMP) is the difference in electrical potential, in millivolts, between the inner and outer surfaces of the membrane under resting, un-stimulated conditions — the polarised state. Its magnitude varies with cell type: about −90 mV in large nerve fibres and large skeletal muscle fibres, about −70 mV in medium-sized neurons, and only −20 to −40 mV in less excitable cells such as red blood cells and epithelial cells. The negative sign means the inside of the membrane is negative relative to the outside. Nerve membrane potential can take several forms: at rest it is the RMP; on stimulation by an adequate stimulus it becomes an action potential; on stimulation by an inadequate, subthreshold stimulus, only a localized potential appears.

### Mechanism
The RMP is due to the unequal distribution of ions across the membrane, determined mainly by selective permeability. K+ is the main intracellular cation and Na+ the main extracellular cation; each diffuses down its own gradient through non-gated leak channels — K+ out, Na+ in — but the resting membrane is about 100 times more permeable to K+ than to Na+, because there are far more K+ leak channels, so K+ outflow dominates and sets most of the potential; intracellular anions (proteins), unable to cross, are left behind on the inner surface. The Nernst equation gives the equilibrium potential of a single ion at 37°C: for K+ alone, EK+ = −61 mV × log(140/4) ≈ −94 mV; for Na+ alone, ENa+ = −61 mV × log(14/140) ≈ +61 mV. The Goldman equation improves on this by weighing Na+, K+ and Cl− together by both their concentrations and their relative membrane permeabilities, and it predicts about −86 mV — roughly 95 percent of the real resting potential — because K+'s permeability advantage keeps the true value close to K+'s own equilibrium potential rather than exactly at it. The Na+-K+ pump supplies the remainder: it is electrogenic, moving three Na+ out for every two K+ in, and contributes about −4 mV on top of the permeability-driven figure.

### Key determinants
Two separate contributions add up to the resting potential, and the book keeps them apart on purpose: about 95 percent from selective ion permeability (the Goldman figure), and a small remainder, about −4 mV, from the Na+-K+ pump's own electrogenicity. Because K+ permeability so outweighs Na+ permeability at rest, raised or lowered extracellular K+ — hyperkalaemia or hypokalaemia — has marked, serious effects on the RMP of nerve and muscle, while changes in extracellular Na+ affect it far less.

### Clinical significance
Because K+ permeability so dominates the resting potential, disturbances of extracellular K+ are the ones that move it clinically: the book states this directly, without a named disease example on this page, but the same logic underlies the potassium disorders discussed under nerve excitability later in the chapter — a resting potential built mostly on the K+ gradient is a resting potential mostly at the mercy of that gradient's disturbance.

### Common misconceptions
Two recur. The first is treating the Goldman-predicted −86 mV and the pump's −4 mV as competing explanations rather than additive contributions to the same real potential — the book adds them, it does not choose between them. The second is assuming Na+ permeability matters as much as K+ permeability at rest; the 100-fold difference in leak channel numbers is exactly why the resting potential sits so close to the K+ equilibrium potential and so far from the Na+ one.

## summary
An action potential is a three-phase wave — depolarisation, repolarisation, hyperpolarisation — recorded after a latent period that itself gives the conduction velocity. It obeys the all-or-none law: once triggered, it is the same size every time, and a stronger stimulus does not make it bigger. It propagates along an unmyelinated axon by a local circuit of current flowing from the depolarised patch to the next resting one, and only in one direction physiologically — orthodromic — because a synapse, unlike an axon, will not carry an antidromic impulse past it. This article sits alongside the sibling batch's ionic-basis and refractory-period article, which already covers what the sodium and potassium channels do underneath this shape.

## sections
### Definition
An action potential is a series of rapid changes in membrane potential following stimulation of a nerve fibre by an adequate stimulus, recorded with two microelectrodes. It is preceded by a latent period — the interval between the stimulus and the start of the response, corresponding to the time the impulse takes to travel from the stimulating electrode to the recording electrode; knowing the electrode distance turns the latent period directly into a conduction velocity (a 2 msec latent period over 4 cm gives 20 m/sec). The wave has three phases: depolarisation, from −90 mV through the −65 mV firing level to a +35 mV overshoot; repolarisation, rapid for its first 70 percent and slow for the last 30, back to the resting level; and hyperpolarisation, slightly past the resting level before it settles. The spike — the sharp rise and fall together — lasts about 2 msec; the hyperpolarisation lasts 35 to 40 msec.

### Mechanism
Excitability itself changes across the wave: during the initial depolarisation up to the firing level it is increased, and through the rest of the action potential the fibre is refractory, which protects the nerve from extremely rapid repetitive stimulation and ensures the impulse travels one way only, since the segment it has just left cannot be re-excited by it. Once generated, the action potential obeys the all-or-none law: it propagates with the same amplitude, duration and shape whatever the stimulus strength, threshold or far above it, as long as conditions stay constant — a subthreshold stimulus produces nothing propagated at all. In an unmyelinated axon, the action potential at one point becomes the stimulus for the next: reversal of polarity sets up a potential difference between the active patch and the adjacent resting membrane, and a local circuit of current flows between them, positive charges moving passively toward the area of negativity on both surfaces; the adjacent patch depolarises, reaches threshold, fires its own action potential while the first patch returns to rest, and the whole process repeats down the fibre, with conduction speed proportional to the square root of fibre diameter. If the fibre is stimulated in the middle, two impulses set off in opposite directions.

### Key determinants
In a living animal, impulses normally travel one way only — from receptor or synapse toward the axon's termination — and this is orthodromic conduction; the reverse is antidromic. Because a synapse, unlike an axon, only carries a signal in one direction, an antidromic impulse fails to pass the first synapse it meets and dies out there, which is why normal function only ever shows orthodromic propagation even though the axon itself could carry current either way.

### Clinical significance
The local-circuit mechanism is the reason a demyelinating or compressive lesion of an axon slows conduction rather than only weakening it — the current still has to depolarise every adjacent patch of exposed membrane in sequence, and anything that increases the resistance or leakiness of that local circuit slows the chain. The one-way rule that orthodromic/antidromic conduction sets up is also the reason a peripheral nerve injury's effects are felt in the sensory and motor directions consistently, rather than randomly, since the synapse enforces the direction even when the axon itself is undamaged enough to conduct either way.

### Common misconceptions
Three recur. The first is picturing the action potential as one wave physically travelling along the axon like a wave on a rope, rather than a chain of freshly regenerated, identical action potentials each triggered by its neighbour's local circuit. The second is assuming a stronger stimulus makes a bigger single-fibre action potential; above threshold, amplitude is fixed by the all-or-none law, and only recruiting more fibres (a different, whole-nerve phenomenon) changes the total signal. The third is assuming antidromic conduction cannot happen at all; the axon conducts it perfectly well, and what stops it is the synapse, not the fibre.

## sections
### Definition
Accommodation of the nerve fibre is what happens when a subthreshold stimulus is raised gradually, slowly, up to what would otherwise be threshold level: no response is produced, and the nerve is said to be accommodated. Nerve fibres are classified into three types by thickness and conduction velocity: A-fibres, 2 to 20 micrometres, 20 to 120 m/sec, 0.5 msec spike, exemplified by somatic motor fibres (further subdivided into alpha, beta, gamma and delta); B-fibres, 1 to 5 micrometres, 5 to 15 m/sec, 1.0 msec spike, exemplified by preganglionic autonomic fibres; C-fibres, under 1 micrometre, 0.5 to 2 m/sec, 2.0 msec spike, exemplified by postganglionic autonomic fibres. Neurotrophins are proteins necessary for neuronal development, growth and survival, secreted by glial cells, muscles, or other structures a neuron innervates.

### Mechanism
Accommodation happens because the same slow rise in current that opens Na+ activation gates slowly also gives the Na+ inactivation gates and the K+ activation gates time to catch up: the slow "opening" of Na+ channels with slow Na+ entry is balanced by inactivation closure of those same Na+ channels and opening of K+ channels, so the depolarising drive never outpaces the processes opposing it and the firing level is never reached. Neurotrophins are internalized at the axon terminal, near the target cell that released them, and then carried by retrograde transport back along the axon to the neuronal cell body — the opposite direction from most of the axon's own outgoing cargo.

### Key determinants
Beyond conduction speed, the three fibre classes differ in sensitivity to environmental factors, and not always in the direction diameter alone predicts: local anaesthetics depress transmission in the thin, slow C fibres before they touch the thick A fibres; pressure on a nerve can abolish conduction in A fibres while C fibres stay relatively intact; and B fibres, not the thinnest C fibres, are the most susceptible to hypoxia, with C fibres the least affected.

### Clinical significance
The differential sensitivity table is exactly what a local anaesthetic block or a compressed nerve produces clinically: pain and temperature sensation (carried by thin fibres) is lost before touch and motor function (carried by thick fibres) under a local anaesthetic, while sustained pressure on a limb does the reverse, knocking out motor and fine touch before pain. Accommodation is the physiological basis for why a gradually applied electrical or mechanical stimulus can fail to provoke a response that an abrupt one of the same final intensity would produce.

### Common misconceptions
Two recur. The first is assuming thinner, slower fibres are always the most vulnerable to every kind of insult; the book's own table contradicts this for hypoxia, where B fibres, not C fibres, are hit hardest. The second is treating accommodation as simply "not reaching threshold"; the stimulus does reach what would be threshold if applied quickly — what defeats it is the slow rate of rise giving the opposing channels time to act.

## summary
Neuromuscular transmission carries a nerve impulse from an alpha motor neuron across a structurally specialised junction to a skeletal muscle fibre. The motor end plate, thickened and folded, is rich in acetylcholine receptors; the synaptic cleft between it and the nerve terminal carries acetylcholinesterase on its basal lamina. Transmission is one-directional, carries a fixed delay of about 0.5 msec, fatigues with repeated use, and is shaped by three classes of drug — acetylcholine-like agonists, anticholinesterases, and curariform blockers — plus by Ca2+ and Mg2+. Even at rest, single vesicles rupture spontaneously and produce a miniature end-plate potential, the same quantal event that, multiplied by an arriving impulse, produces the full response.

## sections
### Definition
Neuromuscular transmission is the transmission of nerve impulses from an alpha motor neuron to skeletal muscle fibres. The alpha motor neuron's axon divides into terminals, each supplying several muscle fibres, but each individual fibre receives only one axon terminal, packed with acetylcholine vesicles. The nerve ending sits in a depression in the muscle membrane; the extracellular space between them is the synaptic cleft, carrying acetylcholinesterase bound to the basal lamina. The muscle membrane there is thickened and folded into junctional folds, forming the motor end plate, rich in acetylcholine receptors.

### Mechanism
An arriving nerve impulse opens voltage-gated Ca2+ channels at the terminal; Ca2+ enters and ruptures acetylcholine vesicles by exocytosis. Acetylcholine crosses the cleft and binds its receptor, a ligand-gated channel, on the end plate; the channel opens, Na+ flows in, and the end plate depolarises — the end-plate potential (EPP). The EPP is itself graded and non-propagated, but it acts as a stimulus that depolarises the adjacent muscle membrane to its firing level, generating action potentials on either side of the end plate that propagate in both directions along the fibre and trigger contraction. Acetylcholine then dissociates from its receptor and is hydrolysed by acetylcholinesterase in the cleft — degradation the book states is necessary to prevent multiple contractions from a single impulse. New vesicles are later formed from invaginations of the presynaptic membrane and refilled for reuse. At rest, a few vesicles rupture spontaneously with no impulse at all, producing a minute depolarisation at the end plate — the miniature end-plate potential — the same quantal unit that a full impulse simply multiplies many times over.

### Key determinants
Four properties define the transmission itself: it is unidirectional, nerve to muscle only; it carries a delay of about 0.5 msec, the time needed for release, permeability change, Na+ inflow and depolarisation to threshold; it fatigues easily as vesicles are exhausted by repeated stimulation; and it is sensitive to ions, since Ca2+ entry triggers release while excess Mg2+ competes with Ca2+ and greatly reduces it. Three drug classes act on it. Acetylcholine-like agonists — methacholine, carbachol, low-dose nicotine — are not broken down by cholinesterase, so their effect persists for minutes to hours. Anticholinesterases — neostigmine, physostigmine, di-isopropyl fluorophosphate — let acetylcholine accumulate until it repetitively stimulates the fibre. Curariform drugs — curare — compete with acetylcholine for the receptor and block transmission outright.

### Clinical significance
Myasthenia gravis, an autoimmune disease with antibodies against the acetylcholine receptor, weakens and tires skeletal muscle, sometimes fatally through respiratory muscle paralysis; it is treated with an anticholinesterase such as neostigmine, which raises the acetylcholine available to whatever receptors are left. That treatment logic depends directly on the mechanism above: more acetylcholine, held around longer, compensates for fewer functioning receptors, the same principle by which any anticholinesterase in this article's drug-class list works.

### Common misconceptions
Two recur. The first is grouping neostigmine with curare because both act on the acetylcholine receptor pathway; they act oppositely — an anticholinesterase increases the acetylcholine available at the receptor, while curare blocks the receptor from acetylcholine altogether. The second is assuming a miniature end-plate potential needs some weak nerve signal; it happens at rest, from spontaneous vesicle rupture, with no impulse involved at all.

## summary
The body holds over four hundred voluntary skeletal muscles, whose contraction depends entirely on their nerve supply and which together produce locomotion, posture, heat and venous return. Myosin's two heavy chains form the flexible cross-bridge heads that will later cycle against actin, whose active site tropomyosin covers at rest — held there by the three troponin subunits, I, T and C. The muscle fibre's own action potential resembles the nerve's but finishes well before contraction does, which is why skeletal muscle, unlike cardiac muscle, can be tetanised. A single fibre obeys the all-or-none law exactly as a nerve does, and the muscle twitch is the smallest unit of mechanical response that one action potential produces.

## sections
### Definition
Skeletal muscles are attached to the bones; the body contains over four hundred voluntary skeletal muscles, whose contraction depends on their nerve supply, and skeletal muscle performs four functions: force production for locomotion and breathing, force production for posture and joint stabilisation, heat production, and helping venous drainage. (Morphology and the sarcomere are named as their own headings in the book, but each prints only "refer to histology" with no independent content here, so no separate concept is minted for either; they are covered by the histology department's own concept batch.)

### Mechanism
The myosin molecule has two heavy chains and four light chains; the heavy chains form a helix whose terminal portions, with the light chains, form two arms ending in globular heads — the cross-bridges — each carrying an actin-binding site, an ATP-binding site and an ATPase site, flexible at two hinges. The actin molecule is two chains coiled as a helix, carrying a myosin-binding active site that tropomyosin covers at rest. Troponin, attaching tropomyosin to actin, has three subunits: troponin I binds actin strongly; troponin T binds tropomyosin strongly; troponin C binds Ca2+ strongly, and Ca2+ binding troponin C is what initiates contraction. Electrically, the fibre resembles a nerve fibre with differences: resting potential about −90 mV, action potential lasting 2 to 4 msec, conducted at about 5 m/sec, and preceding the mechanical contraction by about 2 msec. During its own action potential the fibre is refractory exactly as a nerve fibre is, but because the electrical event finishes so far ahead of the mechanical one, the fibre has already regained full excitability by the time it is contracting — which is why skeletal muscle, unlike cardiac muscle, can be tetanised.

### Key determinants
A single skeletal muscle fibre obeys the all-or-none law: it contracts maximally or not at all, and a threshold stimulus already produces that maximal single-fibre contraction — a supra-threshold stimulus adds nothing more at the single-fibre level (the whole muscle still grades its response, but only by recruiting more fibres, a separate mechanism covered elsewhere in this module). The muscle twitch is the brief contraction-then-relaxation produced by one action potential, starting about 2 msec after depolarisation — the same 2 msec offset the electrical-changes section states between the action potential and the contraction it triggers.

### Clinical significance
The gap between the muscle action potential's own refractory period and the much longer mechanical twitch it triggers is what makes voluntary tetanic contraction possible at all — without it, a fibre could not be re-stimulated quickly enough to summate twitches into a sustained contraction, which is exactly how normal voluntary movement, not just an experimental tetanus, is generated. The same electrical/mechanical separation is absent in cardiac muscle, whose own action potential is deliberately prolonged to overlap the contraction and prevent tetanus — a comparison the book does not draw explicitly on this page but that follows directly from what it states here.

### Common misconceptions
Three recur. The first is assuming a skeletal fibre cannot be re-stimulated during a contraction because it is still refractory; the refractory period belongs to the brief electrical event, which is over well before the much longer mechanical twitch has finished. The second is assuming troponin C alone physically blocks the active site; tropomyosin does that, and troponin C's job is to sense Ca2+ and move tropomyosin off the site. The third is extending the single-fibre all-or-none law to the whole muscle without qualification; the whole muscle's response does grade with stimulus strength, but only through recruiting more fibres, not through any one fibre contracting by degrees.

## summary
Isometric and isotonic contraction are defined by the same experimental setup with one variable changed: a heavy load that the muscle cannot lift produces isometric contraction, where the whole muscle's length stays fixed while its internal sarcomeres shorten and stretch the series elastic component instead; a lighter load produces isotonic contraction, which starts isometric and then shortens once tension is enough to lift it. The book compares the two across seven properties, including a stated mechanical efficiency of zero for isometric and 20 to 25 percent for isotonic contraction. This whole heading is excluded from the 2025-2026 final theoretical exam, but every later heading on grading, length-tension and load-velocity in this module assumes it.

## sections
### Definition
Skeletal muscle carries, besides its contractile element, elastic and viscous elements in series with it, mainly in the tendons — the series elastic component. In isometric contraction, an isolated muscle is fixed at one end, a heavy load stretches it to a set degree at the other, and a support then prevents further stretch; on stimulation, the muscle cannot shorten because the load is too heavy, but the sarcomeres inside the fibres do shorten, stretching the series elastic elements, so tension rises to a maximum while the whole muscle's length stays constant. In isotonic contraction, a smaller load is used and stretched the same degree; on stimulation, contraction starts isometric and tension rises until it can lift the load, and from that point the muscle shortens while tension stays constant.

### Mechanism
With a heavier load, the isometric phase before shortening starts lasts longer, and the rate and extent of the isotonic shortening that follows are both less; if the load is too large, the maximum tension the muscle can generate isometrically is never enough to lift it at all, and the contraction never becomes isotonic. The book compares the two types across seven properties: tension rises in isometric contraction but stays constant in isotonic; length stays constant in isometric but shortens in isotonic; myofibril sliding is less in isometric and more in isotonic; duration is shorter for isometric and longer for isotonic; energy need is less for isometric, since no load moves, and greater for isotonic, since the load moves a distance; external work is zero for isometric and positive for isotonic, for the same reason; and mechanical efficiency — the percentage of energy input converted into work — is zero for isometric and 20 to 25 percent for isotonic.

### Key determinants
The book's own worked examples fix the abstract definitions to everyday movement. Tensing the quadriceps to keep the knee stiff while standing is isometric — posture and joint stabilisation against gravity, with no load moved. Lifting a heavy weight with the biceps starts isometric and finishes isotonic, exactly as the mechanism above describes. Running mixes both: isometric when a leg hits the ground, isotonic to move the limbs forward.

### Clinical significance
The zero mechanical efficiency the book assigns to isometric contraction is a genuinely counter-intuitive clinical point: sustained isometric muscle work, such as maintaining posture for long periods, is real metabolic and fatigue-generating effort even though, by this article's own strict definition of work, none of that energy moves an external load — which is exactly why postural fatigue and isometric-exercise cardiovascular strain are both real phenomena despite no limb visibly moving.

### Common misconceptions
Two recur. The first is believing an isometric contraction involves no shortening anywhere in the muscle; the whole muscle's length is fixed, but the sarcomeres inside it do shorten, taken up by the series elastic component rather than by moving the load. The second is assuming isometric contraction does no work because it feels effortful; "no external work" here means specifically that the load is not moved through a distance, the physics definition, not that no metabolic energy is spent.

## summary
A whole skeletal muscle grades its response by two mechanisms that leave the single-fibre all-or-none law untouched: recruiting more motor units as stimulus strength rises, and summating twitches into tetanus as stimulation frequency rises, with Treppe adding a separate, rest-dependent rise in twitch size of its own. How much tension a fully activated fibre can develop then depends on two more variables the book plots as curves: its own sarcomere length, peaking at about 2.2 micrometres of optimal filament overlap, and, for an isotonic contraction, the afterload it must lift, which slows and shortens shortening as it rises.

## sections
### Definition
A motor unit is one spinal motor neuron together with every muscle fibre its branching axon supplies; small units of 3 to 6 fibres serve fine movements such as the hand and eye, while large units of 100 to 200 fibres serve gross movements such as the leg and back. Afterload is the load a muscle encounters only after it has already started to contract, the variable the load-velocity relationship is built around.

### Mechanism
The whole muscle's contraction is graded two ways. Recruitment: increasing stimulus strength activates more motor units, so the whole-muscle response rises gradually until a maximal stimulus activates every unit, after which a supramaximal stimulus adds nothing, since each fibre already responds maximally under its own all-or-none law. Frequency summation: raising stimulation frequency releases more Ca2+ from the sarcoplasmic reticulum each time, so contractions with incomplete relaxation between them (incomplete tetanus, or clonus) fuse, at a high enough rate, into complete tetanus with no relaxation at all; complete tetanus develops about four times the tension of an individual twitch, from accumulated free Ca2+ and continuous cross-bridge cycling. Treppe, the staircase phenomenon, is the separate, progressive rise in the size of individual twitches — each one still allowed to relax fully — to a plateau, during repetitive stimulation of a previously rested muscle, explained by the same rising free Ca2+.

### Key determinants
The length-tension relationship plots increasing initial fibre length (preload) against the maximal active tension isometric contraction can develop: at minimal length, tension is zero; it rises with stretch up to a limit, then falls with further stretch. The mechanism is filament overlap: maximal force occurs at a sarcomere length of about 2.2 micrometres, the muscle's own resting length inside the body, where every thick-filament cross-bridge has an actin filament opposite it; stretching past 2.2 micrometres reduces overlap, so some cross-bridges have nothing to bind; shortening below it makes the two actin filaments overlap each other as well as myosin, which also impedes force. The load-velocity relationship works in isotonic contraction: increasing afterload decreases the velocity of shortening, since each cross-bridge cycle takes longer under load, and decreases the amount of shortening too; the maximal velocity of shortening, V-max, occurs only at zero load, which the book notes is purely theoretical, since a real load is never actually zero.

### Clinical significance
The length-tension curve is the physiological basis for why a muscle stretched too far by, say, an overextended joint or an overinflated organ generates less force than one at its own optimal resting length, and it is also the reason physiotherapy positions a muscle near its resting length before testing maximal strength. Motor unit size correlates directly with the precision a muscle group is built for — the same 3-to-6-fibre-versus-100-to-200-fibre contrast that explains why hand and eye movements can be fine-tuned in a way leg and back movements cannot.

### Common misconceptions
Three recur. The first is confusing Treppe with incomplete tetanus, since both involve rising tension over repeated stimuli; Treppe is a rise in separate twitches each fully relaxed between stimuli, while incomplete tetanus is a fusion of contractions because relaxation is not allowed to finish. The second is assuming more stretch always means more force, true only up to the 2.2-micrometre optimum, after which the same variable that helped starts costing filament overlap instead. The third is treating V-max as an achievable value rather than the theoretical limit the book states it is, since a real afterload can never be zero.

## summary
Muscle fatigue weakens and prolongs contraction and leaves relaxation incomplete, from four causes: metabolite build-up, substrate depletion, impaired junctional transmission, and interrupted blood flow. Behind it is a strict sequence of three ATP-regeneration systems, each one taking over as the last runs out: the phosphagen system, powering the first 10 to 15 seconds; the glycogen-lactic acid system, adding 30 to 40 more; and the aerobic system, sustaining activity indefinitely as long as nutrients and oxygen hold out. The oxygen debt is the extra oxygen consumption paid back afterward to restore what the first two systems used up.

## sections
### Definition
Prolonged, strong contraction leads to muscle fatigue: decreased strength of contraction, prolonged duration, and incomplete relaxation — a contracture. Four things cause it: accumulation of metabolites such as lactic acid, which raises intracellular acidity; depletion of muscle ATP, glycogen and creatine phosphate; diminished transmission at the neuromuscular junction; and interrupted blood flow through the contracting muscle, with loss of nutrient and especially oxygen supply.

### Mechanism
At rest, muscle spends energy maintaining the resting potential, synthesising substances such as glycogen, and producing tone. During contraction, energy demand rises sharply; ATP is the only immediate energy source, hydrolysed anaerobically by the myosin ATPase, and stored ATP alone supports maximal contraction for only 5 to 6 seconds. Three systems reform it, one after another. The phosphagen system transfers energy from phosphocreatine — held at two to three times ATP's own concentration — to ADP almost instantly, powering maximal activity for 10 to 15 seconds, enough for a 100-metre run; phosphocreatine is restored later, during relaxation, by the reverse reaction. The glycogen-lactic acid system adds a further 30 to 40 seconds through anaerobic glycolysis, producing lactic acid, which itself limits the system by causing extreme fatigue; that lactic acid is later partly oxidised to pyruvic acid, partly reconverted by the liver into glucose to replenish muscle glycogen, and partly used as fuel by the heart. The aerobic system oxidises glucose, fatty acids and amino acids in the mitochondria and can sustain activity indefinitely, given enough nutrients and oxygen; free fatty acids are the main substrate for resting muscle and recovery.

### Key determinants
During recovery, ventilation and oxygen consumption stay elevated above resting levels to remove excess lactate and replenish ATP, creatine phosphate and myoglobin-bound oxygen — this extra post-exercise oxygen consumption is the oxygen debt, measured by subtracting basal oxygen consumption from the total consumed until a constant basal level returns. Which system dominates depends on effort duration: the phosphagen system for power surges, the aerobic system for prolonged activity, and the glycogen-lactic acid system for intermediate efforts such as 200-to-800-metre races.

### Clinical significance
The sequence explains why a sprinter and a marathon runner fatigue by entirely different mechanisms even though both are "tired": a sprint exhausts the phosphagen and glycolytic systems within a minute or so, producing lactic-acid-driven fatigue, while endurance fatigue is limited instead by substrate and oxygen delivery to the aerobic system over a much longer time course. The same four causes of fatigue also explain clinically why interrupted blood flow (a tourniquet, a compartment syndrome) produces fatigue and pain far faster than exercise alone would, since it removes both nutrient and oxygen supply at once.

### Common misconceptions
Two recur. The first is reducing fatigue to "running out of ATP" alone; the book lists four separate causes, and junctional transmission failure and blood-flow interruption both contribute independently of substrate depletion. The second is treating the three energy systems as alternatives a muscle chooses between rather than a strict sequence it moves through as each system's own endurance time runs out.

## summary
Four short topics close the department book's account of skeletal muscle. Electromyography records electrical activity by surface disc or needle electrode. Hypertrophy thickens existing fibres, with fibre number unchanged. Denervation runs through a fixed sequence — atrophy, then visible fasciculation from the dying nerve, then invisible fibrillation from the muscle's own hypersensitivity to acetylcholine — each stage distinguished by cause and by which EMG electrode can detect it. Rigor mortis is the same ATP-detachment mechanism already seen in a living, fatigued muscle, made permanent by death, and it has a stated forensic use.

## sections
### Definition
Electromyography is a record of a muscle's electrical activity on a cathode ray oscilloscope, picked up either by a metal disc electrode on the skin overlying the muscle or by a hypodermic needle electrode inserted into it. Muscular hypertrophy is an increase in muscle size from forceful activity, with fibre number unchanged; existing fibres increase in thickness, in total myofibril number, and in ATP, creatine phosphate and glycogen content.

### Mechanism
Nerve injury paralyses the muscle it supplies — a lower motor neuron lesion — through a fixed sequence. Muscle atrophy is a decrease in muscle size as fibres are gradually replaced by fibrous tissue. Muscle fasciculation is spontaneous contraction of whole motor units, strong enough to see under the skin, occurring in the first few days as the degenerating nerve discharges spontaneous impulses; it is picked up by surface disc electrodes. Muscle fibrillation is spontaneous contraction of separate individual fibres, after the motor nerve has fully degenerated, caused by denervation hypersensitivity to circulating acetylcholine; invisible under the skin, it can only be picked up by a needle electrode. Rigor mortis runs on the identical ATP-detachment mechanism already seen in living muscle: several hours after death, every muscle in the body goes into contracture and becomes rigid without any action potentials, because ATP is no longer available to separate the actin and myosin filaments. The muscles stay in rigor until the proteins themselves are destroyed by bacterial putrefaction, 15 to 25 hours later.

### Key determinants
Fasciculation and fibrillation are distinguished by three linked facts, not one: fasciculation comes from the dying nerve itself and appears within days, visibly, and is caught by a surface electrode; fibrillation comes from the muscle's own hypersensitivity after the nerve has completely degenerated, is invisible, and needs a needle electrode. Rigor mortis has a stated medicolegal use: it helps estimate time of death, from the same 15-to-25-hour window that ends it.

### Clinical significance
The fasciculation-then-fibrillation sequence, and which electrode catches which, is the practical basis for using EMG to stage a lower motor neuron lesion clinically — fasciculation on surface EMG points to a recent, still-degenerating nerve injury, while fibrillation on needle EMG points to a nerve injury old enough that degeneration is complete. Rigor mortis's own time window is the physiological fact behind its forensic use in estimating time of death, one of the few places in this chapter where the book states a direct medicolegal application.

### Common misconceptions

## summary
Single-unit smooth muscle, joined by gap junctions, spreads an action potential through a whole sheet as a syncytium; multi-unit smooth muscle, without those bridges, fires each fibre separately under dense neural control. Both types sit on an unstable resting potential, riding slow waves that are not themselves action potentials, until a wave happens to reach about −35 mV and triggers one. That action potential comes in two forms — a brief spike or a long plateau — and runs mainly on voltage-gated Ca2+ channels rather than the Na+ channels nerve and skeletal muscle depend on, which is the whole reason it is so much slower.

## sections
### Definition
Smooth muscle divides into two types. Visceral (single-unit) smooth muscle occurs in large sheets joined by gap junctions, so an action potential spreads easily from fibre to fibre and the whole sheet behaves as one unit obeying the all-or-none law; it is only superficially innervated, is spontaneously active, is controlled by hormones, chemicals and neurotransmitters, and is found mainly in hollow viscera such as the gut, ureters and many blood vessels. Multi-unit smooth muscle is made of individual fibres without interconnecting bridges, so an action potential cannot spread between fibres; it behaves as separate motor units, each fibre obeying the all-or-none law on its own, is densely innervated with contraction under neural control, and is found in the ciliary muscle, the iris, the vas deferens and the erector pili muscles of the skin.

### Mechanism
In relative rest, smooth muscle's membrane potential is unstable, averaging about −50 to −60 mV, with slow waves — such as the slow waves of the gut — superimposed on it; these waves are not action potentials and cannot themselves cause contraction, but once a wave reaches about −35 mV, an action potential is triggered and spreads over the muscle. Smooth muscle action potentials take two forms: spike potentials, which resemble the skeletal-muscle spike or ride on top of slow waves or recur rhythmically as pacemaker potentials, lasting about 50 msec; and action potentials with a plateau, which depolarise similarly but whose repolarisation is delayed for several hundred to several thousand milliseconds, underlying prolonged contraction in some smooth muscle types. The cell membrane of smooth muscle carries many voltage-gated Ca2+ channels but very few voltage-gated Na+ channels, so it is Ca2+ inflow, not Na+, that mainly generates the action potential; Ca2+ channels also open many times more slowly than Na+ channels, which is why smooth muscle action potentials are so much slower than nerve or skeletal muscle ones.

### Key determinants
Whether an action potential can even spread from one fibre to its neighbour depends entirely on which type of smooth muscle is involved: gap junctions make single-unit smooth muscle a functional syncytium, so one triggered fibre effectively fires the whole sheet, while multi-unit smooth muscle's lack of interconnecting bridges means each fibre must be individually innervated and individually triggered, exactly as a skeletal motor unit is.

### Clinical significance
The syncytial spread of single-unit smooth muscle is what lets a wave of gut peristalsis or a ureteric contraction propagate along an organ from a single pacemaker region, without needing point-by-point nerve stimulation the way multi-unit tissue such as the iris does. The plateau form of the action potential, which the book links to prolonged contraction, is consistent with tissues such as the ureter or parts of the vascular system that must sustain tone or a slow peristaltic wave rather than fire and relax quickly.

### Common misconceptions
Two recur. The first is assuming slow waves are themselves action potentials because both appear on the same trace; a slow wave is a fluctuation in resting potential that cannot cause contraction alone, and only becomes a trigger if it happens to reach about −35 mV. The second is assuming smooth muscle's action potential runs on the same sodium-channel mechanism as nerve or skeletal muscle simply because it is called by the same name; the depolarising current here is carried mainly by Ca2+, and the slower Ca2+ channel kinetics are the direct cause of the whole action potential's slower time course.

## summary
Smooth muscle regulates contraction on the myosin side, not the actin side: Ca2+ binds calmodulin instead of troponin, and the complex activates myosin light-chain kinase, whose phosphorylation of the myosin head starts cross-bridge cycling. Cross-bridges that dephosphorylate without detaching — latch bridges — hold tone for a long time on very little ATP, which is why smooth muscle is fatigue-resistant. The same tissue is also plastic: a quick stretch raises tension, but a stretch held in place lets tension fall again as the cross-bridges readjust position, which is the property that lets the bladder fill without much rise in pressure.

## sections
### Definition
Excitation-contraction coupling in smooth muscle begins with Ca2+ entry: depolarisation opens voltage-gated Ca2+ channels, and Ca2+ flows in down its gradient; that entering Ca2+ may trigger further release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum through Ca2+-gated channels. Hormones and neurotransmitters can also open a ligand-gated Ca2+ channel or release Ca2+ from the sarcoplasmic reticulum through IP3-gated channels, so contraction can start without any action potential at all. Plasticity is smooth muscle's property of exerting increased tension when quickly stretched, but tension that gradually decreases if the stretch is held.

### Mechanism
Once intracellular Ca2+ rises, it binds calmodulin, and the calcium-calmodulin complex activates myosin light-chain kinase (MLCK), which phosphorylates the regulatory light chain on the myosin head, triggering ATP hydrolysis and cross-bridge cycling. Phosphorylated cross-bridges keep cycling until myosin light-chain phosphatase dephosphorylates them, so the amount of active myosin at any moment depends on the relative activity of the kinase and the phosphatase. Relaxation follows a fall in intracellular Ca2+, driven by a pump moving it into the extracellular fluid and a pump moving it into the sarcoplasmic reticulum, both slower than skeletal muscle's fast sarcoplasmic reticulum pump — which is why smooth muscle contraction lasts longer. Dephosphorylated cross-bridges do not all detach: those still attached to actin are latch bridges, cycling not at all or very slowly, using very little ATP, and letting smooth muscle hold tone cheaply for a long time. Plasticity works differently again: it results from the myosin cross-bridges readjusting their position on the thin filaments once a stretch is maintained, rather than from any change in Ca2+ signalling.

### Key determinants
Two properties, both about how long a mechanical state persists, sit side by side in this material without being the same thing: latch bridges make a Ca2+-triggered contraction cheap to maintain once it has started, while plasticity determines what happens to tension over time under a sustained external stretch, independent of whether the muscle is actively contracting.

### Clinical significance
Plasticity is the direct physiological reason urine can accumulate in the urinary bladder without much rise in intravesical pressure — the detrusor muscle first tenses against the filling stretch and then relaxes back down as the cross-bridges readjust, letting a hollow organ store an increasing volume without a matching rise in pressure until it nears capacity. Latch bridges are the mechanistic reason vascular and visceral smooth muscle can sustain tone — arteriolar tone, sphincter closure — for hours with a fraction of the ATP a skeletal muscle contraction of comparable force would need.

### Common misconceptions
Two recur. The first is importing troponin from the skeletal mechanism; smooth muscle has no troponin regulating the actin side at all, and the regulation sits on the myosin side through calmodulin and MLCK instead. The second is confusing plasticity with the stretch-triggered contraction that is one of the five factors controlling smooth muscle contraction; they are opposite time-courses of the same tissue's response to stretch — an immediate, brief stretch raises tension (the stretch response), while a stretch that is held raises tension only transiently before it falls again (plasticity).
