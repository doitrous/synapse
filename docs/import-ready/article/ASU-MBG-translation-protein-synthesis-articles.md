## summary
Two existing translation articles in this corpus (Kasr's eukaryotic-focused protein-synthesis chapter and Alexandria's transcription-and-translation overview) already teach the elongation cycle's A-to-P translocation, eukaryotic initiation with Met-tRNAi, and stop-codon-triggered termination. This module's own 65-question "Translation / Protein Synthesis" bank asks four things neither of those articles covers: how prokaryotic initiation differs (Shine-Dalgarno base pairing, formylmethionine, direct P-site entry), the ribosome's third (E) site and why polysome chain length runs 5'-to-3', peptidyl transferase's molecular identity as a ribozyme built from 28S/23S rRNA rather than a protein, and what actually keeps translation accurate (aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase specificity — the "second genetic code" — plus small-subunit decoding). This is a deliberately scoped deepening article: it teaches exactly those four gaps and assumes the reader already has the elongation/initiation/termination basics from the two existing articles it cross-links.

## sections
### Definition
Bacterial and eukaryotic translation both convert an mRNA sequence into a polypeptide using ribosomes, tRNAs and aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, but they start differently. Eukaryotic initiation, taught elsewhere in this corpus, begins with a 5'-cap-dependent scan by the 40S subunit to the AUG codon, where methionyl-tRNA (Met-tRNAi, not formylated) binds before the 60S subunit joins. Prokaryotic initiation instead begins when the 30S ribosomal subunit binds directly to the mRNA at the Shine-Dalgarno sequence — a purine-rich stretch a few bases upstream of the start codon that base-pairs, by hydrogen bonds, with a complementary sequence near the 3' end of 16S rRNA. No 5' cap is involved. The bacterial initiator tRNA carries N-formylmethionine (fMet-tRNAfMet), and unlike every elongator aminoacyl-tRNA — which must enter through the A site — this initiator tRNA is loaded directly into the P site alongside the small subunit, before the 50S subunit joins to complete the 70S initiation complex.

### Mechanism
The elongating ribosome does not have only two tRNA-binding sites. Alongside the A (aminoacyl) site, where each new charged tRNA arrives, and the P (peptidyl) site, which holds the tRNA carrying the growing chain, there is a third site: the E (exit) site, where the tRNA vacated of its amino acid sits immediately before leaving the ribosome. Each translocation step, which moves the ribosome one codon (three nucleotides) toward the mRNA's 3' end, shifts the peptide-bearing tRNA from A to P and, simultaneously, shifts the now-empty tRNA that had been in P into E. Because translation reads 5' to 3' and each ribosome that started earliest has travelled furthest, in a polysome (several ribosomes translating one mRNA simultaneously) the ribosome nearest the 3' end always carries the longest, most nearly finished peptide chain — the one nearest the 5' end has only just begun.

Peptide bond formation, at every one of these cycles, and the final hydrolytic release of the finished polypeptide at termination, are both catalysed by peptidyl transferase. This activity is not carried by a ribosomal protein at all — it is a ribozyme, meaning its catalysis is performed by ribosomal RNA itself: 28S rRNA within the eukaryotic 60S subunit, and the corresponding 23S rRNA within the prokaryotic 50S subunit. Peptidyl transferase is one of the classic proofs, alongside ribonuclease P and self-splicing introns, that RNA can catalyse biological reactions on its own.

### Key determinants
Translation's accuracy is not primarily a ribosomal property. The deeper safeguard sits one step earlier, at the 20 aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases: each recognises exactly one amino acid and exactly one matching tRNA, charging them together with very high specificity. This specificity is sometimes called the "second genetic code," because a synthetase error — attaching the wrong amino acid to a tRNA — would be invisible to every step downstream, which reads only the anticodon, never the amino acid actually carried. A second, smaller check happens at the ribosome's small subunit, whose decoding center monitors how well each incoming aminoacyl-tRNA's anticodon actually pairs with the mRNA codon sitting in the A site, rejecting poor matches before peptidyl transferase can act.

### Clinical significance
Because bacterial and human translation differ at several of the points this article covers — a distinct Shine-Dalgarno-dependent initiation mechanism, a 70S ribosome built from 30S/50S subunits with their own rRNA species (16S, 23S) rather than the human 40S/60S/18S/28S set — several antibiotic classes exploit exactly this apparatus selectively. Aminoglycosides distort the 30S subunit's decoding center, chloramphenicol blocks the bacterial (23S rRNA) peptidyl transferase specifically, and macrolides block the bacterial exit tunnel — all while sparing the structurally distinct human ribosome.

## summary
Two unrelated but frequently tested facts about translation share no existing covering article in this corpus: how many high-energy phosphate bonds translation actually spends per amino acid added, and why prokaryotic mRNA is typically polycistronic while eukaryotic mRNA is monocistronic. This is a deliberately minimal article pairing both, scoped to exactly what this module's own question bank asks.

## sections
### Definition
Translation spends energy at defined steps. Activating a single amino acid onto its tRNA consumes two high-energy phosphate bonds: aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase hydrolyses ATP all the way to AMP and pyrophosphate (PPi), and the cell's subsequent hydrolysis of that pyrophosphate makes the reaction irreversible — equivalent to spending two ATP bonds, not one. During elongation, two further GTP molecules are spent per amino acid added: one when the elongation factor delivers the charged aminoacyl-tRNA to the ribosome's A site, and a second to drive translocation. That totals four high-energy phosphate bonds per amino acid incorporated during elongation. Peptide bond formation itself, catalysed by peptidyl transferase, needs no separate energy input — its energy was already stored in the high-energy ester bond linking the amino acid to its tRNA when it was first activated.

### Mechanism
Because the initiating amino acid is activated but not itself added by an elongation cycle, some departmental teaching simplifies a whole chain's energy cost to three bonds per residue (activation's two bonds plus one GTP for ribosome entry, without a separate translocation term) rather than the fuller four-bonds-per-residue accounting — for instance, arriving at roughly 30 high-energy bonds for a 10-amino-acid chain under the simplified convention. Both conventions exist in different teaching sources, and a question's own numbers usually reveal which one it intends.

Prokaryotic and eukaryotic genes are built for different jobs. A prokaryotic operon is typically transcribed into one polycistronic mRNA — a single transcript carrying more than one protein-coding region, so that one piece of mRNA can direct the ribosome to make several different, functionally related proteins together. Eukaryotic mRNA is monocistronic instead: one coding region per transcript, so translation normally yields one polypeptide per mRNA molecule.

### Key determinants
The polycistronic-versus-monocistronic split is not an isolated fact — it reflects two different overall strategies for gene regulation. Eukaryotic genes, consistent with their one-transcript-one-protein structure, also contain introns (non-coding sequences removed by splicing) and are commonly regulated by enhancers, DNA elements that can influence transcription from a distance. Prokaryotic operons, needing to coordinate several genes' expression together as one functional unit, generally lack both features.

### Clinical significance
The energy cost of translation is not merely arithmetic trivia — it is why protein synthesis is one of a cell's largest single energy expenditures, and why conditions that limit ATP/GTP availability (ischaemia, mitochondrial disease) impair translation before many other cellular processes. The polycistronic-operon strategy is also a validated antibacterial target: several classes of antibiotics disrupt bacterial translation initiation or elongation specifically, exploiting structural features (a 30S subunit reading a Shine-Dalgarno sequence, ribosomes translating a polycistronic message) that human, monocistronic translation does not share.
