## summary
A defect that makes no murmur of its own and often presents in adulthood. What gives it away is a second heart sound that stops responding to breathing.
## sections
### Definition
An atrial septal defect is a communication between the atria allowing shunting between the two circulations. Types are defined by position: secundum, in the region of the fossa ovalis and the commonest; primum, part of an atrioventricular septal defect; sinus venosus, near the caval openings and often with anomalous pulmonary venous drainage; and the rare coronary sinus defect.

A patent foramen ovale is not a septal defect but a persistent flap valve, present in a substantial minority of adults.
### Epidemiology
Atrial septal defect is among the commonest congenital cardiac lesions and the one most likely to present for the first time in adulthood, because the shunt is well tolerated for decades.
### Aetiology and risk factors
Most are sporadic. Some occur within syndromes — Down syndrome is associated with atrioventricular septal defects, and Holt–Oram syndrome with secundum defects and upper limb anomalies. Familial occurrence is described.
### Pathophysiology
Left atrial pressure slightly exceeds right, so blood shunts left to right. Systemic saturation is normal and the patient is not cyanosed; the problem is volume.

The shunted blood loads the right atrium, right ventricle and pulmonary circulation, which handle it repeatedly. The right ventricle dilates over years, and the increased pulmonary flow eventually produces pulmonary vascular changes.

Two clinical consequences follow directly. Because the atria communicate, they fill as a common chamber and the inspiratory increase in venous return is shared between them, so right ventricular output no longer varies with respiration — the second heart sound is **widely and fixedly split**. And because flow across the defect itself is low-velocity and silent, the murmur heard is not from the defect but from the increased flow crossing the pulmonary and tricuspid valves.

The communication also allows **paradoxical embolism**: thrombus from the systemic veins bypassing the pulmonary filter during a transient right-to-left pressure reversal.
### Clinical picture
Frequently asymptomatic in childhood and detected on examination.

In adulthood: exertional breathlessness, reduced exercise capacity, palpitation from atrial arrhythmia — which becomes common with age — and occasionally paradoxical embolic stroke as the presenting event.

Signs: a widely split second heart sound that does not vary with respiration; an ejection systolic murmur at the pulmonary area from increased flow; a mid-diastolic tricuspid flow murmur where the shunt is large; and a right ventricular heave.
### Investigation
ECG typically shows right axis deviation with incomplete right bundle branch block in secundum defects; a primum defect classically shows left axis deviation, which is a useful discriminator.

Chest radiograph may show cardiomegaly and pulmonary plethora.

Echocardiography is diagnostic: it demonstrates the defect, its position and size, the direction of shunting, right heart dilatation and pulmonary pressures. Transoesophageal imaging defines the anatomy for closure and detects sinus venosus defects and anomalous venous drainage, which transthoracic imaging misses.
### Management
Small defects with no right heart dilatation require no intervention.

Closure is indicated where there is right heart volume overload, and it improves symptoms and exercise capacity at any age. Secundum defects are usually closed percutaneously with a device; primum and sinus venosus defects require surgery.

**Closure is contraindicated once irreversible pulmonary vascular disease has developed**, since the defect is then relieving a fixed high-resistance pulmonary bed.

Arrhythmia is managed on its own merits, and the risk of atrial arrhythmia persists after closure in patients closed late.

All criteria and thresholds follow the adult congenital heart disease guideline in force; none is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Right heart dilatation and failure, atrial arrhythmia, pulmonary hypertension progressing in a minority to Eisenmenger physiology, and paradoxical embolism.

Prognosis after closure in childhood or early adulthood is excellent. Late closure improves symptoms but the arrhythmia risk remains.
### Classification
Classification by anatomical position is used because it determines whether percutaneous closure is possible and what associated lesions to look for.
## hold_these
Flow across the defect is silent; the murmur is from increased flow across the pulmonary valve.
Fixed splitting occurs because the atria fill as a common chamber and equalise the respiratory variation.
Closure is contraindicated once irreversible pulmonary vascular disease is established.
## lose_the_mark
Listening for a murmur from the defect itself.
Excluding a defect on a normal transthoracic study when a sinus venosus defect is suspected.
Closing a defect in a patient who has developed Eisenmenger physiology.

## summary
The loudest murmur in the chest often means the smallest problem. Grading this lesion by how it sounds gets it exactly backwards.
## sections
### Definition
A ventricular septal defect is a communication between the ventricles. Defects are described by position — perimembranous, the commonest; muscular; inlet, associated with atrioventricular septal defects; and outlet or subarterial.
### Epidemiology
Ventricular septal defect is the commonest congenital cardiac malformation at birth. Many small muscular and perimembranous defects close spontaneously in childhood, so the prevalence in adults is considerably lower than at birth.

Defects also arise acquired, as a mechanical complication of myocardial infarction.
### Aetiology and risk factors
Mostly sporadic. Associated with chromosomal abnormalities including Down syndrome, and with maternal diabetes, alcohol and certain drug exposures in pregnancy.

Acquired defects follow septal infarction.
### Pathophysiology
Left ventricular pressure greatly exceeds right, so blood shunts left to right during systole and is delivered to the pulmonary circulation, the left atrium and the left ventricle again. The left heart is therefore volume-loaded — which distinguishes this lesion from an atrial defect, where the right heart carries the load.

**Murmur intensity varies inversely with defect size.** A small defect maintains a large pressure gradient, generates high-velocity turbulent flow and a loud harsh murmur, and shunts very little. A large defect equalises ventricular pressures, so the gradient is small, the murmur is soft or absent, and the shunt is large. The dangerous defect is the quiet one.

Sustained high pulmonary flow and pressure remodel the pulmonary arterioles. If resistance rises above systemic, the shunt reverses and the patient becomes cyanosed — **Eisenmenger physiology** — at which point closure is contraindicated.
### Clinical picture
A small defect is asymptomatic with a loud murmur, and often presents as an incidental finding.

A large defect presents in infancy with heart failure once pulmonary vascular resistance falls in the weeks after birth: breathlessness, poor feeding, sweating and failure to thrive.

Signs: a harsh pansystolic murmur at the left sternal edge with a thrill in small defects; a softer murmur with signs of pulmonary hypertension in large ones; and in Eisenmenger physiology, cyanosis, clubbing and a loud pulmonary component of the second sound with the murmur diminished or gone.
### Investigation
ECG may be normal in small defects; large defects show biventricular hypertrophy, and right ventricular hypertrophy dominates once pulmonary vascular disease develops.

Chest radiograph shows cardiomegaly and pulmonary plethora with a large shunt.

Echocardiography defines the position, size, number and direction of shunting, estimates the gradient and pulmonary pressures, and identifies associated lesions and any aortic valve prolapse in outlet defects.

Cardiac catheterisation assesses pulmonary vascular resistance and its reversibility where closure is being considered in the presence of pulmonary hypertension.
### Management
Small defects with no haemodynamic consequence need no closure. Surveillance and awareness of endocarditis risk are sufficient.

Large defects are closed, surgically or in selected cases percutaneously, before irreversible pulmonary vascular disease develops. Timing is the central issue: closing early enough is what preserves the pulmonary circulation.

Heart failure in infancy is managed medically as a bridge to repair.

**Closure is contraindicated once Eisenmenger physiology is established.**

Endocarditis prophylaxis follows the guideline in force, which restricts it to defined high-risk groups rather than applying it to all defects.

All criteria and thresholds follow the guideline in force; none is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Heart failure in infancy, pulmonary vascular disease and Eisenmenger physiology, infective endocarditis, aortic regurgitation from cusp prolapse in outlet defects, and arrhythmia.

Prognosis after timely closure is excellent. Eisenmenger physiology carries a poor long-term prognosis and is largely preventable by early closure.
### Classification
Position determines the associated lesions and the approach to closure; size determines the physiology.
## hold_these
Murmur loudness varies inversely with defect size, so the quiet defect is the dangerous one.
The left heart is volume-loaded in a ventricular septal defect, unlike an atrial defect.
Closure must precede the development of irreversible pulmonary vascular disease.
## lose_the_mark
Grading severity by how loud the murmur is.
Closing a defect once the shunt has reversed.
Applying endocarditis prophylaxis universally rather than to the groups the current guideline defines.

## summary
A fetal shortcut that should have shut. The same physiology that closes it is used deliberately to keep it open in other lesions — which is why it is worth understanding rather than memorising.
## sections
### Definition
The ductus arteriosus connects the pulmonary artery to the descending aorta in fetal life, allowing blood to bypass the unventilated lungs. A patent ductus arteriosus is persistence of that connection after the normal period of closure.
### Epidemiology
Patency is common in preterm infants and becomes less frequent with increasing gestational age. In term infants and adults it is much less common and is often an isolated lesion.
### Aetiology and risk factors
Prematurity is the dominant factor. Others: perinatal hypoxia, high altitude, congenital rubella syndrome, and female sex. It occurs as part of syndromes and, importantly, as a necessary channel in duct-dependent congenital lesions.
### Pathophysiology
Fetal patency is maintained by low arterial oxygen tension and by circulating prostaglandin. After birth, rising oxygen and falling prostaglandin cause smooth muscle constriction and functional closure within days, followed by fibrosis into the ligamentum arteriosum.

If it stays open, the pressure gradient between aorta and pulmonary artery persists throughout the cardiac cycle — aortic pressure exceeds pulmonary pressure in both systole and diastole. Flow is therefore continuous, which produces the **continuous machinery murmur**, and diastolic run-off into the pulmonary artery lowers aortic diastolic pressure and widens the pulse pressure.

The shunt volume-loads the pulmonary circulation, the left atrium and the left ventricle. A large long-standing duct produces pulmonary vascular disease and can reverse, in which case cyanosis appears in the lower body while the upper body remains pink — differential cyanosis — because the duct enters the aorta distal to the head and arm vessels.

The same physiology is exploited therapeutically: in duct-dependent lesions the ductus is kept open deliberately to maintain either pulmonary or systemic flow.
### Clinical picture
A small duct is asymptomatic and found as a murmur.

A large duct in an infant causes heart failure, poor feeding and failure to thrive; in a preterm infant it contributes to respiratory deterioration.

Signs: a continuous machinery murmur, loudest below the left clavicle; a bounding pulse with wide pulse pressure; and a hyperdynamic apex. Where the shunt has reversed, differential cyanosis and clubbing of the toes but not the fingers.
### Investigation
Echocardiography is diagnostic and assesses size, direction of flow, chamber dimensions and pulmonary pressures.

ECG and chest radiograph may show left heart volume overload and pulmonary plethora with a large duct.
### Management
In preterm infants, closure may be achieved pharmacologically by inhibiting prostaglandin synthesis, or by catheter or surgical closure where that fails or is contraindicated. Whether and when to treat a duct in a preterm infant is an area of genuine and continuing debate, and practice follows current neonatal guidance rather than a fixed rule.

In older children and adults, a haemodynamically significant duct is closed, usually by a percutaneous device.

**Closure is contraindicated where the shunt has reversed.**

Where the circulation is duct-dependent, the duct is maintained rather than closed — the opposite intervention, and one that must never be confused with this one.

Agents, doses and closure criteria follow the guideline in force; none is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Heart failure, pulmonary vascular disease and shunt reversal, infective endarteritis, and aneurysm of the duct.

Prognosis after closure is excellent.
### Classification
Ducts are described by size and by haemodynamic significance, which is what determines whether closure is indicated.
## hold_these
Flow is continuous because the aortic-to-pulmonary gradient persists through both systole and diastole.
Differential cyanosis occurs because the duct enters the aorta distal to the head and arm vessels.
In duct-dependent lesions the ductus is kept open deliberately — the opposite goal.
## lose_the_mark
Closing a duct in a patient whose circulation depends on it.
Closing a duct after the shunt has reversed.
Treating every preterm duct as requiring closure, when the evidence and current guidance are genuinely contested.

## summary
Four features from one misplaced septum, and a child who squats because it works. The cyanosis is not fixed — it depends on a balance between two resistances, and that is why the physiology is treatable at the bedside.
## sections
### Definition
Tetralogy of Fallot comprises right ventricular outflow tract obstruction, a ventricular septal defect, an aorta overriding that defect, and right ventricular hypertrophy.

The four are not independent. Anterior and superior deviation of the outlet septum produces the obstruction, leaves the septal defect, positions the aorta over it, and the hypertrophy follows from the obstruction.
### Epidemiology
Tetralogy is the commonest cyanotic congenital heart lesion presenting beyond the newborn period. With surgical repair, survival into adulthood is now the norm, so adult clinicians encounter repaired tetralogy far more often than unrepaired disease.
### Aetiology and risk factors
Mostly sporadic. Associated with 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, which should be considered particularly where there are extracardiac features; with Down syndrome; and with maternal diabetes, phenylketonuria and retinoic acid exposure.
### Pathophysiology
The degree of cyanosis is determined by how much blood crosses the septal defect right to left, and that depends on a **balance between two resistances**: the obstruction at the right ventricular outflow, and the systemic vascular resistance.

Blood leaving the right ventricle takes the easier route. If outflow obstruction is severe or systemic resistance falls, more blood crosses to the left and cyanosis deepens. If systemic resistance rises, more blood is directed through the lungs and cyanosis improves.

This explains the **hypercyanotic spell**: crying, feeding, waking or dehydration raises outflow obstruction through infundibular spasm or lowers systemic resistance, and saturation falls abruptly with deep cyanosis, distress and — in severe spells — loss of consciousness.

It also explains **squatting**. Squatting kinks the femoral arteries and raises systemic vascular resistance, forcing more blood through the pulmonary circuit. The child discovers this without being taught, and it is a physiological manoeuvre rather than a behavioural quirk.

Chronic hypoxaemia produces secondary polycythaemia, and with it hyperviscosity and thrombotic risk.
### Clinical picture
Cyanosis, appearing in infancy as the obstruction progresses; hypercyanotic spells; squatting in older untreated children; exertional limitation; clubbing; and failure to thrive.

Signs: central cyanosis and clubbing; a right ventricular heave; an ejection systolic murmur at the left sternal edge from the outflow obstruction — **not** from the septal defect; and a single second heart sound.

During a spell the murmur becomes quieter or disappears, because less blood is crossing the obstruction — a paradoxical but logical finding.

In repaired tetralogy the picture is different: the sequelae are pulmonary regurgitation, right ventricular dilatation, and arrhythmia.
### Investigation
ECG shows right axis deviation and right ventricular hypertrophy.

Chest radiograph classically shows a boot-shaped heart, from right ventricular hypertrophy with a concave pulmonary artery segment, and reduced pulmonary vascular markings.

Echocardiography is diagnostic, defining all four components, the severity and level of obstruction, the anatomy of the pulmonary arteries and any associated lesions.

Cardiac MRI is central in follow-up after repair, quantifying pulmonary regurgitation and right ventricular volumes, which determine the timing of pulmonary valve replacement.

Genetic testing for 22q11.2 deletion is offered.
### Management
**Hypercyanotic spells** are managed by measures that raise systemic vascular resistance and reduce infundibular obstruction — knee-to-chest positioning is the immediate manoeuvre, and it works by the same mechanism as squatting. Calming the child matters, since distress worsens the spell. Pharmacological management follows the guideline in force. **Agents that reduce systemic vascular resistance worsen the spell and must be avoided.**

**Definitive treatment is surgical repair** — closure of the septal defect and relief of the outflow obstruction — usually in infancy. A palliative shunt is used where primary repair is not feasible.

**Long-term follow-up after repair** is lifelong. Pulmonary regurgitation is the principal late problem, and right ventricular volumes on MRI drive the timing of pulmonary valve replacement, since waiting for symptoms allows irreversible dysfunction. Late ventricular arrhythmia and sudden death are recognised risks requiring surveillance.

All agents, doses, timings and volume thresholds follow the guideline in force; none is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Unrepaired: hypercyanotic spells, polycythaemia and hyperviscosity, cerebral abscess and paradoxical embolism, endocarditis, and death in childhood.

After repair: pulmonary regurgitation, right ventricular dilatation and failure, atrial and ventricular arrhythmia, sudden death, and residual obstruction or septal defect.

Prognosis after repair is good, with most patients surviving into adult life, but it is not a cure — these are patients with a lifelong condition requiring specialist follow-up.
### Classification
The lesion is described by the severity and level of outflow obstruction, which determines the age at presentation and the degree of cyanosis.
## hold_these
The four components follow from one septal malalignment; the hypertrophy is a consequence, not a separate defect.
Cyanosis depends on the balance between outflow obstruction and systemic vascular resistance.
Squatting and knee-to-chest positioning work by raising systemic vascular resistance.
## lose_the_mark
Giving an agent that lowers systemic vascular resistance during a spell.
Attributing the murmur to the septal defect rather than to the outflow obstruction.
Treating repaired tetralogy as cured and discharging the patient from follow-up.

## summary
A curable cause of hypertension found by an examination step that takes five seconds — and missed for decades when nobody feels the femoral pulse.
## sections
### Definition
Coarctation is a narrowing of the aorta, characteristically in the region of the ductal insertion just distal to the left subclavian artery.
### Epidemiology
Coarctation is a relatively common congenital lesion, more frequent in males. Severe coarctation presents in the newborn period as the duct closes; milder coarctation presents in later childhood or adulthood as hypertension, and may be missed for many years.

It is a recognised and curable secondary cause of hypertension in young patients, and it is found by palpating the femoral pulses — which is why that step belongs in every young hypertensive assessment.
### Aetiology and risk factors
Mostly sporadic. Strongly associated with **bicuspid aortic valve**, present in a large proportion of cases, and with **Turner syndrome**. Also associated with ventricular septal defect and with intracranial berry aneurysms.
### Pathophysiology
The narrowing obstructs flow, raising pressure in the vessels arising proximal to it — the head, neck and upper limbs — and reducing perfusion distally.

The upper body hypertension is not purely mechanical: reduced renal perfusion activates the renin–angiotensin system, which raises pressure further. This is why hypertension may persist after repair, and why it is not simply relieved by removing the obstruction.

**Collateral circulation** develops over years through the internal thoracic, intercostal and scapular arteries, bypassing the narrowing. Enlarged intercostal collaterals erode the undersurface of the ribs, producing rib notching on the chest radiograph.

In the newborn, severe coarctation is duct-dependent: systemic perfusion below the narrowing relies on flow through the ductus, and closure precipitates shock.
### Clinical picture
**Newborn**: circulatory collapse as the duct closes, with poor femoral pulses, acidosis and shock.

**Child or adult**: usually asymptomatic and detected as hypertension. Symptoms when present are headache, epistaxis, cold legs, and claudication on exertion.

**Signs**: hypertension in the arms; **radiofemoral delay** and reduced-volume femoral pulses; a blood pressure gradient between arm and leg; a systolic murmur over the left scapula or the back; and continuous murmurs from collaterals. A bicuspid aortic valve murmur is often present.

If the left subclavian arises distal to the narrowing, the left arm pressure is also reduced — so both arms are measured.
### Investigation
Four-limb blood pressure and simultaneous palpation of radial and femoral pulses.

Chest radiograph may show rib notching and the "figure of three" sign from pre- and post-stenotic dilatation.

Echocardiography demonstrates the narrowing and the gradient, and assesses the aortic valve and left ventricle. CT or MR angiography defines the anatomy and the collaterals precisely and is used for planning intervention.

Because of the association with intracranial aneurysm, cerebral imaging is considered in selected patients.
### Management
Newborn duct-dependent coarctation requires maintenance of ductal patency until repair, and is a neonatal emergency.

Beyond infancy, significant coarctation is repaired — surgically, or by balloon angioplasty with stenting in older children and adults, depending on anatomy and age.

**Hypertension frequently persists or recurs after repair** and requires lifelong monitoring and treatment. Repair corrects the obstruction; it does not guarantee normal blood pressure.

Lifelong follow-up is required for recoarctation, aneurysm at the repair site, aortic valve disease and hypertension.

All agents, timings and gradient thresholds follow the guideline in force; none is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Untreated: hypertension and its consequences, left ventricular failure, aortic dissection or rupture, infective endocarditis or endarteritis, and intracranial haemorrhage from berry aneurysm.

After repair: recoarctation, aneurysm formation, persistent hypertension, and the complications of the associated bicuspid valve.

Prognosis after early repair is good but not normal — persistent hypertension and late aortic complications mean these patients need lifelong follow-up.
### Classification
Coarctation is described by its relation to the ductus and by whether the circulation is duct-dependent, since that determines the urgency of intervention.
## hold_these
Palpate radial and femoral pulses simultaneously in every young hypertensive patient.
Hypertension often persists after repair, because the renin–angiotensin activation is not purely mechanical.
Severe neonatal coarctation is duct-dependent, and closure of the duct precipitates shock.
## lose_the_mark
Diagnosing primary hypertension in a young patient without feeling the femoral pulses.
Measuring blood pressure in one arm only, when the left subclavian may arise distal to the narrowing.
Discharging a repaired patient from follow-up.

## summary
Fever and a murmur, and the diagnosis stands or falls on blood cultures taken before the first dose of antibiotic. One well-meant early dose can obscure it for the whole admission.
## sections
### Definition
Infective endocarditis is infection of the endocardial surface of the heart, most often a valve, characterised by a vegetation — an infected mass of platelets, fibrin, organisms and inflammatory cells.
### Epidemiology
Incidence rises with age. The epidemiology has shifted: degenerative valve disease, prosthetic valves, intracardiac devices and healthcare-associated bacteraemia now account for a large share in high-income settings.

**Where rheumatic heart disease is prevalent, as in Egypt, rheumatic valve lesions remain an important predisposing substrate and patients present younger.** This changes both the expected organism profile and the population at risk.
### Aetiology and risk factors
**Predisposing cardiac lesions**: rheumatic valve disease; degenerative valve disease; prosthetic valves; congenital lesions, particularly those producing turbulent high-velocity flow; hypertrophic cardiomyopathy; previous endocarditis; and intracardiac devices.

**Sources of bacteraemia**: dental and oral disease, skin infection, intravenous drug use, indwelling catheters and lines, and invasive procedures. Colorectal malignancy is associated with a particular organism and should prompt investigation of the bowel.

**Organisms**: staphylococci, streptococci including the oral viridans group, and enterococci account for most; the HACEK group and fungi are less common. Culture-negative endocarditis is most often the result of prior antibiotics.
### Pathophysiology
Two things must coincide. Turbulent flow or a foreign surface damages the endothelium, and a sterile platelet–fibrin thrombus forms. A transient bacteraemia then seeds it.

Organisms multiply within the growing vegetation, which is relatively avascular and shields them from phagocytes and from antimicrobial penetration. This is why treatment is prolonged, parenteral and bactericidal rather than short and oral.

The consequences follow three routes.

**Local destruction**: valve perforation causing acute regurgitation; abscess formation in the annulus or aortic root, which may extend into the conduction system and produce heart block — a new conduction abnormality in endocarditis is a warning sign of abscess.

**Embolisation** of vegetation fragments: to the brain causing stroke or abscess, to the spleen, kidneys and limbs, and to the lungs from right-sided disease.

**Immune complex deposition**: glomerulonephritis, and the classical peripheral phenomena.
### Clinical picture
Fever is the commonest feature, often with malaise, weight loss, night sweats and anorexia — an indolent presentation that is easily attributed elsewhere.

A **new or changing murmur** is important, though a murmur may be pre-existing or absent.

**Embolic and immune features**: stroke or focal neurological deficit; splinter haemorrhages; Janeway lesions, which are painless and embolic; Osler nodes, which are painful and immune-mediated; Roth spots on fundoscopy; splenomegaly; and haematuria.

Right-sided endocarditis, associated with intravenous drug use and with devices, presents with fever and pulmonary symptoms from septic emboli rather than with a systemic embolic picture.

**Fever with a murmur is endocarditis until proven otherwise**, and the classical peripheral signs are neither sensitive nor necessary for the diagnosis.
### Investigation
**Blood cultures are the single most important investigation, and they must be taken before antibiotics.** Multiple sets from separate sites are drawn, following the guideline in force for number and timing. A stable patient can wait the time this takes; the cost of not waiting is a culture-negative course of treatment lasting weeks.

**Echocardiography**: transthoracic first, transoesophageal where suspicion is high, where the transthoracic study is negative or inadequate, where a prosthetic valve or device is present, or where a complication such as abscess is suspected. A negative transthoracic study does not exclude the diagnosis.

Inflammatory markers, full blood count, renal function and urinalysis. Further imaging for embolic complications as indicated.

Diagnosis uses formally defined criteria combining microbiological, imaging and clinical findings, applied as specified in the current guideline.
### Management
Prolonged bactericidal antimicrobial therapy, directed by the organism and its sensitivities, given parenterally. Agents, combinations and durations follow the guideline in force and are not reproduced here — they differ substantially between organisms and between native and prosthetic valves.

**Surgery** is indicated for heart failure from valve destruction, for uncontrolled infection including abscess and persistent bacteraemia, and to prevent embolism in defined circumstances. Recognising the indications and referring early matters more than knowing the thresholds by heart, and roughly half of patients require surgery.

Multidisciplinary management with cardiology, microbiology and cardiac surgery is the standard of care.

**Prophylaxis** is now restricted to defined high-risk patients undergoing defined procedures, and the indications have narrowed considerably from historical practice. Follow the current guideline rather than older teaching.
### Complications and prognosis
Heart failure from valve destruction — the commonest cause of death; embolic stroke and other systemic emboli; abscess and conduction disturbance; mycotic aneurysm; glomerulonephritis; and relapse.

Mortality remains substantial despite treatment. Prognosis depends on the organism, the valve involved, whether the valve is prosthetic, the presence of complications and the timeliness of surgery.
### Classification
Endocarditis is classified by valve — native or prosthetic — by side, and by organism, because each of these determines the treatment regimen and the surgical threshold.
## hold_these

Fever with a new murmur is endocarditis until proven otherwise.
A new conduction abnormality suggests aortic root abscess.
## lose_the_mark
Giving antibiotics before cultures in a stable patient with fever and a murmur.
Excluding endocarditis on a negative transthoracic echocardiogram.
Applying historical universal prophylaxis rather than the narrower current indications.

## summary
A sore throat that damages a heart valve years later, entirely preventably. This is the most locally consequential article in the cardiovascular system, and the intervention that matters most costs almost nothing.
## sections
### Definition
Acute rheumatic fever is a delayed immune-mediated inflammatory illness following group A streptococcal pharyngitis, affecting the heart, joints, skin and central nervous system.

Rheumatic heart disease is the chronic valvular damage that results, typically appearing years to decades after the acute illness and progressing with each recurrence.
### Epidemiology
Rheumatic fever has become uncommon in high-income countries but remains a major cause of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality worldwide, and **it remains an important cause of valve disease in Egypt**. It principally affects children and young adults, and rheumatic heart disease is a leading cause of heart disease in young people in endemic settings.

It is a disease of crowding, poverty and limited access to primary care — a streptococcal sore throat that is not treated. Its persistence is therefore a marker of health system reach as much as of biology.

No prevalence figure is asserted; verified Egyptian data are recorded as an evidence gap.
### Aetiology and risk factors
Group A streptococcal pharyngitis is the necessary trigger. Skin infection is not associated with rheumatic fever in the way it is with post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis.

Risk factors: age between five and fifteen years at first attack; crowded living conditions; poverty and limited healthcare access; genetic susceptibility; and — the strongest predictor of severe valve disease — **previous rheumatic fever**, since damage accumulates with each episode.
### Pathophysiology
The mechanism is **molecular mimicry**. Antibodies and T cells raised against streptococcal antigens cross-react with structurally similar host proteins in cardiac valve tissue, myocardium, synovium, skin and basal ganglia.

The interval between pharyngitis and the acute illness is typically two to four weeks — the time required to mount the response — and the organism is not present in the affected tissue. Antibiotics eradicate the trigger and prevent recurrence; they do not reverse immune-mediated damage that has already occurred.

**Acute carditis** is a pancarditis: endocarditis producing valvulitis and regurgitation, myocarditis, and pericarditis. Aschoff bodies are the characteristic myocardial lesion.

**Chronic disease** develops as the acute inflammation heals by fibrosis. Commissural fusion, leaflet thickening and chordal shortening produce stenosis, regurgitation or both. The **mitral valve is affected most often**, followed by the aortic; right-sided involvement is uncommon and rarely isolated.

Critically, the damage is **cumulative**. Each recurrence adds further scarring, which is why preventing recurrence — rather than treating the existing lesion — is the intervention that determines the eventual severity.
### Clinical picture
The acute illness presents two to four weeks after a sore throat that the patient may not recall.

**Major manifestations**: carditis, most often detected as a new murmur; migratory polyarthritis of large joints, characteristically responsive to anti-inflammatory therapy; Sydenham chorea, which may appear months later and in isolation; erythema marginatum; and subcutaneous nodules.

**Minor manifestations**: fever, arthralgia, raised inflammatory markers, and prolonged PR interval.

Diagnosis uses formally defined criteria requiring evidence of preceding streptococcal infection together with a specified combination of major and minor manifestations, and those criteria are applied as set out in the current guideline, with lower thresholds in high-incidence populations.

**Chronic rheumatic heart disease** presents years later with the valve lesion: exertional breathlessness, atrial fibrillation, haemoptysis or embolic events, most often from mitral stenosis.
### Investigation
Evidence of preceding streptococcal infection: throat culture or rapid antigen test, and antistreptococcal antibody titres, which are more often positive by the time rheumatic fever presents.

Inflammatory markers. ECG for PR prolongation and other conduction abnormality.

**Echocardiography in every suspected case**, because subclinical carditis is common and its detection changes both the diagnosis and the duration of prophylaxis. This is a change from older practice that relied on auscultation alone.
### Management
**Treat the streptococcal infection** to eradicate the organism, even if the pharyngitis has resolved.

**Anti-inflammatory therapy** for arthritis and carditis, following the guideline in force.

**Manage heart failure** where carditis is severe.

**Secondary prophylaxis is the intervention that matters most.** Continuous antimicrobial prophylaxis after a first attack prevents recurrent streptococcal infection and therefore recurrent rheumatic fever, and it is what prevents progression to severe valve disease. Duration depends on whether carditis occurred and on its severity, and continues for many years — often into adulthood. Adherence is the practical challenge and is where clinical effort should be concentrated.

**Chronic valve disease** is managed as described in the valvular disease articles, with balloon mitral valvotomy particularly relevant in this younger population with pliable valves.

All agents, doses, regimens, criteria and durations follow the guideline in force; none is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Chronic rheumatic heart disease with mitral and aortic valve lesions; heart failure; atrial fibrillation and thromboembolic stroke; infective endocarditis on the damaged valve; pulmonary hypertension; and complications in pregnancy, where the increased volume and rate frequently unmask previously silent mitral stenosis.

Prognosis depends almost entirely on whether recurrence is prevented. A single attack without carditis may leave no permanent damage; repeated attacks produce severe disease requiring surgery in early adult life.
### Prevention
**Primary prevention** is prompt diagnosis and treatment of streptococcal pharyngitis, which prevents the first attack.

**Secondary prevention** is continuous prophylaxis after a first attack.

**Population measures** — reducing crowding, improving access to primary care, and echocardiographic screening programmes in high-prevalence populations — address the conditions in which the disease persists. This is a condition where the public health intervention outperforms the clinical one.
### Egyptian context
Rheumatic heart disease remains an important cause of cardiovascular disease in Egypt, and mitral stenosis in a young adult should raise it immediately. Three practical consequences: a sore throat in a child in an endemic setting deserves a lower threshold for treatment than a Western guideline might suggest; every patient with a first attack needs prophylaxis established and adherence supported over years; and echocardiography rather than auscultation alone should be used to detect carditis, since subclinical disease changes the prophylaxis decision.
## hold_these
The mechanism is molecular mimicry; the organism is not in the valve, and antibiotics do not reverse established damage.
Valve damage is cumulative across attacks, so preventing recurrence is what determines eventual severity.
Echocardiography detects subclinical carditis that auscultation misses, and that finding changes the prophylaxis decision.
## lose_the_mark
Stopping secondary prophylaxis once the acute illness settles or the patient feels well.
Relying on auscultation alone to exclude carditis.
Treating the established valve lesion as the priority while neglecting prophylaxis against the next attack.

## summary
Vessel size predicts the syndrome, so the classification does the diagnostic work. And in one of these conditions you treat first and confirm afterwards, because the complication cannot be undone.
## sections
### Definition
Vasculitis is inflammation of blood vessel walls, producing stenosis, occlusion, aneurysm or rupture. The vasculitides are grouped by the calibre of vessel predominantly affected, because vessel size determines the clinical syndrome.

This article addresses those with prominent cardiovascular manifestations. Systemic vasculitis as a whole is owned by SYS-IMM.
### Epidemiology
Giant cell arteritis is the commonest primary systemic vasculitis in older people, and rare below the age of fifty. Takayasu arteritis affects young people, predominantly women, and is relatively more prevalent in Asia, the Middle East and North Africa than in Western populations — which raises its local relevance.

Kawasaki disease is a leading cause of acquired heart disease in children in many countries.
### Aetiology and risk factors
Most primary vasculitides are of unknown cause, arising from an interaction of genetic susceptibility and environmental trigger. Age is the strongest determinant of which large-vessel vasculitis occurs.

Secondary vasculitis complicates infection — including tuberculosis, syphilis and hepatitis — connective tissue disease, malignancy and drugs.
### Pathophysiology
Inflammatory infiltration of the vessel wall damages the elastic lamina and the media. Two consequences follow, and both may occur in the same patient: **narrowing and occlusion**, producing ischaemia downstream, and **wall weakening**, producing aneurysm and the risk of rupture.

**Vessel size determines the syndrome.**

**Large-vessel** disease — giant cell arteritis and Takayasu arteritis — affects the aorta and its major branches, producing limb claudication, absent or asymmetric pulses, bruits, blood pressure asymmetry, and aortic aneurysm or regurgitation. In giant cell arteritis, involvement of the ophthalmic circulation threatens vision.

**Medium-vessel** disease — polyarteritis nodosa, Kawasaki disease — affects muscular arteries, producing organ infarction, mononeuritis multiplex, and aneurysms. Kawasaki disease characteristically involves the coronary arteries, causing aneurysm formation.

**Small-vessel** disease — the ANCA-associated vasculitides, IgA vasculitis, cryoglobulinaemic vasculitis — affects arterioles, capillaries and venules, producing palpable purpura, glomerulonephritis and alveolar haemorrhage, with cardiac involvement less prominent.
### Clinical picture
Systemic features are common to all: fever, weight loss, malaise, night sweats and raised inflammatory markers — the "inflammatory syndrome without a source" that should prompt consideration of vasculitis.

**Giant cell arteritis**: new headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication — which is close to specific — visual disturbance or loss, and a tender, thickened, pulseless temporal artery. Polymyalgia rheumatica coexists in many.

**Takayasu arteritis**: in a young patient, limb claudication, absent or asymmetric pulses, bruits, blood pressure difference between arms, hypertension from renal artery involvement, and aortic regurgitation.

**Kawasaki disease**: in a child, prolonged fever with conjunctivitis, mucosal changes, rash, extremity changes and cervical lymphadenopathy.
### Investigation
Inflammatory markers are raised in most and are used to support the diagnosis and to monitor.

**Giant cell arteritis**: temporal artery biopsy, with the caveat that skip lesions cause false negatives; and vascular ultrasound or cross-sectional imaging of the large vessels. **Treatment is not delayed for these.**

**Takayasu arteritis**: CT or MR angiography of the aorta and branches, which demonstrates wall thickening, stenosis and aneurysm. PET imaging assesses disease activity.

**Kawasaki disease**: echocardiography to assess the coronary arteries, at diagnosis and in follow-up.

ANCA testing, complement, cryoglobulins, urinalysis and organ-specific investigation as directed by the suspected category.
### Management
**Giant cell arteritis is treated on clinical suspicion, before confirmation**, because visual loss is irreversible and a short delay for biopsy does not obscure the histology. This is the clearest example in the topic of a sequence dictated by the irreversibility of the complication.

Immunosuppression is the basis of treatment across the group, with corticosteroids as the initial agent and steroid-sparing and biologic agents used according to the condition and the guideline in force.

**Kawasaki disease** is treated promptly to reduce the risk of coronary aneurysm, and the regimen follows the current guideline.

**Takayasu arteritis**: immunosuppression for active inflammation, with revascularisation reserved for critical stenosis and undertaken when inflammation is controlled, since intervening during active disease has poorer results.

Long-term surveillance for aneurysm and stenosis is required in large-vessel disease.

All agents, doses and durations follow the guideline in force; none is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Irreversible visual loss in giant cell arteritis; aortic aneurysm and regurgitation in large-vessel disease; coronary aneurysm, thrombosis and infarction in Kawasaki disease; organ infarction and renal failure in medium- and small-vessel disease; and the complications of long-term immunosuppression.

Prognosis is generally good with prompt treatment, and the dominant determinant is how quickly treatment is started — which is why the diagnosis is one to consider early rather than late.
### Classification
Classification by vessel size is used because it generates the clinical syndrome and directs investigation. Naming the individual conditions is secondary to placing them by calibre.
