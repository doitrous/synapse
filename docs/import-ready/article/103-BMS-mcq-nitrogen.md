## summary
Body protein looks static and is not. It is degraded and resynthesised continuously, and a constant protein content is the balance of two large opposing fluxes rather than a store sitting still. Nitrogen balance is how that flux is measured from outside the body — intake against output — and it has exactly three states, of which the negative one has exactly three causes. Almost every question this chapter asks is a request to place a clinical scenario into one of those states, or one of those causes, and getting there needs only two facts the book states plainly: nitrogen is 16 per cent of protein, and carbohydrate and fat spare protein from being burnt.

## sections
### Definition
Proteins are macromolecules built from covalently linked amino acids, and they are involved in every cellular process: enzymic catalysis, transport and storage, musculoskeletal coordination, immune function, the generation and transmission of the nerve impulse, and the control of growth and differentiation. Nothing else in the body does that range of jobs, which is why protein is spent on energy last rather than first.

A protein of **high biological value** must be easily digested and must contain all the essential amino acids in amounts adequate for the body's requirements. Animal proteins are generally of high biological value and are found in eggs, milk and its products, meat, fish and poultry; plant proteins — legumes, cereals, nuts — are generally lower. The qualification is worth holding: a *mixture* of low biological value proteins may together give a diet of high biological value, because one source supplies what the other lacks.

The caloric value of protein is 4 kcal/g, nearly the same as carbohydrate and less than half that of fat at 9 kcal/g. And the presence of carbohydrate and fat in the diet **spares** protein from oxidation and the production of energy. That is the reason amino acids are burnt only when the other two fuels cannot cover the demand, and it is also why starvation produces negative nitrogen balance so reliably.

### Mechanism
There is continuous degradation and synthesis of cellular proteins. About **75 per cent of the amino acids released are reutilised** and the rest are rapidly degraded. So a constant body protein content is a steady state maintained by two large fluxes, not a static store, and turnover does not stop when growth does.

Amino acids absorbed from the gut are mixed with those released by hydrolysis of body proteins and with those synthesised in the body to form a single **common amino acid pool**. Everything is drawn from that one pool, and it has two fates.

The **anabolic** fate keeps the nitrogen. It builds tissue proteins, plasma proteins, enzymes and hormones, and it builds other nitrogenous substances — glutathione, adrenaline, thyroxine, melanin, niacin, purines, pyrimidines, amino sugars, and the nitrogenous bases of phospholipids.

The **catabolic** fate loses it. Most catabolic reactions begin by cleaving the amino acid into ammonia and a carbon skeleton, usually an α-keto acid. The ammonia is mostly converted to urea and excreted in urine, with a little excreted as ammonia itself. The carbon skeleton is converted to glucose or ketone bodies, or oxidised to carbon dioxide and water — which is where the classification into pure glucogenic, pure ketogenic, and mixed amino acids comes from.

Digestion is the step before all of this: protein digestion starts in the stomach with gastric enzymes and HCl, continues in the small intestine under pancreatic and intestinal proteases, and the resulting L-amino acids are rapidly absorbed by active transport, by at least two mechanisms.

### Key determinants
Nitrogen forms about **16 per cent of protein**, and that single figure is what makes nitrogen balance a usable measurement. Most dietary nitrogen is protein nitrogen and most nitrogenous excretory products come from protein catabolism, so the difference between nitrogen intake and nitrogen output stands in for the balance between protein anabolism and catabolism. Three states exist.

**Nitrogen equilibrium** is output equal to intake. It is the state of the normal healthy adult on an adequate diet, and it does not mean that nothing is happening — it means the two fluxes are matched.

**Positive nitrogen balance** is intake exceeding output. It occurs whenever new tissue is being built: growth, pregnancy, muscular training, and convalescence from a state of negative nitrogen balance. Note what is *not* on that list — a high protein intake by itself. Feed protein to an adult who is building nothing and the surplus nitrogen is excreted, leaving equilibrium.

**Negative nitrogen balance** is output exceeding intake, and the book gives three causes, each with its own clinical examples. **(a) Inadequate protein intake** — starvation, malnutrition, deficiency of one or more essential amino acids, and gastrointestinal disease. **(b) Loss of protein** — chronic haemorrhage, albuminuria, and lactation on an inadequate diet. **(c) Increased protein catabolism** — diabetes mellitus, Cushing's syndrome, hyperthyroidism, and infectious fevers.

The three causes are the answer structure the exam wants: too little in, too much out, too much broken down.

### Clinical significance
Sorting a scenario into the right cause is the whole clinical skill here, and the three causes are told apart by *where* the nitrogen is being lost, not by how ill the patient is.

Albuminuria loses protein through the kidney with intake and catabolism both normal — cause (b). Diabetes mellitus, Cushing's syndrome and hyperthyroidism all raise catabolism with intake and losses normal — cause (c); in each of the three the common thread is an excess of a catabolic hormone over insulin. Gastrointestinal disease is listed under (a) rather than (b), because the failure is of intake and absorption, even though the protein is lost into the gut.

Two consequences follow from protein sparing. The first is that adequate carbohydrate in the diet of a catabolic patient is itself protein-conserving treatment. The second is that starvation and any severe illness produce negative nitrogen balance within days, because the moment carbohydrate and fat stop covering the energy demand, amino acids are burnt and their nitrogen leaves as urea.

The book stops there. It does not give a normal daily protein requirement, a method for measuring nitrogen output, or any figure for how negative a balance has to be before it matters clinically, and none of that is asserted here.

## summary
Catabolism of an amino acid begins by taking the amino group off the carbon skeleton, and the body does it in two moves that are useless on their own. Transamination shifts the amino group about but never releases free ammonia. Oxidative deamination releases ammonia but works efficiently on only one substrate, glutamate. Put them in series and the problem is solved: every amino acid hands its nitrogen to α-ketoglutarate, glutamate collects it, and glutamate alone is deaminated. That series is called transdeamination, and it is the single mechanism this whole section exists to build.

## sections
### Definition
Most catabolic pathways of amino acids begin with removal of the amino group from the carbon skeleton, producing the corresponding α-keto acid. In humans the removed nitrogen ends as urea, and the book sets out the route in three steps: **transamination** with α-ketoglutarate to form glutamate, **oxidative deamination** of glutamate to release NH3, and **synthesis of urea** and its excretion.

Transamination is the transfer of an amino group, usually from an α-amino acid to an α-keto acid, forming a new α-keto acid and a new α-amino acid. The enzymes are the aminotransferases, or transaminases. Five things about them are examined, and each answers a different question.

They are present in the **cytosol and mitochondria of all tissues, especially the liver**. The reaction is **reversible**, which is why the same enzymes serve both the deamination of amino acids and the biosynthesis of the non-essential ones. It requires **pyridoxal phosphate**, the vitamin B6 derivative, as the intermediate carrier of the amino group. Several transaminases exist, each specific for one amino acid and its corresponding α-keto acid — but **α-ketoglutarate is the amino group acceptor in every one of them**, forming glutamate, which is why glutamate is described as the collector of nitrogen from amino acids. And transamination involves **neither the uptake nor the release of free ammonia**, which is the fact the next section exists because of.

The two most important and active reactions are catalysed by **alanine aminotransferase (ALT)**, which moves the amino group between alanine and pyruvate, and **aspartate aminotransferase (AST)**, which moves it between aspartate and oxaloacetate. Both use α-ketoglutarate and glutamate as the other half of the pair.

### Mechanism
Oxidative deamination is what actually liberates ammonia, and the book gives two enzymes for it.

**L-amino acid oxidase** is a flavoprotein containing **FMN**. It is of low activity and works only in the liver and kidneys. It acts on L-amino acids to give the corresponding α-keto acid, NH3 and hydrogen peroxide; the peroxide is then destroyed by catalase to water. The intermediate is an α-imino acid, which hydrolyses spontaneously. Low activity and a two-organ distribution are the reasons this enzyme is not the answer when a question asks how most amino acid nitrogen is removed.

**L-glutamate dehydrogenase** is the enzyme that matters. It is **mitochondrial**, **NAD(P)⁺-dependent**, of **high activity** and **widespread distribution in tissues**, and it converts L-glutamate to α-ketoglutarate with the release of ammonia. It is an essential component of transdeamination and is therefore important for the deamination of most amino acids. The reaction is reversible, so the same enzyme catalyses the resynthesis of L-glutamate from free ammonia and NAD(P)H — which is how the body builds non-essential amino acids out of ammonia it has just released.

**Transdeamination** is the two put in series, and the book states plainly why the series is needed: removal of ammonia from amino acids cannot be explained by transamination alone, because no free ammonia is liberated, nor by oxidative deamination alone, because it works efficiently only on L-glutamate. First an amino acid transaminates onto α-ketoglutarate, forming glutamate. Then glutamate is oxidatively deaminated, releasing the ammonia and regenerating α-ketoglutarate, which is immediately available for a new transamination.

Read the loop once and two things fall out that are otherwise memorised separately. α-Ketoglutarate is a carrier, not a consumed substrate — it comes back every turn. And glutamate is the funnel through which essentially all amino acid nitrogen passes on its way to urea.

### Key determinants
What decides how much nitrogen is removed is simply how much amino acid is entering the catabolic fate, and that is set by the protein-sparing rule: amino acids are deaminated when carbohydrate and fat cannot cover the energy demand. The machinery itself is not the bottleneck.

Two cofactor dependencies are worth naming as determinants because each has a clinical face. Transamination cannot proceed without **pyridoxal phosphate**, so vitamin B6 status sits underneath the entire section. And glutamate dehydrogenase runs on **NAD(P)⁺**, so it is coupled to the cell's redox state in a way the transaminases are not.

The reversibility of both steps is the third determinant, and it is what makes the pathway a two-way street rather than a drain. Run transdeamination forwards and nitrogen leaves the amino acid pool as ammonia. Run glutamate dehydrogenase backwards, then transaminate, and ammonia is fixed back into a non-essential amino acid. The book makes exactly that point when it lists the synthesis of non-essential amino acids from α-keto acids "by reversal of transdeamination" among the anabolic fates of ammonia.

### Clinical significance
Aminotransferases are normally intracellular enzymes, and their plasma levels are low, representing the release of cellular contents during normal cell turnover. Elevated plasma levels therefore indicate **damage to cells rich in these enzymes**, and the pattern is what makes them useful.

AST and ALT are raised in nearly all liver diseases — the book's examples are severe viral hepatitis and toxic injury. AST may **also** be raised in non-hepatic disease, such as myocardial infarction and muscle disorders, and that asymmetry is the examinable point: a raised ALT points at the liver, while a raised AST alone does not.

The vitamin B6 dependency has its own consequence. Every transamination in the body runs on pyridoxal phosphate, so a deficiency does not disable one pathway but degrades the handling of nitrogen generally, and any drug that antagonises B6 does the same.

One negative result is worth stating because students supply it wrongly. The book gives no reference range for plasma ALT or AST, no figure for how high is high, and no rule for the AST:ALT ratio. It states the direction of change and the tissues involved, and nothing further is asserted here.

## summary
Ammonia is produced continuously in every tissue and is toxic, yet blood carries almost none of it. That gap between production and concentration is the whole subject: ammonia is moved in bound, non-toxic forms — glutamine out of the brain, alanine out of muscle — and disposed of in the liver as urea. Two clinical pictures come out of it and they are opposites. Hepatic failure breaks production, so ammonia rises and urea falls. Renal failure breaks excretion, so urea rises. Every question in this section is one of those two directions, or one of the three ways the ammonia the brain cannot clear does its damage.

## sections
### Definition
**Deamination of amino acids is the major source of ammonia.** Minor sources are the deamination of purines and pyrimidines, and bacterial putrefaction in the intestine — which produces small amounts normally but becomes of great importance in liver cirrhosis and hepatic coma, because the gut's ammonia now bypasses a liver that can no longer clear it.

Although ammonia is constantly produced in the tissues, it is present at very low levels in blood, normally **below 0.05 mg/dL**. That is not because little is made; it is because it is disposed of as fast as it appears.

Two anabolic fates keep the nitrogen. Ammonia removed by deamination may be used to build non-essential amino acids from α-keto acids, by reversal of transdeamination. And it may be fixed into the **amide group of glutamine**, which is then drawn on for asparagine, purines, pyrimidines and amino sugars.

### Mechanism
The catabolic and excretory fates are organ by organ, and the transport form differs by organ. That is the part the exam separates.

**Liver.** Formation of urea in the liver is the most important disposal route for ammonia. Urea travels in the blood from the liver to the kidneys, where it passes into the urine. Small amounts of ammonia are also converted to glutamine by glutamine synthetase.

**Brain.** Glutamine is synthesised from glutamate and ammonia by **glutamine synthetase**, and this is the major mechanism for the removal of ammonia in the brain. The glutamine is transported to the kidneys and the liver, where **glutaminase** hydrolyses it back to glutamate and ammonia. Glutamine is therefore a carrier: it is loaded where ammonia is dangerous and unloaded where it can be handled.

**Kidneys.** Ammonia in the kidney is excreted in urine. Sixty per cent of urinary ammonia comes from the action of glutaminase, and the remaining forty per cent from deamination of amino acids in the kidney itself.

**Muscle.** A good part of the amino acid nitrogen in muscle is converted to **alanine** by ALT and transported to the liver — the **glucose–alanine cycle**. In the liver, transdeamination removes the ammonia for urea synthesis.

Hold the two carriers apart, because they are asked about separately: **glutamine from the brain, alanine from muscle.** Glutamine is the better-known transport form and is the reflex wrong answer to the muscle question.

### Key determinants
What keeps blood ammonia low is the capacity of the urea cycle, and normally that capacity **exceeds** the rate at which ammonia is generated. Hyperammonaemia is therefore not a matter of making too much ammonia but of the cycle no longer keeping up.

**Acquired hyperammonaemia** occurs in liver cirrhosis, which may itself follow hepatitis, biliary obstruction or alcoholism. **Hereditary hyperammonaemia** is a deficiency of any of the five urea cycle enzymes; all are rare, and the deficiency is more severe when it affects one of the first two reactions of the cycle. The book tabulates five: type I hyperammonaemia (carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I), type II (ornithine transcarbamoylase), citrullinuria (argininosuccinate synthetase), argininosuccinic acidaemia (argininosuccinase), and hyperargininaemia (arginase).

Read that table for understanding, not for the exam. **The department has cancelled the hyperammonaemia types table from both the end-of-module and the final examination, except type 1.** It is kept here because the pattern it shows — that the two mitochondrial enzymes give the worst disease — is what makes the acquired and inherited forms one subject rather than two, and because a student who has never seen the list cannot reason about which enzyme a case has lost.

### Clinical significance
The brain is the organ that suffers, and the book gives **three separate mechanisms** rather than one.

First, ammonia pulls **α-ketoglutarate towards glutamate formation**. That depletes α-ketoglutarate, which is an essential intermediate of the citric acid cycle, so cellular oxidation and ATP production fall — and that can lead to coma and death. Second, **GABA becomes deficient**: it is the inhibitory neurotransmitter formed from glutamate by decarboxylation, and its deficiency is why convulsions occur. Third, **brain oedema** develops from the osmotic imbalance caused by high levels of both ammonia and glutamine inside astrocytes.

Now set the two organ failures side by side, because the analytes cross over and students reliably merge them into "nitrogen goes up".

In **hepatic failure**, ammonia remains in the blood — hyperammonaemia, or ammonia intoxication — and the plasma urea level **falls**, because the organ that makes urea has failed.

In **renal failure**, the liver goes on making urea normally at a plasma level of 10 to 50 mg/dL, but the product cannot be excreted, so the plasma urea level **rises**. That is uraemia.

Blood urea is therefore a marker of **renal** function, not hepatic function, and a rising urea in a patient with liver disease points to a second problem rather than a worse first one. The reasoning that catches students out is "liver disease is serious, so urea must be high" — the direction is exactly inverted.

## summary
The urea cycle is the most heavily examined pathway in this chapter, and almost everything asked about it is contained in the one equation the book prints: aspartate + NH3 + CO2 + 3 ATP → urea + fumarate. Read that line properly and it tells you the two nitrogen donors, the amino acid the cycle needs, the price in ATP, and the compound at which the pathway hands its borrowed carbon back to the citric acid cycle. Add two structural facts — that it runs only in the liver, and that the first two of its five enzymes are mitochondrial and the last three cytosolic — and the remaining questions are about the single switch, N-acetylglutamate, that couples the whole thing to how much protein was eaten.

## sections
### Definition
The urea cycle is the principal pathway for disposal of the ammonia produced by deamination of amino acids, converting it to a **neutral, non-toxic** form. Urea is an end product and is excreted in urine.

**Site.** Urea formation occurs **only in the liver**, because arginase is present only in the liver. The first two reactions occur in the **mitochondria** and all subsequent reactions occur in the **cytosol**. That split across two compartments is not incidental — it is what forces the ornithine–citrulline antiporter into the pathway, and it is why deficiencies of the first two enzymes behave differently from the last three.

**The overall reaction** is the line to learn:

aspartate + NH3 + CO2 + 3 ATP → urea + fumarate + 2 ADP + 2 Pi + AMP + PPi

Every term of it is examined. Aspartate is the amino acid the cycle needs alongside CO2, NH3 and ATP. Ammonia and aspartate are the two nitrogen sources. Three ATP is the price. And fumarate is what leaves with aspartate's carbon.

### Mechanism
Five enzymes, in order.

**1 — Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I.** Free ammonium ion and bicarbonate are condensed at the expense of **2 ATP** to form carbamoyl phosphate. The reaction is in the **mitochondrial matrix**, it uses ammonia as the nitrogen donor, and it is **absolutely dependent on N-acetylglutamate** for activity. This is the first of urea's two nitrogen atoms.

**2 — Ornithine transcarbamoylase.** Also in the mitochondrial matrix; it transfers the carbamoyl group to ornithine to form **citrulline**. Citrulline is then transported out of the mitochondrion into the cytosol in exchange for ornithine, on an antiporter, and every remaining reaction happens in the cytosol.

**3 — Argininosuccinate synthetase.** Citrulline is joined to **aspartate** to form argininosuccinate. This step requires hydrolysis of ATP to **AMP and PPi**, which the book notes is the equivalent of hydrolysing two molecules of ATP. Aspartate's amino group is the second of urea's two nitrogen atoms.

**4 — Argininosuccinate lyase (argininosuccinase).** Cleaves argininosuccinate into **fumarate and arginine**. Arginine keeps the nitrogen; the four carbons aspartate brought in leave as fumarate.

**5 — Arginase.** Cleaves arginine into **ornithine and urea**. Ornithine returns to the mitochondrial matrix for another turn.

Two things follow that are worth separating from the list. **Ornithine is a regenerated carrier, not a consumed substrate** — it comes out at step 5 and goes back in at step 2, exactly as oxaloacetate does in the citric acid cycle, which is why the pathway is also called the ornithine cycle. And **the ATP arithmetic has two right-looking answers**: three molecules of ATP are consumed, but four high-energy phosphate bonds are broken, because step 3 goes to AMP rather than to ADP. The book's figure, and the department key's, is three.

Now follow the carbon. Fumarate is an intermediate of the **citric acid cycle**, so it is the compound at which the two cycles meet. Fumarase hydrates fumarate to malate; malate dehydrogenase oxidises malate to oxaloacetate; AST transaminates oxaloacetate back to aspartate, which re-enters the urea cycle. The urea cycle borrows a carbon skeleton and gives it back, and this loop is what the phrase **aspartate–argininosuccinate shunt** names. Note where the junction actually is: malate is one step *past* it, and is the reflex wrong answer.

### Key determinants
**N-acetylglutamate is the essential activator of carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I, which is the rate-limiting step of the urea cycle.** That single sentence answers two separate exam questions — which enzyme controls the cycle, and which compound acts solely as an activator rather than as a substrate or an intermediate.

The regulation is elegant because it is self-selecting. N-acetylglutamate is synthesised from acetyl-CoA and glutamate by N-acetylglutamate synthase, in a reaction for which **arginine** is an activator. So after a protein-rich meal the intrahepatic concentration of N-acetylglutamate rises, because the meal supplies both the substrate, glutamate, and the regulator of its synthesis — and the rate of urea synthesis rises with it. The cycle is switched on by evidence that nitrogen is arriving.

Alongside that fast allosteric control the book gives a slower, adaptive one: the **levels of the urea cycle enzymes themselves** change. A high protein diet can increase the concentration of individual urea cycle enzymes ten to twenty fold. Administration of protein catabolic hormones increases them. And starvation elevates them too — which looks contradictory until you see why: starvation breaks down body protein, so ammonia production rises, and the cycle scales up to cope.

Note the shape of that: a high protein diet and starvation both raise urea cycle enzymes, from opposite directions. What they share is a rising ammonia load.

### Clinical significance
Because the capacity of the urea cycle normally exceeds the rate of ammonia generation, blood ammonia is normally very low, below 0.05 mg/dL. Hyperammonaemia is what happens when that margin is lost, and it comes in two forms.

**Acquired hyperammonaemia** occurs in liver cirrhosis, which may follow hepatitis, biliary obstruction or alcoholism.

**Hereditary hyperammonaemia** is deficiency of any one of the five urea cycle enzymes. All are rare, and the disease is **more severe in deficiency of the enzymes catalysing the first two reactions** — the mitochondrial ones, carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I and ornithine transcarbamoylase. The department has cancelled the table of the five named types from both examinations, except type 1, so the individual names of the other four are taught in ART-103-BIO-AMMONIA-METABOLISM for understanding and are not examined; the principle that the first two reactions give the worst disease is worth keeping regardless.

The other clinical fact this cycle carries is the one that trips students in the opposite direction. The urea it makes travels in the blood at a plasma level of **10 to 50 mg/dL** to the kidney. In **hepatic** failure the cycle fails, so ammonia rises and plasma urea **falls**. In **renal** failure the cycle is intact but the product cannot be excreted, so plasma urea **rises** — uraemia. Blood urea is a marker of renal function, and reasoning "liver disease is serious, therefore urea is high" gets it exactly backwards.

## evidence_gaps
Every statement rests on one source, the department book. No independent verification against an international biochemistry reference has been attached, and no claim or citation record is authored by this batch.
The book prints "3ATP" in the overall equation and separately calls step 3 "the equivalent of hydrolysis of two molecules of ATP", and never reconciles the two. Three molecules and four high-energy bonds are both stated here; neither is presented as overriding the other.
The phrase "aspartate–argininosuccinate shunt" does not appear in the book, and the book nowhere states in a sentence that the two cycles are linked. The arc is read off its own urea cycle figure, which draws fumarate, fumarase, L-malate, malate dehydrogenase, oxaloacetate and AST closing back onto aspartate.
The NADH generated at the malate dehydrogenase step of that returning arc is not credited anywhere in the book, so no claim is made here that it offsets the cycle's ATP cost.
The book gives no kinetic detail for arginine's activation of N-acetylglutamate synthase, and says nothing about what happens to the regulation when arginine itself is deficient.
It gives one plasma urea range, 10 to 50 mg/dL, without an assay, a population or an SI conversion.
