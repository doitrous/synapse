## summary
Two lesions of one valve that load the ventricle in opposite ways. In stenosis the moment symptoms appear is the moment the prognosis changes — which makes the history more important than the gradient.
## sections
### Definition
Aortic stenosis is obstruction to left ventricular outflow at valve level, imposing a pressure load on the ventricle. Aortic regurgitation is incompetence of the valve allowing diastolic return of blood from the aorta, imposing a volume load.

Both may coexist as mixed aortic valve disease.
### Epidemiology
Calcific degenerative aortic stenosis is predominantly a disease of older people and is the commonest valve lesion requiring intervention in high-income settings. A congenitally bicuspid valve presents one to two decades earlier.

In Egypt and comparable settings, rheumatic disease remains a significant cause of aortic valve disease and shifts presentation to younger patients. No prevalence figure is asserted; a verified local source is recorded as an evidence gap.
### Aetiology and risk factors
**Stenosis**: calcific degeneration, sharing risk factors with atherosclerosis; congenitally bicuspid valve, which calcifies earlier; and rheumatic disease, which characteristically affects the mitral valve as well.

**Chronic regurgitation**: disease of the leaflets — rheumatic, bicuspid, endocarditis — or of the aortic root, which dilates and pulls the cusps apart, as in hypertension, Marfan syndrome and other connective tissue disorders, syphilitic aortitis and the spondyloarthropathies.

**Acute regurgitation**: infective endocarditis, aortic dissection extending to the root, and trauma. These are emergencies.
### Pathophysiology
**Stenosis** imposes a pressure load. The ventricle hypertrophies concentrically, which preserves output but stiffens the chamber and raises filling pressures. Three consequences follow, and they explain the three cardinal symptoms.

Oxygen demand rises with muscle mass while coronary perfusion pressure falls and diastole shortens, so **angina** occurs even with normal coronary arteries. The obstruction is fixed, so output cannot rise to meet exercise vasodilatation and **syncope** occurs on exertion. Eventually the stiff hypertrophied ventricle cannot maintain filling and forward output, and **heart failure** develops.

The appearance of any of these marks the failure of compensation, and survival without intervention falls sharply from that point. This is why the history, not the gradient, drives the decision.

**Chronic regurgitation** imposes a volume load. The ventricle dilates eccentrically and becomes more compliant, accommodating a large regurgitant volume for years with few symptoms. Aortic diastolic pressure falls as blood returns to the ventricle, and the enlarged total stroke volume raises systolic pressure — widening the pulse pressure and generating the collapsing pulse and the peripheral signs, which are all one phenomenon rather than a list.

**Acute regurgitation** is different in kind. A normal-sized, non-compliant ventricle receives the regurgitant volume with no time to dilate, so left ventricular diastolic pressure rises abruptly, pulmonary oedema develops rapidly, and the pulse pressure is *not* widened. The murmur may be short and quiet. A patient can be gravely ill with unimpressive signs.
### Clinical picture
**Stenosis**: angina, exertional syncope and breathlessness. Signs — a slow-rising, low-volume carotid pulse; a heaving, non-displaced apex; an ejection systolic murmur at the right second intercostal space radiating to the carotids; a soft or absent second heart sound in severe disease; and a fourth heart sound.

**Chronic regurgitation**: long asymptomatic period, then exertional breathlessness and palpitation. Signs — a collapsing pulse and wide pulse pressure; a displaced, volume-loaded apex; an early diastolic murmur at the left sternal edge, best heard sitting forward in expiration; and the numerous peripheral signs that follow from the wide pulse pressure.

**Acute regurgitation**: sudden severe breathlessness, tachycardia, hypotension and pulmonary oedema, with a soft short diastolic murmur and no wide pulse pressure.
### Investigation
Echocardiography is the central investigation: valve morphology, gradient and valve area in stenosis, regurgitant severity, ventricular size and function, and root dimensions.

ECG shows left ventricular hypertrophy with strain and may show conduction disease, since calcification can extend into the conduction system. Chest radiograph may show a calcified valve, or root dilatation in regurgitation.

Where symptoms and echocardiographic severity disagree, stress testing in selected asymptomatic patients, CT for valve calcium scoring, cardiac MRI and invasive haemodynamic assessment resolve the discrepancy. Coronary assessment precedes intervention.

In acute regurgitation, investigation must not delay treatment, and aortic dissection is actively excluded.
### Management
**Stenosis.** There is no medical therapy that alters the natural history. Management of the asymptomatic patient is surveillance at intervals determined by severity, with education about the symptoms that must prompt immediate review. Once symptoms appear, valve replacement — surgical or transcatheter — is the treatment, and the choice between them depends on surgical risk, anatomy, age and comorbidity. Vasodilator therapy is used cautiously in severe stenosis, since the obstruction is fixed and output cannot rise to compensate for a fall in resistance.

**Chronic regurgitation.** Blood pressure control and treatment of the underlying root disease. Intervention is indicated for symptoms, or for defined ventricular size or function thresholds in asymptomatic patients, before irreversible dysfunction develops.

**Acute regurgitation** is a surgical emergency, with stabilisation only as a bridge.

Every threshold, gradient, dimension, agent and dose follows the valvular heart disease guideline in force where the patient is treated. None is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Heart failure, arrhythmia including conduction block from calcification, infective endocarditis, sudden death — uncommon in truly asymptomatic stenosis and a real risk once symptoms appear — and, in regurgitation, irreversible ventricular dilatation if intervention is delayed.

Prognosis after successful valve intervention is good. Untreated symptomatic severe aortic stenosis has a poor prognosis, which is the fact that justifies referring the moment symptoms appear.
### Classification
Severity is graded echocardiographically. The clinically decisive classification, however, is symptomatic against asymptomatic, because that is what determines the timing of intervention in stenosis.
## hold_these
In aortic stenosis the onset of symptoms marks a change in prognosis, not merely in comfort.
Acute aortic regurgitation does not widen the pulse pressure and its murmur may be short and quiet.
Angina occurs in aortic stenosis with normal coronary arteries.
## lose_the_mark
Managing symptomatic severe aortic stenosis medically instead of referring for intervention.
Expecting the chronic signs in acute regurgitation, and being reassured by an unimpressive murmur.
Giving a vasodilator freely in severe aortic stenosis, where output cannot rise to compensate.

## summary
The valve where local epidemiology matters most. Almost all mitral stenosis is rheumatic, it presents decades after a childhood sore throat, and every complication follows from one raised chamber pressure.
## sections
### Definition
Mitral stenosis is obstruction to left ventricular filling at the mitral valve, loading the left atrium. Mitral regurgitation is incompetence allowing systolic return of blood into the left atrium, loading both the atrium and the ventricle.
### Epidemiology
Mitral stenosis is almost always rheumatic, and rheumatic heart disease remains a leading cause of valve disease in Egypt and in many low- and middle-income countries. It affects women more often than men and presents in early to middle adult life — decades younger than degenerative valve disease.

This is the clearest example in the cardiovascular system of a differential that a Western textbook underweights. A young adult presenting with breathlessness, atrial fibrillation or haemoptysis must have mitral stenosis considered locally in a way that would be unusual elsewhere.

Mitral regurgitation is common and has a broader aetiology, with degenerative and functional causes predominating in older patients.

No prevalence figure is asserted; verified Egyptian data are recorded as an evidence gap.
### Aetiology and risk factors
**Stenosis**: rheumatic disease overwhelmingly. Rarely severe annular calcification, congenital disease or infiltration.

**Primary regurgitation** — disease of the valve apparatus: degenerative disease including mitral valve prolapse, rheumatic disease, infective endocarditis, papillary muscle rupture after infarction, and connective tissue disorders.

**Secondary or functional regurgitation** — a normal valve tethered or stretched by a dilated remodelled ventricle, as in ischaemic or dilated cardiomyopathy, or by a dilated annulus in long-standing atrial fibrillation.
### Pathophysiology
**Stenosis.** Rheumatic carditis fuses the commissures, thickens the leaflets and shortens the chordae over years. The obstructed valve raises left atrial pressure, and everything that follows is a consequence of that single pressure.

The atrium dilates, producing atrial fibrillation and stasis with thromboembolism — the embolic risk in rheumatic mitral stenosis is high. Pressure transmits to the pulmonary veins, producing exertional breathlessness, orthopnoea and haemoptysis from ruptured bronchial veins. Sustained pulmonary venous hypertension leads to pulmonary arterial hypertension and eventually right heart failure with tricuspid regurgitation.

The left ventricle is characteristically normal, because it is protected by the obstruction — which is why the apex beat is not displaced. Filling depends on diastolic time and on atrial contraction, so tachycardia and the onset of atrial fibrillation both precipitate decompensation abruptly. Pregnancy, with its increased volume and rate, commonly unmasks previously silent mitral stenosis.

**Regurgitation.** Volume returns to the atrium in systole. Chronically the atrium and ventricle dilate and accommodate it; acutely they cannot, and pulmonary oedema follows at once.

Because the ventricle empties partly into a low-pressure atrium, its afterload is reduced and it ejects easily. The measured ejection fraction therefore flatters contractile function, and a fraction falling towards the low end of normal already signifies significant myocardial impairment.
### Clinical picture
**Stenosis**: exertional breathlessness, orthopnoea, fatigue, palpitation from atrial fibrillation, haemoptysis, and symptoms of thromboembolism. A malar flush may be present.

Signs — a tapping, non-displaced apex beat representing a palpable first heart sound; a loud first heart sound; an opening snap; and a low-pitched mid-diastolic rumbling murmur at the apex, best heard with the bell in the left lateral position, with presystolic accentuation if sinus rhythm persists. Signs of pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure appear later.

**Chronic regurgitation**: long asymptomatic period, then exertional breathlessness and fatigue. Signs — a displaced, volume-loaded apex; a soft first heart sound; a third heart sound; and a pansystolic murmur at the apex radiating to the axilla.

**Acute regurgitation**: sudden severe breathlessness and pulmonary oedema, often with a short and unimpressive murmur.
### Investigation
Echocardiography establishes the diagnosis, the mechanism and the severity: valve morphology and mobility, valve area and gradient in stenosis, regurgitant severity and mechanism, atrial size, ventricular size and function, and pulmonary pressures. Transoesophageal imaging defines the mechanism of regurgitation before surgery and excludes atrial thrombus before intervention.

ECG shows left atrial enlargement in sinus rhythm, atrial fibrillation frequently, and right ventricular hypertrophy where pulmonary hypertension has developed. Chest radiograph may show left atrial enlargement — a double right heart contour, splayed carina, posterior oesophageal displacement — and pulmonary congestion.

Evidence of previous rheumatic fever is sought, and secondary prophylaxis status established.
### Management
**Stenosis.** Rate control lengthens diastole and improves filling — the opposite of the instinct to treat breathlessness with diuretic alone. Anticoagulation is indicated in atrial fibrillation, and the embolic risk in rheumatic mitral stenosis is managed according to the specific guidance for valvular atrial fibrillation, which differs from that for non-valvular disease.

Mechanical relief of the obstruction is by percutaneous balloon mitral valvotomy where valve morphology is suitable and there is no atrial thrombus or significant regurgitation, or by surgical repair or replacement. Balloon valvotomy is particularly valuable in this population because it suits younger patients and can be repeated.

Secondary prophylaxis against recurrent rheumatic fever is continued according to the guidance in force — this is a major local priority, since recurrence worsens the valve lesion.

**Regurgitation.** Treat the cause. In primary regurgitation, surgical repair — preferred to replacement where feasible — is indicated for symptoms or for defined ventricular thresholds. In secondary regurgitation, optimising heart failure therapy comes first, since treating the ventricle may reduce the regurgitation.

Every threshold, agent, dose and prophylaxis regimen follows the guideline in force; none is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Atrial fibrillation, systemic embolism and stroke, pulmonary hypertension, right heart failure, infective endocarditis, and haemoptysis.

Prognosis in mitral stenosis is good after successful relief of obstruction. Untreated, it progresses over years with a marked deterioration once pulmonary hypertension is established. Prevention of recurrent rheumatic fever alters the natural history and is the intervention with the greatest population-level effect.
### Egyptian context
Rheumatic heart disease remains a major cause of cardiovascular morbidity in Egypt, and mitral stenosis is its characteristic lesion. Three practical consequences follow: the differential for breathlessness or atrial fibrillation in a young adult must include it; secondary prophylaxis after rheumatic fever is a priority rather than a formality; and balloon valvotomy is a particularly relevant intervention in a younger population with pliable valves.
## hold_these
Almost all mitral stenosis is rheumatic, and it presents decades after the initial infection.
The left ventricle is normal in mitral stenosis, so the apex is tapping and not displaced.
A normal-looking ejection fraction in significant mitral regurgitation is flattered by the low-pressure leak.
## lose_the_mark
Omitting mitral stenosis from the differential in a young adult with breathlessness or atrial fibrillation.
Treating the tachycardia of mitral stenosis as incidental — shortened diastole is what precipitates the decompensation.
Neglecting secondary prophylaxis against recurrent rheumatic fever.

## summary
Usually not a valve problem at all. The leaflets are normal and the annulus they hang from has been stretched by a right ventricle in trouble — so the question is what put it there.
## sections
### Definition
Tricuspid regurgitation is incompetence of the tricuspid valve allowing systolic reflux into the right atrium. It is classified as primary, where the valve apparatus is diseased, or secondary and functional, where a structurally normal valve fails because the right ventricle and annulus have dilated.

Tricuspid stenosis is obstruction to right ventricular filling and is uncommon.
### Epidemiology
Some degree of tricuspid regurgitation is present in most people and is of no consequence. Clinically significant regurgitation is common and is overwhelmingly functional.

Tricuspid stenosis is rare and, where it occurs, is almost always rheumatic and almost always accompanied by mitral disease — which makes it locally more relevant than in settings where rheumatic disease is uncommon.
### Aetiology and risk factors
**Functional regurgitation**, the great majority: right ventricular dilatation from left heart disease, pulmonary hypertension of any cause, right ventricular infarction or cardiomyopathy; and annular dilatation from long-standing atrial fibrillation.

**Primary regurgitation**: infective endocarditis, which in the right heart is strongly associated with intravenous drug use and with indwelling lines and devices; rheumatic disease; Ebstein anomaly; carcinoid heart disease, which characteristically affects right-sided valves; trauma; and injury from pacing leads.

**Stenosis**: rheumatic disease, rarely carcinoid.
### Pathophysiology
The right ventricle tolerates volume load well, so functional tricuspid regurgitation may be present for a long time before it matters. Once it does, a vicious circle operates: regurgitation dilates the right ventricle further, which stretches the annulus further, which worsens the regurgitation.

Systolic reflux transmits ventricular pressure directly into the right atrium and, because there are no valves between the right atrium and the great veins, onwards into the jugular veins and the liver. This produces the large systolic wave in the venous pulse and the pulsatile liver, and explains why chronic severe regurgitation causes hepatic congestion, ascites and eventually cardiac cirrhosis.

Right-sided murmurs increase on inspiration because the fall in intrathoracic pressure augments venous return and therefore flow across the valve.
### Clinical picture
Symptoms are those of systemic venous congestion and low output: fatigue, abdominal distension and discomfort from hepatic congestion, ankle and sacral oedema, and early satiety.

Signs — a raised jugular venous pressure with a prominent systolic wave; a pansystolic murmur at the lower left sternal edge that increases on inspiration; a right ventricular heave; a pulsatile, tender enlarged liver; ascites and peripheral oedema.

The features of the underlying cause frequently dominate the picture, which is appropriate, since in functional disease that is where the problem lies.
### Investigation
Echocardiography establishes the mechanism — whether the leaflets are diseased or the annulus is dilated — grades severity, measures right ventricular size and function, estimates pulmonary artery pressure, and assesses the left heart, since left heart disease is the commonest underlying cause.

ECG may show right atrial enlargement and right ventricular hypertrophy, and atrial fibrillation is common. Liver function tests are abnormal with chronic congestion. Blood cultures are taken where endocarditis is suspected. Cardiac MRI quantifies right ventricular volumes where echocardiographic assessment is limited.
### Management
Treat the cause. In functional regurgitation that means treating the left heart disease or the pulmonary hypertension responsible, since reducing right ventricular pressure or volume reduces the regurgitation.

Congestion is relieved with diuretic therapy, titrated carefully because the right ventricle is preload-dependent.

Surgical intervention — repair with annuloplasty in preference to replacement — is considered for severe primary disease, for severe secondary regurgitation at the time of left-sided surgery, and for symptomatic severe regurgitation persisting despite medical therapy. Intervening at the time of left-sided surgery is favoured because isolated late tricuspid surgery carries substantially higher risk.

Right-sided endocarditis is managed medically wherever possible.

Every threshold, agent and dose follows the guideline in force; none is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Progressive right heart failure, hepatic congestion and cardiac cirrhosis, ascites, renal dysfunction from venous congestion, atrial fibrillation and cachexia.

Prognosis is determined chiefly by the underlying cause and by right ventricular function. Severe tricuspid regurgitation carries an adverse prognosis that was historically underestimated.
### Classification
Primary against secondary is the classification that matters, because it determines whether the valve or the ventricle is the target of treatment.
## hold_these
Most significant tricuspid regurgitation is functional — the leaflets are normal and the annulus has been stretched.
Right-sided murmurs increase on inspiration, because venous return rises.
A pulsatile liver and a large systolic venous wave follow from the absence of valves between right atrium and great veins.
## lose_the_mark
Investigating the tricuspid valve without asking what dilated the right ventricle.
Missing right-sided endocarditis in a patient with intravenous drug use or an indwelling device.
Over-diuresing a preload-dependent right ventricle.

## summary
The valve that breaks the pattern. Rheumatic and degenerative disease largely spare it, so a murmur here asks a different question — what happened in childhood.
## sections
### Definition
Pulmonary stenosis is obstruction to right ventricular outflow, which may be valvar, subvalvar or supravalvar. Pulmonary regurgitation is incompetence of the valve allowing diastolic reflux into the right ventricle.
### Epidemiology
Pulmonary valve disease is the least common of the four in adult practice. Pulmonary stenosis is one of the commoner congenital cardiac lesions and is frequently isolated.

Significant pulmonary regurgitation in adults is most often a consequence of previous intervention for congenital disease, particularly repair of tetralogy of Fallot, and is therefore an increasingly recognised problem as those patients reach adulthood.
### Aetiology and risk factors
**Stenosis**: congenital in the great majority, either isolated or as part of a syndrome or a complex lesion such as tetralogy of Fallot. Acquired causes — rheumatic and carcinoid — are uncommon, and carcinoid characteristically affects right-sided valves.

**Regurgitation**: most often iatrogenic, after balloon valvuloplasty or surgical repair of congenital outflow obstruction. Also secondary to pulmonary hypertension, which dilates the annulus, and rarely endocarditis or carcinoid.
### Pathophysiology
**Stenosis** imposes a pressure load on the right ventricle, which hypertrophies. Mild and moderate stenosis are frequently tolerated for decades without symptoms. Severe stenosis limits the rise in output on exertion and eventually causes right ventricular failure.

**Regurgitation** imposes a volume load. The right ventricle tolerates this remarkably well for many years, which is precisely the problem: dilatation progresses silently, and by the time symptoms appear the dysfunction may be irreversible. This is why surveillance after congenital repair is based on imaging rather than on symptoms.
### Clinical picture
Frequently asymptomatic and detected as a murmur.

**Stenosis**: exertional fatigue and breathlessness, and in severe disease exertional syncope. Signs — an ejection systolic murmur at the left second intercostal space increasing on inspiration, an ejection click, a widely split second heart sound with a soft pulmonary component, and a right ventricular heave.

**Regurgitation**: an early diastolic murmur at the left sternal edge increasing on inspiration, and signs of right ventricular volume overload.
### Investigation
Echocardiography defines the level and severity of obstruction, the regurgitant severity, and right ventricular size and function.

Cardiac MRI is the reference standard for right ventricular volumes and for quantifying pulmonary regurgitation, and it drives the timing of intervention after congenital repair.

ECG may show right ventricular hypertrophy and right axis deviation. Chest radiograph may show post-stenotic dilatation of the pulmonary trunk.

In an adult presenting with a pulmonary murmur, ask directly about childhood cardiac surgery or catheter intervention, since the history frequently supplies the diagnosis.
### Management
Mild and moderate pulmonary stenosis in an asymptomatic patient requires surveillance rather than treatment.

Severe stenosis is treated by balloon pulmonary valvuloplasty, which is highly effective for typical valvar stenosis, with surgery reserved for dysplastic valves and sub- or supravalvar obstruction.

Pulmonary regurgitation after congenital repair is managed by surveillance with cardiac MRI, and pulmonary valve replacement — surgical or transcatheter — is undertaken when right ventricular volumes or function reach the thresholds defined in the guideline in force, before irreversible dysfunction develops. Waiting for symptoms is the error this surveillance exists to prevent.

Adults with congenital heart disease are managed in specialist services.

Every threshold and technique follows the guideline in force; none is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Right ventricular failure, arrhythmia — atrial and ventricular, particularly late after tetralogy repair — endocarditis, and sudden death in that population.

Prognosis after relief of isolated pulmonary stenosis is excellent. Prognosis in pulmonary regurgitation after congenital repair depends on right ventricular function and on the timing of valve replacement.
### Classification
Obstruction is classified by level — valvar, subvalvar, supravalvar — because the level determines whether balloon intervention will work.
## hold_these
Pulmonary valve disease is predominantly congenital; ask about childhood cardiac surgery.
The right ventricle tolerates pulmonary regurgitation silently for years, so surveillance is by imaging rather than by symptoms.

## summary
Chest pain with ST elevation that belongs to no coronary territory. The pericardium surrounds everything, so its inflammation shows up everywhere — which is exactly how it is told apart from an infarct.
## sections
### Definition
Acute pericarditis is inflammation of the pericardium. The diagnosis is made when at least two of four features are present: characteristic chest pain, a pericardial friction rub, characteristic ECG changes, and a new or worsening pericardial effusion.
### Epidemiology
Pericarditis is a common cause of chest pain presentations, particularly in younger adults, and is more frequent in men. It is a frequent cause of admission for suspected acute coronary syndrome in patients who turn out not to have one.
### Aetiology and risk factors
**Idiopathic** in the majority in high-income settings, most presumed viral.

**Infective**: viral commonly; **tuberculous pericarditis is a major cause where tuberculosis is endemic**, including in Egypt, and it behaves quite differently — subacute, more often with a large effusion, and progressing to constriction. Bacterial and fungal causes occur.

**Non-infective**: after myocardial infarction, both early and as the later immune-mediated Dressler syndrome; uraemia; malignancy; systemic autoimmune disease; radiotherapy; after cardiac surgery or catheter intervention; trauma; and drugs.
### Pathophysiology
Inflammation of the two pericardial layers produces pain, a friction rub as the roughened surfaces move against one another, exudation of fluid, and inflammation of the underlying subepicardial myocardium.

Two consequences explain the clinical picture.

**The pain** is transmitted by somatic afferents in the phrenic nerve, which supplies the pericardium. Because the phrenic nerve arises from C3 to C5, pain refers to the shoulder and trapezius ridge — a distribution unlike that of ischaemic cardiac pain, which is carried by sympathetic visceral afferents. Pain is worse lying flat and on inspiration, and relieved by sitting forward.

**The ECG changes are diffuse** because the pericardium surrounds the whole heart. Subepicardial inflammation generates ST elevation across leads that correspond to no single coronary territory, typically concave upwards, with PR segment depression reflecting atrial involvement. Reciprocal depression is absent except in the leads facing the atria.

Where inflammation extends into the myocardium, troponin rises and the condition is myopericarditis.
### Clinical picture
Sharp, pleuritic retrosternal chest pain, worse lying flat and on inspiration, relieved by sitting forward, often radiating to the trapezius ridge — a feature that is close to specific.

Fever and a preceding viral prodrome are common in the idiopathic form. Tuberculous disease presents more insidiously with weight loss, night sweats and a longer history.

The **pericardial friction rub** is a scratching sound, classically with up to three components, best heard at the left sternal edge with the patient sitting forward in expiration. It is evanescent and may be present at one examination and absent at the next, so a single normal examination does not exclude it.

Look for signs of effusion and tamponade, which change management immediately.
### Investigation
ECG: widespread concave ST elevation with PR depression, evolving over days through T-wave flattening and inversion. The distribution is what distinguishes it from infarction.

Troponin: normal in isolated pericarditis, raised in myopericarditis. A raised value warrants assessment for an acute coronary syndrome and for myocardial involvement.

Inflammatory markers, full blood count, renal function and chest radiograph in all patients. Echocardiography in all patients to detect effusion and assess for tamponade and for ventricular involvement.

Directed investigation for a specific cause where the presentation suggests one, and this is where the local differential matters: tuberculosis should be actively considered where the course is subacute, the effusion large, or the patient has risk factors. Pericardial fluid analysis and biopsy are used where a specific diagnosis is needed.
### Management
Most idiopathic and viral pericarditis is managed with anti-inflammatory therapy and exercise restriction until symptoms resolve and inflammatory markers normalise. The agents, doses, durations and tapering schedules follow the guideline in force and are not reproduced here.

Colchicine as an adjunct is established in current guidance for reducing recurrence, which is worth knowing as a principle even though the regimen is not stated here.

Corticosteroids are avoided as first-line therapy in idiopathic disease because of higher recurrence rates, and are reserved for specific indications.

Specific causes receive specific treatment. Tuberculous pericarditis requires antituberculous therapy and specialist management, and is one of the situations where establishing the aetiology changes everything.

Admission is indicated for features predicting a complicated course, which are defined in the guideline in force.

Where a significant effusion or tamponade is present, that becomes the priority.
### Complications and prognosis
Pericardial effusion and tamponade; recurrent pericarditis, which affects a substantial minority; myopericarditis; and constrictive pericarditis, which is uncommon after idiopathic disease and markedly more common after tuberculous or purulent pericarditis.

Prognosis in idiopathic pericarditis is good. The main burden is recurrence.
### Classification
Pericarditis is classified as acute, incessant, recurrent or chronic by duration, and by aetiology. Both matter: duration determines the treatment strategy, and aetiology determines whether specific therapy exists and what the risk of constriction is.

## summary
How fast it arrived matters more than how much there is. A litre over months may be silent; two hundred millilitres in an hour can kill.
## sections
### Definition
A pericardial effusion is an abnormal accumulation of fluid within the pericardial cavity, beyond the small physiological volume that lubricates cardiac movement.

Its significance is physiological rather than volumetric: what matters is whether pericardial pressure has risen enough to impair filling.
### Epidemiology
Effusions are common and most are small and of no haemodynamic consequence. They are found incidentally on echocardiography performed for other reasons, and their frequency reflects the frequency of the conditions that cause them.
### Aetiology and risk factors
Any cause of pericarditis can produce an effusion. Beyond those:

**Transudative**: heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome, hypothyroidism — which is a recognised cause of a large, slowly accumulating and often silent effusion.

**Exudative**: infection including tuberculosis, malignancy — commonly lung, breast, lymphoma and melanoma — autoimmune disease, uraemia, and radiotherapy.

**Haemopericardium**: trauma, aortic dissection rupturing into the pericardium, free wall rupture after myocardial infarction, and complications of cardiac procedures. These accumulate rapidly and are the ones that cause tamponade at small volumes.
### Pathophysiology
The pericardium is relatively inelastic in the short term but stretches over weeks. That single property governs the whole condition.

Fluid accumulating slowly allows the sac to accommodate a large volume — sometimes more than a litre — with little rise in pressure and few symptoms. Fluid accumulating rapidly into an unstretched pericardium raises pressure steeply, and a volume as small as a couple of hundred millilitres can impair filling.

The pressure–volume relationship of the pericardium is therefore steeply non-linear: pressure rises slowly at first and then very sharply once the reserve is used. This is why a patient with a chronic effusion can decompensate abruptly after a modest further accumulation, and why the reported size of an effusion is a poor guide to its danger.
### Clinical picture
Small effusions are asymptomatic.

Larger effusions cause dull chest discomfort, breathlessness, and symptoms from compression of adjacent structures — dysphagia, hiccups from phrenic irritation, hoarseness, and a barking cough.

Signs are unreliable and often absent: muffled heart sounds, a reduced apex beat, and Ewart sign — dullness with bronchial breathing below the left scapula from compression of the left lower lobe.

The critical assessment is for tamponade, which is a physiological state and is looked for at every examination: hypotension, raised jugular venous pressure, tachycardia and pulsus paradoxus.
### Investigation
Echocardiography is the investigation of choice and establishes presence, size, distribution and — crucially — whether there is haemodynamic compromise: diastolic chamber collapse, respiratory variation in flows, and inferior vena cava plethora.

ECG may show low voltage complexes and, with a large effusion, electrical alternans as the heart swings within the fluid.

Chest radiograph shows an enlarged globular cardiac silhouette only once the effusion is substantial.

Investigate the cause: inflammatory markers, renal and thyroid function, autoimmune screen, tuberculosis assessment where relevant, and imaging for malignancy. Pericardial fluid obtained at drainage is sent for cell count, cytology, protein, culture and mycobacterial studies.
### Management
An asymptomatic effusion without haemodynamic compromise is managed by treating the cause and by surveillance. Many resolve.

Where tamponade is present or developing, drainage is the treatment and takes priority over investigation.

Drainage is also undertaken for diagnostic purposes where a specific aetiology such as tuberculosis or malignancy is suspected and would change treatment, and for large symptomatic effusions.

Recurrent effusions may require a pericardial window or pericardiectomy.

Anticoagulation is used with caution where a haemorrhagic effusion is suspected.

Indications, techniques and thresholds for drainage follow the guideline in force; none is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Cardiac tamponade is the complication that matters. Chronic effusion may progress to constriction, particularly where the cause is tuberculous or purulent.

Prognosis is determined by the cause. An idiopathic effusion carries a good outlook; a malignant effusion reflects advanced disease.
### Classification
Effusions are described by size, by distribution, by composition and by onset. Of these, onset carries the most physiological information, because rate of accumulation determines haemodynamic effect.

## summary
The heart cannot fill because the sac around it is full. It is diagnosed at the bedside and treated by removing the fluid — and the echocardiogram is confirmation, not permission.
## sections
### Definition
Cardiac tamponade is the haemodynamic state in which pericardial pressure rises sufficiently to impair ventricular filling and reduce cardiac output.

It is a physiological diagnosis, not a volumetric one, and it exists on a continuum from subtle impairment to circulatory collapse.
### Epidemiology
Tamponade complicates a minority of pericardial effusions. The causes that produce it most often are those that accumulate rapidly — trauma, procedural complications, aortic dissection, free wall rupture — and those that produce large effusions, particularly malignancy and tuberculosis.
### Aetiology and risk factors
Any cause of pericardial effusion may progress to tamponade. Those most commonly responsible: malignancy; tuberculous and purulent pericarditis; haemopericardium from trauma, dissection, myocardial rupture or a cardiac procedure; uraemia; and idiopathic or viral pericarditis with a large effusion.

Rapid accumulation is the dominant risk factor, which is why a modest effusion after an intervention deserves more concern than a large chronic one.
### Pathophysiology
As pericardial pressure rises it approaches and then exceeds the diastolic pressures within the cardiac chambers. Filling is progressively impaired, beginning with the lowest-pressure chambers — hence right atrial and then right ventricular diastolic collapse on echocardiography.

Because the total volume within the pericardium is now fixed, the ventricles become interdependent: filling of one occurs only at the expense of the other. On inspiration, venous return to the right heart increases, the septum shifts leftwards, left ventricular filling falls, and systolic pressure drops. That is **pulsus paradoxus** — an exaggeration of a normal inspiratory fall, not a reversal of it, and the name misleads.

Stroke volume falls. Output is maintained initially by tachycardia and by vasoconstriction, so blood pressure can be preserved until compensation fails, at which point deterioration is abrupt.

Filling depends entirely on venous pressure exceeding pericardial pressure, so anything reducing preload — diuretics, vasodilators, or the positive intrathoracic pressure of mechanical ventilation — can precipitate collapse.
### Clinical picture
Breathlessness, chest discomfort, restlessness, and in advanced tamponade confusion and collapse.

**Beck triad** — hypotension, raised jugular venous pressure and muffled heart sounds — is the classical description, though all three are present together in a minority.

More reliable findings: tachycardia, a raised jugular venous pressure, and **pulsus paradoxus**. The venous pressure rises rather than falls on inspiration where filling cannot increase, and the pulse volume varies with respiration.

In acute haemopericardium the picture is of sudden collapse; in a slowly accumulating effusion it develops insidiously with progressive breathlessness and fatigue.
### Investigation
The diagnosis is clinical. In a compromised patient, investigation must not delay treatment.

Echocardiography is the confirmatory investigation and also guides drainage: it demonstrates the effusion, diastolic collapse of the right atrium and right ventricle, exaggerated respiratory variation in valve inflow velocities, and a plethoric inferior vena cava.

ECG may show low voltage and electrical alternans. Chest radiograph may show a globular heart, and a normal-sized heart does not exclude tamponade in an acute effusion.

The cause is investigated after or alongside treatment, and pericardial fluid is analysed.
### Management
Tamponade is treated by removing the fluid. Pericardiocentesis, usually echocardiographically guided, is the standard approach; surgical drainage is required for haemopericardium from trauma, dissection or myocardial rupture, and for loculated or recurrent effusions.

While drainage is arranged, the aim is to preserve preload. Intravenous fluid may temporarily improve filling in a hypovolaemic patient. Vasodilators and diuretics are avoided, because they reduce the venous pressure the circulation is depending on. Positive pressure ventilation is hazardous for the same reason and is avoided where possible until the pericardium is drained.

The definitive treatment is drainage, and supportive measures are a bridge to it rather than an alternative.

Techniques, approaches and indications follow the guideline in force; none is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Untreated tamponade is fatal. Complications of drainage include chamber laceration, arrhythmia, pneumothorax and, uncommonly, decompression-related ventricular dysfunction.

Prognosis after prompt drainage is determined by the underlying cause: excellent in idiopathic or procedural tamponade, poor in malignant tamponade, which reflects advanced disease.
### Classification
Tamponade is described as acute or subacute, and as low-pressure where it occurs in a hypovolaemic patient at lower pericardial pressures — a presentation easily missed, because the venous pressure may not be visibly raised.
## hold_these
Tamponade is a clinical diagnosis; echocardiography confirms it and must not delay treatment in a compromised patient.
Pulsus paradoxus is an exaggerated normal inspiratory fall, produced by ventricular competition for a fixed volume.
Filling depends on venous pressure exceeding pericardial pressure, so anything reducing preload can precipitate collapse.

Giving a diuretic or vasodilator to a hypotensive patient with tamponade.
Waiting for an echocardiogram before acting in a patient who is collapsing.
Excluding tamponade because the heart is not enlarged on the chest radiograph.

## summary
Right heart failure in a patient whose heart muscle is fine. It is missed for years, mistaken for liver disease — and unlike almost everything else in this chapter, an operation can cure it.
## sections
### Definition
Constrictive pericarditis is impaired diastolic filling of the heart caused by a thickened, fibrotic, often calcified and non-compliant pericardium.

Filling in early diastole is rapid and unimpeded until the limit of the rigid sac is reached, at which point it stops abruptly.
### Epidemiology
Constriction is uncommon but consistently under-diagnosed, because its presentation resembles hepatic and other non-cardiac disease and patients are frequently investigated for years before the diagnosis is made.

Its aetiological profile depends heavily on setting. Where tuberculosis is endemic, including in Egypt, tuberculous pericarditis is a leading cause. In high-income settings the commonest causes are previous cardiac surgery, mediastinal radiotherapy and idiopathic or viral pericarditis.
### Aetiology and risk factors
Tuberculous pericarditis; previous cardiac surgery; mediastinal radiotherapy; idiopathic or viral pericarditis, in which constriction is uncommon but occurs; purulent pericarditis; connective tissue disease; uraemia; and malignancy.

The interval between the causative illness and the onset of constriction is often years, so the history must be taken far back.
### Pathophysiology
A rigid pericardium fixes the total volume available to the heart, and three consequences follow.

**Filling stops abruptly.** Early diastolic filling is rapid, then halts as the constraint is reached — producing the characteristic dip-and-plateau pressure waveform and, on auscultation, a pericardial knock.

**The ventricles become interdependent.** With total volume fixed, one ventricle fills only at the expense of the other. Respiration therefore produces exaggerated reciprocal variation in filling between the two, and this finding is the most reliable way to distinguish constriction from restrictive myocardial disease.

**The heart is isolated from intrathoracic pressure changes.** Inspiration lowers intrathoracic pressure but that fall is not transmitted through the rigid pericardium to the cardiac chambers, so the usual inspiratory increase in right-sided filling does not occur. The jugular venous pressure therefore rises rather than falls on inspiration — Kussmaul sign.

The myocardium itself is normal, which is why systolic function is preserved and why removing the constraint can restore normal physiology.
### Clinical picture
Progressive fatigue, breathlessness on exertion, and prominent right-sided congestion: abdominal distension from ascites, hepatomegaly, peripheral oedema and weight gain.

Ascites is characteristically out of proportion to peripheral oedema, and this combination — together with hepatomegaly and abnormal liver function — is why patients are frequently investigated for cirrhosis before the heart is considered.

Signs — a markedly raised jugular venous pressure with a prominent rapid y descent; **Kussmaul sign**; a **pericardial knock**, an early diastolic sound corresponding to the abrupt halt in filling; pulsus paradoxus, which is usually less marked than in tamponade; hepatomegaly, ascites and oedema. The apex beat is impalpable or non-displaced.
### Investigation
Echocardiography demonstrates the physiology: respiratory variation in mitral and tricuspid inflow velocities showing ventricular interdependence, septal bounce, dilated inferior vena cava, and preserved systolic function.

CT and cardiac MRI assess pericardial thickness and calcification and are complementary. **Constriction can occur with a pericardium of normal thickness**, so normal imaging does not exclude it — the physiology is the more reliable evidence.

Chest radiograph may show pericardial calcification, which is close to diagnostic where present.

Cardiac catheterisation with simultaneous left and right ventricular pressure recording demonstrates equalisation of diastolic pressures, the dip-and-plateau waveform, and discordant respiratory variation between the ventricles — and resolves cases where non-invasive assessment is inconclusive.

The essential differential is restrictive cardiomyopathy, and distinguishing them is the purpose of much of this investigation. Natriuretic peptide tends to be lower in constriction than in restriction, since the myocardium is not stretched.

Investigation of the cause includes assessment for tuberculosis where relevant.
### Management
**Pericardiectomy is the definitive treatment**, and it is potentially curative — which is what makes the diagnosis worth pursuing. It is a substantial operation with meaningful perioperative mortality, and outcomes are better when it is performed before advanced cachexia, severe hepatic dysfunction or myocardial atrophy have developed. Late referral worsens results, which is an argument for early diagnosis rather than for delay.

Diuretic therapy relieves congestion and is a bridge rather than a treatment.

Where constriction is transient — occurring during resolving pericarditis — a period of anti-inflammatory therapy may allow it to resolve without surgery, and this possibility is assessed before committing to operation.

Tuberculous constriction requires antituberculous therapy alongside surgical consideration.

Timing, technique and adjunctive therapy follow the guideline in force; none is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Progressive right heart failure, cardiac cirrhosis, protein-losing enteropathy, cachexia and atrial arrhythmia.

Untreated, the condition progresses. After successful pericardiectomy the prognosis is good, particularly where the cause was idiopathic or viral; results are less good after radiotherapy-induced constriction, where the myocardium is also affected.
### Classification
Constriction is distinguished from restriction by the site of disease — sac against muscle — and this is the classification that determines whether an operation can help.

The pericardium may be of normal thickness, so normal imaging does not exclude the diagnosis.
