## summary
The glucuronic acid pathway is a minor side-branch of glucose oxidation that turns glucose 6-phosphate into UDP-glucuronic acid. In most mammals it goes one step further, to vitamin C; humans and a handful of other species lost the enzyme that takes that last step, which is exactly why vitamin C is a vitamin for us and not for a rat. What the pathway still does for every human cell is supply the activated glucuronic acid the liver uses to conjugate bilirubin and a wide range of drugs and other foreign compounds for excretion.

## sections
### Definition
The glucuronic acid pathway (also called the uronic acid pathway) is an oxidative route of glucose metabolism that runs from glucose 6-phosphate, through glucose 1-phosphate and UDP-glucose, to UDP-glucuronic acid — the activated form of glucuronic acid the rest of the pathway's reactions depend on.

### Mechanism
UDP-glucose dehydrogenase oxidises UDP-glucose's C6 hydroxymethyl group to a carboxylic acid, producing UDP-glucuronic acid and consuming two NAD+. From there the pathway can run in two directions depending on the species. In most mammals, UDP-glucuronic acid is reduced and decarboxylated across several further steps to L-gulonolactone, and then oxidised by L-gulonolactone oxidase to L-ascorbic acid (vitamin C). Humans, other primates and guinea pigs carry an inactive, mutated gene for L-gulonolactone oxidase, so this last conversion cannot happen — the pathway simply stops one step short, and none of the intermediate metabolites accumulate abnormally, because UDP-glucuronic acid is drawn off for a second job instead. That second job is conjugation: the liver enzyme UDP-glucuronyl transferase transfers glucuronic acid from UDP-glucuronic acid onto bilirubin, onto steroid hormones, and onto a very wide range of drugs and other xenobiotics, making each of them more water-soluble and easier to excrete in bile or urine.

### Key determinants
Which direction the pathway can run is fixed by whether an animal's genome carries a working copy of L-gulonolactone oxidase — this is a species-level, not a regulatory, determinant. Within humans, the pathway's conjugating (glucuronidation) capacity is inducible: some of the same drugs it conjugates also increase UDP-glucuronyl transferase's own expression, and hepatic disease that reduces liver enzyme mass reduces this capacity along with everything else the liver does.

### Clinical significance
Because humans cannot complete the pathway to ascorbic acid, vitamin C must come from the diet, and its absence produces scurvy — a fact whose mechanism only makes sense once the missing enzyme is named. The pathway's conjugating role is clinically active every day: it is one of the two routes (with glycine conjugation) by which the liver detoxifies benzoate, and it is the specific route that converts unconjugated bilirubin to the water-soluble conjugated form excreted in bile — a step that fails in neonatal and hepatic jaundice.

### Common misconceptions
That the pathway is simply switched off in humans. It runs normally as far as UDP-glucuronic acid and continues to conjugate bilirubin and xenobiotics exactly as in other species; only the single further step to ascorbic acid is blocked, by the absence of one enzyme.

## summary
Gluconeogenesis rebuilds glucose from pyruvate by reversing glycolysis at its three irreversible steps, using four different enzymes to bypass them. Two of those steps happen inside the mitochondrion, so the pathway needs a way to get a four-carbon intermediate back out to the cytosol — the malate shuttle — and it needs to pay for the privilege: six high-energy phosphate bonds per glucose made, three times what glycolysis earned running the other way.

## sections
### Definition
Gluconeogenesis is the synthesis of glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors — lactate, glycerol, glucogenic amino acids and propionate — by reversing glycolysis except at its three irreversible steps, which four dedicated enzymes bypass instead: pyruvate carboxylase, phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase, fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase and glucose 6-phosphatase.

### Mechanism
Pyruvate carboxylase, a biotin-dependent mitochondrial enzyme, carboxylates pyruvate to oxaloacetate, spending one ATP. Oxaloacetate has no transporter across the inner mitochondrial membrane, so before it can be handed to cytosolic phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase it must leave as something else: mitochondrial malate dehydrogenase reduces it to malate, which crosses on the malate-α-ketoglutarate carrier, and cytosolic malate dehydrogenase then re-oxidises the malate back to oxaloacetate, generating a cytosolic NADH that the pathway's later glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase step needs. Only then does phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase convert oxaloacetate to phosphoenolpyruvate, spending one GTP. Counting for two pyruvate molecules — needed to build one six-carbon glucose — the pathway spends two ATP at pyruvate carboxylase, two GTP at PEP carboxykinase, and two more ATP at the reversed phosphoglycerate kinase step: six high-energy phosphate bonds in total, plus the two NADH the malate shuttle itself supplies.

### Key determinants
Substrate availability (lactate from muscle and red cells, glycerol from lipolysis, alanine from muscle protein breakdown) and hormonal state: glucagon and cortisol favour gluconeogenesis, while insulin suppresses it. The pathway only proceeds when the liver's energy state can afford the six-ATP-equivalent cost, which is why active fatty acid oxidation — itself supplying ATP and the acetyl-CoA that activates pyruvate carboxylase — normally accompanies active gluconeogenesis rather than competing with it.

### Clinical significance
The malate shuttle's requirement explains why any lesion or poison affecting mitochondrial malate dehydrogenase or the malate transporter blocks gluconeogenesis even when pyruvate carboxylase itself is intact — oxaloacetate simply cannot leave the mitochondrion by any other route. The six-ATP cost is also why prolonged starvation, which depletes the fatty-acid-oxidation-derived ATP the liver needs, eventually limits how much glucose gluconeogenesis can still supply to the brain and red cells.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming the malate-aspartate shuttle always carries reducing equivalents into the mitochondrion, as it does during glycolysis. In gluconeogenesis the traffic runs the opposite way: a mitochondrial carbon skeleton (as malate) leaves for the cytosol, and the NADH produced there is consumed by gluconeogenesis itself, not delivered to the electron transport chain.

## summary
Fructose and galactose each have their own dedicated liver pathway into glycolysis, and each pathway has a classic inherited block. Hereditary fructose intolerance traps fructose 1-phosphate behind a missing aldolase B and starves the liver of usable phosphate and ATP; classic galactosemia traps galactose-1-phosphate behind a missing uridyltransferase and, through the sugar alcohol galactitol, damages the lens, liver and brain. The two diseases are natural teaching partners because their logic — a specific enzyme missing, a specific phosphorylated intermediate accumulating, specific downstream damage — is identical in shape.

## sections
### Definition
Hereditary fructose intolerance is deficiency of aldolase B, the liver enzyme that splits fructose 1-phosphate — made from dietary fructose by an intact fructokinase — into dihydroxyacetone phosphate and glyceraldehyde. Classic galactosemia is deficiency of galactose-1-phosphate uridyltransferase, the liver enzyme that exchanges galactose-1-phosphate's phosphate group for UDP-glucose's glucose, producing UDP-galactose and glucose-1-phosphate.

### Mechanism
In hereditary fructose intolerance, fructokinase still phosphorylates ingested fructose to fructose 1-phosphate, but with aldolase B missing that intermediate cannot be split further and accumulates in the liver. Trapped fructose 1-phosphate sequesters inorganic phosphate, which secondarily depletes ATP and inhibits both glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis; the result is severe hypoglycaemia after fructose or sucrose ingestion, together with fructosuria, because fructose cannot be processed past the block. In classic galactosemia, galactokinase still phosphorylates ingested galactose to galactose-1-phosphate, but with the transferase missing that intermediate accumulates in liver, lens, kidney and brain; excess galactose is also diverted by aldose reductase to galactitol, an osmotically active sugar alcohol that draws water into the lens and produces cataracts.

### Key determinants
Both diseases are autosomal recessive and both are exposure-dependent: features appear only once the substrate sugar (fructose or galactose) enters the diet, which for galactosemia means milk feeding from birth and for fructose intolerance means the introduction of fruit, sucrose or fructose-sweetened foods, often at weaning.

### Clinical significance
Untreated classic galactosemia causes hepatosplenomegaly, jaundice, cataracts and mental retardation, and can be rapidly fatal from E. coli sepsis in the neonatal period; treatment is lifelong galactose (and lactose) exclusion. Hereditary fructose intolerance presents with vomiting, hypoglycaemia and failure to thrive on fructose exposure and is managed by strict fructose and sucrose avoidance; because both diseases are managed by removing one sugar from the diet, misidentifying which enzyme is missing leads to the wrong sugar being restricted.

### Common misconceptions
That galactosemia causes haemolytic anaemia — it does not; the disease's recognised features are hepatic, cerebral, ocular and infective, not haematological, and this is precisely the false option the department's own question bank tests. Equally, believing fructose intolerance leaves urine free of fructose is backwards: fructosuria is present precisely because the block stops fructose being metabolised further.

## summary
The oral glucose tolerance test gives a standard 75 g glucose load and reads how plasma glucose behaves over the next two hours. Three bands — normal, impaired glucose tolerance, and diabetes mellitus — are defined mainly by the two-hour value, and reading the peak instead of the two-hour figure is the commonest way to misclassify a real result.

## sections
### Definition
The oral glucose tolerance test measures plasma glucose fasting and after a standard 75 g oral glucose load, most decisively at the two-hour sample, to test how efficiently the body disposes of a glucose challenge — a more sensitive probe of glucose handling than a single fasting value.

### Mechanism
After the glucose load, insulin secretion should rise promptly enough that plasma glucose peaks (commonly around one hour) and then falls back toward the fasting value by two hours. A two-hour value under 140 mg/dL, together with a fasting value under 100-110 mg/dL, is normal tolerance. A two-hour value of 140-199 mg/dL, with the fasting value still below the diabetic threshold, is impaired glucose tolerance — the pancreas is coping less efficiently, but not yet failing outright. A two-hour value of 200 mg/dL or more, or a fasting value of 126 mg/dL or more on its own, is diagnostic of diabetes mellitus.

### Key determinants
Insulin secretory capacity and peripheral insulin sensitivity both set how quickly the glucose curve returns toward baseline; anything that impairs either — early beta-cell failure, or the insulin resistance of obesity and pregnancy — shifts the two-hour value upward even before a fasting abnormality appears, which is exactly why the test exists alongside the fasting glucose.

### Clinical significance
Impaired glucose tolerance identifies patients at high risk of progressing to diabetes and of macrovascular disease even before diabetic thresholds are met, so the test's three-way classification, not just a normal/abnormal cut, is what drives whether a patient is offered lifestyle intervention now. Reading only the peak value, or only the fasting value, in a full OGTT time-course throws away the information the test was ordered to provide.

### Common misconceptions
Classifying a result by its highest recorded value rather than by the two-hour sample — a patient can peak well above 200 mg/dL at one hour and still fall to within the impaired-tolerance band by two hours, which the definitions above classify as impaired glucose tolerance, not diabetes.

## summary
Lecithin — phosphatidylcholine — is built by attaching an activated choline head group to diacylglycerol. The activation runs through CDP-choline, made from choline and CTP by way of phosphocholine, and this nucleotide carrier is the one detail this topic is reliably examined on.

## sections
### Definition
Lecithin (phosphatidylcholine) is synthesised by the choline branch of the Kennedy pathway: diacylglycerol combines with activated choline, carried as CDP-choline, releasing CMP and forming the finished phospholipid.

### Mechanism
Choline is first phosphorylated by choline kinase, using ATP, to phosphocholine. Phosphocholine is then activated by CTP, in a reaction catalysed by CTP:phosphocholine cytidylyltransferase, to form CDP-choline (releasing pyrophosphate) — this is the pathway's committed, rate-limiting step. Finally, phosphocholine transferase transfers the phosphocholine group from CDP-choline onto diacylglycerol, forming phosphatidylcholine and releasing CMP. This route is the major source of lecithin in most tissues; the liver alone can also make it by methylating phosphatidylethanolamine three times using S-adenosylmethionine, but that route is minor by comparison.

### Key determinants
Diacylglycerol availability and choline supply both limit the pathway; choline can be obtained from the diet or synthesised de novo (as phosphatidylethanolamine, via the liver-only methylation route above), which is why choline is only conditionally essential.

### Clinical significance
Lecithin made this way is a major component of cell membranes and of pulmonary surfactant, and CDP-choline itself (citicoline) is used clinically as a source of choline in some neurological and ophthalmological contexts — its relevance to this pathway is that it is literally the same activated intermediate the synthesis route is named for.

### Common misconceptions
Naming UDP-choline, GDP-choline or ATP-choline as the activated donor in this reaction. The nucleotide carrier is specifically CDP-choline, formed from CTP — not from UTP, GTP or ATP — and this is the exact distractor the department's own question bank is built around.

## summary
Cyanide kills by binding cytochrome oxidase — Complex IV of the electron transport chain — not by touching hemoglobin or blood oxygen transport at all. With Complex IV blocked, the whole chain backs up, oxidative phosphorylation stops, and tissues that depend most on it fail within minutes despite blood being fully oxygenated.

## sections
### Definition
Cyanide is a rapidly lethal poison whose toxic mechanism is inhibition of cytochrome c oxidase, Complex IV of the mitochondrial electron transport chain — the enzyme that transfers electrons to molecular oxygen at the chain's terminal step.

### Mechanism
Cyanide ion binds tightly to the ferric (Fe³⁺) iron of cytochrome a3 within Complex IV, preventing that iron from accepting electrons and passing them on to oxygen. Because Complex IV is the chain's only route to oxygen, every complex upstream of it also backs up: NADH and FADH2 cannot be reoxidised, the proton gradient collapses, and ATP synthase stops making ATP. Cells respond by switching to anaerobic glycolysis, producing lactate and a metabolic acidosis, even though arterial blood is carrying a normal or even supra-normal oxygen content — this is histotoxic hypoxia, hypoxia at the tissue level despite normal oxygen delivery, and it is what separates cyanide poisoning from every cause of hypoxia that works by limiting how much oxygen reaches the tissues in the first place.

### Key determinants
The tissues that fail first and fastest are those most dependent on oxidative phosphorylation for ATP — brain and heart — which is why cyanide poisoning causes rapid loss of consciousness, seizures and cardiac arrest rather than a slower, organ-selective picture.

### Clinical significance
Because cyanide does not impair oxygen carriage, pulse oximetry and even arterial oxygen content can look normal or high in a poisoned patient while cellular energy production has already stopped — the diagnostic clue is a lactic acidosis with a normal or high venous oxygen saturation (arteriovenous oxygen difference is abnormally narrow, because tissues cannot extract and use the oxygen delivered to them). Treatment strategies exploit alternative electron- or cyanide-handling chemistry (for example, inducing methaemoglobin to compete for cyanide, or supplying sulfur donors for detoxification to thiocyanate) rather than trying to improve oxygen delivery, which is already adequate.

### Common misconceptions
Believing cyanide acts like carbon monoxide, by binding haemoglobin and blocking oxygen transport in blood. It does not form a haemoglobin complex and blood oxygen content is essentially unaffected; the entire lethal mechanism is intracellular, at Complex IV, which is exactly the distractor pair the department's own question bank tests.
