## summary
A mode of inheritance is the pattern by which a trait passes from parent to offspring across a pedigree, and that pattern is itself evidence for where the responsible gene sits and how it behaves. This article works through the vocabulary (genotype, phenotype, allele, zygosity) and the six single-gene patterns — autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, X-linked dominant, X-linked recessive, Y-linked and mitochondrial — that a pedigree can show, plus the two determinants (variable expressivity and new mutation) that complicate a strict Mendelian read.

## sections
### Definition
Genotype is an individual's genetic constitution at a locus — which alleles they carry. Phenotype is the physical, observable expression of that genotype. An allele is one alternative form of a gene. A genotype's zygosity state names which two alleles it carries: homozygous for two identical alleles, heterozygous for two different alleles, and compound heterozygous specifically for two different mutant alleles of the same gene, with no normal copy present at all. A mode of inheritance is the pattern by which a trait passes from parent to offspring across a pedigree, and that pattern — who is affected, and in what proportion of their children — is itself evidence for where the responsible gene sits and how it behaves.

### Mechanism
Autosomal dominant inheritance produces a vertical pedigree, with an affected individual in every generation, because one copy of the mutant allele on an autosome is enough to produce the phenotype. Either sex is affected equally, only one parent needs to be affected to transmit the trait, and each pregnancy of an affected-by-unaffected mating carries an independent 50% risk regardless of earlier pregnancies. Male-to-male transmission is possible in autosomal dominant inheritance, and together with equal involvement of either sex, that is what distinguishes an autosomal pattern from a sex-linked one.
Autosomal recessive inheritance needs two copies of the mutant allele to produce the phenotype, so it typically requires two carrier, heterozygous, clinically normal parents. A carrier-by-carrier mating carries a 25% risk of an affected child, a 50% chance of a carrier child and a 25% chance of a homozygous-normal child at every pregnancy. Because both parents are usually unaffected, autosomal recessive disease tends to cluster within one sibship rather than appearing in every generation, and consanguinity raises the chance that both parents happen to carry the same rare recessive allele.
X-linked recessive inheritance affects males almost exclusively, because a male's single X chromosome carries no second, normal copy to mask a recessive allele. A carrier mother transmits the allele to 50% of her sons, who are affected, and to 50% of her daughters, who become carriers. An affected father transmits the allele to all of his daughters, making every one of them an obligate carrier, and to none of his sons, who receive his Y chromosome instead — there is never male-to-male transmission in an X-linked pattern of either kind.
X-linked dominant inheritance needs only one copy of the mutant allele on the X chromosome to produce the phenotype. An affected father transmits the condition to all of his daughters and to none of his sons. An affected heterozygous mother transmits it to 50% of her children, regardless of sex. X-linked hypophosphataemic rickets is the standard teaching example, chosen specifically because it separates this pattern from both autosomal dominant inheritance, where sex does not affect transmission, and X-linked recessive inheritance, where an affected father's daughters are carriers rather than affected.
Y-linked, or holandric, inheritance is the one pattern in which male-to-male transmission is obligatory in every affected lineage: an affected father transmits the trait to all of his sons, because only sons inherit a Y chromosome, and to none of his daughters.
Mitochondrial inheritance follows neither the autosomes nor the sex chromosomes. It passes only through the mother, because the egg's cytoplasm, and the mitochondria within it, forms the zygote's cytoplasm, while sperm mitochondria are not retained after fertilisation. An affected mother transmits the mutation to all of her children regardless of sex; an affected father transmits it to none of his children.

### Key determinants
A strict Mendelian read of a pedigree is only the starting point. Within autosomal dominant disease, the identical mutation can still produce a milder or more severe phenotype in different affected relatives, a phenomenon called variable expressivity, so severity alone cannot rule a relative in or out of carrying the mutation. When an autosomal dominant disorder appears in a child of two unaffected, non-consanguineous parents, the standard explanation is a new, or de novo, mutation arising in a parental germ cell at conception, not reduced penetrance and not a hidden recessive mechanism; achondroplasia is the worked teaching example, since most cases arise in families with no prior history. Consanguinity is the determinant that most changes an autosomal recessive pedigree's odds, because a shared ancestor raises the chance that both parents inherited the same rare allele. Every X-linked pattern's own logic follows from one fact about chromosome inheritance: a father always gives his Y chromosome, never his X, to a son.

### Clinical significance
A genetic-counselling question almost always reduces to two steps: read the pedigree to name the pattern, then apply that pattern's own recurrence-risk rule. A beta-thalassaemia carrier-by-carrier couple asking about a future pregnancy is answered with the autosomal recessive figure, 25%, not a guess based on severity. A parent with Marfan syndrome, an autosomal dominant condition, and an unaffected partner faces an independent 50% risk at every pregnancy — a healthy first child changes nothing about the second child's risk, and a question implying otherwise is testing exactly this misconception. Distinguishing X-linked recessive disease, where affected fathers have carrier daughters and unaffected sons, from X-linked dominant disease, where affected fathers have affected daughters, changes what a clinician tells a family about who else in the pedigree needs testing.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming any pattern with an affected parent and an affected child must be autosomal dominant — an X-linked dominant pedigree looks identical except that an affected father's sons are always spared.
Reading a healthy sibling as evidence against an autosomal recessive diagnosis elsewhere in the family — the risk is per conception, not a guarantee, and 75% of a carrier-by-carrier couple's children are clinically well, whether homozygous-normal or carriers.
Believing a heterozygous carrier of an autosomal recessive condition shows any sign of the disease — carriers are, by definition, clinically normal.

## summary
Not every heritable pattern follows the six single-gene rules. Genomic imprinting silences one parent's copy of specific genes, so which parent contributed a lost copy determines the syndrome; trinucleotide repeat expansion disorders can worsen across generations (anticipation); and multifactorial disorders follow no fixed Mendelian fraction at all, with risk instead rising continuously with relatedness, family history and severity. All three are exam-tested exceptions to the pattern-recognition rules taught in the companion Mendelian article.

## sections
### Definition
Genomic imprinting is the silencing of one parent's copy of a gene in the germline, so that only the other parent's copy is normally active in the offspring. Genetic anticipation is the tendency of some inherited disorders to appear at an earlier age, and with greater severity, in each successive generation of an affected family. A multifactorial disorder results from the combined effect of several genes and environmental factors acting together, rather than from a single gene behaving in a Mendelian way.

### Mechanism
At chromosome 15q11-13, only the paternally-inherited copy of the Prader-Willi region is normally active; losing it, most often through a deletion of the paternal chromosome 15 or through maternal uniparental disomy, causes Prader-Willi syndrome. A nearby gene, UBE3A, works the opposite way round: only the maternally-inherited copy is normally active, so losing that active maternal copy causes Angelman syndrome. The two syndromes are reciprocal disorders of the same chromosomal region, distinguished entirely by which parent's copy was lost, which is why the same deletion can produce two different clinical pictures depending on which parent it came from.
Trinucleotide repeat expansion disorders are caused by a repeated three-nucleotide sequence within or near a gene expanding beyond a disease threshold. Huntington's disease is the worked example: a CAG repeat within the coding region, translated as a polyglutamine tract, becomes disease-causing at roughly 36 repeats or more. Because the repeat tends to expand further with each transmission, particularly through the paternal germline, the age of onset falls and the severity rises across successive generations of an affected family — the anticipation this pattern is named for.
A multifactorial disorder does not follow a fixed Mendelian recurrence fraction such as 25% or 50%. Instead, the recurrence risk to a relative rises with a closer degree of relationship to the affected individual, with a greater number of already-affected relatives in the family, and with greater severity of the affected individual's own malformation.

### Key determinants
The determinant in genomic imprinting is entirely parental origin, not gene dosage — a student who reasons only from "one copy lost" without asking which parent it came from will confuse Prader-Willi and Angelman syndromes, since the same physical deletion produces either syndrome depending on that one fact. The determinant in a trinucleotide repeat disorder is which side of the disease threshold the repeat count sits on, and that count itself is unstable and prone to expand at meiosis, which is the reason the same disease gene can present differently across a family's generations. The determinant in a multifactorial disorder's risk is cumulative rather than singular: relatedness, family history and severity all move the risk in the same direction, and none of them alone fixes it the way a single dominant or recessive allele would.

### Clinical significance
A family history of Huntington's disease with progressively earlier onset in younger generations is not evidence of a new, more aggressive disease — it is anticipation, and it should prompt repeat-length testing rather than a search for a second condition. A clinician evaluating hypotonia and developmental delay in an infant needs to know which parent's chromosome 15 has been affected before counting a deletion as diagnostic of Prader-Willi rather than Angelman syndrome. When counselling a family about a multifactorial condition such as a neural tube defect, the recurrence-risk figure quoted to a couple depends on how many relatives are already affected and how severe the index case was, not on a single number that applies to every family alike.
