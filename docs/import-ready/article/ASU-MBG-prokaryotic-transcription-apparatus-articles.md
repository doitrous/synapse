## summary
Bacteria transcribe every RNA class with one multi-subunit RNA polymerase, and that single enzyme's behaviour at both ends of transcription — how it starts and how it stops — is what this module's own question bank tests. This is a deliberately minimal article: it teaches exactly the prokaryotic-apparatus facts eight banked questions ask for (sigma factor, the holoenzyme, the Pribnow box, and the two termination mechanisms), scoped to that and no wider, per a chief-of-staff ruling that a tested concept must not be left without a covering article.

## sections
### Definition
Bacteria transcribe mRNA, rRNA and tRNA with a single RNA polymerase, in contrast to eukaryotes, which use three separate polymerases (I, II and III) for those same three RNA classes. That one bacterial enzyme is itself an aggregate of several different subunits, which is why it is called a holoenzyme. The holoenzyme is the core enzyme — the catalytic subunits alone, sufficient for elongation once transcription is under way — plus a detachable sigma subunit that the core enzyme needs specifically for accurate initiation.

### Mechanism
Sigma factor is what lets the holoenzyme find the correct place to start. It recognises the Pribnow box, a consensus sequence (TATAAT) centred about ten base pairs upstream of the transcription start site, and directs the holoenzyme to open the DNA there and begin synthesising RNA. Once elongation is under way, sigma factor can dissociate, leaving the core enzyme to continue on its own. Transcription ends in one of two ways. Rho-dependent termination needs the rho protein, which follows behind the polymerase along the growing RNA transcript and eventually catches up to it, helping to unwind the RNA-DNA hybrid and release both the polymerase and the finished RNA. Intrinsic, or rho-independent, termination needs no separate protein factor at all: a G-C-rich, self-complementary sequence near the 3' end of the new RNA folds back on itself into a hairpin, and that hairpin combined with a following run of U residues destabilises the RNA-DNA hybrid enough to release the transcript on its own.

### Key determinants
Whether termination needs the rho protein or happens intrinsically depends entirely on the sequence encoded at the end of that particular gene — a G-C-rich inverted repeat followed by a run of U residues is what makes a terminator intrinsic, and its absence is what makes a gene depend on rho instead. Neither initiation nor termination in bacterial transcription depends on a dedicated helicase enzyme: RNA polymerase itself locally opens the double helix it is transcribing, which is why "helicase activity" is not one of the enzymes actually required for transcription, unlike DNA replication, which does depend on a dedicated helicase (DnaB in E. coli).

### Clinical significance
This apparatus is also the reason several antibiotic classes work: rifampin, for instance, inhibits the bacterial RNA polymerase's core enzyme directly, and because bacteria rely on this single enzyme for every RNA class, blocking it halts all bacterial transcription at once — a selectivity advantage that depends on bacterial RNA polymerase being structurally distinct enough from the three separate human polymerases for the drug to spare human transcription.

## hold_these
Bacteria use one RNA polymerase (a holoenzyme = core enzyme + sigma factor) for every RNA class; eukaryotes use three separate polymerases.
Sigma factor recognises the Pribnow box (TATAAT, about -10) to initiate transcription at the correct site.
Bacterial termination is either rho-dependent (needs the rho protein) or intrinsic (a G-C-rich hairpin plus poly-U, no protein factor needed).
Transcription needs no dedicated helicase — RNA polymerase unwinds the DNA it transcribes on its own.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming bacteria have three separate RNA polymerases the way eukaryotes do.
Assuming every bacterial transcript needs the rho protein to terminate.
Attributing promoter recognition to the core enzyme rather than to the sigma subunit specifically.
