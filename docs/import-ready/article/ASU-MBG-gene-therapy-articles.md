## summary
Gene therapy corrects a genetic disorder by inserting a normal gene into human cells, and whether that correction is heritable turns entirely on whether the germline or somatic cell line is targeted. The recombinant DNA technology that makes this — and molecular cloning more broadly — possible starts with restriction endonucleases, bacterial enzymes that recognise specific palindromic sequences and cut DNA there, leaving either sticky (overlapping) or blunt (non-overlapping) ends; the article works through how many fragments a given number of cut sites produces in a linear versus a circular molecule, and then through the cloning workflow itself — cutting out a target gene, joining it to a plasmid vector to form chimeric DNA, transfecting a host cell, and selecting transformants by a marker gene. It closes with CRISPR-cas9 as a distinct gene-editing strategy and the worked clinical case this chapter tests most: adenosine deaminase (ADA) deficiency as the first disorder ever treated with gene therapy.

## sections
### Definition
Gene therapy is the insertion of a normal gene into human cells to correct a disorder caused by a defective or missing one — distinct from live vector vaccines, molecular cloning, and stem cell therapy, which are related but different technologies tested as distractors against it. Germline gene therapy modifies reproductive cells and so is heritable, passed to the patient's offspring; somatic gene therapy modifies non-reproductive body cells and so is not heritable, confined to the treated individual — this is the only form currently used clinically. Restriction endonucleases occur normally in bacteria as part of a defence system against foreign DNA, and each recognises a specific palindromic sequence (one that equals its own reverse complement, e.g. ACGGCCGT) rather than cutting DNA at random.

### Mechanism
A restriction endonuclease's cut leaves either sticky ends — staggered cuts producing complementary single-stranded overhangs, described as overlapping fragment ends, which let two fragments cut with the same enzyme anneal specifically — or blunt ends, cuts at the same position on both strands producing flush, non-overlapping ends. Counting fragments depends on the DNA's topology: a linear molecule with n cut sites yields n+1 fragments (it already had two free ends before cutting), while a circular molecule such as a plasmid with n cut sites yields exactly n fragments (it had no free ends to begin with). Molecular cloning uses this machinery in a fixed workflow: a restriction endonuclease first cuts out the target DNA segment, that segment is joined to a cut vector to form chimeric DNA (also called hybrid or recombinant DNA — three names for the same product), the chimeric DNA is transfected into a host cell, and successfully transformed host cells are finally selected using the vector's marker gene. The standard vector is a bacterial plasmid — double-stranded circular DNA — and a functional vector must be capable of replicating inside the host cell, contain a restriction site, and carry a marker gene; being circular is typical of plasmids but is not itself one of the three essential features, since viral vectors are not circular. The same cloning technology underlies monoclonal antibody production for diagnostic tests. CRISPR-cas9 is classified separately, as a gene-editing strategy distinct from recombinant DNA/cloning, PCR-based amplification, and gene therapy itself; Cas9 is the enzyme it uses to cut DNA at a site specified by a guide RNA.

### Key determinants
The single distinction every question in this chapter turns on is: is this fact about the enzyme's mechanism (recognition, cutting, sticky/blunt ends, fragment counting), the cloning workflow that uses that mechanism (chimeric DNA, vector, ordering of steps), or a separate classification question (gene therapy vs cloning vs gene editing vs PCR, and germline vs somatic heritability). Keeping the sequence of the cloning workflow straight — cut, join, transfect, select — and keeping the linear-vs-circular fragment-counting rule straight, are the two places this chapter most rewards careful reasoning over memorisation.

### Clinical significance
Adenosine deaminase (ADA) deficiency, an autosomal recessive severe combined immunodeficiency in which bone marrow lymphoblasts cannot replicate to generate immune-competent lymphocytes, was the first disorder researchers treated with gene therapy, and gene therapy to replace the ADA gene is the only option among blood-product transfusions that can permanently cure the patient, because transfusions supply donor material temporarily without correcting the underlying genetic defect.

### Common misconceptions
Treating "restriction site" as any DNA sequence rather than specifically a palindromic one. Reversing sticky (overlapping) and blunt (non-overlapping) end definitions. Applying the linear-DNA fragment-counting rule (n+1) to a circular plasmid, which instead follows the n-fragments rule. Naming "must be circular" as an essential vector feature rather than a typical-but-not-required property of plasmid vectors specifically. Selecting a transfusion option as curative for ADA-SCID rather than recognising that only gene replacement corrects the underlying defect.

## hold_these
Gene therapy: inserts a normal gene to correct a disorder; germline is heritable, somatic is not.
Restriction endonucleases: normally bacterial; recognise palindromic sequences; sticky ends are overlapping, blunt ends are non-overlapping.
Fragment counting: linear DNA with n sites gives n+1 fragments; circular DNA with n sites gives n fragments.
Cloning workflow order: restriction-digest target DNA, form chimeric DNA, transfect host cell, select by marker gene.
Vector essential features: replicates in host, contains a restriction site, carries a marker gene (circularity is typical of plasmids, not essential).
CRISPR-cas9: a gene-editing strategy; Cas9 is the cutting enzyme.
ADA deficiency: the first disorder treated with gene therapy; gene therapy to replace ADA is the only permanently curative option among the transfusion distractors.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing gene therapy, molecular cloning, gene editing (CRISPR) and PCR as interchangeable rather than distinct classifications tested against each other.
Reversing which gene-therapy line (germline vs somatic) is heritable.
Reversing sticky vs blunt end definitions, or misreading "sticky" as the strands sticking to the enzyme itself.
Applying the wrong fragment-counting rule to a circular vs linear molecule.
Selecting a transfusion option as a permanent cure for ADA-SCID.
