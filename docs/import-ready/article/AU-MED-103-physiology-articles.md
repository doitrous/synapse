## summary
The full blood count (CBC) is read parameter by parameter: plasma is what remains once
the cells are spun out of an anticoagulated sample; haematocrit is the fraction of that
sample which is red cells; MCV, MCH and MCHC are calculated red-cell indices that classify
an anaemia by cell size and haemoglobin content; ESR times how fast the red cells settle
out; and the total white-cell count separates leukocytosis from leukopenia. Each moves in
a specific, predictable direction with specific causes, and reading them together — not
any one in isolation — is what makes a CBC diagnostic.

## sections
### Purpose and indications
The CBC is requested as a routine screen, to follow a known treatment, or to work up a
specific symptom such as fatigue, recurrent infection or bruising. Reading it well means
knowing which single parameter a clinical question is really asking about, rather than
reading the whole panel as one undifferentiated "normal" or "abnormal".

### Principle
Plasma is whole blood with the red cells, white cells and platelets removed — obtained by
centrifuging an anticoagulated sample. Unlike serum, plasma still carries the clotting
factors, because the anticoagulant prevented clotting rather than letting it happen and
removing the clot into a pellet. Haematocrit reads the same centrifuged sample a different
way: it is the percentage of the column's volume that the packed red cells occupy. MCV,
MCH and MCHC are not measured directly — each is calculated from haematocrit, the red-cell
count and the hemoglobin value. ESR instead measures a dynamic process: how far red cells
settle down a standing column of anticoagulated blood in one hour, which speeds up whenever
plasma proteins that promote red-cell stacking (rouleaux) — fibrinogen and certain globulins
— are raised.

### How it is performed
A capillary or venous sample is drawn into an anticoagulant. For haematocrit, a capillary
tube of the sample is centrifuged for 30 seconds at 3000 rpm and read against a ruler scale
from 0 to 100. For ESR, the same kind of sample stands undisturbed in a Westergren tube, and
the height of clear plasma above the settled red cells is read at one hour (and again at two).
The white-cell count and the red-cell indices are read directly from the automated or manual
count together with the haemoglobin and haematocrit values already obtained.

### Normal findings
Haematocrit is normally 40–54% in men and 40–50% in women. MCV is normally 81–93 fL, MCH
27–33 pg, and MCHC about 34 g/dL.
ESR is normally 3–5 mm in the first hour for men and 5–7 mm for women, roughly doubling by the second hour.
The total white-cell count is normally 4,000–10,000 cells/mm3.

### Abnormal findings and interpretation
Haematocrit rises in polycythaemia or dehydration and falls in anaemia, overhydration, or
dilution by intravenous fluid such as a saline infusion — which lowers it by diluting the
plasma without changing the red-cell mass at all. MCV, MCH and MCHC together classify an
anaemia as microcytic hypochromic (classically iron deficiency), normocytic normochromic
(acute blood loss or bone-marrow depression), or macrocytic (B12 or folate deficiency). ESR
rises physiologically in menses and pregnancy, and pathologically with inflammation,
infection, malignancy or tissue injury, because the raised fibrinogen and globulins increase
red-cell stickiness; it falls, unusually, in congenital afibrinogenaemia, where the very
protein that drives rouleaux formation is absent. A raised total white-cell count is
leukocytosis, most often from infection or malignancy; a low count is leukopenia, seen with
marrow suppression or certain drugs.

### Limitations and pitfalls
Haematocrit alone cannot distinguish a true anaemia from a diluted sample, because both a
falling red-cell mass and a rising plasma volume move it the same direction — it needs the
red-cell count and clinical context alongside it. ESR is sensitive but deliberately
non-specific: a raised value says tissue damage or inflammation is present somewhere, not
which disease is causing it, and it is used to follow a known condition's course rather than
to name a new one from itself.

## hold_these
Haematocrit moves with plasma volume as much as with red-cell mass — a saline infusion lowers it without changing how many red cells the patient actually has.
MCV, MCH and MCHC are calculated from haematocrit, red-cell count and haemoglobin, not measured on their own.
ESR is a sensitive, non-specific marker: menses and pregnancy raise it physiologically, and congenital afibrinogenaemia is the one condition that lowers it.

## lose_the_mark
Reading a normal haematocrit as proof a patient is not anaemic, without checking whether the sample is also diluted.
Confusing MCH (an amount of haemoglobin per cell) with MCHC (a concentration per volume of packed cells) — they use different denominators.
Treating a raised ESR as diagnostic of a specific disease rather than as a general, non-specific sign to be read alongside other findings.

## summary
Three tests probe haemostasis from three different angles. Bleeding time tests the platelet
plug by timing a standardised skin puncture. Coagulation time tests the clotting cascade
itself by timing how long a blood drop takes to form a visible fibrin mesh. PT (reported as
the INR) and aPTT refine that further, examining the extrinsic and intrinsic pathways
separately and doubling as the tests used to monitor warfarin and heparin therapy
respectively. Keeping the three apart — and knowing which blood element or pathway each one
is actually sensitive to — is what makes a prolonged result interpretable.

## sections
### Purpose and indications
These tests separate a platelet-plug problem from a clotting-cascade problem, and — for PT
and aPTT specifically — let a clinician monitor anticoagulant therapy or investigate a
suspected clotting-factor deficiency.

### Principle
Bleeding time reflects vasoconstriction and platelet-plug formation sealing a small standard
wound; it is not sensitive to the coagulation cascade at all, so a factor deficiency does not
prolong it. Coagulation time instead reflects the cascade itself — the appearance of a fibrin
mesh in a drawn blood sample — and is not sensitive to platelet number. PT examines the
extrinsic pathway (tissue factor activates factor VII) and the pathways' common factors;
aPTT examines the intrinsic pathway (triggered by a factor-XII activator and a phospholipid
surface) and the same common factors from the other side.

### How it is performed
For bleeding time, a standard puncture (thumb tip or ear lobe) is blotted with paper every
30 seconds, without touching the wound, until bleeding stops. For coagulation time, a drawn
blood drop is checked every 30 seconds for a visible fibrin mesh. For PT, an anticoagulated,
centrifuged plasma sample has tissue factor and calcium added back, and the time to clot
formation is measured; for aPTT, a phospholipid and a factor-XII activator are added instead.

### Normal findings
Bleeding time is normally 2–5 minutes. Coagulation time is normally 5–10 minutes.
PT is normally 11–15 seconds (INR 0.8–1.2); aPTT is normally about 35 seconds.

### Abnormal findings and interpretation
Bleeding time is prolonged in immune thrombocytopenic purpura or any platelet-number or
platelet-function disorder — but is unaffected by a clotting-factor deficiency. Coagulation
time is prolonged by hereditary clotting-factor disease (haemophilia) or by acquired disease
impairing factor synthesis (liver failure, vitamin K deficiency) — but is unaffected by
platelet count. PT/INR is used to monitor warfarin; an INR of 3.0 means the patient's PT is
three times the standard PT, i.e. more anticoagulated than the standard reference. aPTT is
used to monitor heparin and to investigate factor VIII/XI deficiency.

### Limitations and pitfalls
None of these three tests interchange with either of the others: a normal coagulation time
does not rule out a platelet disorder, and a normal bleeding time does not rule out a
clotting-factor deficiency, because each test is deliberately insensitive to what the other
two measure.

## summary
Coagulation starts when tissue factor, exposed by damage, activates factor VII and drives the
extrinsic pathway — with calcium ions required throughout, down to the final step of fibrin
polymerisation. Left unchecked, this cascade would clot the whole circulation from one small
injury. It is not unchecked: intact endothelium continuously releases prostacyclin to keep
circulating platelets from aggregating, and heparin-facilitated antithrombin III inactivates
any clotting factors that escape the site of injury. Naming the trigger and naming the brakes
are two halves of the same idea.

## sections
### Definition
The extrinsic pathway is the coagulation route triggered by tissue factor (factor III), a
membrane protein exposed only when tissue is damaged. Natural anticoagulants are the
mechanisms that keep this and the intrinsic pathway confined to the site of real injury.

### Mechanism
Tissue damage exposes tissue factor, which activates factor VII and starts the extrinsic pathway — the faster-acting of the cascade's two initiating routes. Calcium ions are needed
at several points across both the extrinsic and intrinsic pathways, including the final
polymerisation of fibrin monomers into a stable mesh; this is exactly why a blood sample drawn
into a calcium-chelating anticoagulant (citrate, oxalate, EDTA) will not clot until calcium is
added back. Once initiated, coagulation is restrained by several brakes working together:
intact endothelium releases prostacyclin, which opposes platelet aggregation over the whole
vessel wall; heparin — a naturally occurring anticoagulant made by mast cells and basophils —
facilitates antithrombin III, which inactivates activated factors IXa, Xa, XIa and XIIa; and
the liver clears activated clotting factors that do escape into the general circulation.
Vitamin K is a separate requirement running through all of this: it is the cofactor the liver needs to make factors II, VII, IX and X in a functional, calcium-binding form, and its deficiency — or its pharmacological antagonism by warfarin — leaves the liver secreting these factors uncarboxylated and inactive, which is read in the laboratory as a prolonged coagulation time or PT/INR.

### Key determinants
The strength of the extrinsic trigger depends on how much tissue factor is exposed, which
scales with the extent of tissue damage. The effectiveness of the natural brakes depends on
an intact, healthy endothelium — the mechanism they provide is a property of the vessel wall
itself, not a circulating factor alone, which is why endothelial damage (from a foreign
surface, an atheromatous plaque, or prolonged immobility slowing flow) removes a check on
clotting even before any new material is exposed.

### Clinical significance
A laboratory test that reproduces the extrinsic trigger under controlled conditions (adding
tissue factor and calcium back to a plasma sample) is the prothrombin time — which is why PT
is described as "examining the extrinsic pathway". Drugs that potentiate the natural brakes —
heparin, which needs antithrombin III to act — are used therapeutically to prevent
intravascular thrombosis, and their effect is monitored through the same cascade they act on.

## summary
Two related pictures show erythropoiesis responding to demand. After bleeding or
haemolysis, the marrow releases red cells before they are fully mature, and the rising
reticulocyte count on a CBC is the sign that it is doing so correctly. Polycythaemia is the
opposite direction: too many red cells, either because the marrow itself is diseased and
overproducing independent of erythropoietin (primary), or because a normal marrow is
answering a genuinely raised erythropoietin drive from chronic hypoxia or, rarely, a
tumour (secondary).

## sections
### Definition
The reticulocyte count is the proportion of circulating red cells still young enough to carry
ribosomal RNA, visible with a supravital stain. Polycythaemia is a red-cell count, haemoglobin
and haematocrit above normal, split into primary (marrow disorder) and secondary
(erythropoietin-driven) causes.

### Mechanism
When red cells are lost faster than usual, erythropoietin drives the marrow to release cells
before they are fully mature; these still carry ribosomal remnants, are counted as
reticulocytes.
a rising reticulocyte count is the laboratory sign the marrow is responding to a real demand — a low reticulocyte count in the same clinical setting is the worrying finding, because it means the marrow cannot respond. Primary polycythaemia
(polycythaemia vera) is a bone-marrow disorder that overproduces red cells independent of
erythropoietin, so the erythropoietin level is often low precisely because the marrow no
longer needs the signal. Secondary polycythaemia is the opposite logic: a normal marrow
responding appropriately to a raised erythropoietin drive, most often chronic hypoxia from
altitude, chronic lung disease, or chronic cardiac disease, and less commonly a tumour that
secretes erythropoietin itself.

### Key determinants
Reticulocyte response depends on how much red-cell loss has occurred and whether the marrow
itself is intact. Polycythaemia's classification depends entirely on the erythropoietin
level and its driver: raised and appropriately driven (secondary) versus low and irrelevant
to a disordered marrow's output (primary).

### Clinical significance
A raised reticulocyte count after acute bleeding or haemolysis confirms the marrow is
compensating, and its absence in the same setting is a red flag for marrow failure.
Polycythaemia raises blood viscosity, which is itself a separate clinical concern (see the
CBC-reading article) — but the two classes of polycythaemia are managed completely
differently, because treating a hypoxic driver (secondary) does nothing for a primary marrow
disorder, and vice versa.

## summary
Each haem group's iron atom forms six coordination bonds. Four are fixed to its own
porphyrin ring; the fifth is permanently occupied by the proximal histidine of the globin
chain; the sixth is the oxygen-binding site itself, occupied by oxygen in oxyhaemoglobin and
left empty in deoxyhaemoglobin. Keeping the fifth and sixth bonds distinct is the whole point
— one never changes with oxygenation, and the other is what oxygenation actually means.

## sections
### Definition
The sixth coordination bond of haem iron is the reversible oxygen-binding site; the fifth is the permanent link to the globin protein itself.

### Mechanism
Iron sits at the centre of each haem's porphyrin ring, coordinated by the ring's four
pyrrole nitrogens. A fifth bond links it to the imidazole nitrogen of the proximal histidine
of the globin chain — present whether or not oxygen is bound. The sixth bond is the variable
one: in oxyhaemoglobin it holds oxygen; in deoxyhaemoglobin it is unoccupied. Oxygen binding
at the sixth site pulls the iron into the porphyrin plane, a shift that is transmitted through
the proximal histidine to the rest of the globin chain — the structural basis of
haemoglobin's cooperative binding, though that cooperativity is a separate concept from the
bond geometry itself.

### Key determinants
Which state the sixth bond is in depends only on local oxygen tension — high in the lungs,
where it binds; low in the tissues, where it releases. The fifth bond does not depend on
anything: it is a fixed structural feature of every hemoglobin molecule, oxygenated or not.

### Clinical significance
Distinguishing the fifth from the sixth bond is what lets a student read an exam question
about either state of haemoglobin without confusing what the two bonds actually mean: a
question about deoxyhaemoglobin's iron most often really asks about the empty sixth site,
not about the (unchanged) fifth.

## published_summary

## published_sections

## hold_these
The fifth coordination bond of haem iron is always the proximal histidine, in both oxy- and deoxyhaemoglobin.
The sixth coordination bond is the oxygen-binding site: occupied by oxygen in oxyhaemoglobin, empty in deoxyhaemoglobin.

## lose_the_mark
Treating the fifth and sixth coordination bonds as interchangeable, or as both changing with oxygenation — only the sixth does.

## summary
ABO and Rh groups are named for antigens on the red-cell surface, and a person's plasma
carries antibodies against whatever antigen they lack — which is both how blood grouping is
tested (mixing a sample against known antisera and watching for agglutination) and why group
O is the universal red-cell donor (its cells carry neither A nor B antigen for a recipient's
antibodies to attack). The same antigen-antibody logic explains Rh disease: an Rh-negative
mother sensitised by an Rh-positive fetus can, in a later pregnancy, attack a second
Rh-positive fetus's red cells — a single dose of anti-D given after every delivery prevents
that sensitisation from ever happening.

## sections
### Definition
ABO and Rh blood groups are defined by antigens carried on the red-cell surface. Compatibility
testing exploits the fact that plasma carries antibodies only against the antigens a person's
own red cells lack.

### Mechanism
Mixing a blood sample against known anti-A and anti-B antisera and watching for agglutination
identifies which ABO antigen (if any) the sample's red cells carry; a sample showing no
agglutination against either antiserum is compatible for transfusion with that antiserum's
source.
Group O red cells carry neither the A nor the B antigen, so they trigger no ABO antibody reaction in a recipient of any ABO group — this is why group O, Rh-negative blood is the universal red-cell donor. Rh disease follows the same logic across a pregnancy rather
than a transfusion: fetal red cells crossing into an Rh-negative mother's circulation, usually
at delivery, can sensitise her against the Rh(D) antigen; in a later pregnancy with another
Rh-positive fetus, her anti-D antibodies cross the placenta and destroy fetal red cells,
causing haemolytic disease of the newborn. A single dose of anti-D immunoglobulin given to
the mother after every delivery clears any fetal cells from her circulation before she can be
sensitised, preventing the disease in a subsequent pregnancy.

### Key determinants
Compatibility depends only on which antigens are present on the donor's cells and which
antibodies are present in the recipient's plasma — never on blood volume or any other factor.
Rh sensitisation depends on fetal cells actually reaching the maternal circulation in
sufficient number, which is why it becomes a risk mainly after a birth, a miscarriage, or an
invasive obstetric procedure, and why anti-D given promptly after each such event prevents it.

### Clinical significance
An incompatible transfusion causes a haemolytic transfusion reaction as the recipient's own
antibodies destroy the donor's red cells. Anti-D prophylaxis is a preventive intervention, not
a treatment — it must be given after every delivery, not only the first, because each
Rh-positive pregnancy is a fresh opportunity for sensitisation if it is missed.

## summary
Every immunoglobulin is built from two heavy and two light chains, but it is the heavy
chain's type — mu, delta, gamma, alpha or epsilon — that defines its class, giving five
classes in total: IgM, IgD, IgG, IgA and IgE. Two of those classes have a distribution and a
role worth knowing on their own: IgA predominates in external secretions, defending mucosal
surfaces at the point of entry, and IgE, bound to mast cells and basophils, mediates the
immediate hypersensitivity reactions of allergy.

## sections
### Definition
Immunoglobulin class is defined by the type of heavy chain in the antibody's constant region — not by its light chain, which is shared (kappa or lambda) across every class.

### Mechanism
The light chain (kappa or lambda) is common to all five classes, so it never distinguishes
them; the heavy-chain constant region does — mu for IgM, delta for IgD, gamma for IgG, alpha
for IgA, and epsilon for IgE. IgA is secreted as a dimer that survives the mucosal environment,
which is why it — not IgG, the dominant class in serum — predominates in saliva, tears,
colostrum and the respiratory and gastrointestinal mucus, neutralising pathogens before they
cross the barrier. IgE instead circulates at the lowest concentration of any class but binds
high-affinity receptors on mast cells and basophils; when an allergen cross-links IgE already
bound to these cells, it triggers degranulation and the release of histamine and other
mediators within minutes — the mechanism of immediate (type I) hypersensitivity.

### Key determinants
Which class an antibody belongs to is fixed by which heavy-chain gene segment is expressed —
a property of the B cell producing it, not of the antigen it binds. IgA's secretory dominance
and IgE's mast-cell binding are each a property of the class itself, not of any particular
antibody's specificity.

### Clinical significance
Salivary and mucosal antibody testing looks specifically for IgA, not total immunoglobulin.
An immediate allergic reaction — urticaria, allergic rhinitis, anaphylaxis — points to IgE and
mast-cell/basophil activation, distinguishing it mechanistically from the delayed or
antibody-independent hypersensitivity reactions that other immune mechanisms produce.
