## summary
The dermis is the connective tissue under the epidermis, and it is built in two layers that differ on six axes at once. The department book prints them only as a paired table, and the 2025 end-of-year paper asked for exactly that comparison. The single fact that makes the table make sense rather than something to memorise is that the epidermis has no blood vessels of its own.

## sections
### Definition
The dermis is the deep connective tissue layer lying under the epidermis. It is derived from mesoderm and it is thicker than the epidermis. Its upper surface is not flat: it throws up projections called dermal papillae, which fit into matching concavities in the epidermis called epidermal ridges.

The dermis is formed of two layers, the papillary layer and the reticular layer.

The papillary layer is the thinner superficial layer and it forms the dermal papillae.
The reticular layer is the thicker deep layer.

Two things that sit beside the dermis are not part of it. The hypodermis — the deep layer of adipose connective tissue that corresponds to the superficial fascia — is not part of the skin at all, and the department book is explicit about that. The epidermis above is epithelium, not connective tissue.

### Mechanism
One fact drives most of the table: the epidermis is avascular and receives its nutrition by diffusion. Everything that has to be delivered to the epidermis has to arrive through the dermis immediately underneath it, and diffusion over distance is slow. So the layer directly under the epidermis is the vascular one, and it is loose rather than dense, because loose connective tissue leaves room for the vessels.

The interdigitation between dermal papillae and epidermal ridges does two jobs at once. It provides the attachment of epidermis to dermis, and it increases the surface area available for nutrition of the epidermis. Both jobs need the vessels to be in the papillae, and both are helped by the zigzag rather than a straight boundary.

The reticular layer does not need to feed anything. It carries load. Its type I collagen is arranged in bundles rather than as fine fibres, and bundled collagen resists tension. That is why the deep layer is dense connective tissue and the superficial layer is loose, and why the reticular layer is the thicker of the two.

The receptors follow the same logic. Meissner's corpuscles respond to light touch, and light touch has to be sampled close to the surface, so they lie in the papillary layer. Pacinian corpuscles respond to deep pressure and vibration, which reach further, so they lie deep with Ruffini's end organs and Krause's end bulbs.

### Key determinants
The book compares the two layers on six axes, and the paper marks on them.

**Thickness and position.** The papillary layer is the thinner, superficial layer, and it is the one that forms the dermal papillae. The reticular layer is the thicker, deep layer.

**Type of connective tissue.** The papillary layer is loose connective tissue. The reticular layer is dense connective tissue.

**Cellularity.** The papillary layer is more cellular; the reticular layer is less cellular. Both hold the same cast — fibrocytes, lymphocytes, macrophages, mast cells and adipocytes — so the difference is one of number, not of cell type.

**Fibres.** The papillary layer has fine connective tissue fibres: type III collagen with elastic fibres. The reticular layer has type I collagen arranged in bundles, again with elastic fibres.

**Vascularity.** The papillary layer is more vascular, and the book gives the reason in the same line: to nourish the epidermis. The reticular layer is less vascular.

**Receptors.** The papillary layer carries Meissner's corpuscles. The reticular layer carries Pacinian corpuscles, Ruffini's end organ and Krause's end bulb.

### Clinical significance
The zigzag interdigitation between dermal papillae and epidermal ridges is what forms the finger prints, and the book records these as being of medico-legal importance.

Rapid stretch of the skin ruptures the fibre bundles in the dermis. Scar tissue forms in the area and appears as white lines — the pregnancy lines are the example the book gives.

Ageing acts on both layers. Atrophy of the connective tissue elastic fibres reduces the elasticity of the skin and produces wrinkling. The blood vessels of the dermis become more fragile, which leads to bruising. Sebaceous glands produce less oil, and the subcutaneous fat layer thins so it insulates less.

Three things fix the epidermis to the dermis, and a student asked why skin does not simply shear off should be able to name them: the basement membrane of the epidermis, hemidesmosomes between the basal epidermal cells and that basement membrane, and the dermal papillae interdigitating with the epidermal ridges.

### Common misconceptions
Counting the hypodermis as the third layer of the skin. The book names it, describes it, and then says it is not a part of the skin — it is subcutaneous tissue, corresponding to the superficial fascia.

Expecting a visible line between the two dermal layers. The book separates them on what the tissue is made of and how much blood it carries, not on a boundary you can point at, and a stained section shows a gradient.

## summary
Bone holds four cell types, and two of them do opposite jobs. The osteoblast lays matrix down; the osteoclast takes it away. They differ in where they come from, where they sit, what they look like down a light microscope and what their ultrastructure is — and every one of those differences follows from the job. The 2025 end-of-year paper asked for all four axes and gave the question 6 marks, the largest in the histology section.

## sections
### Definition
Bone contains four cells: the osteogenic (osteoprogenitor) cell, the osteoblast, the osteocyte and the osteoclast. Three of them are one lineage. The osteogenic cell divides and gives rise to the osteoblast; the osteoblast, once surrounded by the calcified matrix it has made, becomes an imprisoned cell called an osteocyte. The osteoclast is not part of that sequence at all.

The osteoblast is the bone-forming cell.
The osteoclast is the bone-destroying cell.

### Mechanism
The osteoblast is a secretory cell, and its whole appearance is the appearance of a cell making protein for export. It secretes the organic part of the matrix; the newly formed organic matrix is called osteoid. It then calcifies that matrix by means of alkaline phosphatase, which helps the deposition of calcium. A cell doing this needs ribosomes on membranes to make the protein, mitochondria to power it and a Golgi apparatus to package it — which is exactly the ultrastructure it has, and exactly why its cytoplasm is darkly basophilic at the light-microscopic level, since rough endoplasmic reticulum takes up basic dye.

The osteoclast does the reverse, and needs different tools. Bone matrix is 35% organic and 65% inorganic, so it has to be attacked twice. The osteoclast contains carbonic anhydrase, which drives the reaction of water and carbon dioxide to bicarbonate and hydrogen ion; releasing that hydrogen ion through the ruffled border acidifies the medium under the cell and dissolves the calcium salts. It then secretes lysosomal proteolytic (osteolytic) enzymes, which lyse the organic part of the matrix. Acid first for the mineral, enzymes second for the collagen.

Both halves of that need surface area, so the membrane facing the bone is thrown into folds — the brush border seen by light microscopy, which electron microscopy resolves as a ruffled surface bearing microvilli. Both halves need lysosomes, which is why the cytoplasm is rich in them. And the cell has to be big enough to excavate, which is what the fusion of many progenitors buys: the osteoclast is multinucleated because it was assembled from several cells, not because one nucleus divided.

### Key determinants
The paper names four axes. Take them in the order it prints them.

**Origin.** The osteoblast arises from osteogenic cells. The osteoclast is formed by fusion of mononuclear haemopoietic progenitor cells. That single line explains the multinucleation and separates the two lineages for good.

**Site.** Osteoblasts are present immediately under the periosteum, in the form of a continuous single layer covering the bone surface, and are also present under the endosteum. Osteoclasts are present on the bone surface near the bone marrow, within a cavity called Howship's lacuna — a hollow the cell has itself dissolved out.

**Light microscopy.** The osteoblast is an oval cell with minute few processes and an eccentric nucleus, and the size of the cell depends on its activity. Its cytoplasm is darkly basophilic with a negative Golgi image near the nucleus, and it is rich in alkaline phosphatase. The osteoclast is a large irregular cell with a brush border facing the near bony surface, multinucleated with 6 to 12 nuclei, and with foamy acidophilic cytoplasm.

**Electron microscopy.** The osteoblast shows the characters of protein-forming cells: it is rich in rough endoplasmic reticulum, mitochondria and a well-developed Golgi apparatus. The osteoclast has a ruffled surface which shows microvilli, and its cytoplasm is rich in lysosomes, mitochondria and Golgi apparatus and shows multiple vesicles.

Two details are worth carrying. The negative Golgi image is not an artefact: the Golgi apparatus excludes the basic dye that stains the rest of the cytoplasm, so it appears as a pale area beside the nucleus and is positive evidence of active secretion. And the "foamy" acidophilic cytoplasm of the osteoclast is the light-microscopic appearance of the multiple vesicles that electron microscopy resolves.

### Clinical significance
Osteoporosis is a common bone disease, characterised by progressive loss of normal bone density that increases the risk of bone fracture. It is caused by increased bone resorption over bone formation — that is, by the osteoclast outrunning the osteoblast rather than by either cell being abnormal in itself.

The same balance runs normally: the book lists bone remodelling in ossification as one of the osteoclast's two functions, alongside bone resorption.

The osteoclast is also a phagocytic cell. It contains exocytotic vesicles that carry lysosomal enzymes out to Howship's lacuna, and endocytotic vesicles that carry degraded bone products into the cell; the contents of the endocytotic vesicles are then discharged by exocytosis from the basolateral aspect of the cell.

### Common misconceptions
Deriving the osteoclast from the osteogenic cell. It comes from the haemopoietic line, by fusion, and this is the single most common error on this comparison.

Assuming every osteoblast becomes an osteocyte. The book is specific: 10% to 20% of osteoblasts differentiate into osteocytes in lacunae, some flatten and cover the matrix as bone lining cells, and the majority undergo apoptosis.

Confusing the osteoclast with the megakaryocyte because both are large and multinucleated in appearance. The department book prints a comparison between the two precisely because students mix them up; the osteoclast sits in Howship's lacuna and the megakaryocyte does not.

## summary
A cardiac muscle fibre is not one long cell. It is many separate myocytes joined end to end, and the junction between them is the intercalated disc. Light microscopy shows the myocyte's own size, shape and single central nucleus, and the disc as a line; electron microscopy shows the myocyte's diad tubular system and its granule inclusions, and shows the disc is folded, with two components in two orientations doing two different jobs — mechanical anchorage across the fibre, electrical coupling along it. Purkinje fibres take that logic to its extreme: built purely for gap-junction conduction speed, they keep the coupling and drop the disc's mechanical anchorage entirely. The 2025 end-of-year paper asked for the disc's LM/EM picture in one 3-mark question; the department's own written-assessment revision set separately asks for the myocyte's full structure and for the Purkinje fibre's five distinguishing features.

## sections
### Definition
Cardiac muscle forms the middle layer of the heart, the myocardium, which is the main bulk of the heart wall and is thicker in the ventricles than in the atria. It is made of branched, interconnected muscle fibres, each surrounded by a delicate sheath of connective tissue endomysium rich in capillaries.

Each cardiac muscle fibre is made up of many separate cells, called cardiac myocytes, linked to each other. Intercalated discs represent the sites of junctions between the sarcolemma of adjacent cardiac muscle cells in the fibre.

By LM, intercalated discs are clear lines that appear transversely at intervals along the length of cardiac muscle fibre.

### The cardiac myocyte: full LM and EM picture
By light microscopy a cardiac myocyte is a cylindrical cell about 80 micrometres in length and 15 micrometres in diameter that branches and joins to adjacent cells. Its sarcolemma is a thin cell membrane — thinner than a skeletal fibre's, which is thickened by fusion with basal lamina and endomysium. Its nucleus is single (occasionally two), oval and central, unlike the multiple peripheral nuclei of a skeletal fibre. Its sarcoplasm is acidophilic and shows non-clear transverse striations — less distinct than skeletal muscle's, though still present, because a cardiac myocyte still has sarcomeres — together with the intercalated discs described above.

By electron microscopy the sarcoplasm holds fewer myofibrils than skeletal muscle, and numerous large mitochondria with closely packed cristae — a fibre that never rests needs more fuel-burning capacity per unit volume than one that contracts occasionally. Its sarcoplasmic reticulum is less developed than skeletal muscle's, and its arrangement with the T-tubule forms a diad, not a triad: one T-tubule with a single terminal cisterna, at the level of the Z line rather than the A-I junction where skeletal muscle's triads sit.

Three kinds of inclusion sit in the sarcoplasm. Glycogen granules and lipofuscin granules are both storage/waste products — lipofuscin accumulates with age and, in enough quantity, is seen grossly as brown atrophy of the heart. The third, found only in atrial muscle cells, is different in kind rather than degree: atrial granules contain atrial natriuretic hormones that affect urinary sodium and water excretion, which makes the atrial myocyte an endocrine cell as well as a contractile one — a cardiac muscle fact with no equivalent anywhere in skeletal or smooth muscle.

### Mechanism
The disc has to solve two problems at once, and it solves them in two different places.

The first problem is that the fibre pulls on itself. Cells joined end to end and contracting many times a minute, for a lifetime, will separate unless they are anchored. So the transverse component of the disc — the part that crosses transversely the cardiac muscle fibre, at right angles to the pull — carries the mechanical junctions. Desmosomes and adherent junctions (fascia adherens) are located in this region of the disc; they bind cardiac muscle cells firmly together to prevent their separation during repetitive contractions. The fascia adherens is where the thin filaments of the terminal sarcomere anchor, so the pull of contraction is handed on to the next cell rather than tearing the membrane.

The second problem is that the myocardium has to contract as one. That needs electrical continuity, and electrical continuity means gap junctions. But a gap junction is a delicate structure and the transverse face is where the force is. So the gap junctions are placed where the force is not: the lateral component of the disc lies parallel to muscle fibres, and gap junctions are located in this segment of the disc, allowing the contraction signals to pass from cell to other. Their position in the lateral parts of the disc protects them from the contraction forces.

That is the design in one sentence: strength across the pull, communication along it.

### Key determinants
**The light-microscopic picture.** Intercalated discs are clear lines that appear transversely, at intervals, along the length of the cardiac muscle fibre. They are one of the three features that identify cardiac muscle on a stained section, alongside branching fibres and a single central nucleus in each cell. The sarcoplasm by light microscopy is acidophilic and exhibits non-clear transverse striations — less distinct than in skeletal muscle — as well as the discs.

**The electron-microscopic picture.** The disc is formed of transverse and lateral regions. The transverse component crosses the fibre and carries desmosomes and adherent junctions, the fascia adherens. The lateral component lies parallel to the fibre and carries gap junctions.

**What the disc is not.** It is not a straight partition. The step between the transverse and lateral parts is why a disc drawn as a single flat line is wrong, and why the light-microscopic "line" is a simplification of something folded.

**The comparison the book draws.** Skeletal muscle has no cell junctions at all, because each fibre is a single cell. Smooth muscle has gap junctions but no discs. Only cardiac muscle has the intercalated disc, with fascia adherens, desmosome and gap junction together.

### Purkinje fibres
Purkinje muscle fibres are highly specialised cardiac muscle fibres that constitute the atrioventricular (A-V) bundle and its branches. The moderator band, located in the right ventricle, contains Purkinje fibres, which are grouped into bundles surrounded by their own connective-tissue sheath. Their job is speed: they rapidly transmit the contractile impulse to various parts of the ventricular cardiac muscle via gap junctions, faster than ordinary myocardium — which is why the conduction system uses a dedicated fibre type rather than relying on cell-to-cell spread through ordinary myocytes alone.

That speed is bought with a distinct histological picture, five features against an ordinary cardiac myocyte. Purkinje fibres are larger in diameter than ordinary cardiac muscle fibres. Their nucleus is usually eccentric in position, rather than central. Their sarcoplasm is pale and vacuolated, from excess glycogen. They show no striations, because they have few myofibrils, situated peripherally, parallel to the sarcolemma, rather than filling the cell. And — the feature that connects this section back to the rest of the article — they carry no intercalated discs at all.

That last feature is the design logic in one line. An ordinary myocyte needs the intercalated disc's transverse component to keep it anchored to its neighbours through repetitive contraction; a Purkinje fibre's whole job is signal speed, not sustained contractile continuity, so it keeps the gap junction that gives it that speed and sheds the mechanical anchorage it does not need.

### Clinical significance
Cardiac muscle does not contain progenitor cells, so it cannot regenerate. When cardiac myocytes are injured, as in myocardial infarction, they heal by fibrous tissue. Fibrous tissue neither contracts nor conducts, so an infarcted area is lost twice over — and the gap junctions that would have carried the signal through it are gone with the cells.

The department book records myocardial infarction as complete obstruction of a coronary artery leading to cardiac muscle cell death, which releases proteins such as troponin-T and creatine kinase into the bloodstream; measuring these cardiac enzymes early in a patient presenting with chest pain is what allows early diagnosis and rapid treatment.

Purkinje fibres (see the dedicated section above) are the exception that proves the intercalated disc's design logic: a fibre built purely for gap-junction conduction speed has no need of, and carries no, intercalated discs at all.

### Common misconceptions
Drawing the intercalated disc as one flat plate across the fibre. It has two components in two orientations, and putting the gap junctions in the transverse part removes the reason they survive contraction.

Treating a cardiac muscle fibre as a single multinucleated cell like a skeletal myofibre. It is several cells; that is exactly why it needs discs and why the nucleus is single and central rather than multiple and peripheral.

Expecting the discs to look like Z lines. Z lines are inside a sarcomere and are found in every striated muscle; intercalated discs are cell boundaries and are found only in cardiac muscle.

## summary
Smooth muscle is the easiest of the three muscle types to describe and the hardest to remember, because almost every feature is defined by what it does *not* have. The way to hold it is as a set of substitutions: caveolae instead of T-tubules, dense bodies instead of Z lines, irregularly arranged filaments instead of sarcomeres, calmodulin instead of troponin. The 2025 end-of-year paper asked for the electron-microscopic picture for 3 marks.

## sections
### Definition
Smooth muscle fibres are non-branched, spindle-shaped fibres; each fibre is a single cell with a wider central area and tapering ends. Cells are arranged so that their tapered parts lie adjacent to the broad parts of the neighbouring cells, so they form sheets — which is why, in transverse section, smooth muscle cells appear with variable diameter. They are the smallest of the three muscle cells, 8 µm in diameter, with a length that varies from 20 µm in small blood vessels to 500 µm in the pregnant uterus.

They lie in the walls of hollow viscera — digestive, urinary, genital and respiratory tracts — in the walls of blood vessels, and in the skin and the eye. Smooth muscle fibres are connected together by gap junctions; each fibre is surrounded by thin reticular fibres, the endomysium, and bundles are surrounded by a thin collagenous perimysium carrying vessels and nerves.

### Mechanism
Read the ultrastructure as four substitutions, each one the consequence of a cell that contracts slowly and does not need to contract in step with a nerve impulse arriving at one point.

**Caveolae in place of T-tubules.** The sarcolemma is thin and surrounded by a basal lamina. It shows no T-tubules; instead there are invaginations along the cell surface, called caveolae. There is no tubular system at all. The caveolae can control calcium release and muscle contraction. A skeletal fibre is large and needs T-tubules to carry depolarisation into its depth quickly; a smooth muscle cell is 8 µm across and slow, so surface invaginations are enough.

**Dense bodies in place of Z lines.** Actin filaments insert into sarcoplasmic and sarcolemma-associated dense bodies, which correspond to the Z line in striated muscle, and extend into the sarcoplasm to interact with myosin filaments. Because the dense bodies are scattered through the cytoplasm and anchored to the membrane rather than lined up in register, the filaments they hold run in many directions and the cell shortens as a whole rather than along one axis.

**Irregular filaments in place of sarcomeres.** Myofibrils, of thick myosin and thin actin filaments, are irregularly arranged. Therefore striations do not appear in smooth muscle fibres. The filaments are there; the register is not.

**Calmodulin in place of troponin.** There is no troponin; instead there is calmodulin protein.

One more feature ties the sheet together. Abundant intermediate desmin filaments also insert into the dense bodies, and the attachments of actin and intermediate filaments to the dense bodies transmit contractile force to adjacent smooth muscle cells. The cells pull on each other through the cytoskeleton, which is what lets a sheet of them narrow a lumen.

### Key determinants
This is the list the paper marks.

**Sarcolemma.** Thin, surrounded by basal lamina. No T-tubules. Caveolae along the cell surface instead. No tubular system. The caveolae control calcium release and contraction.

**Nucleus.** Single, oval and central. Its shape is like a corkscrew during contraction — a useful sign, because it means the section was taken from contracted muscle.

**Sarcoplasm.** Acidophilic. Contains numerous mitochondria, sarcoplasmic reticulum, free ribosomes, a small Golgi apparatus and glycogen granules, present mainly at the perinuclear region.

**Myofilaments.** Thick myosin and thin actin, irregularly arranged, so no striations.

**Dense bodies.** Sarcoplasmic and sarcolemma-associated; correspond to the Z line of striated muscle. Actin inserts into them and extends into the sarcoplasm to interact with myosin.

**Intermediate filaments.** Abundant desmin filaments, also inserting into the dense bodies, transmitting contractile force to adjacent cells.

**Regulatory protein.** No troponin; calmodulin instead.

### Clinical significance
Smooth muscle produces slow, sustained, involuntary contraction, modulated by autonomic innervation and hormones, and it can secrete connective tissue matrix and fibres as in the wall of a blood vessel — a muscle cell that is also a fibroblast when it needs to be.

Unlike cardiac muscle, it repairs. Smooth muscle can grow by hypertrophy and increase in number by hyperplasia, as in hypertensive arteries and the pregnant uterus, and regeneration after damage occurs by mitotic division; pericytes can repair the smooth muscle of blood vessels. The 25-fold length change between a small vessel and the pregnant uterus in the book's own figures is the same capacity seen from outside.

Oxytocin is a potent stimulator of smooth muscle contraction and plays an essential role in uterine contraction during parturition, which is why it is used to induce or enhance labour.

### Common misconceptions
Concluding that smooth muscle has no contractile filaments because it has no striations. It has both actin and myosin; they are arranged irregularly rather than in register, and that is the whole reason no bands appear.

Expecting a tubular system because the other two muscle types have one. Smooth muscle has none — not a triad, not a diad, nothing. Caveolae are surface invaginations, not a tubular system, and the book says so in the same sentence.

Reading a corkscrew nucleus as an abnormality. It is the normal appearance of the nucleus in a contracted cell.

Calling the dense bodies Z lines. They correspond to the Z line functionally, as the anchorage for actin, but they are scattered and membrane-associated rather than transverse plates, which is precisely why the cell is not striated.

## summary
Cartilage is a rubbery, avascular connective tissue that bears mechanical stress without any blood supply of its own. Everything about it — its two cells, its perichondrium, its two growth mechanisms — follows from that one constraint: nourishment can only ever arrive by diffusion, so the tissue is built around getting nutrients in from the surface rather than through vessels.

## sections
### Definition
Cartilage is a specialised type of connective tissue in which the matrix is rubbery yet flexible, so it can bear mechanical stress. Like connective tissue proper, its cells are widely separated by a considerable amount of intercellular matrix, and it arises from undifferentiated mesenchymal cells. It is avascular, with no lymph vessels or nerves, nourished only by diffusion of oxygen and nutrients from the surrounding connective tissue or, at a joint, from synovial fluid.

Cartilage is built of cells and matrix. The cells are chondroblasts and chondrocytes. The matrix is formed of collagen and elastic fibres, embedded in varying proportions in an abundant, firm, rubbery ground substance. The three types of cartilage — hyaline, yellow elastic and white fibrocartilage — are distinguished by how much ground substance they carry and which fibres are embedded in it; hyaline cartilage has the typical structure and the other two are variants of it.

Cartilage supports soft tissue with some flexibility, gives tissue attachment and bears weight, absorbs shock, provides a smooth sliding surface for joints, keeps the airway patent, and drives the growth of bones before and after birth.

### Mechanism
The chondroblast and the chondrocyte are the same cell at two stages, and the difference between them is exactly the difference between growing cartilage from the outside and growing it from the inside.

The chondroblast arises from undifferentiated mesenchymal cells, which withdraw their processes, proliferate and transform. It always sits on the surface of the cartilage, at the inner aspect of the perichondrium — never buried. By light microscopy it is flat to oval or spindle-shaped with deeply basophilic cytoplasm and a pale, prominent-nucleolus nucleus, and it can divide. By electron microscopy it shows the full ultrastructure of a protein-forming cell: euchromatic nucleus, abundant ribosomes and rough endoplasmic reticulum, a large Golgi apparatus, many mitochondria. It secretes type II collagen and matrix, and appositional growth — growth of cartilage from outside, by cells added at the surface — is its job.

Once a chondroblast has secreted enough matrix to be completely surrounded by it, it is imprisoned in a lacuna and is now a chondrocyte. Young chondrocytes are superficial, ovoid, parallel to the surface, pale basophilic with a rounded dark nucleus, usually alone in a lacuna ringed by a darkly stained capsule. As they mature and sink deeper into the matrix they become rounded or triangular, their cytoplasm rich in fat and glycogen that dissolves during preparation. Deep chondrocytes still divide, once or twice, producing clusters of 2 to 8 cells — isogenous groups, or cell nests — sharing one lacuna. By electron microscopy they still show protein-forming features, but less prominently than the chondroblast, and old chondrocytes add large lipid droplets and glycogen granules. Chondrocytes maintain cartilage by continuously secreting new matrix around themselves: interstitial growth, growth from inside.

Neither cell works alone. The perichondrium, a dense fibrous capsule around the cartilage — absent only at a joint's articular surface — is what makes the chondroblast's job possible at all. Its outer fibrous layer, rich in vessels and nerves, is the actual nutrition route by diffusion for the avascular tissue beneath; its inner chondrogenic layer is where chondroblasts live and where new cartilage cells are made during growth. An articular surface, lacking a perichondrium, has no equivalent supply route and depends entirely on synovial fluid — one reason articular cartilage injury heals so poorly.

### Key determinants
**Chondroblast versus chondrocyte, cell by cell.** Origin: chondroblast from undifferentiated mesenchymal cells; chondrocyte from a mature chondroblast. Site: chondroblast always on the surface at the inner perichondrium; young chondrocytes superficial, old chondrocytes deep. Light microscopy: chondroblast flat/oval/spindle with deep basophilia; chondrocyte ovoid (young) or rounded/triangular (old), paler basophilic, in a capsuled lacuna, alone or in an isogenous group of 2 to 8. Electron microscopy: both show protein-forming features, chondroblast's more prominent; old chondrocytes add lipid droplets and glycogen. Function: chondroblast drives appositional growth from outside; chondrocyte drives interstitial growth from inside and maintains the matrix.

**Perichondrium, layer by layer.** Outer fibrous layer: white fibrous connective tissue, fibroblasts secreting type I collagen, rich in vessels and nerves — the nutrition route. Inner chondrogenic layer: rich in chondroblasts — the growth route. Present around hyaline and elastic cartilage; absent at an articular surface and absent around white fibrocartilage altogether.

**Why the matrix looks empty of fibres under the light microscope.** Cartilage collagen is type II, and by light microscopy the matrix appears homogeneous and transparent — not because the fibres are absent, but because they are very thin and share the same refractive index as the surrounding matrix. They can be shown after enzymatic digestion of the ground substance. The ground substance itself, produced by chondroblasts and chondrocytes, is rubbery, homogeneous and markedly basophilic — sulfated glycosaminoglycans, chiefly chondroitin sulphate — and stains metachromatically with some basic stains; it is a mix of proteoglycans, glycoproteins and water.

### Clinical significance
Because cartilage has no blood supply, its own repair is intrinsically slow and largely limited to what the perichondrium's chondroblasts can lay down from the surface — which is exactly why articular cartilage, with no perichondrium at all, is notoriously poor at healing after injury and a common site of degenerative joint disease. The isogenous groups seen on a slide of ageing cartilage are a direct marker of chondrocyte division having already happened at that spot — a kind of growth-history stamp a pathologist can read off the tissue.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming cartilage repairs at the speed of bone because both are connective tissues. Bone is highly vascular; cartilage is not, and depends entirely on diffusion — the two tissues sit at opposite ends of the vascularity spectrum despite the family resemblance.

Reading an "empty" lacuna on a slide as evidence the cell died before sectioning. In life the chondrocyte completely fills its lacuna; the gap is a shrinkage artefact of tissue preparation, not a sign of a missing or dead cell.

Assuming every hyaline cartilage surface has a perichondrium. The one explicit exception the book gives is the articular surface of a joint.

## summary
Hyaline, yellow elastic and white fibrocartilage are the same basic tissue tuned three different ways for three different jobs: a flexible airway skeleton, a springy ear that recovers its shape, and a tough bone-to-bone attachment that resists tearing. The intervertebral disc, built from two of these three types stacked concentrically, is where that engineering logic becomes a single clinically important structure.

## sections
### Definition
Hyaline cartilage is the most common type, translucent with a glassy appearance (hyalo = glass), and it has the typical structure of cartilage — the other two types are variants of it. It sits in the fetal skeleton, the articular surface of bones, costal cartilage, the epiphyseal plate, and the upper respiratory passages: nose, larynx, trachea and bronchi.

Yellow elastic cartilage is yellow in the fresh state, opaque and flexible, and is covered by perichondrium. White fibrocartilage is a tough type important in bone-to-bone attachment, with characters intermediate between hyaline cartilage and dense regular white fibrous connective tissue, and it is not surrounded by perichondrium.

The intervertebral disc lies between the bodies of two adjacent vertebrae. It has an outer fibrous ring, the annulus fibrosus, of white fibrocartilage with type I collagen, and an inner soft jelly-like mass, the nucleus pulposus, containing type II collagen.

### Mechanism
Each cartilage type is built for the mechanical demand of its site, and the differences all trace back to one variable: how much elastic fibre versus collagen bundle sits in the matrix, and how much matrix there is at all.

Yellow elastic cartilage keeps hyaline cartilage's basic structure but embeds a large number of branching elastic fibres in the matrix, alongside a few type II collagen fibres; its chondrocytes form small, mostly 2-cell isogenous groups. Elastic fibres recoil after stretch, which is exactly the property the ear pinna, the Eustachian tube, the epiglottis, some laryngeal cartilages and the external auditory canal need: each has to deform under an external force and spring back rather than stay bent.

White fibrocartilage abandons the perichondrium and the ground-substance-rich matrix altogether. It is dense type I collagen in parallel thick bundles, with cartilage cells in lacunae lined up in rows between the bundles, in only very scanty matrix. Type I collagen bundles resist tensile stretch, which is what a structure joining two bones under load needs — the shoulder and hip joints, the mandibular joint, the symphysis pubis, the sternoclavicular joint, the semilunar cartilages of the knee, and the intervertebral disc all load their fibrocartilage in tension or compression rather than needing it to spring back to shape.

The intervertebral disc puts these two logics to work together, nested concentrically. The outer annulus fibrosus is white fibrocartilage: tough, tensile, resisting the ring's own outward bulge under compressive load. The inner nucleus pulposus is a soft, jelly-like, type-II-collagen mass: it behaves like the compressible core of a shock absorber, distributing load evenly onto the annulus around it. If the annulus tears or weakens, the nucleus pulposus can herniate through it — disc prolapse — and compress the nerve roots that run beside the vertebral column, causing severe pain.

### Key determinants
**Perichondrium.** Present around hyaline and yellow elastic cartilage; absent around white fibrocartilage (and absent at any hyaline articular surface).

**Fibres in the matrix.** Hyaline: type II collagen, invisible by light microscopy. Yellow elastic: large numbers of branching elastic fibres plus a few type II collagen fibres. White fibrocartilage: dense type I collagen in parallel thick bundles, almost no ground substance.

**Sites.** Hyaline: fetal skeleton, articular surfaces, costal cartilage, epiphyseal plate, upper respiratory passages. Yellow elastic: ear pinna, Eustachian tube, epiglottis and some laryngeal cartilages, external auditory canal. White fibrocartilage: shoulder and hip joints, mandibular joint, symphysis pubis, sternoclavicular joint, semilunar cartilages of the knee, intervertebral disc.

**Function.** Hyaline: smooth joint surface, airway support, bone growth template. Yellow elastic: very flexible, recovers shape after deformation. White fibrocartilage: strong and tough, resists great tensile stretch, attaches bones with limited mobility.

**The intervertebral disc's two named parts.** Annulus fibrosus: outer, white fibrocartilage, type I collagen. Nucleus pulposus: inner, soft jelly-like, type II collagen.

### Clinical significance
Disc prolapse — herniation of the nucleus pulposus through the annulus fibrosus — compresses nerve roots and is a common, clinically important cause of severe back pain and radiating nerve root symptoms, and it is a direct mechanical consequence of the two-tissue design described above: the annulus's job is to contain the nucleus, and when that containment fails the soft core has nowhere to go but outward, onto whatever structure is nearest.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming any cartilage that is not hyaline must be fibrocartilage. Yellow elastic cartilage is the third, separate type, distinguished from fibrocartilage by having a perichondrium, abundant ground substance and elastic (not just collagen) fibres.

Reversing the intervertebral disc's collagen types. The tough outer annulus fibrosus carries type I collagen, built for tension; the soft inner nucleus pulposus carries type II collagen, the same collagen type as hyaline cartilage matrix, built for cushioning under compression.

Giving white fibrocartilage a perichondrium by analogy with the other two types. The book states explicitly that it has none.

## summary
The 2025 end-of-year paper asked only for osteoblast versus osteoclast, but the department book actually gives all four bone cells a full origin/site/LM/EM/function treatment, and gives the periosteum and endosteum their own structure too. This article covers the two coverings and the two cells the exam-driven article missed: the osteogenic cell that starts the whole lineage, and the osteocyte that ends it.

## sections
### Definition
Periosteum is a layer of connective tissue covering the outer surface of bone. It has two layers: an outer fibrous layer of dense type I collagen, fibroblasts and blood capillaries, and an inner osteogenic layer, a cellular layer of osteogenic cells. Endosteum is a delicate layer of connective tissue containing a single layer of osteogenic cells, lining the inner bone surface.

The osteogenic (osteoprogenitor) cell arises from undifferentiated mesenchymal cells and pericytes and lives in the inner layer of the periosteum and in the endosteum. The osteocyte, the mature bone cell, arises from the osteoblast and lives singly in a bony lacuna.

### Mechanism
The periosteum's inner osteogenic layer is where bone growth actually starts, and the book is specific about when it becomes prominent: it thickens markedly in growth and at fracture, exactly when osteogenic cells are dividing fastest to supply new osteoblasts. Its blood capillaries feed the bone, its outer collagen protects it and gives muscles somewhere to attach, and its osteogenic cells differentiate into osteoblasts that lay down matrix and become osteocytes — appositional growth from the outside. The endosteum runs the same logic on the inner surface, adding bone growth from inside the marrow cavity.

The osteogenic cell is the branch point of the whole bone-cell lineage, and it is not a fixed one. By light microscopy it is a flat cell with a flat nucleus and pale basophilic cytoplasm; by electron microscopy it shows only free ribosomes — a resting cell, not yet secreting. It divides and gives rise to osteoblasts, and is well developed and numerous exactly where new bone is being made fast: fracture healing and young age. But in an area of poor vascularity, the same cell can instead give rise to chondroblasts — bone's fallback plan when the blood supply cannot yet support direct ossification, which is part of why a fracture callus often forms cartilage before it forms bone.

The osteocyte is where the osteoblast's story ends. Once an osteoblast is surrounded by the calcified matrix it made, it is imprisoned in a lacuna and becomes an osteocyte — oval, with fine processes, lightly basophilic cytoplasm rich in alkaline phosphatase. Its lacuna connects to its neighbours' lacunae through canaliculi, and that connection is not decorative: the matrix around it is calcified and no tissue fluid can otherwise pass through it, so the canalicular network is the only nutrient and waste route the buried cell has, maintained by gap junctions between neighbouring processes. Only one osteocyte occupies a lacuna, and because it cannot divide, bone has no interstitial growth — the one respect in which bone's growth logic is strictly simpler than cartilage's.

### Key determinants
**Periosteum versus endosteum.** Periosteum: two layers (outer fibrous, inner osteogenic), covers the outer bone surface, thickens in growth and fracture. Endosteum: single osteogenic-cell layer, lines the inner bone surface.

**Osteogenic cell.** Origin: undifferentiated mesenchymal cells and pericytes. Site: inner periosteum, endosteum. LM: flat cell, flat nucleus, pale basophilic cytoplasm. EM: free ribosomes only. Function: divides to osteoblasts (numerous in fracture/young age); can give chondroblasts in poor vascularity.

**Osteocyte.** Origin: osteoblast. Site: singly in a bony lacuna, linked by canaliculi. LM: oval cell, fine processes, oval nucleus, lightly basophilic cytoplasm with alkaline phosphatase. EM: processes in canaliculi, gap junctions to neighbours, less rough endoplasmic reticulum than the osteoblast but still Golgi and mitochondria. Function: forms and calcifies matrix, maintains matrix viability; cannot divide, so no interstitial growth.

### Clinical significance
A fracture is, mechanically, a sudden demand on exactly the two layers this article covers: the periosteum's inner osteogenic layer thickens and multiplies to supply the osteoblasts a healing fracture needs, and where the local blood supply is too poor to keep up, the same osteogenic cells switch to making chondroblasts instead — a cartilage callus that is only later replaced by bone once vascularity catches up. That switch is the same intracartilaginous ossification machinery the rest of the Bone chapter describes for normal long-bone growth, repurposed for repair.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming the osteogenic cell always becomes an osteoblast. In poor vascularity it becomes a chondroblast instead — the book states this explicitly, and it is the reason fracture healing can pass through a cartilaginous stage.

## summary
Four short, easily separated topics sit together in the book between the bone-cell tables and Compact Bone: what bone matrix is made of, two different ways to make it thin enough to look at down a microscope, two independent ways to classify a bone, and one small comparison table that stops the osteoclast being confused with a very differently-behaved marrow cell.

## sections
### Definition
Bone matrix is formed of two main parts: an organic part (35%), mainly type I collagen fibres in thick bundles plus ground substance (glycosaminoglycans and glycoproteins), and an inorganic part (65%), mainly calcium salts as phosphate and carbonate, which cause bone's hardness. The matrix is arranged as lamellae of calcified collagen bundles embedded in calcified ground substance.

There are two methods for classifying bone: anatomically, by shape, into long, short, flat and irregular bones; and histologically, by structure, into compact and spongy bone.

### Mechanism
Bone's hardness is also its histology problem. A section has to be cut thin enough to see through, and calcium makes that hard, so the book gives two opposite solutions. Decalcification treats the bone with a mineral acid (10% nitric acid), removing the calcium so the softened bone can be cut and stained — and because the mineral, not the tissue, is what is removed, a decalcified section shows the cells and soft tissues. Grinding does the opposite: the bone is dried, cut with a saw, then thinned on a carborundum wheel, and because nothing has been chemically dissolved out, the resulting unstained section shows the mineral architecture — lamellae, lacunae, canaliculi, Haversian and Volkmann's canals — but no cells at all, since nothing was done to preserve or stain them. Grinding is not used for spongy bone, whose thin trabeculae cannot survive the mechanical process.

The two classification systems for bone answer two independent questions, and the book keeps them apart on purpose. "What shape is this bone?" gives long, short, flat and irregular. "What does this bone look like inside?" gives compact and spongy. A single long bone normally contains both compact bone (its shaft) and spongy bone (its ends) — shape and internal structure are orthogonal, not two names for the same fact.

The organic/inorganic split in bone matrix explains why decalcification and grinding produce such different-looking sections in the first place: decalcification strips away the 65% that makes bone hard and radio-opaque, leaving the 35% organic scaffold (and the cells embedded in it) intact and stainable; grinding leaves the mineral in place and destroys everything organic instead. The two preparation methods are, in effect, selectively removing one half of the same matrix composition described here.

### Key determinants
**Bone matrix.** Organic (35%): type I collagen bundles + ground substance (GAGs, glycoproteins). Inorganic (65%): calcium phosphate and carbonate, on collagen surfaces and within ground substance, causes hardness. Arrangement: lamellae of calcified collagen in calcified ground substance.

**Decalcification versus grinding.** Decalcification: mineral acid removes calcium; shows cells and soft tissue. Grinding: mechanical thinning, no chemical step; shows lamellae/lacunae/canaliculi/Haversian and Volkmann's canals but no cells; not used for spongy bone.

**Classification.** Anatomical (by shape): long, short, flat, irregular. Histological (by structure): compact, spongy.

**Osteoclast versus megakaryocyte.** Site: Howship's lacuna on the bone surface / bone marrow. Diameter: about 150 µm / 50 to 70 µm. Cytoplasm: foamy acidophilic / basophilic. Nucleus: multinucleated / single, multilobed. Function: bone resorption / platelet formation.

### Clinical significance
Osteoporosis, a common bone disease with progressive loss of normal bone density and increased fracture risk, is caused by bone resorption outrunning bone formation — a disease of the organic/inorganic matrix balance this article opens with, not a separate topic. Distinguishing the osteoclast from the megakaryocyte matters practically on a marrow or bone biopsy: both are large, and a pathologist who reads a marrow megakaryocyte as bone resorption, or an osteoclast in Howship's lacuna as a marrow cell, has misread the specimen's own anatomical context.

### Common misconceptions
Expecting a ground bone section to show cells. It cannot: grinding preserves mineral architecture at the cost of everything organic, which is the opposite trade decalcification makes.

Reversing bone matrix's organic/inorganic percentages. The mineral (inorganic) part is the larger share at 65%; collagen (organic) is 35%.

Confusing the osteoclast and the megakaryocyte on the strength of "both are big, multinucleated-looking marrow-associated cells". The megakaryocyte has one multilobed nucleus and lies free in the marrow; the osteoclast is genuinely multinucleated and lies in its own excavation on the bone surface.

## summary
Compact and spongy bone are the same matrix and the same cells arranged two different ways, one built for maximum strength along a load axis, the other built to be as light as possible while still resisting force from many directions. The Haversian system is what makes compact bone's strength-to-weight trade work, and its absence is what makes spongy bone possible at all.

## sections
### Definition
Compact bone forms the shaft of long bones and also covers the surface of spongy bone. Spongy (cancellous) bone is present in the centre of flat, short and irregular bones, and in the epiphysis of long bones. It is formed of irregular, branching, anastomosing bony trabeculae enclosing irregular bone marrow cavities, with no Haversian systems.

### Mechanism
Compact bone is built, from outside in, as a stack of nested structures, each with a job. The periosteum covers it. Beneath that, external circumferential lamellae — osteocytes in lacunae between lamellae of calcified collagen — run parallel to the bone's own surface, under the periosteum. Inside those sits the structural unit of compact bone: the Haversian system, or osteon, a cylinder running parallel to the bone's long axis, built of a central Haversian canal (blood vessels and nerves in loose connective tissue) surrounded by 5 to 20 concentric lamellae with osteocytes between them. No single osteon works alone — Volkmann's canals run transversely or obliquely, connecting Haversian canals to each other and to the periosteum or marrow cavity, so the whole shaft is one connected vascular network rather than a bundle of isolated tubes. Between osteons, irregularly arranged interstitial lamellae fill the gaps — the remnants of older osteons that were partly resorbed and replaced. Nearer the marrow cavity, internal circumferential lamellae parallel the inner circumference, and the endosteum lines the surface. Finally, perforating fibres of Sharpey — calcified collagen from tendons and ligaments — cross the periosteum to fuse with the external circumferential lamellae, physically anchoring a tendon into the bone rather than merely resting against it.

Spongy bone skips almost all of that. Without Haversian systems, its trabeculae are simply lamellae with osteocytes between them, branching and anastomosing to enclose marrow cavities directly rather than channelling vessels through a central canal. That is not a simpler version of compact bone's design; it is a different design for a different mechanical job. A shaft under compressive and bending load along one long axis benefits from compact bone's concentrated, longitudinally-aligned lamellae. A vertebral body or the end of a long bone, loaded from many directions at once, gets more strength per unit weight from a branching lattice that can align its trabeculae along whichever direction the local stress actually runs — which is why spongy bone sits at the epiphyses and inside flat, short and irregular bones, exactly where load direction is least predictable.

### Key determinants
**Compact bone, outside to inside.** Periosteum → external circumferential lamellae → Haversian systems (osteons: canal + 5-20 concentric lamellae, connected by Volkmann's canals) → interstitial lamellae → internal circumferential lamellae → endosteum. Sharpey's fibres anchor tendons/ligaments through the periosteum into the external circumferential lamellae.

**Haversian canal versus Volkmann canal.** Haversian: longitudinal, inside one osteon, carries vessels/nerves in loose connective tissue. Volkmann: transverse or oblique, crosses between osteons, connects Haversian canals to each other and to the surface or marrow.

**Spongy bone.** Sites: centre of flat, short, irregular bones; epiphysis of long bones. Structure: branching, anastomosing trabeculae of lamellae with osteocytes, enclosing irregular marrow cavities. No Haversian systems. Covered by periosteum on the bone surface; marrow cavities lined by endosteum.

### Clinical significance
Compact bone's dependence on an intact Haversian-Volkmann vascular network is exactly why a badly comminuted fracture through the shaft — one that severs many osteons' canals at once — heals more slowly than a simple crack: the blood supply the healing process needs has itself been interrupted at multiple points, not just at the fracture line. Spongy bone's greater surface area and marrow content is part of why it is the preferred site for a bone marrow biopsy, typically taken from an iliac crest rich in cancellous bone.

### Common misconceptions
Treating the Haversian canal and the Volkmann canal as the same structure with two names. Haversian canals run longitudinally inside an osteon; Volkmann canals run transversely between osteons — different orientation, different job.

Assuming spongy bone is simply "unfinished" or immature compact bone. It is a distinct, permanent structural design with no Haversian systems at all, present in specific mechanical contexts, not a developmental stage compact bone passes through.

Forgetting that a single long bone normally contains both types: compact bone in its shaft, spongy bone at its epiphyses.

## summary
Every bone in the body is made by one of two routes: intramembranous ossification, which turns a mesenchymal membrane directly into bone for flat bones, or intracartilaginous ossification, which destroys and replaces a cartilage model piece by piece for long, short and irregular bones. The second route is the more elaborate of the two, and it is also the one that explains why a growing child's long bones can lengthen at all.

## sections
### Definition
Bones of the body are formed by one of two methods: intramembranous ossification, occurring in flat bones from a mesenchymal connective tissue membrane, or intracartilaginous (endochondral) ossification, which replaces a cartilage model with bone in long, short and irregular bones. A long bone is formed of a shaft, the diaphysis, and two ends, the epiphyses.

### Mechanism
Intramembranous ossification is the more direct route. Undifferentiated mesenchymal cells in a connective tissue membrane condense at vessel-rich spots called ossification centres, differentiate through osteogenic cells into osteoblasts, and those osteoblasts lay down osteoid, which calcifies; some osteoblasts end up imprisoned as osteocytes. As osteoblasts on the trabecular surfaces keep adding matrix, the trabeculae thicken, branch and anastomose into spongy bone, and marrow cells arriving with the vasculature fill the spaces between them. At the bone's margins, this spongy bone is later remodelled into compact bone, and a specialised connective tissue investing the bone there becomes the periosteum. No cartilage model is ever involved.

Intracartilaginous ossification starts from the opposite point: a pre-existing cartilage model that has to be destroyed as it is replaced. The primary ossification centre appears in the middle of the diaphysis. Rising vascularity in the perichondrium there turns chondrogenic cells into osteogenic cells, which differentiate to osteoblasts — and because that same perichondrium is now producing bone rather than cartilage, it becomes periosteum. Those osteoblasts form a periosteal bone collar around the cartilage model, under the new periosteum. That collar is not simply reinforcement — it actively blocks diffusion of oxygen and nutrients to the cartilage cells trapped beneath it, and starved chondrocytes at the model's centre enlarge, deposit calcium, and die, leaving irregular cavities where they used to be. Osteoclasts then punch holes through the bone collar, letting a vascular core of mesenchymal cells and vessels invade those cavities; the mesenchymal cells around the new vessels become osteogenic cells, then osteoblasts, laying down irregular spongy trabeculae with marrow filling the gaps between them. Osteoclasts finish the job by merging the irregular marrow spaces into one regular cavity, while the trabecular lamellae reorganise into the concentric, regular pattern of Haversian systems — compact bone.

The secondary ossification centre, in the epiphysis, runs through the same steps until the cartilage there is replaced by spongy bone too — but it stops short of finishing the model entirely, and on purpose. Two pieces of cartilage survive: the epiphyseal plate, between epiphysis and diaphysis, and the articular cartilage at the very ends of the bone. The epiphyseal plate is where a bone actually gets longer, and it does so by running the ossification sequence continuously, zone by zone, so that new cartilage is always being made just as fast as it is being replaced by bone below it: a resting zone of ordinary hyaline cartilage; a proliferative zone where cells multiply into parallel rows; a zone of hypertrophy where cells swell as glycogen and alkaline phosphatase build up; a zone of calcification where the swollen chondrocytes calcify their surrounding matrix and then die, leaving empty spaces; a zone of invasion where an avascular bud of capillaries and mesenchymal cells pushes in through holes the osteoclasts have opened in the periosteal collar; and a zone of ossification where those mesenchymal cells become osteogenic cells, then osteoblasts, depositing irregular spongy trabeculae. A remodelling stage follows — osteoclasts resorbing here, osteoblasts depositing there — collapsing the irregular cavities into a single marrow space, and a final stage of compact bone formation lays down the Haversian system around it.

### Key determinants
**Intramembranous ossification, in order.** Mesenchymal membrane → ossification centre (UMCs condense) → osteogenic cells → osteoblasts → osteoid → calcification → some osteoblasts become osteocytes → trabeculae thicken/branch/anastomose into spongy bone → marginal remodelling to compact bone → periosteum forms at the margins.

**Intracartilaginous ossification: primary centre, in order.** Cartilage model → perichondrium vascularises → periosteum forms → periosteal bone collar → collar blocks diffusion → central chondrocytes hypertrophy, calcify, die → osteoclasts open holes in the collar → vascular invasion → osteogenic cells → osteoblasts → spongy trabeculae → osteoclasts merge marrow cavities and remodel into Haversian compact bone.

**Secondary centre and the epiphyseal plate's six zones, in order.** Resting cartilage → proliferative → hypertrophy → calcification → invasion → ossification → (remodelling → compact bone formation). What survives ossification entirely: the epiphyseal plate (longitudinal growth) and the articular cartilage (joint surface).

### Clinical significance
Because the epiphyseal plate is where longitudinal growth actually happens, its closure — when the last zone of cartilage is finally ossified and no resting cartilage remains to feed the sequence — is what ends a person's height growth, and an epiphyseal plate injury in a child can permanently shorten or angulate the affected bone if the growth zones themselves are damaged rather than just the surrounding bone. Articular cartilage's survival as the one other unossified remnant is also why a joint surface, decades later, is still cartilage rather than bare bone: the secondary ossification centre was built to stop exactly there.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming intramembranous ossification passes through a cartilage stage. It does not — flat bones ossify directly from mesenchyme, with no cartilage model at any point, which is the entire distinction between the two methods.

Thinking the bone collar is purely protective. The book is explicit that it also blocks diffusion to the cartilage beneath it, which is the trigger for chondrocyte death and the start of the invasion sequence — the collar is a cause of central cartilage death, not just a shield.

Believing the whole cartilage model eventually becomes bone. Two named exceptions remain cartilage permanently: the epiphyseal plate (until growth ends) and the articular cartilage (for life).

## summary
A skeletal muscle is not a single structure but three nested connective-tissue coats holding together bundles of long, striated, multinucleated fibres, and each fibre is itself a stack of sarcomeres — the true contractile unit, defined precisely as the segment between two Z lines, whose named bands and lines are a direct map of where its actin and myosin filaments sit. That sarcomere lattice is read out by the triad tubular system: a T-tubule at every A-I junction, flanked by two terminal cisternae, spreads depolarisation from the surface into the fibre's depth and triggers the calcium release that starts the sliding described above.

## sections
### Definition
Muscle tissue is mesodermal: undifferentiated mesenchymal cells differentiate into myoblasts, embryonic progenitors that differentiate into myocytes, which can form any of the three muscle types — skeletal, cardiac or smooth. Skeletal muscles attach to the skeleton, except for the muscles of the face and tongue, the pharynx and upper third of the oesophagus, the diaphragm, and the cremasteric muscles.

Skeletal muscle consists of fibres held together by three nested connective-tissue layers: the epimysium, dense connective tissue around the whole muscle, penetrated by its major vessels and nerves; the perimysium, descending from the epimysium to wrap a bundle (fascicle) of fibres, carrying large vessels and nerves; and the endomysium, reticular fibres around each single fibre, carrying small vessels and fine nerves. Each fibre is a long, cylindrical, non-branched cell (except in the face and tongue), 10 to 100 micrometres in diameter, with multiple oval peripheral nuclei — one per fused myoblast — and acidophilic sarcoplasm with clear transverse striations.

### Mechanism
The three connective-tissue layers exist to solve one mechanical problem: a fibre contracting alone does nothing useful unless its force is transmitted to the skeleton and the fibres around it are held in register. Endomysium binds individual fibres and nourishes them by diffusion from its small vessels; perimysium bundles fibres into fascicles that can act as a functional unit; epimysium binds the whole muscle and carries the vessels and nerves large enough to supply it. Force generated by any one fibre is transmitted outward through this connective tissue web rather than by the fibre acting alone.

Inside the fibre, the sarcoplasm holds myofibrils running its full length, a well-developed sarcoplasmic reticulum around them specialised for calcium storage and release, numerous mitochondria in rows between myofibrils, and myoglobin and glycogen for oxygen supply and energy. Each myofibril is built of repeating sarcomeres, and the sarcomere is the whole reason the fibre looks striated at all. It is defined as the segment of a myofibril between two adjacent Z lines, containing one A band and the two flanking halves of an I band, and it is the basic contractile unit — the smallest segment capable of shortening on its own.

The named bands follow directly from where the two filament types sit. Thick myosin filaments occupy only the A band. Thin actin filaments attach at the Z line, run through the whole I band, and continue into the A band as far as the H zone. So the A band is dark because it holds both filaments overlapping; the H zone, its central region, is paler because only myosin sits there; the M line, bisecting the H zone, comes from cross-links between adjacent myosin filaments; the I band is light because only actin is present; and the Z line itself is dense with condensed actin and the accessory proteins that keep the whole lattice in register. Under the polarising microscope, that filament density difference becomes an optical one: the A band alters polarised light in two planes (anisotropic) and the I band does not (isotropic) — which is where the letters A and I actually come from.

Contraction changes the picture in a specific, filament-sliding way rather than by the filaments themselves shrinking. As the sarcomere shortens, the I band shortens and the H zone is abolished, because actin slides further into the A band's territory — but the A band's own length, fixed by the myosin filament's length, stays constant throughout.

### The triad tubular system
The sarcolemma does not just enclose the fibre; it invaginates deep into it. Many such invaginations form transverse tubules — T-tubules — in the sarcoplasm, encircling every myofibril at the A-I band junction, and their lumens are continuous with the extracellular space. That last fact is the whole point of the system: it lets a signal at the cell surface reach every myofibril's depth without diffusing there.

Each T-tubule lies between a pair of wide terminal cisternae of the sarcoplasmic reticulum, and the T-tubule plus its two flanking cisternae together form a triple structure — the triad tubular system, named for the three-part unit itself, not for any one component alone. Its role is to couple electrical excitation to the calcium release that lets contraction begin: depolarisation spreads along the membranes of the T-tubules into the depth of the muscle fibre, and this stimulates the sarcoplasmic reticulum to rapidly release Ca²⁺ into the sarcoplasm.

### Key determinants
**Three coats, largest to smallest.** Epimysium: whole muscle, major vessels/nerves. Perimysium: one fascicle, large vessels/nerves. Endomysium: one fibre, small vessels/fine nerves.

**The fibre by light microscopy.** Size: 10-100 µm diameter, variable length. Shape: cylindrical, non-branched (except face/tongue). Sarcolemma: thick (fused with basal lamina and endomysium). Nuclei: multiple, oval, peripheral — one per fused myoblast. Sarcoplasm: acidophilic, clear transverse striations.

**The sarcomere, filament by filament.** Z line: actin attachment, dense, defines the sarcomere's boundary. I band: actin only, light, either side of the Z line. A band: myosin + overlapping actin, dark, anisotropic, constant length. H zone: myosin only, central pale region of the A band. M line: myosin cross-links, bisects the H zone.

**On contraction.** I band shortens. H zone is abolished. A band length stays constant. Filaments slide; they do not shorten.

**The triad.** One T-tubule (sarcolemmal invagination, lumen continuous with extracellular space) at the A-I junction, flanked by two terminal cisternae of sarcoplasmic reticulum — three parts, one triad. Depolarisation along the T-tubule triggers Ca²⁺ release from the terminal cisternae into the sarcoplasm.

### Clinical significance
Reading a muscle biopsy or an electron micrograph for A-band length is one of the more direct histological ways to distinguish active shortening from a fixed, non-contracting fibre: because the A band's length is fixed by filament length rather than by contractile state, any apparent change in A-band length on a slide points to a fixation or sectioning artefact rather than a physiological one, while a shortened I band and an abolished H zone are the expected, real signature of a fibre fixed mid-contraction.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming the sarcomere is simply "one repeat unit" without a precise boundary. It is defined exactly, Z line to Z line, and that precision is what lets the book state which bands change length on contraction and which does not.

Believing myosin or actin filaments physically shorten during contraction. Filament length is fixed; the A band's constant length through contraction is the book's own evidence for this — shortening comes entirely from the filaments sliding past each other.

Confusing perimysium and endomysium. Perimysium wraps a fascicle (many fibres); endomysium wraps one fibre. Both are thin, both carry small vessels, and mixing them up is the most common slip on this topic.

## summary
Skeletal muscle is not histologically uniform — red, white and intermediate fibres coexist in the same muscle — and its capacity to repair itself lives entirely in one reserve cell population, the satellite cell. Where that regenerative machinery breaks, in muscular dystrophy, the muscle cannot recover from ordinary wear the way it normally does.

## sections
### Definition
Skeletal muscle fibres are classified into three types: red fibres, white fibres, and intermediate fibres. At the junction of muscle with tendon, the musculotendinous junction, the muscle fibres taper off and the connective tissue components of the muscle continue directly into the tendon, with the tendon's collagen fibres inserting among the muscle fibres.

Inactive reserve myoblasts persist in adult muscle, lying between the sarcolemma and the basal lamina, and are called satellite cells.

### Mechanism
Fibre typing is not cosmetic variation — it is a real histological signature with diagnostic use. Classification of fibre types in a muscle biopsy has clinical value in diagnosing muscle disease, including disease due to mitochondrial disorders, because the proportion and pattern of red, white and intermediate fibres shifts in specific, recognisable ways when the underlying metabolic or contractile machinery is disturbed.

Satellite cells are what makes skeletal muscle repair possible at all. After injury, these normally inactive reserve myoblasts activate, proliferate and fuse to form new muscle fibres — the entire regenerative capacity of skeletal muscle sits in this one cell population, sitting quietly between the sarcolemma and the basal lamina until called on.

That dependency is exactly what makes muscular dystrophy so damaging: it is a progressive degeneration of skeletal muscle fibres in which a lack of dystrophin in satellite cells means they fail to replace the degenerating fibres, so muscle function progressively decreases — not because the fibres themselves are uniquely fragile, but because the one mechanism that would normally replace them is disabled. Hypertrophy runs the opposite, and much simpler, process: existing fibres enlarge, as in exercise, without requiring satellite cells to make new ones at all.

At the other end of the fibre, the musculotendinous junction is where contractile force generated inside the fibre is finally handed off to the skeleton: muscle connective tissue continues directly into tendon collagen, so the pull generated by sliding filaments inside the sarcomere is transmitted, uninterrupted, into the tendon and onward to bone.

### Key determinants
**Fibre types.** Red, white, intermediate — biopsy typing has diagnostic value, including for mitochondrial muscle disease.

**Satellite cells.** Location: between sarcolemma and basal lamina. Trigger: injury. Sequence: activation → proliferation → fusion → new fibre. This is the entire regenerative mechanism of skeletal muscle.

**Hypertrophy versus dystrophy.** Hypertrophy: enlargement of existing fibres (exercise), satellite cells not required. Dystrophy: progressive fibre degeneration, satellite cells present but dystrophin-deficient and unable to replace lost fibres, so muscle function falls.

**Muscle cramps.** Sudden, painful contractions from lowered blood flow, low blood potassium, or vigorous exercise without warm-up; usually the lower leg.

**Musculotendinous junction.** Muscle fibres taper; muscle connective tissue continues directly into tendon collagen, which inserts among the muscle fibres.

### Clinical significance
Muscular dystrophy demonstrates a general principle worth carrying beyond this one disease: a tissue's regenerative capacity can fail even when the tissue's own structural proteins are intact everywhere except in the one reserve population responsible for repair — dystrophin loss specifically in satellite cells, not in mature fibres generally, is stated as the mechanism. Muscle cramps, by contrast, are framed as a functional, reversible disturbance (blood flow, potassium, inadequate warm-up) rather than a structural one, which is why stretching and correcting the precipitant, rather than any structural repair, resolves them.

### Common misconceptions
Treating hypertrophy and dystrophy as opposite points on one severity scale. They are mechanistically unrelated: hypertrophy enlarges existing, healthy fibres; dystrophy is a failure of the satellite-cell repair system itself.

Assuming any large biopsy-visible fibre difference is pathological. Red, white and intermediate fibres coexist normally in the same muscle; fibre-type pattern only becomes diagnostic when read against a specific clinical question.

Picturing the musculotendinous junction as a sharp, discrete boundary. The book describes tapering fibres and continuous connective tissue, a gradual handoff rather than an abrupt splice.

## summary
The department book closes its Muscle Tissue chapter with its own table, setting skeletal, cardiac and smooth muscle side by side across fourteen features at once. Read row by row it is mostly a recap of what the three muscle-type articles already state one at a time; read as a whole it is the fastest way to answer a "compare" question, and the department's own written-assessment revision set asks for exactly this table.

## sections
### Definition
The book's own heading is "Difference between types of muscle fibers," printed as a table with three columns — Skeletal Muscle, Cardiac Muscle, Smooth Muscle — and fourteen rows: site, size, single fibre, shape, branching, sarcolemma, striation, nuclei, sarcomeres, tubular system, cell junctions, regeneration, action, innervation, and a final "Modifications" row.

Site: skeletal muscle is attached to bone; cardiac muscle forms the heart wall; smooth muscle lies in the wall of the viscera. Size: skeletal fibres are largest, cardiac fibres medium-sized, smooth fibres smallest. Single fibre: a skeletal fibre is a single cell and a smooth fibre is a single cell; a cardiac fibre is several cells (myocytes) linked together. Shape: skeletal and cardiac fibres are cylindrical; smooth fibres are spindle-shaped.

### Mechanism
The table is not fourteen unrelated facts; most of its rows follow from the same two underlying differences already argued in the three muscle-type articles — whether the fibre is one cell or many, and how far its contractile apparatus is organised.

Branching, sarcolemma and nuclei follow the one-cell-or-many split directly. Skeletal fibres rarely branch, are thick-sarcolemma'd (fused to basal lamina and endomysium) and carry multiple peripheral nuclei — the signature of many fused myoblasts in one cell. Cardiac fibres branch and join, are very thin-sarcolemma'd, and carry one central nucleus per myocyte, because each myocyte is its own cell rather than a fusion product. Smooth fibres do not branch, are thin-sarcolemma'd, and carry one central nucleus, because each is a single, unfused cell from the start.

Striation, sarcomeres and tubular system follow how organised the contractile apparatus is. Skeletal muscle has the most highly ordered filament lattice — regular sarcomeres, clear striation — and needs the fastest, most complete excitation-contraction coupling, hence a triad (one T-tubule, two terminal cisternae) at every A-I junction. Cardiac muscle has sarcomeres too, but arranged less regularly, giving "non-clear" striation rather than none, and needs less sarcoplasmic reticulum, hence only a diad at the Z line. Smooth muscle has no sarcomeres at all — its actin and myosin are irregularly arranged — so it has no striation and no tubular system; it uses surface caveolae instead.

Cell junctions and regeneration follow from what each muscle type is built to do continuously. Skeletal fibres, as single large cells with their own nerve supply, need no junctions to their neighbours and cannot fuse new fibres from nothing, but recruit satellite cells (reserve myoblasts) to regenerate. Cardiac fibres, which must contract as one electrical and mechanical unit for a lifetime, need the intercalated disc's three junction types and, once mature, cannot regenerate at all. Smooth muscle, built to remodel throughout life (as in a pregnant uterus), keeps both a simpler gap-junction-only coupling and the ability to regenerate by mitosis or from pericytes.

Action and innervation follow the same line one level up: skeletal muscle is voluntary and motor-innervated; cardiac and smooth muscle are both involuntary and autonomically innervated.

### Key determinants
**Site, size, single fibre.** Skeletal: bone, largest, single cell. Cardiac: heart wall, medium, several linked cells. Smooth: viscera wall, smallest, single cell.

**Shape and branching.** Skeletal: cylindrical, rarely branches. Cardiac: cylindrical, branches. Smooth: spindle-shaped, non-branched.

**Sarcolemma and striation.** Skeletal: thick, striated. Cardiac: very thin, non-clear striation. Smooth: thin, non-striated.

**Nuclei and sarcomeres.** Skeletal: multiple/peripheral, regular sarcomeres. Cardiac: one/central, irregular sarcomeres. Smooth: one/central, no sarcomeres.

**Tubular system and cell junctions.** Skeletal: triad (A-I junction), no junctions. Cardiac: diad (Z line), intercalated disc (fascia adherens, desmosomes, gap junctions). Smooth: none (caveolae instead), gap junctions only.

**Regeneration, action, innervation.** Skeletal: satellite cells, voluntary, motor. Cardiac: cannot regenerate, involuntary, autonomic. Smooth: mitosis/pericytes, involuntary, autonomic.

**Named modification.** Skeletal: muscle spindle. Cardiac: Purkinje fibres. Smooth: none printed.

### Clinical significance
The row most directly worth an exam mark by itself is regeneration: cardiac muscle's total inability to regenerate is exactly why an infarcted area heals by fibrous scar rather than new myocardium, while skeletal muscle's satellite cells and smooth muscle's mitotic and pericyte-derived regeneration are why those two tissues can recover structurally from comparable injury in a way heart muscle cannot.

### Common misconceptions
Treating "striated" as all-or-nothing. Cardiac muscle is not unstriated — it shows non-clear striation because it still has sarcomeres, just irregular ones; only smooth muscle, with no sarcomeres, is genuinely unstriated. A two-way striated/unstriated split loses the row that actually separates cardiac from both neighbours.

Assuming every muscle type has some tubular system. Smooth muscle has none at all — caveolae are a different structure (surface invaginations, not a tubule network) and are not "a smaller tubular system."

Assuming the intercalated disc's three junction types are three alternatives rather than three parts of one structure. All three — fascia adherens, desmosomes, gap junctions — are present together in one cardiac muscle fibre; the comparison with skeletal muscle (no junctions) and smooth muscle (gap junctions alone) is about which types of junction, not how many are present in cardiac muscle at once.

## summary
Skin is one organ built in two thickness variants, thick and thin, that differ in every named epidermal layer and in whether they carry hair — and the two variants are stitched to the dermis beneath them by the same zigzag junction wherever they occur. The hypodermis, despite sitting right underneath, is excluded from the skin by name.

## sections
### Definition
Skin is a protective covering of the whole body, the largest and heaviest organ, accounting for 15 to 20% of body weight. It is formed of two layers, the outer epidermis and the inner dermis. The hypodermis, a deep layer of adipose connective tissue beneath the skin corresponding to the superficial fascia, is not part of the skin; it binds the skin loosely to underlying tissue and allows free movement.

According to epidermal thickness, skin is classified as thick or thin. Thick (non-hairy) skin has a thick epidermis (400 to 1400 micrometres) and a thick horny layer, and is present in the palms and soles. Thin (hairy) skin covers the rest of the body, with the eyelid carrying the thinnest skin of all.

### Mechanism
The thick/thin distinction is not one difference but a whole cluster of differences that all move together, because epidermal thickness and hair-bearing capacity are mechanically and developmentally linked. Palms and soles need to resist abrasion, not grow hair, so every layer that contributes to a tough, resilient surface is expanded there: the Malpighian layer (the mitotically active basale-plus-spinosum) is thicker, the granular layer runs 3 to 5 cells deep rather than a single layer, the clear layer is clearly present rather than barely apparent, and the horny layer is very thick rather than very thin. The trade-off is that thick skin carries no hair follicles, sebaceous glands, or arrector pili muscles at all — an appendage-free zone built purely for surface durability — though it does carry more sweat glands than thin skin, since palms and soles are still needed for temperature-regulating sweating. Thin skin runs the opposite trade: thinner at every epidermal layer, but it is where hair follicles, sebaceous glands and arrector pili actually live, and its dermal papillae are correspondingly fewer, smaller and more irregular than thick skin's numerous, large, regular papillae — because thin skin has less need for the mechanical interlocking that a heavily loaded surface benefits from.

Wherever the epidermis and dermis meet, in either skin type, the boundary is not flat. Dermal papillae project up from the dermis into matching concavities in the epidermis, the epidermal ridges, and this zigzag interdigitation does two jobs at once: it physically attaches epidermis to dermis, and it increases the surface area across which the avascular epidermis can be nourished by diffusion from the vascular dermis just beneath. Three factors together hold the epidermis fixed to the dermis: the epidermal basement membrane itself; hemidesmosomes anchoring the basal epidermal cells to that membrane; and the mechanical interlock of the papillae and ridges. On the surface, this same zigzag pattern, replicated at a larger scale, is what produces fingerprints — of recognised medico-legal importance.

### Key determinants
**Thick versus thin skin.** Sites: palms/soles/digit tips versus the rest of the body. Epidermis: thicker (400-1400 µm) versus thinner. Malpighian layer: thicker versus thinner. Granular layer: 3-5 cells versus single layer. Clear layer: present versus less apparent. Horny layer: very thick versus very thin. Dermal papillae: more, large, regular versus fewer, small, irregular. Hair follicles/sebaceous glands/arrector pili: absent versus present. Sweat glands: more numerous versus less numerous.

**Dermal-epidermal junction.** Structure: dermal papillae interdigitating with epidermal ridges, zigzag-shaped. Two functions: attachment of epidermis to dermis; increased surface area for epidermal nutrition. Surface consequence: fingerprints, medico-legally important.

**Three fixation factors.** The epidermal basement membrane; hemidesmosomes (basal cells to basement membrane); dermal papillae interdigitating with epidermal ridges.

### Clinical significance
Skin colour, visible through the epidermis especially in light-skinned individuals, is a real diagnostic surface: it pales in anaemia and turns blue when oxygenated blood is insufficient, as in heart and lung disease — a direct, everyday clinical use of the epidermis being translucent enough to show the vascular dermis beneath it. Fingerprints, produced by the dermal papillae/epidermal ridge interdigitation, remain medico-legally important precisely because that pattern is fixed at this structural level and does not change.

### Common misconceptions
Counting the hypodermis as a third layer of the skin. The book names it, describes it, and states outright that it is not part of the skin.

Assuming thick skin has more appendages overall because it is the "reinforced" skin type. It has more sweat glands, but it has no hair follicles, sebaceous glands or arrector pili at all — those three appendages belong to thin skin exclusively.

Expecting a flat boundary between epidermis and dermis. The junction is deliberately zigzag-shaped, and that shape is functional (attachment, nutrition), not incidental.

## summary
The epidermis is a single, continuously renewing production line, and its five named layers are five stages of the same journey: a basal cell divides, moves upward, fills with keratin, seals itself with lipid, dies, and is finally shed as a scale. Reading the layers this way — as one process seen at five snapshots — is more useful for an exam than memorising five unrelated lists.

## sections
### Definition
The epidermis is an outer epithelial layer, a keratinised stratified squamous epithelium derived from ectoderm, thicker over the soles than the palms, avascular and nourished by diffusion, and rich in free nerve endings. It is formed of keratinocytes (85% of its cells) and non-keratinocytes. According to keratinocyte maturation, the epidermis has five layers: the stratum basale, stratum spinosum, stratum granulosum, stratum lucidum and stratum corneum.

### Mechanism
The stratum basale is where the whole process starts. Its single layer of low columnar cells rests on a clear, wavy basement membrane, with basophilic cytoplasm and a large basal nucleus, and shows intense mitotic activity — the source of the epidermis's own renewal, complete every 2 to 4 weeks. Desmosomes join these cells to each other and to the layer above; hemidesmosomes are the separate junction anchoring them specifically to the basement membrane beneath. Melanocytes and Merkel's cells both live in this layer, alongside the dividing keratinocytes.

Daughter cells pushed upward enter the stratum spinosum, 4 to 8 layers of polyhedral cells with less basophilic cytoplasm and central rounded nuclei, joined to their neighbours by desmosomes whose attachment points create the spine-like processes that give the layer its name — the spaces between them are shrinkage artefacts, not real gaps. Mitosis is not confined to the stratum basale alone: the book defines the Malpighian layer as the stratum basale and stratum spinosum together, and mitotic figures are confined to that combined layer.

Continuing upward, the cell enters the stratum granulosum, 3 to 5 layers of spindle-shaped cells whose deeply basophilic, granular cytoplasm holds two distinct granule types. Keratohyalin granules, containing phosphate-rich proteins including filaggrin and trichohyalin, drive keratinisation itself — they promote the aggregation of keratin filaments into tonofibrils, which is the actual step that converts a granular cell into a keratinised cell. Membrane-coated lamellar granules run a separate job: their lipid-bilayer contents are released into the intercellular spaces as a cement substance that seals the barrier between cells. One granule builds the internal keratin scaffold; the other seals the gaps around it.

By the stratum lucidum, only apparent in thick skin, the cell is already dying: it is thin, clear, homogeneous, its nucleus disappearing by karyolysis, its organelles lost to lysosomal activity, its cytoplasm packed with tonofibrils in an electron-dense keratohyalin-derived matrix. The stratum corneum is the finished product: heavily keratinised dead cells, called scales, with no nuclei and no organelles at all, held together only by remnant desmosomes in thickened cell membranes.

### Key determinants
**Stratum basale.** LM: single low columnar layer, basophilic, large basal nucleus, intense mitosis (renewal every 2-4 weeks); carries melanocytes and Merkel's cells. EM: desmosomes to neighbours/spinosum, hemidesmosomes to basement membrane, free ribosomes/polysomes, 10 nm keratin filaments.

**Stratum spinosum.** LM: 4-8 layers, polyhedral, less basophilic, central round nuclei, prickly desmosomal processes; carries Langerhans cells; thicker with more desmosomes on the soles. EM: tonofilament bundles ending in desmosomal plaques, highly interdigitating borders. Basale + spinosum = the mitotic Malpighian layer.

**Stratum granulosum.** LM: 3-5 layers, spindle-shaped, deep basophilia, flat pale nuclei. EM: keratohyalin granules (filaggrin/trichohyalin → tonofibril aggregation → keratinisation) and lamellar granules (lipid sheets → intercellular seal).

**Stratum lucidum.** LM: thin, clear, homogeneous, more apparent in thick skin, karyolytic nuclei. EM: extremely flattened, thickened membranes, few desmosome remnants, organelles/nuclei largely gone, dense tonofibril-keratohyalin matrix.

**Stratum corneum.** LM: thick eosinophilic layer of dead scales. EM: thickened membranes with desmosome remnants, mature keratin filaments in amorphous matrix, no nuclei, no organelles.

### Clinical significance
Psoriasis is a common skin disease with an increased number of proliferating cells in the stratum basale and spinosum — that is, an accelerated version of exactly the renewal process this article describes — producing greater epidermal thickness and abnormal keratinisation with a defective skin barrier. Epidermal translucency, which depends on how much of this layered structure is intact and how thick the outer dead layers are, is also why skin colour is diagnostically useful: it pales in anaemia and turns blue when oxygenation is insufficient.

### Common misconceptions
Restricting mitosis to the stratum basale. The book defines the mitotically active Malpighian layer as basale plus spinosum together.

Confusing the two stratum granulosum granule types by function. Keratohyalin granules drive keratin filament aggregation inside the cell; lamellar granules seal the intercellular spaces with lipid — one is intracellular scaffolding, the other is an extracellular barrier.

Expecting nuclei or organelles anywhere in the stratum corneum. By that point both are gone; a nucleus seen this superficially belongs to a lower layer.

Assuming hemidesmosomes join keratinocyte to keratinocyte. They specifically anchor the basal cell to the basement membrane; ordinary desmosomes join cell to cell.

## summary
The 15% of epidermal cells that are not keratinocytes are three unrelated specialists sharing the same address: Langerhans cells watch for antigen, melanocytes make pigment invisibly, and Merkel cells sense light touch. Each is defined by a diagnostic ultrastructural feature that a routine H&E section either cannot show at all, or shows only as an unexplained pale cell among the keratinocytes.

## sections
### Definition
Langerhans cells originate from bone marrow precursors that migrate via the blood to the dermis and then the epidermis, representing 3 to 8% of epidermal cells, found mainly in the stratum spinosum. Melanocyte precursors arise from the neural crest and migrate to the skin early in development, lying between and just below the stratum basale. Merkel's cells are ectodermal, modified epithelial cells present between cells of the basal layer, abundant in highly sensitive skin such as the fingertips.

### Mechanism
Langerhans cells are the epidermis's own immune sentinels, and their whole structure reflects that job. Stellate-shaped, with a dark nucleus and pale, clear cytoplasm on H&E — they can also be picked out with vital stains — they carry, by electron microscopy, a prominent Golgi complex, numerous lysosomes, and the diagnostic feature: tennis-racquet-shaped Birbeck granules, some containing hydrolytic enzymes. Deliberately, they lack keratin filaments, desmosomes, melanin granules, and any cell junction to the keratinocytes around them — an antigen-presenting cell has no reason to be structurally integrated into the keratin-making machinery, and needs instead to bind antigen that contacts the skin and carry it to T lymphocytes, giving Langerhans cells a real role in reactions like allergic dermatitis.

Melanocytes solve a completely different problem — protecting the skin from ultraviolet light — and their invisibility on a routine slide is itself informative: H&E-stained sections do not demonstrate melanocytes at all, because the stain that reveals keratinocyte structure does not pick out this cell's rounded, pale-nucleus body or its long irregular processes reaching between neighbouring keratinocytes. Only electron microscopy or special stains show the cell doing what its structure says it does: abundant rough endoplasmic reticulum, a prominent Golgi apparatus and mitochondria — the full ultrastructure of an active protein-synthesising cell — plus melanosomes, the pigment-containing granules that are the actual product. The cell synthesises tyrosinase, the enzyme essential for melanin synthesis, and ultraviolet light itself speeds that synthesis up — a direct feedback loop between the stimulus (UV) and the response (more pigment) built into the cell's own biochemistry.

Merkel's cells run yet a third, sensory job. A free sensory nerve fibre traverses the basal lamina to terminate as a disc-shaped expansion directly beneath the Merkel cell, forming the Merkel cell-neurite complex — a purpose-built, one-to-one mechanoreceptor unit rather than a diffuse nerve ending. By electron microscopy the cell is attached to neighbouring keratinocytes by desmosomes (unlike the Langerhans cell, which has none), holds electron-dense granules resembling those of neuroendocrine (APUD) cells elsewhere in the body, and has a deeply invaginated nucleus — structural evidence for the cell's second, neurosecretory function alongside its mechanoreceptor role.

### Key determinants
**Langerhans cell.** Origin: bone marrow, via blood, to epidermis. Site: mainly stratum spinosum. LM: stellate, dark nucleus, pale clear cytoplasm; identifiable by vital stain. EM: Golgi, numerous lysosomes, Birbeck granules (tennis-racquet shape); no keratin filaments, no desmosomes, no melanin, no junctions to keratinocytes. Function: antigen presentation to T lymphocytes.

**Melanocyte.** Origin: neural crest (ectoderm). Site: between/below stratum basale, processes into basale and spinosum. LM: not seen on H&E; pale rounded nucleus where special stains are used. EM: rough endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi, mitochondria, melanosomes, euchromatic nucleus with prominent nucleolus; no desmosomes to keratinocytes, hemidesmosomes to basal lamina. Function: melanin synthesis via tyrosinase, sped up by UV light.

**Merkel's cell.** Origin: ectoderm, modified epithelial cell. Site: between basal cells, abundant at fingertips and some hair follicle bases. LM: resembles epidermal cells; a free sensory nerve fibre ends beneath it as a disc-shaped expansion (Merkel cell-neurite complex). EM: desmosomes to keratinocytes, APUD-like electron-dense granules, deeply invaginated nucleus. Function: light-touch mechanoreception and neurosecretion.

### Clinical significance
Albinism is the absence of melanin production from a genetic defect in tyrosinase synthesis — a direct failure of the enzyme this article names as essential for melanin synthesis. Vitiligo presents as white depigmented patches; its exact cause is unknown, though an autoimmune process may be involved. Sun tanning is acquired darkening from ultraviolet exposure, and while it is the same UV-driven synthesis increase described here working as intended, excessive exposure can cause sunburn, accelerated skin ageing, and increased skin cancer risk. Langerhans cells' antigen-presenting role is part of why the skin participates in immune reactions such as allergic dermatitis, rather than being a purely passive barrier.

### Common misconceptions
Expecting to identify melanocytes on an ordinary H&E slide. The book is explicit that H&E does not demonstrate them.

Assuming Langerhans cells are joined to keratinocytes like any other epidermal cell. The book states the opposite: no cell junctions exist between them.

Treating albinism and vitiligo as the same disorder because both cause pale skin. Albinism is a genetic enzyme defect present from birth, affecting melanin production everywhere; vitiligo is patchy, acquired, and likely autoimmune, destroying melanocytes that were previously pigmenting normally.

Confusing the Merkel cell-neurite complex with a generic free nerve ending. It is a specific, named, disc-shaped synapse-like structure beneath one particular cell.

## summary
A hair follicle is an epidermal downgrowth that both makes the hair it holds and stores the stem cells that can rebuild the epidermis after a wound; the arrector pili muscle attached to it converts a sympathetic nervous signal into the small, involuntary, familiar mechanical event of goose skin.

## sections
### Definition
During development, cells of the epidermis grow down into the dermis to form the skin appendages: hair follicles and hairs, sebaceous glands, and sweat glands. Hair is a keratinised epithelial thread embedded in a hair follicle, an epidermal sheath from which it develops. The arrector pili muscle is a bundle of smooth muscle fibres extending from the mid shaft of the hair follicle to the papillary layer of the dermis.

### Mechanism
The hair follicle is not only a growth structure for the hair it contains — it is also a stem-cell reservoir for the epidermis at large. Epidermal stem cells, undifferentiated epithelial cells residing in the follicle, do not contribute to the population of basal stem cells of the epidermis under ordinary conditions; instead their normal job is driving the growth of hair follicles and sebaceous glands specifically. That division of labour changes the moment the epidermis is actually injured or lost: the same follicular stem cells then migrate toward the wound surface and take part in the initial healing, a reserve capacity that only activates under damage.

The arrector pili muscle turns a nervous signal into a mechanical one. In fear or cold, sympathetic stimulation contracts the muscle, and because it runs from the follicle's mid shaft to the papillary dermis at an angle, that contraction erects the hair, depresses the skin directly over the muscle, and elevates the skin around the hair — goose skin. Being smooth muscle under sympathetic (not somatic) control is exactly why the response is involuntary: a person cannot simply choose to raise their own hair the way they flex a skeletal muscle.

### Key determinants
**Skin appendages, origin.** All three (hair follicles/hairs, sebaceous glands, sweat glands) grow down from the epidermis into the dermis during development.

**Hair and the follicle.** Hair: keratinised epithelial thread. Follicle: the epidermal sheath the hair develops from.

**Epidermal stem cells.** Location: reside in the hair follicle. Normal function: grow hair follicles and sebaceous glands; do not feed the epidermis's own basal stem cell pool. Injury function: migrate to the wound surface, participate in initial healing.

**Arrector pili.** Structure: smooth muscle bundle, mid follicle shaft to papillary dermis. Trigger: sympathetic stimulation (fear, cold). Effect: hair erection, skin depression over the muscle, skin elevation around the hair (goose skin). Control: involuntary (smooth muscle, autonomic).

### Clinical significance
The wound-healing role of hair-follicle epidermal stem cells is one reason a wound that spares hair follicles (a superficial abrasion, for instance) tends to re-epithelialise faster than one that destroys the dermis and its follicles down to a depth from which no follicular stem cells remain — the follicles are not just cosmetic structures but a redundant source of epidermal regeneration the skin can call on beyond its basal layer alone.

The goose-skin response ties this article back to the rest of the Integumentary System chapter in one movement: sympathetic stimulation contracts the arrector pili, which depresses the skin directly over the muscle and elevates it around the hair, at the exact site where the muscle inserts into the papillary layer of the dermis described in the dermis article — the same papillary layer whose looser, more cellular connective tissue is what lets the skin surface actually move visibly when the muscle beneath it contracts.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming hair-follicle epidermal stem cells routinely top up the epidermis's basal stem cells. The book states the opposite: under normal conditions they do not contribute to that population at all, and only step in specifically after injury.

Expecting the arrector pili to be striated muscle because the hair visibly stands up quickly and can be triggered by emotion. It is smooth muscle, and its sympathetic (not somatic motor) control is exactly why the response is involuntary.

## summary
A sebaceous gland makes its secretion by destroying its own secretory cells, and every part of its structure exists to support that unusual arrangement: a basal layer that keeps dividing to replace what is about to be sacrificed, and a short wide duct built only to let the resulting mixture of oil and cell debris out.

## sections
### Definition
Sebaceous glands develop as outgrowths of the external sheath of the hair follicle. They are simple alveolar (acinar) or branched alveolar exocrine glands, sited in the dermis of thin skin, usually associated with hairs and rarely without them, as in the eyelids.

### Mechanism
The gland's structure is built entirely around a secretory process that consumes the secreting cell itself. Each alveolus is lined by two cell populations doing two sequential jobs. Basal, flattened germinal cells sit at the periphery and do nothing but divide, producing the large polyhedral cells that make up the rest of the alveolus. Those large polyhedral cells then gradually fill with numerous lipid droplets as they move inward, away from the basal layer and its blood supply, progressively committing to a one-way process. That process ends in holocrine secretion: the cell undergoes programmed cell death (apoptosis), and both the lipid secretory product and the cell's own debris are discharged together through the gland's short duct — there is no separate step where the product alone is exported and the cell survives, the way a merocrine gland works. Because every secretory cycle destroys the cells that produced it, the basal germinal layer's continuous mitosis is not incidental housekeeping; it is the only thing that keeps the gland able to secrete again.

The duct itself is built for a job that requires no elaborate segments: short and wide, opening into the upper third of the hair follicle, lined by stratified squamous epithelium continuous with the follicle's own lining — the sebum simply has to travel a short distance from the alveolus to the follicle lumen and then to the skin surface.

### Key determinants
**Type and development.** Simple or branched alveolar (acinar) exocrine gland; develops as an outgrowth of the hair follicle's external sheath.

**Site.** Dermis of thin skin; usually with hairs, rarely without (eyelids).

**Secretory part.** Basal flattened germinal cells (mitosis, produce the next layer) and large polyhedral vacuolated cells (progressively fill with lipid).

**Mode of secretion.** Holocrine: the whole cell dies (apoptosis) and both product and debris are discharged together.

**Duct.** Short, wide, opens into the upper third of the hair follicle, lined by stratified squamous epithelium continuous with the follicle.

**Function.** Keeps thin skin and hair soft, prevents cracking; antifungal and antibacterial.

### Clinical significance
Acne is inflammation of a sebaceous gland caused by obstruction of its duct by sebum — a whitehead. If the plugged sebum mixes with melanin, the lesion is called a blackhead. Both are direct, mechanical consequences of the duct's own narrow, purpose-built anatomy being blocked, not a separate disease process layered on top of it.

Sebum's two stated functions — keeping thin skin and its hairs soft, and acting as an antifungal and antibacterial agent — both depend on the gland continuing to secrete normally. A blocked duct removes both protective effects at exactly the site of the blockage at the same time it creates the mechanical problem of trapped sebum, which is part of why an obstructed follicle is prone to becoming inflamed rather than simply backing up harmlessly.

### Common misconceptions
Calling sebaceous secretion merocrine or apocrine by analogy with sweat glands. It is holocrine: the entire secretory cell dies and becomes part of the output, which is why continuous basal-layer mitosis is structurally necessary here in a way it is not for a merocrine gland.

Assuming sebaceous glands exist in thick skin. The book places them in thin skin only, consistent with thick skin (palms, soles) lacking hair follicles, sebaceous glands and arrector pili altogether.

Treating whitehead and blackhead as different diseases rather than the same obstruction with and without melanin mixed in.

## summary
Sweat glands come in two designs built for two different jobs: eccrine glands, everywhere but the palm-and-sole-heavy, all-purpose cooling system present from birth; and apocrine glands, a smaller, puberty-onset population confined to a few sites, built to produce a scent-carrying secretion rather than to regulate temperature.

## sections
### Definition
Sweat glands are simple tubular coiled glands, sited deep in the dermis all over the body except the glans penis and nail beds. Eccrine glands lie all over that same distribution, are more numerous, and predominate in thick skin. Apocrine glands occur only in the thin skin of the axillary, pubic and perineal regions, are less numerous, are absent from thick skin, and only begin functioning at puberty.

### Mechanism
The two gland types differ in almost every structural detail, and each difference tracks back to a different job. Eccrine glands are small, with a narrow lumen, and their secretory part has three cell types: large clear cells, the more numerous type, broad at the base and narrow at the apex, pale from glycogen, joined to each other by intercellular canaliculi; small dark cells, less numerous, narrow at the base and wide at the apex, with dark glycoprotein-containing granules; and myoepithelial cells. The watery secretion made by the clear cells passes through the canaliculi to the lumen, where it mixes with the dark cells' protein product — two cell types cooperating to build one secretion. The resulting fluid is clear, watery, low in protein, made of water, sodium chloride, urea and ammonia, and its whole purpose is body temperature regulation: a gland built for continuous, high-volume, low-cost output. Its duct spirals through both dermis and epidermis and opens directly onto the skin surface, lined by two cuboidal cell layers — the shortest possible route from gland to evaporating surface.

Apocrine glands are large, with a wide lumen, and their secretory part has only two cell types: simple cuboidal cells, with eosinophilic cytoplasm and apical granules discharged by exocytosis, and myoepithelial cells. Their secretion is viscous and initially odourless, becoming offensive only through bacterial action afterward — a scent-precursor rather than a finished scent. Rather than opening directly onto the skin, the apocrine duct spirals through the dermis and opens into a hair follicle instead, lined by two cuboidal cell layers, and the gland does not begin functioning until puberty — consistent with a role in scent signalling rather than in the survival-critical, present-from-birth job of temperature regulation that eccrine glands do.

Despite the historical names, both glands are described by the book under the same "mode of secretion" heading: merocrine, by exocytosis. The apocrine gland's traditional name implies a different mechanism (cytoplasm pinched off with the secretion), but the department book's own account of both glands places their actual output mode in the same category.

### Key determinants
**Sites.** Eccrine: all over the body except glans penis/nail beds, more numerous, predominate in thick skin. Apocrine: thin skin of axilla, pubis, perineum only; less numerous; absent from thick skin; functional only from puberty.

**Secretory part.** Eccrine: small, narrow lumen, three cell types (clear, dark, myoepithelial). Apocrine: large, wide lumen, two cell types (simple cuboidal, myoepithelial).

**Secretion.** Eccrine: clear, watery, low protein (water, NaCl, urea, ammonia); function is temperature regulation. Apocrine: viscous, initially odourless, becomes offensive through bacterial action.

**Mode of secretion.** Both: merocrine, by exocytosis (despite the name "apocrine").

**Excretory duct.** Eccrine: spirals through dermis and epidermis, opens onto the epidermis, two cuboidal layers. Apocrine: spirals through the dermis, opens into a hair follicle, two cuboidal layers.

### Clinical significance
Because eccrine sweat is the body's main evaporative cooling mechanism, its distribution — dense, present from birth, and thickest in thick skin — is what makes heat regulation possible across almost the entire body surface, while apocrine glands, confined to a few sites and inactive until puberty, play essentially no role in that thermoregulatory function; their delayed onset and restricted distribution are both consistent with a scent-related rather than a survival-critical purpose.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming "apocrine" describes the actual secretion mechanism. The book's own mode-of-secretion heading places both gland types under merocrine (exocytosis); the name is historical rather than mechanistic.

Placing apocrine glands in thick skin. The book states they are absent from it altogether — only eccrine glands are found there, and they predominate there.

Sending apocrine secretion directly onto the skin surface. It opens into a hair follicle, not onto the epidermis; only the eccrine duct opens directly onto the skin.

Assuming apocrine glands are functional from birth like eccrine glands. They begin functioning only at puberty.
