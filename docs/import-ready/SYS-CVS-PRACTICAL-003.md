### Where the missing volume came from
Ashraf, 22, donates 450 ml of blood at a university drive. He stands up as soon as the needle is out, feels hot and sick, and faints. He is lying on a couch, alert, pulse 96 and regular, blood pressure 104/68.
Concept: CON-CVS-5692E7C9DCE9F5
Difficulty: Easy
Q: 450 ml is about 9% of his blood volume. Which vascular compartment held most of the blood he has just lost?
*= The systemic veins, which hold most of the blood volume at any moment
Why: Correct. The systemic veins are the main reservoir, so a withdrawal is drawn predominantly from venous capacitance rather than from the arterial side.
* The systemic arteries, because they carry blood under the highest pressure
Why: Chosen by students who equate pressure with volume. The arteries are the high-pressure conduit, but they contain a small fraction of the total volume.
* The pulmonary circulation, which acts as the body's blood store
Why: The misconception that the lungs are a reservoir. The pulmonary circulation holds a modest, fairly fixed volume and is not the main capacitance bed.
* The capillaries, because they are by far the most numerous vessels
Why: Number is confused with capacity. Capillaries are numerous but individually tiny, and hold only a small share of the total volume.
Rationale: Most of the circulating volume sits in the systemic veins, which is why they are called the capacitance vessels. Understanding where a bleed comes from is what makes the compensations that follow predictable.

### Why his pressure has not fallen further
Concept: CON-CVS-1E8F1FB07C77D0
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Twenty minutes after the donation his haematocrit has fallen slightly, although he has been given nothing to drink. What explains the fall?
*= Reduced capillary hydrostatic pressure has drawn interstitial fluid into the circulation
Why: Correct. Losing volume lowers capillary hydrostatic pressure, so the Starling balance shifts towards absorption and interstitial fluid dilutes the remaining blood.
* The bone marrow has begun to release new red cells
Why: The student who reaches for the marrow because the loss was of red cells. Marrow responses take days, not minutes, and would raise rather than dilute the cell fraction.
* Splenic contraction has released stored plasma into the circulation
Why: Confuses the human spleen with that of species in which it is a genuine red-cell reservoir. In humans this contributes very little.
* The kidney has already retained sodium and water in response to the loss
Why: The renal response is real but slow, and cannot account for a measurable change within twenty minutes.
Rationale: Autotransfusion is the earliest volume defence: the fluid comes from the interstitium, across the capillary wall, within minutes. It dilutes the blood, which is why an early haematocrit understates the size of an acute bleed.

### Which reflex failed when he stood
Concept: CON-CVS-A302CAFBD5028C
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: He was well while lying down and fainted within seconds of standing. Which receptors should have prevented that, and where are they?
*= Arterial baroreceptors, stretch receptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch
Why: Correct. They sense the fall in arterial pressure on standing and drive the reflex increase in rate and vasoconstriction that normally defends cerebral perfusion.
* Peripheral chemoreceptors in the carotid and aortic bodies
Why: Picked by students who remember that something important sits at the carotid bifurcation. Chemoreceptors respond to oxygen, carbon dioxide and pH, not to a change in stretch.
* Atrial volume receptors, which sense filling and adjust urine output
Why: These exist and do respond to volume, but their output is a slow fluid-balance response, not the second-by-second correction standing requires.
* Muscle spindles in the calf, which trigger the venous pump on standing
Why: Confuses the skeletal muscle pump, which is a mechanical aid to venous return, with a pressure-sensing reflex.
Rationale: Standing shifts blood into the compliant leg veins. The arterial baroreceptors are the fast sensors of the resulting fall in pressure. When volume is already low, the correction they drive may be insufficient, and the patient faints.

### Why he is thirsty
Concept: CON-CVS-5201719B770125
Difficulty: Moderate
He tells you he is unusually thirsty, more than the missed breakfast would explain.
Q: What is driving the thirst?
*= Angiotensin II and reduced baroreceptor discharge acting on the thirst mechanism
Why: Correct. Both arms of the volume response reach the thirst centres: angiotensin II generated by the volume loss, and the fall in inhibitory baroreceptor traffic.
* A rise in plasma sodium concentration from the loss of whole blood
Why: The osmotic route to thirst is real, but whole blood is lost isotonically, so sodium concentration does not rise. This catches students who assume all thirst is osmotic.
* Dryness of the oral mucosa after several hours without drinking
Why: Local dryness contributes to the sensation but is not the mechanism the volume loss recruits, and would not be the answer being tested here.
* Increased antidiuretic hormone acting directly on the salivary glands
Why: Antidiuretic hormone does rise, but it acts on the renal collecting duct; the thirst it accompanies is centrally mediated, not salivary.
Rationale: Thirst is part of the volume response and not an incidental symptom. Recognising it as a sign of hypovolaemia rather than of a dry mouth changes how seriously a post-donation faint is taken.

### Why the compensation is incomplete
Concept: CON-CVS-59DE21D055FA34
Difficulty: Hard
Q: His mean systemic filling pressure has fallen less than the 9% volume loss alone would predict. Which venous property explains that?
*= Reverse stress relaxation, in which the veins slowly narrow around the reduced volume
Why: Correct. Vascular smooth muscle gradually takes up the slack after a volume loss, so the pressure filling the system falls less than the volume change alone would give.
* The high compliance of the veins, which lets pressure fall very little for a large volume change
Why: Compliance is genuinely high, but on its own it predicts a *smaller* pressure change for any volume change in either direction. It does not describe the slow, active narrowing this question is about, and it is not a compensation.
* Arteriolar constriction raising total peripheral resistance
Why: This does occur and defends arterial pressure, but resistance is downstream of the filling pressure and does not determine it.
* The skeletal muscle pump returning blood from the legs
Why: The muscle pump aids venous return moment to moment; it does not alter the pressure that would fill the system at zero flow.
Rationale: Stress relaxation and its reverse are how the venous reservoir resizes itself. After a loss, the veins narrow slowly around the remaining volume, which limits the fall in filling pressure, venous return, cardiac output and arterial pressure. It is why a young donor can look almost normal after losing nearly a tenth of his blood.
## debrief
The case follows one 450 ml loss through four defences, in the order they act: the venous reservoir it came out of, the interstitial fluid drawn in within minutes, the baroreflex that acts within seconds of standing, and the slow narrowing of the veins around the smaller volume. Thirst is the fifth, and the one most easily dismissed as trivial. Each is a different mechanism working on a different timescale, which is why a healthy donor can lose that much and walk out.

### Where the coronary arteries begin
Hany, 54, a builder, has two hours of central crushing chest pain that began while he was carrying cement upstairs. He smokes twenty a day and has treated hypertension.
Concept: CON-CVS-8424E2DDED2365
Difficulty: Easy
Q: The team is about to discuss his coronary circulation. From which vessel do the coronary arteries arise?
*= The ascending aorta, which gives the right and left coronary arteries as its only branches
Why: Correct. The two coronary arteries are the sole branches of the ascending aorta, arising from the aortic sinuses just above the valve.
* The aortic arch, alongside the brachiocephalic and left common carotid arteries
Why: Chosen by students who group the coronaries with the great vessels of the arch. The arch branches supply the head and upper limbs; the coronaries arise before it.
* The pulmonary trunk, which lies immediately anterior to the aortic root
Why: An anatomically important error: an anomalous coronary from the pulmonary trunk is a rare disease, not the normal arrangement.
* The descending thoracic aorta, via branches passing upwards to the heart
Why: Reverses the direction of supply. Nothing ascends from the descending aorta to feed the heart.
Rationale: The coronaries arise from the ascending aorta, in the sinuses just above the valve cusps. Their origin explains why they fill in diastole, when the cusps fall back and the sinuses are pressurised.

### What his last two months of symptoms mean
Concept: CON-CVS-2480077C89FDC3
Difficulty: Easy
For two months he has had a milder version of this pain on climbing stairs, relieved within minutes by rest.
Q: What does that earlier pattern indicate about his coronary arteries?
*= A major branch was narrowed, so supply became inadequate only when demand rose
Why: Correct. Narrowing limits maximum flow. At rest the supply is sufficient; on exertion the demand exceeds it and ischaemia produces pain.
* A branch was already completely occluded, and the muscle had begun to die two months ago
Why: The student who does not separate narrowing from occlusion. Complete occlusion produces pain at rest and infarction, not a symptom that reliably stops with rest.
* The pain came from the chest wall, since it was reproducible with activity
Why: Reproducibility with exertion points to a supply-demand problem, not a musculoskeletal one, which is typically reproducible with movement and palpation instead.
* A coronary artery was in spasm each time he climbed the stairs
Why: Spasm exists, but it characteristically occurs at rest and unpredictably rather than reliably with the same exertion.
Rationale: Narrowing and occlusion produce different clinical patterns from the same vessel. Exertional pain relieved by rest is the signature of a flow-limiting stenosis; prolonged pain at rest is the signature of occlusion.

### Which artery the anterior wall depends on
Concept: CON-CVS-7FEAA20AB5CBD0
Difficulty: Moderate
His ECG shows ST elevation in leads V1 to V4.
Q: Which artery supplies the territory those leads face, and where does it run?
*= The anterior interventricular artery, descending in the anterior interventricular groove towards the apex
Why: Correct. It runs in the anterior interventricular groove with the great cardiac vein, reaching the inferior border just to the right of the apex, and supplies the anterior wall and much of the septum.
* The circumflex artery, running in the left part of the coronary sulcus
Why: The circumflex runs in the coronary sulcus, not the interventricular groove, and supplies the lateral wall — changes would appear in I, aVL, V5 and V6.
* The right coronary artery, running in the right coronary sulcus
Why: Picked by students who default to the right coronary artery for any infarct. It supplies the inferior wall in most people, giving changes in II, III and aVF.
* The posterior interventricular artery, in the posterior interventricular groove
Why: This does supply septum, but the posterior third and the inferior wall, and it faces away from the anterior chest leads.
Rationale: Territory is read backwards from the leads to the groove the artery runs in. V1 to V4 face the anterior wall and septum, which is the anterior interventricular artery's territory.

### What has happened to the muscle
Concept: CON-CVS-1A060C49C5C0C1
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: The artery is now completely occluded. What is the consequence for the myocardium it supplied?
*= Infarction — the muscle in that territory dies unless flow is restored
Why: Correct. Complete occlusion of an artery or a major branch removes the supply entirely, and the dependent myocardium infarcts.
* Reversible ischaemia only, because collateral vessels take over immediately
Why: Collaterals exist and may limit the damage, but they do not open instantly in a previously well-perfused heart. This is the misconception that the heart is fully redundant.
* Stunning without cell death, since cardiac muscle tolerates ischaemia indefinitely
Why: The opposite of the truth. Cardiac muscle is highly aerobic and tolerates ischaemia poorly.
* Hypertrophy of the affected segment as it works harder to compensate
Why: Confuses the chronic response of a pressure-loaded ventricle with the acute response to no blood supply at all.
Rationale: Occlusion of a coronary artery or a major branch causes infarction with death of the myocardium it supplied. The distinction from the earlier narrowing is not academic: it changes the timescale from minutes of pain to permanent loss of muscle.

### Reading the lateral leads
Concept: CON-CVS-63D0C4219C40EA
Difficulty: Moderate
The consultant asks about the ST changes that also appear in leads I and aVL.
Q: Which artery would explain lateral involvement, and how does it run?
*= The circumflex artery, running in the coronary sulcus around the left border towards the back of the heart
Why: Correct. The circumflex travels in the anterior and then posterior coronary sulcus, ending by anastomosing with the right coronary artery, and supplies the lateral wall.
* The right marginal artery, running along the acute margin of the heart
Why: The right marginal artery does run along a border, but the right one, and it supplies the right ventricle.
* The anterior interventricular artery, extended beyond its usual territory
Why: A wrap-around anterior interventricular artery can supply part of the apex and inferior wall, but the lateral wall is the circumflex's territory.
* The posterior interventricular artery, arising from whichever vessel is dominant
Why: Confuses the vessel that defines dominance with the one supplying the lateral wall.
Rationale: Two grooves, two systems: the interventricular grooves carry the arteries to the septum and the anterior or inferior walls, and the coronary sulcus carries the circumflex and the right coronary artery around the heart. Reading a territory means naming the groove.
## debrief
The case moves in one direction throughout: from the lead on the ECG, to the wall of the heart that lead faces, to the groove the artery runs in. Two months of exertional pain and two hours of rest pain are the same anatomy at two different degrees of obstruction. If you can name the groove, you can name the artery.

### Why the heart beats at all
Mrs Wafaa Sherif, 78, is brought in after collapsing in the street. She is alert now. Her pulse is 38 and regular, blood pressure 96/60.
Concept: CON-CVS-C9E53B5A691D19
Difficulty: Easy
Q: Her heart is beating without any nervous input reaching it. Which property of the pacemaker cells accounts for that?
*= Automaticity and rhythmicity — they generate spontaneous, regular action potentials
Why: Correct. Pacemaker cells depolarise spontaneously to threshold and do so at regular intervals, so the heart beats without external command.
* Conductivity, the ability to pass an impulse from cell to cell
Why: A real property of cardiac tissue, and the one that spreads the beat, but it explains propagation rather than initiation.
* Excitability, the ability to respond to a stimulus by depolarising
Why: Chosen by students who conflate responding to a stimulus with generating one. Excitability requires something to excite the cell.
* Contractility, the ability to shorten when activated
Why: The mechanical response that follows the electrical event, and not what starts it.
Rationale: Automaticity means the cell reaches threshold by itself; rhythmicity means it does so regularly. Together they are why a denervated or transplanted heart still beats.

### What has gone wrong
Concept: CON-CVS-F48CFC6D7458A2
Difficulty: Moderate
Her ECG shows P waves at 84 per minute and QRS complexes at 38 per minute, with no fixed relationship between them.
Q: What does that pattern represent?
*= Atrioventricular block — conduction from atria to ventricles through the conducting system is impaired
Why: Correct. Atrial and ventricular rates are independent, which means the impulse is not getting from one to the other.
* Sinus bradycardia, with the sinoatrial node firing slowly
Why: The student who sees a slow ventricular rate and stops there. The atrial rate here is 84, so the sinoatrial node is not the problem.
* Atrial fibrillation with a slow ventricular response
Why: Atrial fibrillation has no discrete, consistently shaped P waves. This trace has regular P waves at a normal rate.
* A ventricular escape rhythm caused by an infarct of the sinoatrial node
Why: The escape rhythm is real, but it is the consequence of the block rather than the diagnosis, and the sinoatrial node is plainly still working at 84.
Rationale: Two independent rates, atrial normal and ventricular slow, is atrioventricular block. Naming the level of failure — rather than the slow rate — is what determines what happens next.

### What the ECG shows in lesser degrees
Concept: CON-CVS-FAC483F74DFA04
Difficulty: Moderate
Her old ECG from last year is retrieved.
Q: What would you expect a lesser degree of the same problem to have shown on that earlier trace?
*= A prolonged PR interval, or occasional P waves not followed by a QRS complex
Why: Correct. Impaired atrioventricular conduction shows first as delay, and then as intermittent failure of individual impulses to reach the ventricles.
* A widened QRS complex with a normal PR interval
Why: A wide QRS indicates slow conduction within the ventricles rather than block between atria and ventricles. This catches students who read any conduction problem as a bundle branch problem.
* Absent P waves with an irregular ventricular rhythm
Why: That describes atrial fibrillation, a disorder of atrial activation, not of atrioventricular conduction.
* ST-segment elevation in the inferior leads
Why: Confuses a cause with the finding. An inferior infarct can cause block, but the ST change is not the manifestation of block.
Rationale: Atrioventricular block is a spectrum, and the ECG shows it as either a longer PR interval or P waves that are not conducted. Looking for those two things on an old trace is how the problem is dated.

### Why the ventricles do not simply follow the atria
Concept: CON-CVS-A6482415169BD3
Difficulty: Hard
Q: The atria are contracting perfectly well at 84. Why can that activity not simply spread across into the ventricular muscle?
*= The fibrous skeleton electrically insulates atrial from ventricular myocardium, leaving the conducting system as the only route
Why: Correct. The fibrous rings are non-conducting, so if the atrioventricular conduction pathway fails there is no alternative path for the impulse.
* The atrial and ventricular myocytes have different resting membrane potentials, so current cannot pass
Why: Both have similar resting potentials, and in any case a difference in potential would favour, not prevent, current flow. This catches students who reach for an electrophysiological explanation of an anatomical fact.
* The coronary sulcus contains the coronary vessels, which physically separate the chambers
Why: The vessels lie in the sulcus, but they are not what blocks conduction; the fibrous rings are.
* The atrioventricular valves close during systole and interrupt the electrical pathway
Why: Confuses a mechanical event during the cycle with a permanent structural property. Conduction does not depend on whether a valve is open.
Rationale: The insulation is what makes the delay possible and coordinated filling possible. It is also why block is so consequential: with one route and no alternative, losing it leaves the ventricles to rely on their own slow escape rhythm.

### Where the only bridge runs
Concept: CON-CVS-CF48F93FFC8A7A
Difficulty: Challenging
The cardiologist says the level of the block will determine how reliable her escape rhythm is.
Q: What is the anatomical course of the single conducting bridge across the insulation, and why does its course matter here?
*= The atrioventricular bundle passes from the atrioventricular node through the fibrous skeleton to reach the interventricular septum, so a lesion anywhere along that short course leaves the ventricles dependent on a distal escape
Why: Correct. The bundle penetrates the fibrous skeleton and continues onto the septum. The further down the course the interruption lies, the more distal — and slower and less reliable — the escape pacemaker must be.
* The bundle runs in the coronary sulcus alongside the circumflex artery before entering the septum, so a circumflex occlusion always causes block
Why: Confuses the conducting pathway with a vessel's course. Ischaemia can cause block, but the bundle does not travel in the coronary sulcus.
* Several accessory bundles cross the fibrous skeleton in every heart, so a block at one point is always bypassed
Why: The misconception that redundancy is normal. An accessory pathway is an abnormality found in a minority of hearts, not a routine second route.
* The bundle runs within the free wall of the left ventricle, so escape rhythms arise near the apex and are fast
Why: Misplaces the bundle and inverts the rule. Distal escape pacemakers are slower and less dependable, not faster.
Rationale: One bundle crosses one insulating plate. Everything about atrioventricular block — that it happens at all, that its level matters, and that a low block gives a slow and fragile escape — follows from that single piece of anatomy.
## debrief
The case is built on one structural fact: the fibrous skeleton separates the atria from the ventricles electrically, and only the atrioventricular bundle crosses it. Automaticity explains why the ventricles beat at all once the bridge fails; the level of the interruption explains how fast and how reliable that rescue rhythm will be. Reading the atrial rate before the ventricular rate is what keeps a slow pulse from being called sinus bradycardia.

### Naming the sensation
Nada, 24, a pharmacy student, describes her heart missing a beat and then giving one hard thump. Episodes last a second or two, happen at rest, and settle when she exercises. She drinks five cups of coffee a day in exam season.
Concept: CON-CVS-3909BF5F3376A2
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What is the beat she feels as a "trip" most likely to be?
*= A premature beat — an extra beat arising earlier than the next expected one
Why: Correct. An impulse arising early produces a beat out of sequence, and the pause that follows it is what the patient perceives as the missed beat.
* A dropped beat, in which the heart fails to contract at all
Why: The patient's own words, taken literally. What she perceives as a gap is the compensatory pause after an early beat, not an absent one.
* A run of tachycardia lasting one or two seconds
Why: Chosen by students who assume all palpitation is tachycardia. She has checked her pulse during an episode and it is not fast.
* A pause caused by intermittent failure of atrioventricular conduction
Why: Would produce a pause without a preceding early beat, and would not explain the forceful beat she describes afterwards.
Rationale: A premature beat is an extra beat, not a missing one. The sequence the patient reports — trip, gap, thump — is exactly the sequence an early beat, a compensatory pause and a well-filled beat produce.

### Why the next beat feels so strong
Concept: CON-CVS-D50119DD90A9D7
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: The beat after the pause is the one she notices most. Why is it more forceful?
*= The longer pause allowed more passive filling, so the ventricle ejects a larger stroke volume
Why: Correct. Most resting filling is passive and takes time; a longer interval before the next beat means a larger end-diastolic volume and a more forceful ejection.
* Adrenaline released by the fright of the missed beat strengthens that single contraction
Why: A plausible-sounding but wrongly timed explanation: a circulating catecholamine response could not be confined to the very next beat.
* The premature beat itself is the forceful one, felt a moment late
Why: The reverse of what happens. The premature beat comes early on a poorly filled ventricle and is typically weak, sometimes not felt at the wrist at all.
* Atrial contraction is stronger after a pause and does most of the filling
Why: Atrial contraction contributes about a fifth of resting filling. It is the passive component, which has more time, that accounts for the larger volume.
Rationale: At rest about four-fifths of ventricular filling happens passively before the atrium contracts. Anything that lengthens diastole increases that passive contribution, and the next beat ejects more.

### What makes a cell fire early
Concept: CON-CVS-8AC3AE83A9DFA9
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: An early impulse can arise from an abnormal depolarisation that follows the preceding action potential. What is that called?
*= An afterdepolarisation
Why: Correct. An afterdepolarisation is an abnormal depolarisation triggered by a preceding action potential, and if it reaches threshold it starts a new one.
* A pacemaker potential
Why: The normal slow diastolic depolarisation of a pacemaker cell. It is the mechanism of normal automaticity, not of an abnormal early beat.
* A refractory potential
Why: Not a real entity. Students reach for it because refractoriness is the property that normally prevents an early beat.
* A threshold potential
Why: The membrane voltage at which an action potential fires, not a depolarisation in its own right.
Rationale: Afterdepolarisations are triggered activity: they depend on a preceding beat, which is what separates them from the spontaneous automaticity of a pacemaker cell.

### Which kind, and when
Concept: CON-CVS-B4232A29680959
Difficulty: Moderate
Her episodes cluster when she is sitting still after several strong coffees.
Q: A depolarisation arising after repolarisation is complete, driven by calcium overload, is which type — and what does it do if it reaches threshold?
*= A delayed afterdepolarisation, which activates inward current and can trigger an action potential
Why: Correct. It occurs after repolarisation, is favoured by intracellular calcium loading, and triggers a full action potential once threshold is reached.
* An early afterdepolarisation, occurring after repolarisation is complete
Why: Contradicts itself. Early afterdepolarisations interrupt repolarisation rather than following its completion.
* A delayed afterdepolarisation, which cannot trigger a propagated beat
Why: Half right, and the half that is wrong matters: reaching threshold is exactly what allows it to trigger an action potential that spreads.
* A subthreshold oscillation, which never produces a clinical beat
Why: Below threshold nothing propagates — but that is the case in which no premature beat occurs, which is not what she is describing.
Rationale: Delayed afterdepolarisations follow the action potential and are favoured by calcium loading, including by adrenergic drive. Reaching threshold is the step that converts an abnormality of membrane voltage into a beat the patient feels.

### Why exercise settles it
Concept: CON-CVS-8452EED3F9CFB2
Difficulty: Hard
Q: She notices the symptom least when she is exercising, even though exercise raises circulating adrenaline. What best explains that?
*= Circulating catecholamines raise the sinoatrial firing rate, so the faster sinus rhythm outpaces the ectopic focus and leaves no gap for an early beat
Why: Correct. Catecholamines increase sinoatrial firing by the same mechanisms as sympathetic stimulation. A faster sinus rate captures the ventricle before an ectopic impulse can arise, a phenomenon known as overdrive suppression.
* Exercise lowers circulating catecholamine concentrations, removing the trigger
Why: The opposite of what happens. Catecholamines rise with exercise.
* The ectopic focus is destroyed by the increased coronary flow of exercise
Why: An implausible mechanism. Increased flow does not eliminate a focus of triggered activity.
* Her attention is occupied during exercise, so the beats occur but are not perceived
Why: Perception genuinely does vary with attention, and this is a real confounder — but it does not explain the electrophysiology, and the question asks what best explains the finding.
Rationale: The paradox resolves once you separate the trigger from the rhythm competing with it. Catecholamines make ectopic activity more likely *and* raise the sinus rate; at exercise the faster sinus rhythm wins, and the ectopic beats have no window in which to arise.
## debrief
Three separate ideas produce one symptom: the early beat itself, the pause that follows it, and the well-filled beat after the pause. Get the sequence right and the patient's description becomes diagnostic. The apparent paradox at the end — that exercise, which raises adrenaline, makes it better — is what separates a student who has memorised the trigger from one who has understood that two rhythms are competing.
