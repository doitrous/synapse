### The volume left behind
Concept: CON-RES-37824D44CE9505
Difficulty: Easy
Q: A spirometry trace shows quiet tidal breathing. What is the functional residual capacity?
*= The volume remaining in the lungs after a normal expiration
Why: Correct. Functional residual capacity is what is left at the end of a quiet breath out, before the next breath begins.
* The volume remaining after a maximal forced expiration
Why: That is the residual volume, which is smaller and is one component of functional residual capacity.
* The volume moved during one quiet breath
Why: That is the tidal volume, which is what the trace's oscillation shows rather than the level it oscillates around.
* The maximum volume the lungs can hold
Why: That is total lung capacity, reached after a maximal inspiration.
Explanation: Functional residual capacity is the resting position of the respiratory system — the volume the lungs return to when inspiratory and expiratory recoil balance.

### The extra that can still be pushed out
Concept: CON-RES-8D83124FD3487C
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: From the end of a normal expiration, the subject blows out as hard as possible. What has been measured?
*= The expiratory reserve volume
Why: Correct. Expiratory reserve volume is the additional volume that can be forced out beyond a normal expiration.
* The residual volume
Why: Residual volume is what remains *after* that forced effort and cannot be exhaled at all.
* The vital capacity
Why: Vital capacity is measured from maximal inspiration to maximal expiration, not from the resting end-expiratory level.
* The inspiratory reserve volume
Why: That is measured in the opposite direction, from the top of a normal inspiration.
Explanation: Reserve volumes are defined by the direction of the effort and the point it starts from. Naming the starting point is what separates them.

### Building a capacity from volumes
Concept: CON-RES-4C74B927892586
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: How is inspiratory capacity composed?
*= Tidal volume plus inspiratory reserve volume
Why: Correct. Inspiratory capacity is everything that can be inspired from the resting end-expiratory level: the normal tidal breath plus the reserve above it.
* Inspiratory reserve volume plus residual volume
Why: Residual volume lies at the far end of the scale and takes no part in an inspiratory measurement.
* Vital capacity minus tidal volume
Why: Vital capacity minus tidal volume leaves the two reserve volumes combined, not the inspiratory capacity.
* Total lung capacity minus residual volume
Why: That is the definition of vital capacity.
Explanation: A capacity is a sum of volumes. Building each from its components, rather than memorising them, is what makes an unfamiliar one derivable in the exam.

### The other sum
Concept: CON-RES-F6F63B084F0575
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: How is functional residual capacity composed?
*= Expiratory reserve volume plus residual volume
Why: Correct. What remains after a quiet expiration is the volume that could still be forced out, plus the volume that could not.
* Tidal volume plus expiratory reserve volume
Why: Tidal volume has already left the lungs at the point functional residual capacity is defined.
* Residual volume plus inspiratory reserve volume
Why: Combines volumes from opposite ends of the scale, which corresponds to nothing measurable.
* Vital capacity minus inspiratory capacity
Why: That leaves the expiratory reserve volume alone, omitting the residual volume.
Explanation: Functional residual capacity is the volume the lungs sit at between breaths, and it contains a part that can be exhaled and a part that cannot — which is why spirometry alone cannot measure it.

### What the spirometer cannot see
Concept: CON-RES-DFDD451616A515
Difficulty: Hard
Q: Why can functional residual capacity not be measured by spirometry?
*= Because it contains the residual volume, which cannot be exhaled and so never passes through the spirometer
Why: Correct. A spirometer measures gas that moves. Any capacity containing residual volume is therefore beyond it, however hard the subject blows.
* Because the measurement takes too long for a subject to tolerate
Why: A practical objection rather than the reason in principle, and it is not why the measurement fails.
* Because functional residual capacity varies too much between breaths to be measured
Why: It is a stable, reproducible quantity; the obstacle is that part of it never leaves the lungs.
* Because spirometers measure flow rather than volume
Why: Spirometers report volume, and volume-time and flow-volume displays are both derived from the same movement of gas.
Explanation: The rule generalises: spirometry can measure any volume or capacity that moves, and none that includes the residual volume. That is why functional residual capacity and total lung capacity both need a different technique.

### The technique that reaches it
Concept: CON-RES-787C8F7B4F09DD
Difficulty: Easy
Q: By which technique is functional residual capacity measured?
*= Helium dilution
Why: Correct. A known quantity of helium is diluted into the gas already in the lungs, and the resulting concentration gives the volume it was diluted into.
* Forced expiration into a spirometer
Why: The method that cannot reach it, because the residual volume never leaves the lungs.
* Peak expiratory flow measurement
Why: Peak flow reports the maximum flow rate achieved, not a volume.
* Measuring the tidal volume over one minute
Why: That gives minute ventilation, a flow of gas per unit time rather than a resting lung volume.
Explanation: An indicator that mixes with the gas already present can measure a volume that never comes out — which is exactly the problem spirometry cannot solve.

### The reason both are out of reach
Concept: CON-RES-E458F6E546BAF6
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Why can neither functional residual capacity nor total lung capacity be obtained by spirometry?
*= Because forced expiration cannot empty the lungs completely, so both include gas that never passes the spirometer
Why: Correct. Both capacities contain the residual volume, and no expiratory effort can expel it.
* Because both are too large to be contained by a standard spirometer
Why: A mechanical limit that modern equipment does not have, and not the reason in principle.
* Because both change continuously with posture, so no stable value exists
Why: Posture does alter them, which is why measurement conditions are standardised — but they are measurable, just not by spirometry.
* Because both are calculated rather than measured quantities
Why: They can be measured directly by dilution or plethysmography; the obstacle is specific to spirometry.
Explanation: One reason covers both: any capacity containing the residual volume is beyond a device that only sees gas in motion.

### What the spirometer is good for
Concept: CON-RES-E05C22F3A83BFB
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Which measurements are forced-expiratory tests?
*= Forced vital capacity, timed forced vital capacity, and maximum voluntary ventilation
Why: Correct. These are the forced tests: the total volume forced out, the fraction of it delivered in a set time, and the maximum ventilation sustained over a short period.
* Functional residual capacity, residual volume and total lung capacity
Why: These are precisely the quantities forced expiration cannot reach.
* Tidal volume, respiratory rate and minute ventilation
Why: These are quiet-breathing measurements requiring no forced effort.
* Peak flow, diffusing capacity and arterial oxygen saturation
Why: A mixture of a flow measurement, a gas-transfer measurement and an oximetric one, none of which is a forced-expiratory volume test.
Explanation: The forced tests measure how much can be moved and how fast, which is why they are the ones that separate obstruction from restriction.

### Why the resting volume matters
Concept: CON-RES-697CABD8585158
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What does the functional residual capacity do for gas exchange between breaths?
*= It holds a reservoir of gas that continues to aerate blood, limiting swings in blood oxygen and carbon dioxide
Why: Correct. Because the lungs are never empty, exchange continues during the expiratory pause, and arterial gases stay comparatively steady between breaths.
* It prevents the alveoli from collapsing by holding them open mechanically
Why: Maintaining volume does oppose collapse, and surfactant matters here too, but the question asks about its role in gas exchange.
* It allows the lungs to be completely emptied on the next expiration
Why: The opposite: it is the volume that is *not* emptied.
* It has no role in exchange, being reserve volume only
Why: Treats it as inert, which is exactly the misconception — a lung emptying fully between breaths would produce large swings in arterial gases.
Explanation: Breathing is intermittent but gas exchange is continuous, and the functional residual capacity is what makes that possible.

### When the chest wall is the problem
Concept: CON-RES-52BCF311FC9DFA
Difficulty: Hard
Q: A patient with a marked kyphoscoliosis has a reduced vital capacity with normal airways. Why?
*= The deformed chest wall restricts expansion, so the volume that can be moved is reduced even though the airways are normal
Why: Correct. Vital capacity depends on how far the thorax and lungs can expand and empty; a chest-wall deformity limits that mechanically.
* The airways are narrowed by the deformity, raising resistance
Why: This would be an obstructive pattern, and the question specifies normal airways.
* The residual volume is increased, leaving less room for the vital capacity
Why: Air trapping raises residual volume in obstruction; the restriction here is imposed from outside.
* The respiratory muscles are paralysed by the spinal deformity
Why: Kyphoscoliosis restricts by geometry rather than by denervating the muscles, and paralysis would be a different diagnosis.
Explanation: The lung is only as expandable as the box around it. That is why a restrictive pattern can arise from the chest wall, the pleura or the lung tissue, and why the airways can be entirely normal.

### Naming the category
A family is brought in from a flat with a faulty gas heater. All four are headachy and nauseated. Their oxygen saturations read 98% on a pulse oximeter.
Concept: CON-RES-22FEB4F22E7D79
Difficulty: Easy
Q: Hypoxia in which the blood cannot carry enough oxygen, although the lungs and circulation work normally, is called what?
*= Anaemic hypoxia
Why: Correct. Anaemic hypoxia is a failure of oxygen carriage — too little functioning haemoglobin — with normal ventilation and perfusion.
* Hypoxic hypoxia
Why: That is a failure of oxygenation in the lung, giving a low arterial oxygen tension, which is not the problem here.
* Stagnant hypoxia
Why: A failure of delivery due to inadequate flow, as in shock or local thrombosis.
* Histotoxic hypoxia
Why: A failure of the tissue to use oxygen it has received, as in cyanide poisoning.
Explanation: Four categories, four points in the chain: getting oxygen into the blood, carrying it, delivering it, and using it. Naming the point that has failed is what directs the treatment.

### Why the carriage fails here
Concept: CON-RES-F6434978BBC927
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Why can carboxyhaemoglobin not carry oxygen?
*= Carbon monoxide occupies the oxygen-binding sites on haemoglobin
Why: Correct. Carbon monoxide binds the same sites with far greater affinity, so those molecules are unavailable to carry oxygen.
* Carbon monoxide denatures the haemoglobin molecule irreversibly
Why: The binding is reversible, which is what makes treatment by displacement possible.
* Carboxyhaemoglobin cannot reach the tissues because it is too large
Why: The molecule's size is unchanged; the problem is what is bound to it.
* Carbon monoxide destroys the red cells carrying it
Why: Haemolysis is not the mechanism of carbon-monoxide poisoning.
Explanation: Occupied sites, not damaged ones. That is why the poisoning is reversible and why raising the inspired oxygen displaces the carbon monoxide.

### The second injury
Concept: CON-RES-AA4C315125E587
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Beyond reducing carriage, what does carboxyhaemoglobin do to the oxygen that is still bound?
*= It raises the affinity of the remaining haemoglobin, shifting the oxygen dissociation curve to the left
Why: Correct. The remaining sites hold their oxygen more tightly, so less is released to the tissues — a second injury on top of the reduced carriage.
* It lowers the affinity, shifting the curve to the right
Why: A right shift would release oxygen more readily, which would mitigate rather than worsen the problem.
* It has no effect on the remaining haemoglobin
Why: Treats the molecules as independent, missing the cooperative effect that makes this poisoning worse than an equivalent anaemia.
* It prevents the remaining haemoglobin from binding oxygen at all
Why: The remaining sites bind oxygen normally; the problem is that they release it reluctantly.
Explanation: Carbon-monoxide poisoning injures twice — less oxygen carried, and what is carried held too tightly to give up. That is why it is more dangerous than an anaemia of the same oxygen-carrying capacity.

### The sign that is often absent
Concept: CON-RES-CDBFCE15A0F579
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What appearance is classically described in carbon-monoxide poisoning?
*= Cherry-red skin and mucous membranes
Why: Correct. Carboxyhaemoglobin is bright red, which classically colours the skin and mucous membranes — although it is a late and unreliable sign in practice.
* Central cyanosis with blue lips
Why: Cyanosis requires deoxygenated haemoglobin, and carboxyhaemoglobin is bright red, which is why cyanosis is characteristically absent.
* Jaundice of the sclerae
Why: A sign of bilirubin, not of an abnormal haemoglobin species.
* Pallor with cold peripheries
Why: A non-specific sign of poor perfusion, not characteristic of this poisoning.
Explanation: The colour is why the patient does not look hypoxic and why the oximeter reads normally — both instruments, eye and machine, are misled by a bright red haemoglobin that carries nothing.

### How much has been absorbed
Concept: CON-RES-7AB0731A15AD7B
Difficulty: Challenging
Q: What determines the carboxyhaemoglobin level a patient reaches, and what does that imply for the family in this case?
*= The concentration of carbon monoxide and the duration of exposure — so those who were in the flat longest will have the highest levels, whatever their symptoms suggest
Why: Correct. Uptake accumulates with both the ambient concentration and the time exposed, so exposure history predicts the level better than the severity of symptoms, which varies with age and comorbidity.
* The patient's haemoglobin concentration alone, so the most anaemic will be worst affected
Why: Anaemia worsens the consequences of a given level but does not determine the level reached.
* The respiratory rate alone, so only those breathing fastest are at risk
Why: Ventilation influences the rate of uptake, but concentration and duration are the determinants named here, and everyone in the room is exposed.
* It cannot be predicted, so exposure history is of no use in triage
Why: Exactly the wrong conclusion: the history is what identifies who needs measuring, particularly the asymptomatic.
Explanation: Concentration multiplied by time. That is why everyone from the same room is assessed and measured, including those who feel well, rather than only those who look unwell.

### The layers of the trachea
Concept: CON-RES-A570461528F6E3
Difficulty: Easy
Q: A transverse section of trachea is on the stage. What are its wall layers, from the lumen outwards?
*= Mucosa, submucosa, a cartilaginous and muscular layer, and adventitia
Why: Correct. The tracheal wall is built as mucosa, submucosa, the cartilage-and-muscle layer, and an outer adventitia.
* Mucosa, muscularis mucosae, submucosa and serosa
Why: That is the plan of the gut wall, and a serosa would imply a peritoneal or pleural covering the trachea does not have.
* Epithelium and adventitia only, with no submucosa
Why: Omits the submucosa, which carries the seromucous glands that appear on the slide.
* Endothelium, media and adventitia
Why: The plan of a blood vessel; the trachea is lined by epithelium, not endothelium.
Explanation: Naming the layers in order is what makes the slide readable, and it is the same sequence that changes progressively as the airway divides.

### Where the cartilage stops
Concept: CON-RES-0D7E41562C7DE1
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What happens to the cartilage of the airway wall as the airways divide distally?
*= It decreases progressively and eventually disappears altogether
Why: Correct. Cartilage becomes sparser with successive divisions and is absent from the bronchioles.
* It increases, to hold the smaller airways open
Why: Reverses the trend, and would remove the very collapsibility that makes bronchioles the site of variable resistance.
* It remains constant throughout the conducting airways
Why: The progressive loss is one of the defining features distinguishing bronchus from bronchiole.
* It is replaced by bone in the smallest airways
Why: Invents a tissue transition that does not occur.
Explanation: Cartilage disappearing is what defines a bronchiole, and it is why bronchioles can be narrowed by muscle in a way that bronchi cannot.

### The muscle that closes the ring
Concept: CON-RES-FA87019E68AD30
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: The tracheal cartilage is C-shaped rather than a complete ring. What lies at the free ends?
*= The trachealis, a band of smooth muscle bridging the gap
Why: Correct. The trachealis spans the open posterior part of each cartilage, and its contraction narrows the tracheal lumen.
* A fibrous ligament with no muscular component
Why: Fibrous tissue is present, but the defining structure is the smooth muscle, which is why the calibre is adjustable.
* A continuation of the cartilage, completing the ring
Why: If the ring were complete the trachea could not accommodate the oesophagus behind it during swallowing.
* Skeletal muscle continuous with the oesophagus
Why: The trachealis is smooth muscle; the oesophagus is a separate tube behind it.
Explanation: The gap faces the oesophagus, and the muscle bridging it lets the trachea narrow and accommodate a swallowed bolus — anatomy with an immediate functional reason.

### Where the cleaning apparatus ends
Concept: CON-RES-B0D786E9E0644D
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What happens to goblet cells and cilia in the most distal airways?
*= Both are progressively lost, so the terminal airways have neither
Why: Correct. Goblet cells and ciliated cells both disappear distally, which is why the distal airways depend on macrophages rather than on the mucociliary escalator.
* Both increase, to protect the alveoli from inhaled particles
Why: Reverses the gradient; protection distally is cellular rather than mucociliary.
* Goblet cells are lost but cilia persist to the alveolus
Why: Mucus without cilia, or cilia without mucus, would each be useless; both are lost together.
* Cilia are lost but goblet cells persist, so mucus accumulates normally
Why: Mucus produced with no cilia to move it would be a liability, and this is not the normal arrangement.
Explanation: The mucociliary escalator ends before the alveolus, which is why alveolar defence is the macrophage's job and why particles reaching that depth are cleared slowly.

### Reading the gradient itself
Concept: CON-RES-43C345B42FF921
Difficulty: Hard
Q: Two unlabelled airway sections are shown, one with a tall epithelium and one with a low one. Which is more distal, and what general rule are you applying?
*= The one with the lower epithelium — airway epithelium decreases progressively in thickness as the airways divide
Why: Correct. Epithelial height falls distally, from pseudostratified columnar in the trachea to a low cuboidal lining in the terminal bronchioles, so the thinner epithelium is the more distal.
* The one with the taller epithelium, because distal airways need more secretory cells
Why: Reverses the gradient, and secretory cells become fewer distally rather than more numerous.
* Neither; epithelial height is constant and only the diameter changes
Why: Diameter does fall, but so does epithelial height, and the two changes together are what identify the level.
* It cannot be determined without seeing whether cartilage is present
Why: Cartilage is a useful confirmatory feature, but the epithelial gradient alone answers the question asked.
Explanation: Every feature of the airway wall thins distally together — epithelium, lamina propria, glands, cartilage — so any one of them places a section along the tree, and several together make the identification secure.
