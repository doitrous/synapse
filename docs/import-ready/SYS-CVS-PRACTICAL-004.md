### Naming the load
Mr Sobhy Nassar, 61, is seen in clinic with a blood pressure of 186/104 that has been poorly controlled for years. He is breathless on climbing one flight of stairs. An echocardiogram shows a thickened left ventricular wall and a normal ejection fraction.
Concept: CON-CVS-AD0881F8B57C91
Difficulty: Easy
Q: The registrar says his ventricle is working against a high afterload. What does afterload mean?
*= The load the ventricle must work against once it starts to eject
Why: Correct. Afterload is the opposing load the ventricle meets during ejection, which for the left ventricle is dominated by aortic pressure and systemic vascular resistance.
* The volume of blood in the ventricle at the end of filling
Why: That is preload. Students who hold the two words as a pair but not their meanings routinely swap them.
* The force the ventricle generates during contraction
Why: The force generated is the ventricle's response, not the load it responds to. Afterload is what is imposed on it.
* The pressure remaining in the ventricle after ejection is complete
Why: Invents a quantity from the word "after". Afterload is present during ejection, not after it.
Rationale: Afterload is the opposition to ejection, not the filling before it and not the force the muscle makes. Getting the definition right is what makes the next step predictable.

### What a raised afterload does to the volumes
Concept: CON-CVS-8E0A89302117C2
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: If afterload rises and nothing else changes, which ventricular volume changes most?
*= End-systolic volume rises, because ejection stops earlier against the higher load
Why: Correct. A higher opposing load means the ventricle cannot shorten as far, so more blood is left behind at the end of systole.
* End-diastolic volume falls, because less blood enters the ventricle
Why: Reverses the sequence. Filling happens before ejection and is not directly set by the load ejection meets; if anything, the residual volume raises end-diastolic volume on the following beat.
* Both end-diastolic and end-systolic volume fall proportionately
Why: Chosen by students who assume the ventricle simply operates at a smaller size. Afterload acts on emptying, not on filling.
* Neither changes, because stroke volume is fixed by the heart rate
Why: Stroke volume is not fixed, and heart rate does not set it. This is the misconception that cardiac output is regulated only by rate.
Rationale: Afterload acts on the emptying half of the cycle. It leaves more blood behind, so end-systolic volume is the volume that moves, and stroke volume falls unless something else compensates.

### The hormone acting directly on the vessels
Concept: CON-CVS-2886EDD0864ABC
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Angiotensin II contributes to his high pressure. What is its direct effect on the blood vessels themselves?
*= It constricts vascular smooth muscle, particularly in the arterioles, raising resistance
Why: Correct. Angiotensin II is a direct and potent vasoconstrictor, and arteriolar constriction is what raises systemic vascular resistance.
* It acts only through aldosterone, by causing sodium and water retention
Why: The commonest misconception here: the renal action is real but slower, and it is not the direct vascular effect the question asks about.
* It dilates the arterioles, and raises pressure by increasing cardiac output instead
Why: Inverts the effect. Nothing about angiotensin II dilates the systemic arterioles.
* It acts on the veins alone, raising pressure by increasing venous return
Why: Venoconstriction contributes, but the effect is not confined to the veins, and arteriolar constriction is the dominant pressure effect.
Rationale: Angiotensin II raises pressure by two separable routes: a fast, direct constriction of vascular smooth muscle, and a slow renal retention of salt and water. Naming which route is being asked about is the whole of the question.

### Where the sensors report from
Concept: CON-CVS-61D2DE3408AC86
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: His baroreceptors have been exposed to this pressure for years. By which nerves do the aortic-arch baroreceptors send their signals to the brainstem?
*= The aortic nerve, which joins the vagus nerve
Why: Correct. Aortic-arch baroreceptor afferents travel in the aortic nerve, which runs with and joins the vagus.
* The glossopharyngeal nerve, directly from the arch
Why: The glossopharyngeal nerve carries the *carotid sinus* afferents. Students who learn one baroreceptor pathway often apply it to both.
* The sympathetic chain, entering the cord at the upper thoracic levels
Why: The sympathetic chain carries the efferent limb of the reflex, not the afferent signal from the stretch receptors.
* The phrenic nerve, which passes over the arch on its way to the diaphragm
Why: The phrenic nerve does pass nearby, and proximity is exactly why it is chosen — but it is motor to the diaphragm and carries no baroreceptor traffic.
Rationale: Two baroreceptor sites, two afferent nerves: carotid sinus by the glossopharyngeal, aortic arch by the aortic nerve and vagus. The efferent limb is separate again, and confusing afferent with efferent is what makes the reflex hard to reason about.

### Why fluid did not push his pressure higher still
Concept: CON-CVS-245C56665C0240
Difficulty: Hard
He was given intravenous fluid overnight on a previous admission. His pressure rose at first and then settled back part of the way, without any drug being given.
Q: Which venous property explains the partial return towards baseline?
*= Stress relaxation, in which the veins gradually widen around the larger volume
Why: Correct. Vascular smooth muscle relaxes over minutes to hours when stretched, so mean systemic filling pressure, venous return, cardiac output and arterial pressure all rise less than the added volume alone would predict.
* Reverse stress relaxation, in which the veins slowly narrow around the volume
Why: The right mechanism, wrong direction. Narrowing follows a volume loss; this patient was given volume.
* The baroreflex, which reset within minutes to the new pressure
Why: A tempting answer, and the baroreflex does buffer acute change — but the question names a venous property, and resetting would not alter the filling pressure the veins generate.
* Increased urine output, which removed the added volume within the hour
Why: Renal excretion does eventually remove the load, but it is slower than the settling described and is not a property of veins.
Rationale: The venous reservoir resizes itself in both directions: it widens slowly after a volume gain and narrows slowly after a loss. Both are stress relaxation, and both blunt what the volume change alone would have done to pressure.
## debrief
The case separates three things a student is prone to merge: the load the ventricle ejects against, the volume that load changes, and the mechanisms setting the load in the first place. Afterload acts on end-systolic volume because it acts on emptying. Angiotensin II raises the load by two routes on two timescales. And the veins quietly resize around whatever volume they are given, which is why a fluid load does not raise pressure by as much as the arithmetic suggests.

### How long the gate normally holds
Miss Yara Kamal, 27, has sudden palpitations that began abruptly while she was sitting at her desk. Her pulse is 190 and regular. She is alert, with a blood pressure of 108/70.
Concept: CON-CVS-0C1DCBDF079969
Difficulty: Easy
Q: The impulse is normally held up at the atrioventricular node. Roughly how long is that delay?
*= About a tenth of a second
Why: Correct. The atrioventricular nodal delay is of the order of 0.1 seconds, which is long enough for the atria to finish emptying before the ventricles contract.
* About a hundredth of a second
Why: Ten times too short. A delay this brief would not separate atrial from ventricular contraction usefully.
* About half a second
Why: Ten times too long. At a normal rate this would occupy most of the cardiac cycle and leave no time for filling.
* There is no measurable delay; conduction is effectively instantaneous
Why: The misconception that the conducting system exists only for speed. Its slowest segment is deliberate.
Rationale: The delay is short in absolute terms but is the longest hold anywhere in the conducting system, and it is what allows atrial contraction to complete its contribution to filling before the ventricles contract.

### How long a normal cycle lasts
Concept: CON-CVS-DB8585BDDB67E7
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: At a normal resting rate of 75 beats per minute, how long is one cardiac cycle, and how is it divided?
*= About 0.8 seconds, of which roughly 0.3 is systole and 0.5 diastole
Why: Correct. Sixty seconds divided by 75 beats gives 0.8 seconds per cycle, and at rest diastole is the longer phase.
* About 0.8 seconds, of which roughly 0.5 is systole and 0.3 diastole
Why: The right cycle length with the phases the wrong way round. At rest the heart spends more time filling than ejecting.
* About 1.3 seconds, divided equally between systole and diastole
Why: Divides 100 rather than 60 by the rate. The arithmetic error is common under time pressure.
* About 0.4 seconds, of which most is systole
Why: Would correspond to a rate of 150, and inverts the resting balance of the phases.
Rationale: At rest, diastole is the longer phase, which is why tachycardia costs filling time before it costs anything else. At 190 beats per minute the cycle is nearer 0.3 seconds, and almost all of the loss comes out of diastole.

### Why a vagal manoeuvre is tried first
Concept: CON-CVS-A87E8F7EE8DC58
Difficulty: Moderate
She is asked to bear down against a closed glottis.
Q: How does increased parasympathetic activity act on the sinoatrial pacemaker cells?
*= It slows their rate of spontaneous depolarisation, so they take longer to reach threshold
Why: Correct. Parasympathetic activity slows the pacemaker potential, lengthening the time to threshold and so slowing the rate.
* It raises the threshold voltage the cells must reach before firing
Why: A plausible-sounding alternative that reaches the same outcome by the wrong mechanism. The change is in the slope of depolarisation, not in the threshold.
* It prevents the cells from repolarising fully after each action potential
Why: Describes an abnormality of repolarisation, which would tend to promote arrhythmia rather than slow the rate.
* It blocks conduction from the sinoatrial node to the atrial muscle
Why: Confuses an effect on rate with an effect on conduction out of the node. Vagal activity acts on both the node's firing and on atrioventricular conduction, but not by blocking exit from the sinoatrial node.
Rationale: The pacemaker fires when its slow depolarisation reaches threshold, so anything that flattens that slope slows the heart. The same vagal traffic also slows conduction through the atrioventricular node, which is what a vagal manoeuvre is exploiting here.

### What the fast rate is costing her
Concept: CON-CVS-C2030AF413E220
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: At 190 beats per minute her stroke volume has fallen. Which part of filling is most compromised, and what partly offsets it?
*= Passive filling is cut short by the loss of diastolic time, and a proportionately greater atrial contribution partly offsets it
Why: Correct. Diastole shortens disproportionately with rate, so the passive phase suffers most; atrial contraction contributes a larger share of a smaller total, as it does in exercise.
* Atrial contraction is lost first, and passive filling compensates
Why: The reverse. Atrial systole occupies late diastole and is the part that survives; it is the long passive phase that is cut.
* Filling is unaffected, because the ventricle fills faster at higher rates
Why: The misconception that the heart simply speeds everything up proportionately. Filling rate does rise, but not enough to replace the lost time.
* Filling is unaffected, because the coronary arteries fill in diastole instead
Why: Confuses ventricular filling with coronary perfusion. Both suffer when diastole shortens, but they are different problems.
Rationale: Rate takes its time out of diastole. The passive phase is the casualty, and the atrial contribution rises proportionately — the same shift that happens usefully in exercise, happening here without the benefit.

### The mechanism behind the drug
Concept: CON-CVS-BD9C1C359D7284
Difficulty: Hard
The manoeuvre fails, and the team discusses adenosine.
Q: By what cellular mechanism does adenosine act on cardiac cells?
*= It inhibits cAMP production, which reduces contractility and slows nodal tissue
Why: Correct. Adenosine acts through receptors that inhibit adenylate cyclase, lowering cAMP; the fall in cAMP reduces cardiac contractility and slows the nodal tissue that depends on that signalling.
* It blocks fast sodium channels, slowing conduction through the working myocardium
Why: The mechanism of a different drug class, and it would act on the ventricular myocardium rather than the node.
* It raises cAMP, increasing contractility while slowing the node
Why: Right that cAMP is the pathway, wrong direction — and internally contradictory, since raising cAMP would speed the node rather than slow it.
* It acts only on vascular smooth muscle and has no cardiac effect
Why: Adenosine does dilate vessels, and that is where its side effects come from, but a purely vascular action would not explain a cardiac effect at all.
Rationale: cAMP is the common currency: catecholamines raise it and speed the node, adenosine lowers it and slows both nodal tissue and contractility. Holding the pathway rather than the drug list is what makes the effect predictable.
## debrief
The case is about time. A tenth of a second at the atrioventricular node is what buys coordinated filling; eight tenths of a second is a resting cycle, of which the longer half is diastole; and at 190 beats per minute it is that longer half that disappears. The two interventions in the case, vagal and pharmacological, both work on the same target — the rate of spontaneous depolarisation in nodal tissue — by opposite ends of the same cAMP pathway.

### The two coats
Mr Ehab Rizk, 34, has three days of sharp central chest pain, worse lying flat and easier sitting forward, following a flu-like illness. A rub is audible at the left sternal edge.
Concept: CON-CVS-7C9D59D257AC65
Difficulty: Easy
Q: The pericardium is inflamed. How is it constructed?
*= A tough outer fibrous layer, lined by a serous layer that reflects onto the heart itself
Why: Correct. The fibrous pericardium is the outer sac; the serous pericardium lines it as a parietal layer and continues over the heart as the visceral layer, with a thin film of fluid between them.
* A single fibrous sac in direct contact with the myocardium
Why: Ignores the serous lining and the cavity, and would leave nothing to produce a rub or an effusion.
* Two serous layers, with no fibrous component
Why: Loses the tough outer layer that limits how far the sac can stretch — which is precisely what matters when fluid collects quickly.
* A fibrous outer layer and a muscular inner layer continuous with the atria
Why: Invents a muscular coat. The layer against the heart is serous, not muscular.
Rationale: Two layers, and each does a different job: the fibrous sac limits distension, and the two serous surfaces slide on each other. Inflammation of the sliding surfaces is what produces both the pain and the rub.

### Where the sac sits
Concept: CON-CVS-AE28ABD8CE2B0B
Difficulty: Easy
Q: In which compartment of the thorax do the heart and its pericardium lie?
*= The middle mediastinum
Why: Correct. The heart within the pericardium occupies the middle mediastinum, between the two pleural cavities.
* The superior mediastinum, above the plane of the sternal angle
Why: The great vessels pass through the superior mediastinum, and their presence there is why this is chosen. The heart itself lies below it.
* The posterior mediastinum, behind the heart
Why: The posterior mediastinum contains the oesophagus and descending aorta, and is defined by lying behind the pericardium.
* Within the left pleural cavity, which is why the apex is felt on the left
Why: The apex does point left, but the heart is not inside a pleural cavity; it lies between them.
Rationale: Naming the compartment is what makes the relations usable: the pleural cavities lateral, the oesophagus and descending aorta behind, the sternum in front. Every one of those relations matters when the sac fills.

### Why leaning forward helps
Concept: CON-CVS-10EC8DB8FAF3BF
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: His pain is worst lying flat and least sitting forward. Which anatomical feature of the pericardium best explains a posture-dependent pain?
*= The pericardium is fixed anteriorly to the sternum, so posture changes how the inflamed surfaces are loaded against it
Why: Correct. Anterior fixation to the sternum means the sac's relationship to the chest wall is not free to change, so a change in posture alters the contact and the pain.
* The pericardium is attached only to the diaphragm, so posture has no anterior effect
Why: The diaphragmatic attachment is real and important, but treating it as the only fixation leaves posture-dependent anterior pain unexplained.
* The pericardium hangs freely in the chest, so the heart simply falls forward when he leans
Why: A free sac is the intuitive picture and the wrong one. The pericardium is anchored front and below.
* The pain is pleural rather than pericardial, and posture acts on the lung
Why: Pleuritic pain is a genuine differential and does vary with breathing — but a rub at the left sternal edge after a viral illness, easing on leaning forward, points at the pericardium.
Rationale: The pericardium is tethered, not floating: anteriorly to the sternum and below to the diaphragm. Those attachments are why posture changes the pain, and why the sac cannot simply move out of the way when it fills.

### The vessels above it
Concept: CON-CVS-930E4596CA5673
Difficulty: Moderate
The registrar asks him to trace the great vessels on the echocardiogram.
Q: How do the pulmonary trunk and the ascending aorta lie in relation to one another as they leave the heart?
*= They spiral around each other, so their relative positions change along their course
Why: Correct. The pulmonary trunk begins anterior to the aorta and passes to its left, and the two spiral around each other as they ascend — a consequence of how the outflow tract divides in development.
* They run parallel, with the aorta always directly posterior to the pulmonary trunk
Why: True at one level only. Taking a single cross-section as the whole relationship is the commonest error in reading outflow anatomy.
* They run parallel side by side, the aorta always to the right
Why: Again describes one level and freezes it, and misses the crossing entirely.
* The pulmonary trunk arises from the left ventricle and the aorta from the right
Why: Reverses the connections. This is transposition of the great arteries, an abnormality, not the normal arrangement.
Rationale: The spiral is why one echocardiographic view shows the vessels side by side and another shows one in front of the other. Remembering that the relationship changes along the course prevents a normal heart being read as abnormal.

### When the sac fills
Concept: CON-CVS-BB7AC16AB9B338
Difficulty: Challenging
Ten days later he returns with a raised jugular venous pressure and prominent venous pulsation in the neck.
Q: Why does a rise in pressure inside the pericardium show itself so directly in the neck veins?
*= There are no valves between the right atrium and the venae cavae, so right atrial pressure is transmitted straight up the jugular veins
Why: Correct. The absence of valves makes the jugular veins a continuous manometer for right atrial pressure, so a pericardial pressure that impedes filling registers immediately in the neck.
* The jugular veins have valves that close and hold a column of blood when pressure rises
Why: Exactly the wrong way round. Valves would isolate the neck from atrial pressure and abolish the sign.
* The rise reflects left atrial pressure transmitted backwards through the lungs
Why: Describes the route to pulmonary congestion, not to a visible venous pulsation. The jugular veins report the right side.
* The carotid pulse becomes more visible when cardiac output falls
Why: Confuses the arterial pulse with the venous one; distinguishing the two at the bedside is the skill this sign depends on.
Rationale: The neck is a window onto the right atrium precisely because nothing intervenes. The fibrous sac limits how far the pericardium can stretch, so fluid accumulating in it raises the pressure the atrium must fill against — and that pressure appears, unmodified, in the jugular veins.
## debrief
Everything in this case follows from two structural facts: the sac has a tough outer layer that will not stretch quickly, and it is tethered in the middle mediastinum rather than floating. The first explains why a rising pressure inside it impedes filling; the second explains why the pain moves with posture. The neck veins report the consequence directly because there is no valve between them and the right atrium.

### The cells wrapped around the capillary
Mr Tarek Abdelhamid, 58, is admitted with severe sepsis. His hands are cold and mottled, capillary refill is four seconds, and his blood pressure has been restored with fluid but his skin has not improved.
Concept: CON-CVS-5D4C49C48AA325
Difficulty: Easy
Q: Which cells sit outside the capillary endothelium, sharing its basement membrane?
*= Pericytes
Why: Correct. Pericytes lie against the endothelium within the basement membrane, wrapping processes around the capillary.
* Smooth muscle cells arranged in a continuous circular layer
Why: A continuous muscular coat belongs to arterioles and larger vessels. Capillaries have no such layer, which is what makes their control different.
* Fibroblasts forming an outer adventitial sheath
Why: Fibroblasts are present in surrounding connective tissue but are not the cells applied to the endothelium within the basement membrane.
* Macrophages resident in the capillary wall itself
Why: Tissue macrophages sit nearby and matter in sepsis, but they are not a structural component of the capillary wall.
Rationale: A capillary is endothelium, basement membrane and pericytes. Knowing there is a contractile cell there at all is what makes local control of capillary perfusion possible to explain.

### What those cells do
Concept: CON-CVS-CC810A201244F0
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What is the functional consequence of pericytes on the capillary they surround?
*= They contract and relax, adjusting the calibre of the capillary and so the flow through it
Why: Correct. Pericyte tone regulates capillary diameter, giving control of perfusion at the level of the individual capillary rather than only upstream.
* They secrete the plasma proteins that generate the oncotic pressure
Why: Plasma proteins are made in the liver. This mistakes a local structural cell for a source of circulating protein.
* They form the tight junctions that determine capillary permeability
Why: Junctions are between endothelial cells. Pericytes influence the barrier but do not constitute it.
* They are inert supporting cells with no effect on flow
Why: The older teaching that all capillary control is upstream at the arteriole. Pericytes give an additional, local level of control.
Rationale: Perfusion is controlled at two levels: the arteriole that feeds a capillary bed, and the pericytes on the capillaries themselves. Both have to fail before a bed is unrecruitable.

### Why an active tissue gets more blood
Concept: CON-CVS-CAFCFCE5767545
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: In a normally perfused tissue, what happens locally when metabolic activity rises?
*= Accumulating metabolites relax the vessels supplying the bed, increasing capillary perfusion
Why: Correct. Local metabolites act on the supplying vessels to increase the number of perfused capillaries and the flow through them, matching supply to demand where the demand actually is.
* Sympathetic outflow to that tissue is switched off by the brainstem
Why: Central control cannot resolve which capillary bed within a muscle is working. This is the misconception that all vascular control is neural.
* Circulating adrenaline is released specifically into that tissue's supply
Why: A circulating hormone reaches everywhere at once and cannot be targeted to one active bed.
* Blood viscosity falls in the active tissue, so flow increases passively
Why: Viscosity changes are minor and not the mechanism of local metabolic control.
Rationale: Local metabolic control is what matches perfusion to demand tissue by tissue. It is also the mechanism that fails in the case at hand: in sepsis the bed stops responding to its own signals.

### Where the fluid leaves
Concept: CON-CVS-08AA7F26A9BD28
Difficulty: Moderate
He becomes progressively oedematous despite the fluid being given.
Q: Which vessel wall is the principal site at which fluid and cells leave the circulation in inflammation?
*= The postcapillary venule, whose thin wall of endothelium and scanty support is the main site of leakage and emigration
Why: Correct. The postcapillary venule is where inflammatory mediators open the endothelium, and where leucocytes cross.
* The muscular arteriole, because it carries the highest pressure into the bed
Why: Pressure is highest upstream, which is why students pick it, but the arteriolar wall is thick and muscular and is not where leakage occurs.
* The elastic artery, whose fenestrated elastic laminae allow fluid to pass
Why: Confuses fenestrations in elastic laminae, a structural feature of the arterial wall, with the fenestrated capillaries of certain organs.
* The lymphatic capillary, which fills from the interstitium
Why: The lymphatic drains the interstitium rather than filling it; failure of drainage worsens oedema but is not where the fluid arrives from.
Rationale: The postcapillary venule is the business end of inflammation. Its thin wall is why oedema and leucocyte emigration happen there and not at the arteriole, however high the arteriolar pressure.

### Why restoring the pressure did not restore the flow
Concept: CON-CVS-1AD44A19DA47AD
Difficulty: Hard
Q: His mean arterial pressure is now adequate, yet his skin remains mottled and his lactate is rising. What has happened in the microcirculation?
*= In late shock the microvasculature no longer responds normally, so perfusion stays inadequate despite a restored driving pressure
Why: Correct. Late, refractory shock is characterised by microvascular failure: the bed stops matching perfusion to demand, and correcting the upstream pressure no longer corrects the flow through it.
* The arterioles remain maximally constricted by sympathetic outflow, and will relax once the pressure is high enough
Why: Treats the problem as a persisting normal reflex. If it were, restoring pressure and reducing the reflex drive would have restored the flow.
* The capillaries have been physically destroyed and cannot be reopened
Why: Overstates the lesion. The failure is functional before it is structural, which is why it is potentially reversible early.
* Blood is being shunted through arteriovenous anastomoses that are under normal control
Why: Shunting does occur, and it is part of the picture — but describing it as normal control misses that the regulation itself has failed.
Rationale: Arterial pressure is the driving force, not the delivery. Once the microvasculature stops regulating, an adequate pressure can coexist with an inadequate perfusion — which is why the skin, the lactate and the urine output are read rather than the blood pressure alone.
## debrief
The case works downwards from the arteriole to the individual capillary, and shows that perfusion has controls at every level: arteriolar tone, pericyte tone, and the local metabolites that signal demand. The last decision is the one that matters clinically — a restored blood pressure is not a restored circulation, because the pressure is upstream of the failure.
