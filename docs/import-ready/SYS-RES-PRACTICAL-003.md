### Where one surface becomes another
Concept: CON-RES-044FB05CEE2B7A
Difficulty: Easy
Q: A lung specimen is on the bench. What does the anterior border separate?
*= The costal surface from the mediastinal surface
Why: Correct. The anterior border is the sharp edge where the surface facing the ribs meets the surface facing the mediastinum.
* The costal surface from the diaphragmatic surface
Why: That junction is the inferior border, which runs along the base of the lung.
* The apex from the costal surface
Why: The apex is a region rather than a surface, and no border is defined between them in this way.
* The upper lobe from the lower lobe
Why: Lobes are separated by fissures, not by borders. A border is an edge of the whole lung.
Explanation: Borders are edges between surfaces; fissures divide lobes. Keeping the two words apart is what makes a description of a specimen unambiguous.

### Where the two anterior borders run
Concept: CON-RES-0624EEA6DB23DE
Difficulty: Easy
Q: On the chest wall, where do the anterior borders of both lungs run in their upper part?
*= Downwards from behind the sternoclavicular joint to the level of the sternal angle
Why: Correct. Both anterior borders begin behind the sternoclavicular joints and converge behind the sternum as far as the sternal angle.
* Downwards from the mid-clavicular line to the sixth rib
Why: That describes part of the *inferior* border's surface marking, not the anterior border's upper course.
* Along the lateral border of the sternum on the right only
Why: Both lungs have an anterior border, and both begin at the sternoclavicular joint.
* Behind the manubrium only, not reaching the sternal angle
Why: They reach the sternal angle, below which the left border deviates laterally for the cardiac notch.
Explanation: The two borders run together behind the upper sternum and part company at the sternal angle, where the left is displaced by the heart — which is why the cardiac notch is on the left.

### The impression on the right lung
Concept: CON-RES-D10F9CAE51D91C
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A triangular impression is seen anteriorly on the mediastinal surface of the right lung. What made it?
*= The ascending aorta
Why: Correct. The ascending aorta produces a triangular impression on the anterior part of the right lung's mediastinal surface.
* The arch of the aorta
Why: The arch grooves the *left* lung above its hilum, not the anterior right.
* The superior vena cava
Why: The cava does groove the right lung, but its groove is vertical and runs towards the hilum rather than forming this anterior triangle.
* The right atrium
Why: The heart produces a broad cardiac impression; the discrete triangular mark described is aortic.
Explanation: Each mediastinal structure leaves its own mark, and the marks are how a lung specimen is sided and oriented before anything else is examined.

### The groove above the left hilum
Concept: CON-RES-912F4BEC0BBE15
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: On the left lung, a groove lies above the hilum and continues downwards behind it. What runs in it?
*= The aortic arch, its groove continuing as that of the descending aorta
Why: Correct. The arch grooves the left lung above the hilum, and that groove runs on as the descending aorta's groove behind the hilum.
* The azygos vein, arching above the hilum
Why: The azygos arch is a right-sided structure and grooves the right lung.
* The oesophagus, which grooves the left lung above the hilum
Why: The oesophageal groove is present but runs behind the hilum rather than arching above it, and on the right it is more prominent.
* The left phrenic nerve, which is too small to groove the lung
Why: Correctly notes that the nerve leaves no groove, but then names it as the answer.
Explanation: One continuous groove made by one continuous vessel, arching over the hilum and then descending behind it. Recognising it is what identifies a left lung immediately.

### Which arch belongs to which side
Concept: CON-RES-3587EEFEDA34BB
Difficulty: Hard
Q: Two lungs are presented without labels. Which arching structure lies above each root, and how does that settle which lung is which?
*= The azygos arch lies above the right root and the aortic arch above the left, so the arching groove identifies the side
Why: Correct. Each root is arched over by a different structure — azygos on the right, aortic arch on the left — and the groove each leaves is a reliable way to side a specimen.
* The aortic arch lies above both roots, so the grooves cannot distinguish the sides
Why: The aortic arch is a left-sided relation; nothing about it crosses to arch the right root.
* The azygos arch lies above the left root and the aortic arch above the right
Why: The correct pair of structures with the sides reversed, which is the commonest error when siding a lung.
* The pulmonary artery arches above both roots
Why: The pulmonary arteries enter the roots rather than arching over them, and they are the same on both sides.
Explanation: Two arches, two sides, one reliable rule. Combined with the number of lobes and the position of the cardiac notch, it makes siding a lung specimen a matter of evidence rather than of guessing.

### The groove above the right hilum
Concept: CON-RES-7B3F9A26D5765F
Difficulty: Easy
Q: On the right lung a groove lies above the hilum, joining two other grooves. Which are they?
*= The groove of the azygos vein below and that of the superior vena cava above
Why: Correct. The azygos arch grooves the lung above the hilum and connects the vertical azygos groove behind with the caval groove in front and above.
* The grooves of the aortic arch and the descending aorta
Why: Those are the left lung's grooves; neither is present on the right.
* The grooves of the oesophagus and the trachea
Why: Both are related to the right lung, but neither is joined by an arching groove above the hilum.
* The grooves of the pulmonary artery and vein
Why: These vessels enter at the hilum and do not leave arching grooves above it.
Explanation: The azygos arch is the hinge between two vertical grooves, which is why the right mediastinal surface reads as a continuous route from the posterior chest wall to the superior vena cava.

### What runs with it
Concept: CON-RES-4DCA05069A0480
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Which structures are related to the azygos-arch groove?
*= The arch of the azygos vein itself and the right vagus nerve
Why: Correct. The groove reflects the azygos arch, and the right vagus nerve is closely related as it passes to the posterior aspect of the root.
* The azygos arch and the left phrenic nerve
Why: The left phrenic nerve is a left-sided structure and lies on the pericardium, not in a right-sided groove.
* The superior vena cava and the right phrenic nerve
Why: The right phrenic nerve descends on the pericardium and lateral to the cava rather than in this groove.
* The thoracic duct and the right sympathetic chain
Why: The thoracic duct ascends on the left of the vertebral column at this level and does not relate to the azygos arch groove.
Explanation: A groove is not only a mark left by a vessel; it names a neighbourhood. Knowing the vagus lies there is what makes the relationship clinically relevant during hilar surgery.

### The groove behind the other groove
Concept: CON-RES-B5B9265E8558D0
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: On the right lung, where does the azygos-vein groove descend in relation to the oesophageal groove?
*= Behind it
Why: Correct. The azygos vein lies posterior to the oesophagus at this level, so its groove descends behind the oesophageal groove.
* In front of it
Why: Reverses the relationship; the oesophagus is the more anterior of the two.
* Directly within it, the two sharing one groove
Why: They produce two distinguishable grooves, which is what allows the mediastinal surface to be read in detail.
* Lateral to it, on the costal surface
Why: Both are mediastinal relations; neither grooves the costal surface.
Explanation: Front to back on the right mediastinal surface: cava and heart in front, then the oesophagus, then the azygos vein. Holding that order is what makes an unlabelled specimen legible.

### What the alveolar wall does not contain
Concept: CON-RES-C139C75AFF0EA1
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Which structure is absent from the alveolar walls themselves?
*= Lymphatic vessels
Why: Correct. The alveolar walls contain no lymphatics; lymphatic vessels begin in the connective tissue around the alveolar ducts, bronchioles and vessels.
* Capillaries
Why: The alveolar wall is defined by its dense capillary network, which is the whole point of the structure.
* Elastic fibres
Why: Elastic tissue is present in the alveolar wall and is central to lung recoil.
* Type I pneumocytes
Why: Type I pneumocytes form most of the alveolar lining surface.
Explanation: Fluid leaving the alveolar wall must first reach the surrounding connective tissue before it can be drained. That is why alveolar oedema clears slowly and why lymphatic drainage of the lung begins one step back from the gas-exchange surface.

### The lung that has never breathed
Concept: CON-RES-D0E08FFB438AB5
Difficulty: Hard
Q: A lung from a stillborn infant is placed in water and sinks. What does that indicate, and on what principle?
*= That it has not been aerated — before birth the lung is firm and airless, so its density exceeds that of water
Why: Correct. An unexpanded lung contains no air, is firm and dense, and therefore sinks; an aerated lung floats. The observation is used to establish whether respiration occurred.
* That the lung is consolidated by infection acquired before birth
Why: Consolidation can also make a lung sink, which is exactly why the test is interpreted alongside the rest of the examination — but the finding described is the normal state of a lung that has never breathed.
* That the lung is normal, since all lung tissue is denser than water
Why: Aerated lung is less dense than water and floats, which is the basis of the comparison.
* Nothing, since the density of lung tissue is unrelated to aeration
Why: Aeration is precisely what changes the density, and it is the only variable the test examines.
Explanation: The float test asks one question — has air entered the lung — and answers it by density. Like any single sign it is read with the whole examination, since consolidation or freezing can also sink an aerated lung.

### Where the bronchial arteries run
Concept: CON-RES-91E3CD4EED8E76
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What course do the bronchial arteries take within the lung, and how far do they reach?
*= They follow the bronchi, running on their posterior walls, as far as the respiratory bronchioles
Why: Correct. The bronchial arteries accompany the bronchial tree along its posterior aspect and supply it down to the level of the respiratory bronchioles.
* They follow the pulmonary arteries to the alveolar capillaries
Why: The pulmonary arteries carry the functional circulation to the alveoli; the bronchial arteries are a separate nutritive supply that stops short of them.
* They run in the pleura and supply the lung from its surface inwards
Why: They do supply the visceral pleura, but their course within the lung follows the bronchial tree.
* They accompany the pulmonary veins back towards the hilum
Why: Reverses the direction of an arterial supply and pairs it with the wrong vessel.
Explanation: The lung has two circulations: a functional one for gas exchange and a nutritive one for its own tissue. The bronchial arteries are the second, and they follow the airway.

### What that supply is for
Concept: CON-RES-47AF74612E619C
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What do the bronchial arteries nourish?
*= The lung stroma, the bronchial walls and the visceral pleura
Why: Correct. They supply the supporting tissue of the lung, the walls of the airways themselves, and the visceral pleura.
* The alveolar walls, where gas exchange occurs
Why: The alveolar walls are supplied by the pulmonary circulation, which is why a pulmonary embolus does not always infarct the lung.
* The parietal pleura and the chest wall
Why: The parietal pleura is supplied from the chest wall's own vessels, not from the bronchial arteries.
* The mediastinal lymph nodes
Why: Those have their own supply from mediastinal vessels.
Explanation: The double supply is why lung infarction after a pulmonary embolus is not inevitable — the bronchial circulation can sustain the tissue even when the pulmonary supply is interrupted.

### Where that blood goes afterwards
Concept: CON-RES-27B7D7F1915BFE
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What do the bronchial veins drain?
*= The territories supplied by the bronchial arteries
Why: Correct. The bronchial veins return blood from the regions the bronchial arteries supply, although part of that blood also drains into the pulmonary veins.
* The alveolar capillary bed
Why: That drains into the pulmonary veins as part of the functional circulation.
* The parietal pleura and intercostal spaces
Why: Those drain to the azygos system through the intercostal veins.
* The whole of the lung, both circulations together
Why: The functional circulation returns by the pulmonary veins; the bronchial veins account only for part of the nutritive return.
Explanation: Arteries and veins of the same circulation serve the same territory. The overlap between bronchial and pulmonary venous drainage is also why a small amount of deoxygenated blood normally reaches the left atrium.

### Following the vessels on a section
Concept: CON-RES-454D45D9ADFD50
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: On a section of lung, where would you look for the bronchial vessels relative to a bronchus?
*= Along its posterior wall, which is the surface they follow
Why: Correct. The bronchial vessels run on the posterior walls of the bronchi, so that is where they are found on a section.
* Within the bronchial lumen
Why: Vessels run in the wall and surrounding tissue, never in the airway lumen.
* Encircling the bronchus evenly on all sides
Why: Their relationship is consistent rather than circumferential, which is what makes it a useful landmark.
* Only at the hilum, not within the lung substance
Why: They enter at the hilum but continue with the bronchial tree well into the lung.
Explanation: A consistent relationship is a findable one. Knowing the vessels lie posteriorly is what lets a student locate them on an unlabelled section rather than searching at random.

### The vessel beside the bronchiole
Concept: CON-RES-F54CC7C4CAF586
Difficulty: Hard
Q: On a slide, a small artery lies immediately beside a bronchiole. How do you decide whether it is a branch of the bronchial or of the pulmonary artery?
*= By its relationship and calibre — a bronchial branch accompanies the airway wall itself and is small, while a pulmonary arterial branch runs alongside the bronchiole as a paired vessel of comparable size
Why: Correct. Both are found near a bronchiole, and they are distinguished by which structure the vessel is applied to and by the fact that a pulmonary artery branch matches the airway in calibre as the two divide together.
* Only by staining for elastic tissue, since their positions are identical
Why: Elastic staining helps, and pulmonary arteries are relatively elastic — but the positional relationship is the first discriminator on a routine slide.
* By whether it contains red cells, since only the pulmonary artery carries blood
Why: Both carry blood; one is nutritive and the other functional.
* It cannot be distinguished; all vessels beside a bronchiole are bronchial
Why: The pulmonary artery divides alongside the airway throughout the lung, so most vessels paired with a bronchiole are pulmonary.
Explanation: Two circulations meet beside every bronchiole, and telling them apart is the point of the exercise: one supplies the tissue you are looking at, the other is passing through on its way to the alveoli.

### The lining of the conchae
Concept: CON-RES-F818BF7BB20E31
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What epithelium lines the nasal conchae?
*= Respiratory epithelium — pseudostratified ciliated columnar with goblet cells
Why: Correct. The conchae are lined by respiratory epithelium, which warms, humidifies and traps particles from inspired air.
* Stratified squamous keratinised epithelium
Why: That lines the nasal vestibule at the entrance, not the conchae themselves.
* Simple squamous epithelium
Why: Too thin for a surface exposed to inspired air, and characteristic of the alveolus instead.
* Olfactory epithelium throughout
Why: Olfactory epithelium is confined to the roof of the nasal cavity and the adjacent superior concha and septum, not to the conchae generally.
Explanation: The nose changes epithelium three times over a short distance — squamous at the vestibule, respiratory over the conchae, olfactory at the roof — and each change follows a change in function.

### Why a blocked nose blocks
Concept: CON-RES-1A28D007D56110
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What causes the nasal obstruction of an allergic or inflammatory reaction?
*= Congestion of the venous swell bodies in the mucosa, which thickens it and narrows the airway
Why: Correct. The nasal mucosa contains venous erectile tissue; when it engorges, the mucosa swells and the airway narrows.
* Contraction of smooth muscle in the nasal wall, as in the bronchi
Why: The nose has no equivalent bronchial-type muscular narrowing; obstruction is vascular and mucosal.
* Accumulation of mucus alone, with no change in the mucosa
Why: Secretion contributes to the symptom, but the swelling of the mucosa is what narrows the passage.
* Collapse of the nasal cartilages during inspiration
Why: Alar collapse occurs in some people on forceful inspiration but is not the mechanism of allergic obstruction.
Explanation: The obstruction is vascular, which is why a vasoconstrictor relieves it quickly and an antihistamine does not.

### Beneath the nasopharyngeal epithelium
Concept: CON-RES-A811FF8C5B42AD
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What characterises the lamina propria of the nasopharynx?
*= It is rich in lymphoid tissue, aggregated as the pharyngeal tonsil
Why: Correct. Abundant lymphoid tissue lies in the nasopharyngeal lamina propria, forming the pharyngeal tonsil in the roof.
* It is almost acellular, consisting of dense collagen only
Why: Understates a tissue whose defining feature is its lymphoid population.
* It contains skeletal muscle fibres throughout
Why: Muscle lies deep to the mucosa in the pharyngeal wall, not within the lamina propria.
* It contains cartilage, continuous with the nasal septum
Why: Cartilage supports the nose anteriorly and the auditory tube laterally, but does not lie in the nasopharyngeal lamina propria.
Explanation: The first part of the airway is also an immunological gateway. The lymphoid tissue is why adenoidal enlargement obstructs the nasopharynx in children.

### The neuron that can be replaced
Concept: CON-RES-CCE62F7217A235
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What is unusual about the olfactory receptor neurons?
*= They are regularly replaced from a population of basal stem cells throughout life
Why: Correct. Olfactory receptor neurons are among the few neurons that regenerate, being continuously replaced from basal cells in the epithelium.
* They are never replaced, so smell declines irreversibly from birth
Why: The general rule for neurons, applied where the exception holds, and the reason this is chosen.
* They divide themselves to produce daughter neurons
Why: The mature neurons do not divide; a separate basal stem-cell population supplies the replacements.
* They are replaced only in childhood, ceasing at puberty
Why: Turnover continues throughout life, although it becomes less efficient with age and injury.
Explanation: Recovery of smell after a viral illness depends on this population. Its existence is also why the olfactory epithelium is studied as a model of neural regeneration.

### Tracing the fibres upwards
Concept: CON-RES-74413C2E6FFA70
Difficulty: Challenging
Q: A section of the roof of the nasal cavity shows bundles of unmyelinated fibres passing upwards. Where are they going, and what makes this route clinically important?
*= They are olfactory nerve fibres passing through the cribriform plate to the olfactory bulb — a direct route between the nasal cavity and the cranial cavity
Why: Correct. The fibres collect into bundles that traverse the cribriform plate to reach the bulb. Because that route breaches the skull base, a cribriform fracture can leak cerebrospinal fluid into the nose and admit infection to the meninges.
* They are branches of the maxillary nerve carrying sensation from the mucosa
Why: General sensation is indeed trigeminal, which is why this is plausible — but those fibres do not pass through the cribriform plate.
* They are postganglionic sympathetic fibres to the nasal vessels
Why: Autonomic fibres reach the nose by a different route and do not form the bundles crossing the cribriform plate.
* They are lymphatic channels draining to the cervical nodes
Why: Lymphatics are not nerve fibres, and they drain downwards rather than through the skull base.
Explanation: The olfactory route is the one place where the airway and the cranial cavity are separated by a perforated plate of bone. That anatomy explains anosmia after head injury, cerebrospinal fluid rhinorrhoea, and why a nasal infection can reach the meninges.
