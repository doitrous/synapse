### Where the swelling has to be
Tarek, 4, has a hoarse cry and a barking cough after two days of coryza. He is not drooling and can swallow.
Concept: CON-RES-4D4B125FF50E60
Difficulty: Easy
Q: His voice has changed. Which structure must be involved?
*= The laryngeal mucosa — acute laryngitis is an acute catarrhal inflammation of that lining
Why: Correct. The voice is produced at the vocal folds, which are mucosal structures, so any swelling of the laryngeal lining alters it.
* The tracheal mucosa below the cords
Why: Tracheitis produces cough and stridor but does not itself change the voice, which is generated above it.
* The pharyngeal mucosa alone
Why: Pharyngitis makes swallowing painful; it does not alter phonation.
* The bronchial mucosa
Why: Bronchial disease produces wheeze and cough, and is far below the structures that generate voice.
Rationale: The voice localises the lesion. A hoarse child has laryngeal involvement whatever else is present, and that is what makes the airway calibre a concern.

### How the organism got started
Concept: CON-RES-E0B3E37B828F65
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What is the common sequence by which a respiratory pathogen damages this lining?
*= Attachment to the epithelium, then necrosis of those cells, then an inflammatory response
Why: Correct. Adherence comes first, cell death follows, and the inflammatory reaction to it produces the swelling and secretions.
* Inflammation first, which then allows the organism to attach
Why: Reverses the sequence; adherence is the initiating event.
* Direct invasion of the bloodstream, with airway signs appearing later
Why: Bacteraemia can follow, but the local injury begins at the epithelial surface.
* Toxin release acting on the respiratory centre
Why: A systemic mechanism used by a few organisms, not the common local one.
Rationale: Attach, kill, inflame. That sequence explains why the epithelium is shed, why clearance fails during the illness, and why a bacterial infection so often follows a viral one.

### When the throat looks worse
Concept: CON-RES-A93F9D04787B4C
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A second child has marked pharyngeal hyperaemia with exudate. Which organisms are associated with that appearance?
*= Beta-haemolytic streptococcus and adenovirus
Why: Correct. The more severe exudative pharyngitis is characteristically associated with these two.
* Rhinovirus and respiratory syncytial virus
Why: Common causes of coryza and bronchiolitis, typically without a marked exudate.
* Bordetella pertussis
Why: Produces a paroxysmal cough illness rather than an exudative pharyngitis.
* Candida albicans in a healthy child
Why: Candidal plaques suggest immunosuppression or recent antibiotics, and look adherent and white rather than exudative.
Rationale: Two organisms produce the same appearance, which is exactly why appearance alone cannot decide whether an antibiotic is warranted.

### The glands in his neck
Concept: CON-RES-5BFE7F3DEA6061
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: His cervical nodes are enlarged. Which set of features would confirm they are acutely inflamed rather than sinister?
*= Separate, mobile, soft, painful and tender
Why: Correct. Acute inflammatory nodes enlarge but stay discrete and mobile, and are soft and tender.
* Matted, hard, fixed and painless
Why: That combination suggests malignant infiltration, and it is the contrast that makes the acute description useful.
* Rubbery, painless and fixed to deep structures
Why: Suggests lymphoma rather than acute inflammation.
* Not palpable at all, since acute infection does not enlarge nodes
Why: Acute infection characteristically enlarges the draining nodes.
Rationale: Every lump in the neck is placed on a spectrum from acutely inflamed to malignant, and the five features above are the acute end of it.

### The lesion that recurs
Concept: CON-RES-7B27CD2545F4A5
Difficulty: Hard
Q: A third child has had hoarseness for eight months and warty lesions are seen on the vocal folds. What is the diagnosis, the cause, and why does it recur?
*= Recurrent respiratory papillomatosis — benign epithelial papillomas caused by human papillomavirus types 6 and 11 — recurring because the virus persists in clinically normal adjacent mucosa
Why: Correct. HPV 6 and 11 cause these benign laryngeal papillomas, and because the virus remains in surrounding epithelium the lesions return after removal, often repeatedly through childhood.
* Laryngeal carcinoma, recurring because excision is rarely complete
Why: Carcinoma of the larynx is a disease of adults with a smoking history and is not the cause of chronic hoarseness in a child.
* Vocal nodules, recurring because the child continues to shout
Why: Nodules are a genuine differential for childhood hoarseness, but they are smooth and paired rather than warty and multiple.
* Laryngeal tuberculosis, recurring with reactivation
Why: Rare, associated with pulmonary disease, and ulcerative rather than papillomatous.
Rationale: The recurrence is the diagnostic clue and the therapeutic problem at once. Naming the virus explains both, and it explains why treatment is repeated removal rather than a single cure.
## debrief
The voice localises the lesion to the larynx; the pathogen's sequence explains the swelling; the appearance of the pharynx narrows but does not settle the organism; and the character of the nodes places them on the acute-to-sinister spectrum. The final decision separates a persistent viral papillomatosis from the two conditions most often reached for in a hoarse child.

### The immediate threat
Mr Ragab Hilmy, 71, has a stroke and is found choking during a meal. He is cyanosed and cannot speak.
Concept: CON-RES-0C581F605D7DB7
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What is producing his cyanosis at this moment?
*= Asphyxia — airway obstruction is preventing oxygen reaching the blood
Why: Correct. Obstruction of the airway stops gas exchange, arterial oxygen falls, and enough deoxygenated haemoglobin accumulates to be visible.
* Anaemic hypoxia from the acute illness
Why: A carriage failure, and his haemoglobin has not changed in the minutes described.
* Stagnant hypoxia from reduced cardiac output
Why: Output may fall later, but the initiating event is exclusion of air from the lung.
* Histotoxic hypoxia from the stroke
Why: A failure of cellular utilisation, caused by poisons rather than by a blocked airway.
Rationale: Cyanosis reports deoxygenated haemoglobin, and here the cause is mechanical. Naming the step that has failed is what makes clearing the airway the obvious first action.

### Which lung the material entered
Concept: CON-RES-EA9A6089817998
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Three days later he has a fever and a cavity on the film. Which side is it most likely on, and why?
*= The right, because the right main bronchus is wider, shorter and more vertical, so aspirated material passes into it preferentially
Why: Correct. A solitary aspiration abscess is more common on the right, and the geometry of the right main bronchus is the reason.
* The left, because it is longer and material collects along it
Why: The greater length and more horizontal course make the left less likely to receive aspirated material, not more.
* Either equally, since aspiration is random
Why: The asymmetry of the main bronchi makes it distinctly non-random.
* The right, because the right lung has three lobes
Why: Correct side, wrong reason — lobe count does not direct aspirated material.
Rationale: One anatomical asymmetry predicts the side of an aspiration pneumonia, an inhaled foreign body and a solitary abscess. A structural fact with a directly testable consequence.

### Naming the level of the lesion
Concept: CON-RES-BECD91B06EA39D
Difficulty: Easy
Q: The pathologist describes changes centred on the respiratory bronchiole. Which structure lies immediately proximal to it?
*= The terminal bronchiole
Why: Correct. The terminal bronchiole is the last purely conducting airway; the respiratory bronchiole immediately beyond it is the first that takes part in gas exchange.
* The alveolar duct
Why: The alveolar duct lies distal to the respiratory bronchiole.
* The segmental bronchus
Why: Many generations too proximal.
* The alveolar sac
Why: The most distal structure of all.
Rationale: Terminal, respiratory, duct, sac. That order is the reference against which every distal lung lesion is described, including the two patterns of emphysema.

### What the alveoli contain
Concept: CON-RES-A7AE91E130076E
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: The consolidated lung around the cavity shows alveolar spaces filled with what?
*= Acute inflammatory cells and red cells within a fibrin exudate
Why: Correct. Neutrophils and red cells packed into the alveolar spaces are what make consolidated lung solid and radiodense.
* Air, with thickened alveolar walls
Why: Air-filled alveoli are radiolucent; consolidation is the replacement of alveolar air.
* A protein-poor transudate
Why: That fills alveoli in cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, a different process.
* Fibrous tissue replacing the alveolar walls
Why: Fibrosis is a late outcome, not the acute exudative picture.
Rationale: Consolidation means alveolar air has been replaced. Naming the replacement — exudate here, transudate in heart failure, blood in haemorrhage — is what turns a white lung into a diagnosis.

### Reading the lungs at post-mortem
Concept: CON-RES-A6F61F4C0A2D64
Difficulty: Hard
Q: He dies, and his lungs also show emphysema. They are less voluminous and less pale than a classical panacinar specimen, and obscure the heart less. Which pattern is present, and what does the appearance reflect?
*= Centriacinar — destruction is centred on the respiratory bronchioles and is patchy, so the gross change is less marked than in panacinar disease where the whole acinus is destroyed
Why: Correct. Centriacinar emphysema spares much of the distal acinus and is typically upper-zone and smoking-related, so the lungs are less uniformly voluminous and pale than in panacinar disease.
* Panacinar, since any emphysema produces voluminous pale lungs
Why: Panacinar disease produces that appearance; the specimen described is explicitly less so.
* Both patterns equally, since the appearances are indistinguishable
Why: They are distinguishable to the naked eye, which is the point of the comparison.
* The appearance reflects only the amount of anthracotic pigment
Why: Pigment darkens a smoker's lung and is a real confounder, but volume and the overlap of the heart follow the pattern of destruction.
Rationale: Which part of the acinus is destroyed determines both the naked-eye appearance and the zonal distribution — centriacinar upper-zone in smokers, panacinar lower-zone and in alpha-1-antitrypsin deficiency.
## debrief
One aspiration event runs through four levels of the same lung: the immediate asphyxia, the bronchial geometry that decides the side, the airway level at which the damage centres, and the alveolar contents that make consolidation visible. The final decision is a bench comparison — the pattern of emphysema is read from volume and colour before any microscope is involved.

### Why the saturation is wrong
Concept: CON-RES-F6434978BBC927
Difficulty: Easy
Q: A patient with carbon-monoxide poisoning has a pulse oximetry reading of 98%. Why is the oxygen content of his blood nevertheless low?
*= Carbon monoxide occupies the oxygen-binding sites, so much of the haemoglobin carries none — and the oximeter cannot distinguish carboxyhaemoglobin from oxyhaemoglobin
Why: Correct. The binding sites are taken, so carrying capacity falls; a standard pulse oximeter reads the two species alike and reports a falsely reassuring figure.
* The oximeter is faulty and should be replaced
Why: The device is working as designed; the limitation is which species it can distinguish.
* The haemoglobin concentration is low, so saturation is misleading
Why: Saturation is a percentage and is not invalidated by a low haemoglobin; the issue here is which molecules are being counted as saturated.
* Oxygen is bound but cannot be released to the tissues
Why: That is the additional left-shift injury, which compounds the problem but is not why the saturation reads high.
Explanation: A pulse oximeter measures the proportion of haemoglobin that looks saturated. Carboxyhaemoglobin looks saturated and carries nothing, which is why the number must be disregarded and a co-oximeter used.

### The second injury
Concept: CON-RES-AA4C315125E587
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What does carboxyhaemoglobin do to the oxygen carried by the remaining normal sites?
*= It raises their affinity, shifting the dissociation curve left so less oxygen is released to the tissues
Why: Correct. The remaining sites bind oxygen more tightly, compounding the reduced carrying capacity with impaired unloading.
* It lowers their affinity, shifting the curve right
Why: A right shift would improve unloading and partly compensate, which is the opposite of what happens.
* It has no effect on the remaining haemoglobin
Why: Treats the four binding sites as independent, missing the cooperative effect that makes this poisoning worse than an equivalent anaemia.
* It prevents the remaining sites from binding oxygen at all
Why: They bind normally; the problem is releasing.
Explanation: Two injuries from one exposure — less oxygen carried, and what is carried held too tightly. That is why carbon-monoxide poisoning is more dangerous than an anaemia of the same carrying capacity.

### The colour
Concept: CON-RES-CDBFCE15A0F579
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What colour change is classically described, and why is cyanosis characteristically absent?
*= Cherry-red skin and mucous membranes — and cyanosis is absent because carboxyhaemoglobin is bright red rather than the dark colour of deoxygenated haemoglobin
Why: Correct. Cyanosis requires an accumulation of deoxygenated haemoglobin, and carboxyhaemoglobin is brightly coloured, so the patient does not look hypoxic.
* Central cyanosis, deepening as the level rises
Why: The characteristic absence of cyanosis is one of the features that makes this poisoning easy to miss.
* Pallor with cold peripheries
Why: Non-specific signs of poor perfusion rather than of an abnormal haemoglobin species.
* Jaundice of the sclerae
Why: A sign of bilirubin, unrelated to haemoglobin binding.
Explanation: Both the eye and the oximeter are deceived by a bright red haemoglobin that carries nothing. The diagnosis therefore rests on the history and a measured carboxyhaemoglobin, not on appearance.

### What determines the level
Concept: CON-RES-7AB0731A15AD7B
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Two people were in the same room, one for one hour and one for four. What determines the carboxyhaemoglobin each has reached?
*= The concentration of carbon monoxide and the duration of exposure, so the one exposed for longer will have the higher level
Why: Correct. Uptake accumulates with both ambient concentration and time, so exposure history predicts the level more reliably than symptoms do.
* Body weight alone, so the larger person will have the lower level
Why: Size influences volume of distribution modestly but is not what determines the level reached.
* The severity of symptoms, which correlates precisely with the level
Why: Symptoms correlate poorly, varying with age and comorbidity, which is why measurement is needed.
* Nothing predictable, so history is unhelpful in triage
Why: The opposite: history identifies who needs measuring, including those who feel well.
Explanation: Concentration multiplied by time. That is why everyone from the same room is assessed, and why the asymptomatic are measured rather than reassured.

### Treating it in order
Concept: CON-RES-71F421E3C883F6
Difficulty: Challenging
Q: Put the treatment in order and justify the first step.
*= Terminate the exposure first, then give high-concentration oxygen to displace the carbon monoxide, with exchange transfusion reserved for severe poisoning — because nothing else can work while uptake continues
Why: Correct. Removal from the source stops further uptake; oxygen then competes carbon monoxide off the binding sites; exchange transfusion replaces the carrier itself when poisoning is severe. Each step follows from the mechanism.
* Give oxygen first, and remove the patient from the source afterwards
Why: Oxygen given while exposure continues is working against ongoing uptake, which is why removal comes first.
* Measure the carboxyhaemoglobin first, and treat according to the level
Why: Measurement guides later decisions but must not delay ending the exposure.
* Give a mucolytic to clear the airway, then oxygen
Why: Mucolytics act on secretions and have no bearing on a gas bound to haemoglobin.
Explanation: The order follows the mechanism: stop the input, displace what is bound, and if necessary replace the carrier. Sequencing treatment from mechanism is what makes it recallable under pressure.

### The category with a normal arterial oxygen tension
Four patients are presented on a post-take round, each hypoxic for a different reason.
Concept: CON-RES-22FEB4F22E7D79
Difficulty: Easy
Q: A woman with severe iron-deficiency anaemia has a normal arterial oxygen tension but inadequate delivery. What category is this?
*= Anaemic hypoxia — a failure of oxygen carriage
Why: Correct. Carriage depends on haemoglobin; with normal lungs and circulation, a low haemoglobin reduces delivery while the arterial oxygen tension stays normal.
* Hypoxic hypoxia
Why: That gives a low arterial oxygen tension, which she does not have.
* Stagnant hypoxia
Why: A delivery failure due to inadequate flow, which is not described.
* Histotoxic hypoxia
Why: A failure of utilisation by the tissue, not of carriage.
Rationale: A normal arterial oxygen tension does not mean adequate oxygen delivery. That single point is why an arterial blood gas can be reassuring and wrong.

### The same category, a different cause
Concept: CON-RES-7B609D48BFC644
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A second patient has a normal haemoglobin concentration but an abnormal haemoglobin variant. Why is he also in that category?
*= Because carriage depends on functioning haemoglobin, not on its concentration — an abnormal variant reduces the oxygen actually carried
Why: Correct. Anaemic hypoxia is defined by the carrier, whether there is too little of it or the wrong kind.
* Because an abnormal haemoglobin reduces cardiac output
Why: Output is a delivery matter and is not what an abnormal variant primarily affects.
* Because abnormal haemoglobin prevents oxygenation in the lung
Why: The lung oxygenates whatever can be oxygenated; the defect is in the carrier.
* Because he must also be anaemic for this to apply
Why: Concentration can be normal; the category depends on function, not number.
Rationale: Counting haemoglobin is not the same as measuring what it can carry. Both a low concentration and a defective molecule land in the same category.

### The category confined to one limb
Concept: CON-RES-654A12F4B21CC0
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A third patient has a cold, painful leg after an arterial embolism. What category, and what is notable about its distribution?
*= Stagnant hypoxia, and it is localised — obstruction of flow to one region while the rest of the body is normally perfused
Why: Correct. Arterial or venous obstruction produces a regional delivery failure; the systemic arterial oxygen content is unaffected.
* Hypoxic hypoxia, confined to the leg
Why: Hypoxic hypoxia is a failure of oxygenation in the lung and is necessarily systemic.
* Anaemic hypoxia, since the clot consumes haemoglobin
Why: Clot formation does not meaningfully reduce circulating haemoglobin.
* Histotoxic hypoxia, since the ischaemic cells stop using oxygen
Why: They stop because oxygen is not arriving; the failure is of delivery.
Rationale: Stagnant hypoxia can be local or general. Recognising it as a delivery failure is what makes restoring flow the treatment rather than giving more oxygen.

### The category where oxygen arrives and is not used
Concept: CON-RES-A0F793A4A37DC0
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A fourth patient is intoxicated with alcohol and opioids and is said to have a histotoxic component. What is the proposed mechanism?
*= Interference with the cellular oxidative enzymes, leaving cytochrome oxidised so that oxygen delivered cannot be used
Why: Correct. Alcohol and narcotics can block dehydrogenase activity and leave cytochrome in its oxidised state, impairing utilisation despite adequate delivery.
* Depression of the respiratory centre, which is the whole mechanism
Why: Respiratory depression is real and important in this patient, but it produces hypoxic hypoxia; the histotoxic component is separate.
* Displacement of oxygen from haemoglobin
Why: That describes carbon monoxide, a carriage failure.
* Reduction of cardiac output
Why: That would be stagnant hypoxia.
Rationale: Three of the four categories are failures to deliver oxygen; only this one is a failure to use it. That is why the venous oxygen content is characteristically high — the oxygen arrives and returns unused.

### Putting the four together
Concept: CON-RES-CDBFCE15A0F579
Difficulty: Hard
Q: Which of these four patients would you expect to be cyanosed, and what does that tell you about cyanosis as a sign?
*= Cyanosis needs an absolute amount of deoxygenated haemoglobin, so it appears in hypoxic and stagnant hypoxia but is unreliable in severe anaemia and characteristically absent in carbon-monoxide poisoning, where the haemoglobin is cherry red
Why: Correct. Cyanosis depends on the quantity of deoxygenated haemoglobin present, not on the severity of tissue hypoxia — so a severely anaemic patient may have too little haemoglobin to appear blue, and carbon-monoxide poisoning produces a bright red colour instead.
* All four would be cyanosed, since all are hypoxic
Why: Cyanosis tracks a species of haemoglobin, not the adequacy of tissue oxygenation.
* None would be cyanosed, since cyanosis indicates cardiac disease
Why: Cyanosis is a sign of deoxygenated haemoglobin from any cause.
* Only the anaemic patient, since low haemoglobin causes cyanosis
Why: The reverse — a very low haemoglobin makes cyanosis less likely to appear, because the absolute amount of deoxygenated haemoglobin cannot reach the threshold.
Rationale: Cyanosis is a measurement of one species of haemoglobin, not a measure of oxygenation. Its absence in the two most dangerous categories — severe anaemia and carbon-monoxide poisoning — is exactly why it cannot be used to exclude hypoxia.
## debrief
Four patients, four steps in one chain: oxygenation, carriage, delivery, utilisation. The final decision is the practical warning — cyanosis reports deoxygenated haemoglobin rather than tissue oxygenation, so it is absent in precisely the two situations where a clinician most wants a visible sign.
