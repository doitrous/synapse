### Naming the organ at low power
Difficulty: Easy
A low-power field shows a small bean-shaped organ enclosed by a thin capsule of dense fibrous connective tissue, with a notch on one surface where vessels enter and leave. Thin septa run inwards from the deep surface of the capsule. The outer zone contains regularly arranged rounded aggregations of small lymphocytes, each separated from the capsule and from the septa by a clear space. Deeper in, the tissue is arranged as irregular branching cords with spaces between them.
Q: Which organ is this?
*= A lymph node
Why: Correct. A thin capsule, septa descending from its deep surface, a cortex of regular follicles bounded by clear sinuses, and a medulla of cords and sinuses is the lymph node and only the lymph node.
* The spleen
Why: Picked by students who see follicles and stop there. The spleen has a thick capsule rich in smooth muscle, thick trabeculae radiating from the hilum, and its nodules sit scattered in a red background — and it has no lymph sinuses at all.
* A palatine tonsil
Why: Picked by students who read any mass of follicles as tonsil. A tonsil is covered by stratified squamous epithelium that dips inwards as crypts, and its capsule is incomplete and lies on one side only.
* The thymus
Why: Picked by students who read the septa as lobulation. The thymus has no follicles and no sinuses; its lobules show a dark cortex and a pale medulla containing Hassall's corpuscles.
Explanation: Identify a lymphoid organ in a fixed order — capsule, then what runs in from it, then whether the parenchyma is divided into cortex and medulla, then what occupies each. Capsule thin, septa from the capsule, cortex of follicles, medulla of cords, and clear sinuses throughout is a lymph node. Every other lymphoid organ fails at least one of those four steps.

### The pale centre inside a follicle
Difficulty: Moderate
Higher power is turned on one of the rounded cortical aggregations. Its periphery is a dark rim of closely packed small lymphocytes. Its centre is distinctly paler and holds large cells with pale nuclei, together with occasional cells that have an eccentric nucleus and strongly basophilic cytoplasm, and scattered macrophages.
Q: What is the pale central area, and what does its presence tell you about this follicle?
*= A germinal centre — so this is a secondary follicle, one that has met antigen
Why: Correct. A primary follicle is a uniform aggregate of small B lymphocytes. On exposure to antigen some of them enlarge into activated lymphocytes that collect at the centre, and that pale germinal centre is what converts a primary follicle into a secondary one.
* A paracortical island of T lymphocytes lying within the follicle
Why: Picked by students who know the paracortex is T-dependent and place it inside the follicle. The paracortex lies between the follicular cortex and the medulla, not within a follicle, and it contains no germinal centre.
* A medullary sinus cut across the plane of the follicle
Why: A sinus is a space lined by endothelium and macrophages, not a cellular area. The pale zone here is full of large cells, which is the opposite of a sinus.
* A zone of necrosis, indicating that the node is inflamed
Why: Picked by students who read "pale" as "dead". The cells in a germinal centre are large, active and mitotic; necrosis would show pyknotic debris rather than plump nuclei and plasma cells.
Explanation: Pale does not mean empty. In lymphoid tissue a pale area usually means large activated cells with dispersed chromatin, and dark means small resting lymphocytes with dense nuclei. Reading pallor that way turns a germinal centre from an oddity into a statement that this node has been asked to make antibody.

### The zone with no follicles
Difficulty: Moderate
Between the follicular cortex and the medulla there is a band of diffusely arranged lymphocytes with no follicles and no germinal centres. Running through it are venules lined by unusually tall, almost cubical endothelial cells rather than the flat lining of an ordinary venule.
Q: What is this zone called, and how do its lymphocytes arrive there?
*= The paracortex, the thymus-dependent zone; its T lymphocytes leave the blood by crossing those cubical-lined post-capillary venules
Why: Correct. The paracortex lies between cortex and medulla and is populated by T lymphocytes that have migrated from the thymus, entering the node through post-capillary venules whose cubical lining carries the receptors that let a T cell home there.
* The subcapsular sinus, into which afferent lymph delivers its lymphocytes
Why: Picked by students who know lymphocytes arrive with the lymph. The subcapsular sinus is a space immediately beneath the capsule, and it is a lumen, not a diffusely cellular zone.
* The medullary cord, which lies between cortex and medulla
Why: Medullary cords lie in the medulla itself, are irregular and branching, and contain plasma cells and macrophages as well as B lymphocytes.
* A primary follicle that has lost its germinal centre
Why: Picked by students who read "no germinal centre" as "primary follicle". A primary follicle is still a discrete rounded aggregate; the paracortex has no follicular outline at all.
Explanation: Two entrances, two populations. B lymphocytes arrive with the afferent lymph and settle in the follicles; T lymphocytes arrive from the blood through the cubical-lined post-capillary venules of the paracortex. Finding those venules on a slide is what names the zone, and it also explains why a node depleted of T cells is depleted here and nowhere else.

### Cords and the spaces between them
Difficulty: Moderate
The deep part of the parenchyma is arranged as irregular branching strands of closely packed cells, some of which appear continuous with the overlying cortical follicles. Between the strands, and between them and the septa, lie clear channels lined by flattened cells among which sit large cells with abundant cytoplasm.
Q: Name the strands and the channels, and say what the large cells lining the channels are doing.
*= Medullary cords and medullary sinuses; the large cells are macrophages, and they filter the lymph as it passes
Why: Correct. Medullary cords hold B lymphocytes, plasma cells and macrophages; the medullary sinuses between them are lined by endothelium and macrophages, and the lymph arriving from the cortical sinuses is filtered there before leaving at the hilum.
* Splenic cords and blood sinusoids; the large cells are littoral cells
Why: This is the red pulp of the spleen. Splenic cords lie between white pulps and are packed with red cells; the organ in front of you has follicles bounded by lymph sinuses and no red pulp at all.
* Trabeculae and trabecular sinuses; the large cells are fibroblasts
Why: Trabeculae are connective-tissue septa, pale and fibrous. These strands are cellular and continuous with the cortex above them, which a trabecula never is.
* Medullary cords and lymphatic capillaries; the large cells are endothelial cells only
Why: Half right, and the half that is wrong matters. The lining is not endothelium alone — macrophages sit in and on the wall, and they are the reason the sinus filters rather than merely conducts.
Explanation: Follow the lymph rather than the layers. It enters at the convex surface, fills the subcapsular sinus, runs down the trabecular sinuses into the medullary sinuses, and leaves at the hilum. Macrophages line every one of those spaces, so the path of the lymph is also the surface on which the node does its filtering.

### Telling this slide from a spleen
Difficulty: Hard
A second slide is put up alongside. It also shows an encapsulated organ containing rounded lymphocyte aggregations, but the capsule is thicker, the trabeculae are thick and radiate from one region, the nodules are scattered irregularly in a darker background, and each nodule has a small vessel running eccentrically within it.
Q: Which two features on the second slide most securely exclude a lymph node?
*= Every nodule carries an eccentric central arteriole, and there are no lymph sinuses anywhere
Why: Correct. A Malpighian corpuscle of the spleen is built concentrically around a central (follicular) arteriole, and the spleen has blood sinusoids rather than lymph sinuses. A lymph node has neither.
* The nodules are larger, and the capsule stains more darkly
Why: Picked by students who reach for quantitative differences. Size and staining intensity vary with fixation and with the plane of section, and neither excludes anything.
* The nodules contain germinal centres, which a lymph node does not
Why: Reverses the fact. Germinal centres occur in both organs; the splenic white pulp has one, sitting eccentrically around its arteriole.
* The organ has a hilum, which a lymph node lacks
Why: A lymph node has a hilum too — it is the concave surface where the arteries enter and the efferent lymphatics leave.
Explanation: Distinguish the two on architecture, not on quantity. The lymph node is organised around a path for lymph — regular follicles bounded by sinuses, cords, and sinuses again. The spleen is organised around a path for blood — nodules built on an arteriole, and sinusoids instead of sinuses. Ask which fluid the organ is built to filter, and the slide names itself.

## learning_objective
Identify a lymph node from a stained section, name its capsule, septa, cortical follicles, paracortex, medullary cords and lymph sinuses, distinguish a primary from a secondary follicle, and separate a lymph node from a spleen on architecture rather than on size or staining.

## lab_questions
### Naming the organ at low power
Difficulty: Easy
A low-power field shows a large organ under a thick capsule of dense connective tissue containing smooth muscle and elastic fibres, with a serosal covering on its outer surface. Long thick bands run inwards, most of them radiating from one region and carrying vessels within them. The parenchyma is a dark, diffusely cellular background in which rounded paler nodules of lymphocytes are scattered irregularly. There are no clear lymph-filled channels anywhere in the section.
Q: Which organ is this?
*= The spleen
Why: Correct. A thick muscular capsule, thick vessel-carrying trabeculae radiating from the hilum, lymphoid nodules scattered in a cell-rich background, and the complete absence of lymph sinuses together identify the spleen.
* A lymph node
Why: Picked by students who see lymphoid nodules and go no further. A lymph node has a thin capsule, thin septa from its deep surface, follicles arranged regularly in a cortex, and lymph sinuses — which this section does not have.
* The thymus
Why: The thymus is divided into lobules with a dark cortex and a pale medulla, and it contains no lymphoid nodules at all.
* A palatine tonsil
Why: A tonsil is a surface organ: it is covered by stratified squamous epithelium that dips in as crypts, and its capsule is incomplete and confined to its deep aspect.
Explanation: Read the capsule and the trabeculae before the parenchyma. Thick, muscular and elastic with thick trabeculae from a hilum is a spleen; thin with delicate septa is a node. Then confirm on the parenchyma: nodules scattered in a red background rather than follicles ordered in a cortex.

### The vessel inside the nodule
Difficulty: Moderate
One of the paler rounded nodules is examined at higher power. Within it, but not at its geometric centre, lies a small arteriole with an obvious muscular wall, and the lymphocytes are arranged concentrically around it.
Q: What is this vessel called, and what does finding it settle?
*= The central, or follicular, arteriole — it settles that the nodule is splenic white pulp rather than a lymph-node follicle
Why: Correct. Every Malpighian corpuscle is built concentrically on a central (follicular) arteriole, which lies eccentrically despite its name. A lymph-node follicle has no such vessel, so the arteriole alone distinguishes the two organs.
* An afferent lymphatic, cut in cross-section as it enters the follicle
Why: Picked by students who transfer the lymph node's afferent supply to the spleen. The spleen filters blood; it has no afferent lymphatics, and the vessel here has a muscular arterial wall.
* A post-capillary venule of the thymus-dependent zone
Why: Post-capillary venules are thin-walled and lined by cubical endothelium; this vessel has a muscular media. The T-cell zone here is the sheath around the arteriole, not the vessel itself.
* A trabecular artery, which has left its trabecula in the plane of section
Why: A trabecular artery lies inside a connective-tissue trabecula and is much larger. The arteriole here lies within lymphoid tissue, which is precisely what makes it follicular.
Explanation: One vessel decides the organ. A rounded lymphoid nodule with an eccentric muscular arteriole running through it is splenic white pulp; the same nodule without one is a lymph-node follicle. It also explains the sequence of zones, because everything in the white pulp is arranged by distance from that arteriole.

### Reading the zones outwards from the arteriole
Difficulty: Moderate
The same nodule is examined systematically. Immediately around the arteriole is a sheath of small lymphocytes. Beyond it lies a pale central area of large cells, surrounded in turn by a darker ring of densely packed small lymphocytes, and the periphery of the nodule is a looser zone containing lymphocytes of both classes together with plasma cells and macrophages.
Q: Name the four zones from the arteriole outwards, and say which lymphocyte class occupies the innermost.
*= Periarteriolar lymphatic sheath, germinal centre, follicular zone and marginal zone, with T lymphocytes in the sheath
Why: Correct. The periarteriolar lymphatic sheath is the thymus-dependent zone and ensheathes the arteriole; outside it lie the pale germinal centre, the darker follicular zone of B lymphocytes, and the marginal zone at the periphery.
* Marginal zone, follicular zone, germinal centre and periarteriolar sheath, with B lymphocytes innermost
Why: Reverses the order. The sheath is defined by lying on the arteriole, so it cannot be the outermost zone, and it is a T-cell rather than a B-cell zone.
* Germinal centre, periarteriolar sheath, marginal zone and follicular zone, with T lymphocytes innermost
Why: Gets the innermost cell type right but scrambles the sequence. The germinal centre lies outside the sheath, not inside it.
* Cortex, paracortex, medullary cords and marginal zone, with T lymphocytes in the paracortex
Why: Imports the lymph node's vocabulary. The spleen has no cortex, no paracortex and no medulla; those terms belong to an organ with a different plan.
Explanation: In the white pulp, position is defined by distance from the arteriole, and it maps onto function: T cells on the vessel where blood-borne antigen first arrives, then the B-cell machinery of germinal centre and follicular zone, then the marginal zone where the two populations meet the incoming blood. Naming the zones outwards, in that order, is how the slide is read.

### The background the nodules sit in
Difficulty: Moderate
The darker background between the nodules is examined. It consists of irregular strands of cells packed with erythrocytes, granulocytes, lymphocytes, monocytes, platelets, plasma cells and macrophages, separated by wide, irregular, barrel-shaped channels. The channels are lined by elongated cells with gaps between them, and further macrophages sit within and around the lining.
Q: Name the strands and the channels, and say what the gaps in the lining allow.
*= Splenic (Billroth) cords and blood sinusoids lined by stave cells; the wide intercellular gaps and discontinuous basal lamina let blood cells pass from the cords back into the circulation
Why: Correct. The red pulp is cords and sinusoids. The sinusoidal lining of elongated stave cells has wide intercellular spaces over a non-continuous basal lamina, and that arrangement is what allows cells in the cords to re-enter the blood stream.
* Medullary cords and medullary sinuses; the gaps let lymph drain to the efferent lymphatic
Why: Applies the lymph node's medulla to the spleen. The spleen's channels carry blood, not lymph, and it has no efferent lymphatic path of this kind.
* Splenic cords and lymphatic capillaries; the gaps admit large molecules such as fat droplets
Why: Describes a lymphatic capillary. These channels are wide, barrel-shaped and full of red cells, which no lymphatic capillary is.
* Trabeculae and trabecular veins; the gaps are artefacts of shrinkage during fixation
Why: Picked by students who dismiss an unfamiliar lining as an artefact. The discontinuity of the stave-cell lining is a real feature and is the structural basis of splenic filtration.
Explanation: A sinusoid is defined by what its wall does not do — it does not form a continuous seal. Wide gaps between stave cells and an interrupted basal lamina make the red pulp a place where every circulating cell is squeezed past a macrophage, which is how the spleen removes aged erythrocytes and stores platelets.

### Where the blood goes between artery and vein
Difficulty: Challenging
The student is asked how blood travels from the penicillar arterioles of the red pulp into the venous sinuses, and is told that this has been described in more than one way.
Q: What do the open and closed theories of splenic circulation each propose, and how does the third account reconcile them?
*= The closed theory has the capillaries opening directly into the sinusoids; the open theory has them discharging into the cord tissue, from which blood re-enters through the gaps in the sinusoidal wall; the third account holds that the circulation is closed while the spleen is contracted and open while it is relaxed
Why: Correct. Both routes have been described, and the open-and-closed account makes them states of one organ rather than rival claims, tying the route to the contractile state of the capsule and trabeculae.
* The closed theory has blood entering the cords and the open theory has it entering the sinusoids directly; the third account holds that both occur simultaneously in all conditions
Why: Reverses the two theories. Closed means the vessel is continuous into the sinusoid; open means the blood leaves the vessel into the tissue.
* Both theories describe entry into the cords, differing only over whether macrophages are involved
Why: Removes the actual disagreement, which is about whether the blood leaves the vascular channel at all.
* The open theory applies to the white pulp and the closed theory to the red pulp
Why: Picked by students who attach each theory to a compartment. Both theories describe the same red-pulp circulation; they are alternative routes through it.
Explanation: The disagreement matters because the open route is what makes the spleen a filter. If blood must leave the vessel and squeeze back in through the sinusoidal gaps, every cell is tested for deformability and every macrophage gets a look at it — which is precisely how aged erythrocytes are removed and why the spleen enlarges when that work increases.

## learning_objective
Identify the spleen from a stained section, use the central arteriole to distinguish splenic white pulp from a lymph-node follicle, name the four zones of the white pulp outwards from the arteriole, describe the cords and stave-cell sinusoids of the red pulp, and explain how the open and closed routes account for splenic filtration.

## lab_questions
### Naming the organ at low power
Difficulty: Easy
A low-power field shows a mass of lymphoid tissue lying immediately beneath a free surface. The surface is covered by non-keratinised stratified squamous epithelium, which dips down into the lymphoid tissue as several deep, branching invaginations containing desquamated cells and debris. Lymphatic nodules, many with germinal centres, are arranged around these invaginations. On the deep aspect only, a layer of dense connective tissue separates the mass from the surrounding tissue.
Q: Which organ is this, and what are the invaginations called?
*= A palatine tonsil, and the invaginations are tonsillar crypts
Why: Correct. Non-keratinised stratified squamous epithelium dipping into lymphoid tissue as crypts, with nodules arranged around them and a capsule on the deep aspect only, is the palatine tonsil.
* A lymph node, and the invaginations are trabecular sinuses
Why: Picked by students who read a channel in lymphoid tissue as a sinus. A sinus is an endothelium-lined space inside a fully encapsulated organ; these invaginations are lined by surface epithelium and open onto a free surface.
* The spleen, and the invaginations are splenic cords
Why: The spleen is an intra-abdominal organ with no epithelial surface at all, and splenic cords are strands of cells, not epithelial-lined channels.
* The thymus, and the invaginations are Hassall's corpuscles
Why: Hassall's corpuscles are small solid concentric bodies in the thymic medulla, not invaginations of a surface, and the thymus has no lymphoid nodules.
Explanation: Ask first whether the organ has a free surface. A covering epithelium places the specimen at an interface with the outside world and immediately excludes lymph node, spleen and thymus. Then ask what the epithelium does: dipping inwards as crypts, with nodules arranged around them, names the tonsil.

### The capsule that only goes half way round
Difficulty: Moderate
The dense connective tissue seen on the deep aspect is followed round the specimen and is found to stop where the lymphoid tissue reaches the epithelium.
Q: Why is the tonsil described as incompletely encapsulated, and what follows from that?
*= Its capsule covers only the deep aspect, because the superficial aspect is the epithelial surface itself; the lymphoid tissue therefore meets the lumen of the pharynx directly
Why: Correct. The dense connective tissue separates the tonsil from the structures deep to it, while its free surface is covered by epithelium instead. That direct exposure to the pharyngeal lumen is what puts the tonsil in contact with inhaled and swallowed antigen.
* Its capsule is perforated by afferent lymphatics, as in a lymph node
Why: Picked by students transferring the lymph node's afferent supply. It is the absence of a capsule on the free surface, not perforation of one, that makes the encapsulation incomplete.
* Its capsule is thinned by the smooth muscle within it, so it appears absent
Why: Invents a mechanism. Smooth muscle in a capsule is a feature of the spleen; the tonsillar capsule is simply not present superficially.
* Its capsule is incomplete only in childhood and becomes complete after puberty
Why: Confuses the tonsil with the thymus, which is the lymphoid organ that changes markedly at puberty.
Explanation: Capsules mark boundaries with the body's interior. A tonsil needs a boundary on one side only, because on the other side its job is to be exposed. Reading the capsule as one-sided rather than absent is also what keeps the tonsil distinguishable from the diffuse lymphoid tissue of the gut and airway.

### Two tonsils, two fates for the crypts
Difficulty: Moderate
A second slide from the base of the tongue shows the same non-keratinised stratified squamous epithelium dipping into lymphoid nodules as crypts. On this slide, however, no dense connective tissue is present at the deep aspect, and the ducts of mucous glands are seen opening into the bases of the crypts.
Q: Which tonsil is this, and why does it become infected less often than the palatine tonsil?
*= The lingual tonsil; its mucous gland ducts open into the bases of the crypts and wash them continuously, whereas in the palatine tonsil those ducts open on the surface instead
Why: Correct. In the palatine tonsil the mucous ducts open on the free surface, leaving the crypts to accumulate bacteria, desquamated cells and debris. In the lingual tonsil the ducts discharge into the crypt bases, flushing them, which is why crypt infection is uncommon there.
* The lingual tonsil; it has a complete capsule that prevents organisms from reaching it
Why: Reverses the capsule. The lingual tonsil has no capsule at all, while the palatine tonsil has one on its deep aspect.
* The pharyngeal tonsil; its ciliated epithelium clears the crypts
Why: The pharyngeal tonsil is covered by pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium and has no crypts to clear.
* The palatine tonsil again, sectioned at a different level where the ducts happen to open into the crypts
Why: Picked by students who treat the duct openings as a plane-of-section effect. Where the ducts open is a fixed feature of each tonsil and is the basis of the comparison.
Explanation: The clinical difference between these two tonsils is a plumbing difference. A crypt with a gland discharging into it is rinsed; a crypt with the gland discharging beside it is a pocket. That single detail explains why tonsillitis means the palatine tonsil almost every time.

### The tonsil that has no crypts
Difficulty: Hard
A third slide shows a single midline mass of lymphoid tissue beneath a mucous membrane. Its surface is thrown into folds but shows no invaginations into the lymphoid tissue, and the covering epithelium is pseudostratified columnar ciliated with goblet cells. Dense connective tissue is present on the deep aspect but does not surround the mass.
Q: Which tonsil is this, and what does the epithelium tell you about where the specimen was taken?
*= The pharyngeal tonsil, and its respiratory epithelium places it in the nasopharynx
Why: Correct. The pharyngeal tonsil is a single midline mass under the nasopharyngeal mucosa, covered by folded pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium with goblet cells, and it has no crypts.
* The palatine tonsil, sectioned tangentially so that the crypts are missed
Why: Picked by students who explain away the missing crypts. The epithelium settles it: the palatine tonsil is covered by stratified squamous epithelium, not by respiratory epithelium.
* The lingual tonsil, whose crypts are shallow and may not appear in every section
Why: The lingual tonsil is also covered by stratified squamous epithelium and does have crypts; neither fits this slide.
* A paired mass in the lateral wall of the oropharynx, since all tonsils are paired
Why: Picked by students who generalise from the palatine tonsil. The pharyngeal tonsil is single and midline, which is part of its identification.
Explanation: Let the epithelium place the specimen. Stratified squamous means oropharynx or tongue base and therefore palatine or lingual; pseudostratified ciliated columnar with goblet cells means nasopharynx and therefore pharyngeal. Once the site is fixed, the presence or absence of crypts and the capsule finish the identification.

### What the tissue is doing there
Difficulty: Moderate
The student is asked to account for the arrangement of nodules around the crypts of the palatine tonsil, and for the cells found in the diffuse tissue between them.
Q: What is the functional point of that arrangement?
*= The crypts increase the epithelial surface exposed to swallowed and inhaled antigen, and the nodules and diffuse tissue around them contain the lymphocytes, plasma cells and macrophages that respond by producing antibody
Why: Correct. The tonsil guards the entrance to the digestive and respiratory tracts, and the crypts multiply the area over which antigen is sampled. The nodules with their germinal centres and the surrounding plasma cells and macrophages are the response.
* The crypts drain lymph from the pharynx into the nodules, which filter it as a lymph node does
Why: Picked by students who make the tonsil a lymph node. Crypts are epithelial invaginations open to the pharynx, not lymphatic channels, and the tonsil has no lymph sinuses.
* The crypts secrete mucus, and the nodules supply the cells that keep the mucus sterile
Why: Reverses the direction of secretion. Mucus arrives from glands in the underlying connective tissue; the crypt itself is an epithelial pocket, not a gland.
* The nodules are a store of lymphocytes to be released into the blood, and the crypts are the route by which they leave
Why: Invents an exit route. Lymphocytes leave a tonsil by efferent lymphatics, not through the epithelium into the pharyngeal lumen.
Explanation: Surface area, exposed on purpose. The tonsil's whole plan is to bring lymphoid tissue as close as possible to what is swallowed and inhaled, and the crypt is how a small mass acquires a large sampling surface. Reading the crypt as an exposure device rather than a duct is what makes both the histology and the tendency to tonsillitis follow.

## lab_questions
### Naming the organ at low power
Difficulty: Easy
A low-power field shows an organ enclosed by a thin connective-tissue capsule from which thin septa run inwards and incompletely subdivide it into many lobules. Each lobule has a darkly staining outer zone densely packed with small lymphocytes and a paler inner zone with fewer lymphocytes, in which small rounded acidophilic bodies are scattered. The pale inner zones of adjacent lobules run into one another. There are no rounded lymphatic nodules anywhere in the section.
Q: Which organ is this?
*= The thymus
Why: Correct. Incomplete lobulation by thin septa, a dark cortex and a pale medulla in each lobule, medullae continuous between lobules, acidophilic corpuscles in the medulla, and no lymphatic nodules at all together identify the thymus.
* A lymph node
Why: Picked by students who read cortex and medulla as diagnostic on their own. A lymph node's cortex contains discrete rounded follicles and its parenchyma is bounded by lymph sinuses; neither is present here.
* The spleen
Why: The spleen has a thick muscular capsule, thick trabeculae from a hilum, and rounded nodules built on central arterioles. This section has none of those.
* A palatine tonsil
Why: A tonsil has a covering epithelium with crypts and lymphatic nodules around them. This section has no free surface and no nodules.
Explanation: The thymus is the lymphoid organ identified by what it lacks. No nodules, no germinal centres, no sinuses — and in their place, lobules with a dark cortex, a pale medulla that is continuous from lobule to lobule, and corpuscles found nowhere else. Learning the absences is what makes the slide unmistakable.

### The bodies in the pale zone
Difficulty: Easy
One of the small rounded acidophilic bodies in the pale zone is examined at higher power. It has a homogeneous acidophilic centre made of degenerating cells, surrounded by several concentric layers of flattened cells.
Q: What is this structure, and where in the organ is it found?
*= A Hassall's corpuscle, found only in the thymic medulla
Why: Correct. Hassall's corpuscles are small rounded bodies of centrally degenerating reticular cells surrounded by concentric layers of epithelial reticular cells, and they occur in the medulla. Their number increases with age.
* A germinal centre, found in the cortex of the lobule
Why: Picked by students who read any pale rounded structure as a germinal centre. A germinal centre is a cellular zone of large lymphocytes, and the thymus contains none, since it has no lymphatic nodules.
* A Malpighian corpuscle, built around a central arteriole
Why: Imports the spleen's white pulp. A Malpighian corpuscle is lymphoid tissue around an arteriole; a Hassall's corpuscle is epithelial and has no vessel.
* A calcified lymphatic nodule, indicating involution of the organ
Why: Picked by students who read acidophilia as calcification. Hassall's corpuscles are a normal constituent of the thymic medulla at every age, not a sign of involution.
Explanation: One structure decides the slide. A concentric epithelial body with a degenerating acidophilic centre occurs in the thymic medulla and nowhere else in the body, so finding it identifies both the organ and which zone of the lobule you are in.

### The cells that are not lymphocytes
Difficulty: Moderate
The cortex is examined at high power. Among the densely packed small lymphocytes lie branched cells with large oval pale nuclei and prominent nucleoli, whose long processes contact one another and appear to form a network on which the lymphocytes rest.
Q: What are these branched cells, what is their embryological origin, and how do they differ from the reticular cells of a lymph node?
*= Epithelial reticular cells, derived from endoderm, and unlike the reticular cells of a lymph node they do not produce reticular fibres
Why: Correct. The thymic stroma is epithelial: its reticular cells arise from endoderm, are joined by desmosomes and tight junctions, contain cytokeratin filaments, and form a cellular reticulum rather than a fibre network.
* Reticular cells of mesodermal origin, which produce the reticular fibres that support the organ
Why: Describes the reticular cells of a lymph node or spleen. The thymus is the exception, and being the exception is the point of the question.
* Macrophages, which phagocytose degenerating thymocytes in the cortex
Why: Macrophages are present in the thymic cortex and do exactly that, but they are not branched cells joined into a network by desmosomes, and their nuclei are eccentric and kidney-shaped.
* Lymphoblasts in the outer cortex, before they divide into small lymphocytes
Why: Lymphoblasts are large lymphoid cells, not branched cells with processes, and they do not form a supporting reticulum.
Explanation: The thymus has a double origin, and the slide shows both halves of it. The lymphocytes are mesodermal and come from bone marrow; the framework is endodermal and epithelial. That is why the thymic stroma is a network of cells rather than of fibres, and why the same cells go on to form Hassall's corpuscles and to secrete the thymic hormones.

### What the thymus does not have
Difficulty: Moderate
The whole section is searched and no lymphatic nodules, no germinal centres, no plasma cells and no vessels entering the capsule as afferent lymphatics can be found.
Q: Which of these absences is the one that most directly protects the developing T lymphocytes?
*= The absence of afferent lymphatics, since it keeps circulating antigen out of the organ while thymocytes are being selected
Why: Correct. A lymph node receives antigen through its afferent lymphatics. The thymus has none, and that exclusion, together with the blood-thymic barrier, is what allows thymocytes to mature in an antigen-free environment.
* The absence of germinal centres, since a germinal centre would consume the thymocytes
Why: Picked by students who treat every absence as protective in the same way. A germinal centre is a B-cell structure; its absence follows from the thymus having no B lymphocytes rather than protecting anything.
* The absence of plasma cells, since plasma cells would secrete antibody against the thymocytes
Why: Invents an autoimmune mechanism. Plasma cells are absent because B lymphocytes are absent, and antibody against thymocytes is not what the arrangement guards against.
* The absence of lymphatic nodules, since a nodule would compress the developing cells
Why: Mechanical compression is not the issue. The nodules are absent because the thymus is a primary lymphoid organ that educates lymphocytes rather than mounting responses in them.
Explanation: A primary lymphoid organ has to be a quiet place. Everything the thymus lacks — nodules, germinal centres, B cells, plasma cells, afferent lymphatics — points the same way: this is where lymphocytes are made and selected, not where immune responses are mounted. The absence of afferent lymphatics is the structural expression of that.

### The wall around the developing cells
Difficulty: Hard
The student is asked about the barrier that separates thymocytes in the cortex from antigens circulating in the blood.
Q: What are the layers of the blood-thymic barrier, and why does it exist in the cortex but not in the medulla?
*= A continuous capillary endothelium with tight junctions, a thick continuous basal lamina, a perivascular space containing macrophages, and a complete sheath of epithelial reticular cells — present in the cortex because that is where thymocytes are selected, and unnecessary in the medulla, which holds cells that have already been selected and are about to leave
Why: Correct. Four layers, and the outermost is a complete epithelial sheath. The barrier is a cortical structure because selection is a cortical event; medullary thymocytes have completed it and are on their way into the circulation.
* A fenestrated capillary endothelium, a discontinuous basal lamina, reticular fibres and a layer of macrophages, present throughout the organ
Why: Describes a permeable rather than a sealed wall. Fenestrae and a discontinuous basal lamina would let through exactly what the barrier is there to exclude.
* A single layer of epithelial reticular cells only, present in the cortex because the cortex has no blood supply
Why: Picked by students who reduce the barrier to its outermost layer. The cortex is well supplied with capillaries — the barrier surrounds them rather than replacing them.
* A continuous endothelium and basal lamina only, present in the medulla because Hassall's corpuscles must be protected
Why: Reverses the location and omits half the layers. Hassall's corpuscles are degenerating epithelial cells and need no protection.
Explanation: Read the barrier as a sequence outwards from the lumen — endothelium, basal lamina, perivascular tissue with its macrophages, epithelial sheath — and its purpose reads off the sequence. Each layer removes another route by which a blood-borne antigen could reach a thymocyte still learning what to tolerate, which is why the barrier stops where selection stops.
