## title
Forensic medicine: postmortem changes, wounds and fatal injuries
## subject
fnd
## status
Draft
## owner

## topic

## summary
This article groups the forensic-medicine facts on death, wounds and injury tested by the paper (Q32-38, Q40-41, Q44-46, Q48, Q51). It covers postmortem changes (hypostasis settling in the head and neck of a head-down floating drowned body; arborisation/marbling as a putrefactive change), wounds (contusions from blunt force, patterned imprint abrasions that identify the weapon, crush injury killing through acute renal failure), head injuries (base-of-skull fracture from a fall onto the feet, diastasis as suture separation, polar fissure fracture), burns and firearm injuries (wet unsinged hair in scalds, embedded unburnt powder as tattooing, wide shotgun dispersion meaning a long range), asphyxia (cafe coronary as fatal choking while eating) and infant deaths (maceration as aseptic autolysis of a retained foetus, a neglected umbilical cord causing fatal sepsis).
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the death-and-injury forensic concepts tested by the paper; each linked concept carries one atomic forensic fact and this overview ties them into the themes of postmortem changes, wounds, head and firearm injuries, asphyxia and infant death.
### Mechanism
The facts follow from forensic mechanisms: axial force transmission (fall onto feet fracturing the skull base), tissue response to injury (blunt-force bruising, moist versus dry heat, powder embedding), gravity acting on the dead body's position (hypostasis in the drowned body), decomposition chemistry (marbling), airway obstruction with vagal inhibition (cafe coronary) and muscle breakdown (crush-syndrome myoglobinuria).
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: fall-on-feet fractures the base, not the vault; contusion = blunt force; diastasis = suture separation (paediatric), not a severe fracture; scald hair is wet, not singed; tattooing = embedded unburnt powder (fixed), not wipeable soot; drowned-body hypostasis is in the head and neck; imprint abrasion identifies the weapon; maceration = aseptic autolysis; polar fracture is a fissure fracture; cafe coronary = fatal choking while eating; wide shotgun scatter = long range; a neglected cord causes sepsis; crush injury kills via renal failure; arborisation = putrefaction.
### Clinical significance
These facts underlie autopsy interpretation (estimating time and manner of death, distinguishing burns from scalds, reading firearm range) and the medico-legal handling of sudden deaths and infant deaths.
### Common misconceptions
See each concept's pitfalls field for the exact reversal each distractor exploits (impact site versus fracture site, close versus long range, singed versus wet hair, hypostasis versus marbling, haemorrhage versus sepsis from a neglected cord).

## title
Forensic medicine: identification, sexual offences and medico-legal law
## subject
fnd
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Forensic Medicine
## summary
This article groups the identification, sexual-offences and medico-legal-law facts tested by the paper (Q39, Q42-43, Q47, Q49-50). It covers identification (third-molar eruption placing age above 18; ABO exclusion of an AB child for a group-O father in disputed paternity), sexual offences (the child's hymen lying high in the vaginal canal) and medical law (the six-month shortest legal period of pregnancy; mental incapacity such as Down syndrome invalidating a woman's consent to intercourse; and the onset of the ability to discriminate right from wrong, hence partial criminal responsibility, at seven years).
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the identification and medico-legal-law forensic concepts tested by the paper; each linked concept carries one atomic fact tying dental and genetic identification and legal capacity/viability thresholds into a single picture.
### Mechanism
The facts rest on developmental and genetic biology applied to law: dental eruption sequence for age, ABO allele inheritance for paternity exclusion, the developmental position of the hymen, and legal rules built on viability (shortest legal pregnancy), capacity (consent) and maturity (criminal responsibility).
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: erupted third molars mean age above 18; an O father cannot have an AB child; the child's hymen is high in the canal; the shortest legal period of pregnancy is six months; mental incapacity voids consent; right/wrong discrimination begins at seven years.
### Clinical significance
These facts underlie age estimation, disputed-paternity work (ABO exclusion), the medico-legal examination in sexual offences, and the assessment of consent and criminal responsibility.
### Common misconceptions
See each concept's pitfalls field for the exact reversal each distractor exploits (third-molar age set too young, AB inheritance, superficial versus deep child hymen, capacity thresholds).
