## summary
Every skeletal muscle has exactly two attachments, and this faculty names them by what the muscle attaches to, not by what it attaches with. Four types are taught and four are marked: bone, fibrous raphe, skin, cartilage. Bone is the commonest and subdivides into fleshy and tendinous, which is where students who learned a three-item list from another textbook lose the mark.
## sections
### Definition
Muscle tissue is defined by contraction, the ability of its fibres to shorten. A shortening fibre does work only if both of its ends are anchored, so every skeletal muscle has two attachments and they are named separately.

Origin is the more fixed attachment. Insertion is the more mobile attachment. During contraction the insertion moves towards the origin, and the movement appears at the joint or joints the muscle crosses.

Three qualifications travel with that definition in both Kasr sources, and the examiner expects them stated rather than assumed.

First, the roles reverse. Sometimes the insertion is the fixed point while the origin is the mobile one. Pull-ups are the standard demonstration: the hand is fixed on the bar and the trunk, carrying the origin, travels towards it.

Second, a muscle may have more than one point of origin or more than one point of insertion. Biceps has two heads of origin and inserts by both a tendon and an aponeurosis; pectoralis major has two heads of origin and one insertion.

Third, in the limbs the origins are usually proximal to the insertions. That is a convention of limb architecture, not a law, and it does not hold in the trunk or the head.

### Mechanism
An attachment transmits force from contractile tissue to something that will move. What it transmits force to determines what form the attachment takes, and that is the axis this faculty classifies on.

Attachment to bone is the commonest, and it is made in one of two ways. In a fleshy attachment the muscle fibres run into the periosteum directly over a broad area; the force per unit area is low and the attachment leaves a smooth or only faintly marked surface on the dry bone. In a tendinous attachment the fibres converge on a cord of dense fibrous tissue that concentrates the pull onto a small area; the force per unit area is high and the bone answers it with a tubercle, a tuberosity, a crest or a rough impression. Reading a dry bone is largely reading that answer, which is why osteology and myology are taught together.

Attachment to a fibrous raphe is an attachment to a band or septum of fibrous tissue through which two muscles fuse together. Mylohyoid is the example both sources give. Here neither end reaches bone at the midline; the two halves anchor against each other, and the raphe is the shared anchor.

Attachment to skin is an insertion into the dermis. Contraction moves the skin rather than a bone, which is why the muscles of facial expression can produce a smile, a frown or a wince without moving the skeleton.

Attachment to cartilage is an attachment to a cartilaginous skeleton rather than a bony one. The muscles of the larynx are the example; cricothyroid is the one the department book names.

### Key determinants
Four types of attachment, in the order Dr. Galal's revision gives them, with the examples both sources use. The list is closed and it is numbered, and a student who writes three of the four has answered three-quarters of a six-mark question.

Attachment to bone. The commonest type, by flesh or by tendon.

Attachment to skin. The muscles of the face.

Attachment to a fibrous raphe. The mylohyoid muscles.

Attachment to cartilage. The muscles of the larynx, for example cricothyroid.

The department book gives the same four with bone first, raphe second, skin third and cartilage fourth. Order is not marked; completeness is.

Beside the type of attachment sit two other closed lists from the same chapter, and an examiner who has asked for attachment can ask for either in the same question.

The form of a skeletal muscle depends on the arrangement of its fibres in relation to the line of pull, which is the line running from origin to insertion. Fibres parallel to the line of pull give the strap-like muscle, the strap-like muscle with tendinous intersections, the quadrilateral muscle and the fusiform muscle. Fibres oblique to it give the pennate muscles — unipennate, bipennate, multipennate and circumpennate — and the non-pennate muscles: triangular, spiral, cruciate and circular. A muscle may also have more than one fleshy belly or head: two heads, three heads, four heads, or two bellies.

The action of a muscle is described by its role in a movement, and there are four roles. The prime mover, or agonist, initiates and maintains the movement. The antagonist opposes the prime mover. The fixator, or stabiliser, fixes the origin of the prime mover or stabilises the joint being acted upon. The synergist contracts to eliminate unwanted movement at joints that the prime mover crosses on its way to its insertion, so that the prime mover's action at the joint that matters becomes maximal.

### Clinical significance
The attachment is where a muscle fails mechanically, and the department book closes the muscular-system chapter with the four causes of muscle atrophy it expects a student to name, plus a fifth added in the same list.

Immobilisation after a fracture. Disease of the muscle itself. Injury of the motor nerve of the muscle. Injury of the spinal cord. Affection of the higher centres in the brain, for example hemiplegia.

The list is worth reading as a chain rather than as five separate facts. A muscle is kept in trim by being used; anything that stops it being used wastes it, whether the interruption is at the muscle, at its nerve, at the cord, or at the brain. That is also the order in which a clinical examination works backwards from a wasted muscle to the level of the lesion.

The fleshy-versus-tendinous distinction has its own consequence. A tendon concentrates force on a small area of bone, so a violent contraction against resistance avulses the bony point rather than tearing the tendon. The bony markings that a tendon raises are the same points that a tendon can pull off.

### Common misconceptions
Origin and insertion are not fixed anatomical properties of the two ends of a muscle. They are names for which end is being stabilised in the movement under discussion, and both sources say so explicitly in the same paragraph that defines them. A student who has memorised the pair as immovable facts cannot explain how the same muscle raises the trunk to the bar.

The four types are not a classification of connective tissue. They classify what the muscle attaches to. Flesh and tendon are two ways of making the first type, not two types of their own, and an aponeurosis is a flattened tendon rather than a fifth category. That distinction is where a three-item list learned elsewhere costs marks here.

Omitting the qualification that a muscle may have several origins or insertions, then being unable to account for biceps or triceps.

## summary
A cartilaginous joint unites two bones by cartilage and nothing else. The department asks it as a comparison, and it is a comparison with five columns: example, site, the cartilage itself, whether the joint ossifies, and how much movement it allows. Get the cartilage right and the other four follow, because hyaline cartilage is what a growth plate is made of and white fibrocartilage is what survives a lifetime of loading.
## sections
### Definition
A joint is the contact between two bones or more. Joints are classified into three groups by what lies between the bones: fibrous joints, where the bones are connected by fibrous tissue; cartilaginous joints, where they are connected by cartilaginous tissue; and synovial joints, where a cavity lies between them.

A cartilaginous joint is therefore defined by its uniting tissue and not by its site or its mobility, and it has two types.

The primary cartilaginous joint, also called a synchondrosis, unites the bones by hyaline cartilage. The type example, and the one both Kasr sources name, is the epiphyseal plate.

The secondary cartilaginous joint, also called a symphysis, unites the bones by white fibrocartilage. The type example, and again the one both sources name, is the intervertebral disc.

There is no third type. A student who adds one has drifted into the fibrous group, which has three types of its own — suture in the skull, gomphosis in the teeth, and syndesmosis at the inferior tibio-fibular joint.

### Mechanism
Everything that separates the two types follows from the tissue.

Hyaline cartilage is the tissue that bone grows from. A primary cartilaginous joint is not built to move; it is built to lengthen. The epiphyseal plate is a plate of hyaline cartilage lying between the epiphysis and the diaphysis of a growing long bone, and the bone grows in length by the proliferation of that plate's cells. Because the joint's whole purpose is growth, it has a finite life. When the bone matures the plate stops dividing and ossifies, epiphysis fuses to diaphysis, and the joint ceases to exist. That is why a primary cartilaginous joint is temporary and immobile: it is a construction site, not a hinge.

White fibrocartilage is the tissue that resists compression while deforming a little under it. A secondary cartilaginous joint is built to stay. In the intervertebral disc the articulating surface of each bone is covered by a thin layer of hyaline cartilage, and the two are separated by a disc of white fibrocartilage. The joint is strengthened by fibrous ligaments which do not form a complete capsule around it — a point worth stating, because it is what distinguishes this arrangement from a synovial joint, where a complete fibrous capsule is the first structure listed. The limited mobility of a symphysis is facilitated by the elasticity of that fibrocartilaginous disc; the disc deforms, and the bones move a little because it does.

The site rule follows from the same logic. A primary cartilaginous joint is where a bone is lengthening, so it lies at the ends of growing long bones. A secondary cartilaginous joint is where the two halves of the body meet, so it lies in the midline.

### Key determinants
Dr. Galal's revision sets the comparison out as a table with five rows, and the department book gives the same content as two numbered lists. The five rows are the answer to the exam question, and each is worth stating in the source's own words.

Example. Primary: the epiphyseal plate. Secondary: the intervertebral discs.

Site. Primary: between the epiphysis and the diaphysis of growing long bones — the department book puts it as "at the ends of long bones", in the developing long bones of children. Secondary: the midline.

Type of cartilage. Primary: hyaline. Secondary: white fibrocartilage, with the articulating surfaces themselves covered by a thin layer of hyaline cartilage.

Ossification. Primary: temporary, and it ossifies. Secondary: permanent.

Mobility. Primary: immobile. Secondary: limited mobility, facilitated by the elasticity of the white fibrocartilage.

Two details sit outside the five rows and are still marked. The secondary joint is strengthened by fibrous ligaments which do not form a complete capsule around the joint. And the primary joint's cartilage is the growth plate itself, so it belongs to the account of how a long bone grows in length as much as it belongs to the classification of joints.

### Clinical significance
The primary cartilaginous joint is the one that has a clinical timetable, and Dr. Galal's revision states it under the growth of long bones.

The epiphyseal plate grows the bone in length by the proliferation of its cells. When the bone becomes mature the plate stops dividing and ossifies, which fuses epiphysis to diaphysis. The process is under hormonal control, and it happens two years earlier in females. The plate at one end of a bone ossifies two to three years earlier than the plate at the other end, and the end that ossifies last — the one that goes on growing longest — is called the growing end.

That has two consequences the sources connect to it. The nutrient artery of a long bone enters through an oblique nutrient foramen and runs away from the growing end, which is how the growing end can be identified on a dry bone. And an epiphyseal plate is a plane of hyaline cartilage crossing the whole width of a growing bone, which is a plane of relative weakness in a child that does not exist in an adult.

The secondary cartilaginous joint has no timetable, because it is permanent. Its clinical importance is that it is present for life and takes load for life: the intervertebral discs remain fibrocartilaginous, and the small movement each one permits is what adds up, over the length of the column, into the mobility of the trunk.

### Common misconceptions
Not every cartilaginous joint disappears. Only the primary ones ossify. A symphysis is there for life, and a student who has learned "cartilaginous joints are temporary" has learned half a fact and will answer the ossification row wrong for both columns.

Hyaline is not a synonym for primary. Both types contain hyaline cartilage. In the primary joint the hyaline plate is the whole uniting tissue; in the secondary joint hyaline cartilage is only the thin covering on each bone's articulating surface, and the uniting tissue between them is white fibrocartilage. Naming the covering as the joint's tissue answers the central row of the table wrong.

Limited mobility is not the same as being a synovial joint. The secondary cartilaginous joint moves a little because its disc deforms, not because it has a cavity, a synovial membrane or a complete capsule. It has none of those.

## summary
The decidua is the endometrium after implantation, and the department asks it in four parts: definition, features, parts, fate. The three parts are named by where they lie in relation to the conceptus, and each has exactly one fate. Basalis is underneath and becomes the maternal half of the placenta; capsularis is the lid and is lost; parietalis lines the rest and meets the lid at the end of the third month, closing the uterine cavity.
## sections
### Definition
The decidua is the endometrium of the uterus after the blastocyst has implanted. The word is Latin and means falling off, which is the whole of its fate in one word: the tissue is shed at delivery.

It is not a new tissue. Both Kasr sources describe it as an exaggerated endometrial secretory phase — the same endometrium the ovarian cycle was already producing, carried further because implantation has occurred and the corpus luteum has not regressed.

The features the department expects are the three that make the exaggeration visible, and it introduces them as the reasons the decidua is thicker.

The endometrial cells increase in number and in size.

The glands become spiral and are full of secretions; the department book adds that they are dilated.

The arteries become spiral and show arterio-venous anastomosis.

### Mechanism
Reading the three parts is a matter of asking where each piece of endometrium sits once the blastocyst has burrowed into it. The whole endometrium becomes decidua; the names divide it by position.

Decidua basalis is the part between the implanted embryo and the myometrium — the floor beneath the conceptus. It is the part in contact with the chorion frondosum, the part of the chorion carrying well-developed tertiary villi, and the two together become the placenta. That is why basalis alone persists.

Decidua capsularis is the part covering the embryo, separating it from the uterine cavity — the lid over the conceptus, raised as the sac grows. It lies against the chorion laeve, the part of the chorion whose villi degenerate, and it degenerates with it.

Decidua parietalis is the part lining the rest of the uterine cavity — everything the conceptus is not under and not covered by.

The fate of the last two is a consequence of a growing sac in a fixed cavity. As the conceptus enlarges it pushes the capsularis outwards across the cavity until the lid meets the wall. The department book dates the meeting precisely, and does it in two steps: at the beginning of the third month the amnion comes into contact with the chorion, forming the amniochorionic membrane and obliterating the chorionic cavity; at the end of the third month that amniochorionic membrane, covered by decidua capsularis, comes into contact with decidua parietalis, and the uterine cavity is obliterated. The two fused deciduae are expelled after delivery of the baby.

The basalis takes the opposite course, because it is the one part with a blood supply the conceptus has learned to tap. As the decidual plate it becomes the maternal part of the placenta, and the department book gives its structure from outside inwards: decidua basalis, containing maternal arterioles and venules as well as uterine glands; the cytotrophoblastic shell; and the syncytiotrophoblast that lines the intervillous spaces.

### Key determinants
The three parts and their three fates, in the source's own order and wording. This is the six-mark answer.

Decidua basalis. The part between the implanted embryo and the myometrium of the uterus. Fate: it persists, as the decidual plate, to form the maternal part of the placenta.

Decidua capsularis. The part that covers the embryo, separating it from the uterine cavity. Fate: it fuses with the parietalis, obliterating the uterine cavity, and degenerates.

Decidua parietalis. The part that lines the rest of the uterine cavity. Fate: it fuses with the capsularis, obliterating the uterine cavity, and degenerates. The fused deciduae are expelled after delivery.

Note that the department book gives the last two one shared fate rather than two separate ones, and that the fate sentence names the obliteration of the uterine cavity as part of it. Both are marked, and a student who writes only "they degenerate" has left out the event the sentence exists to state.

### Clinical significance
The decidua basalis is the maternal half of the organ of exchange, so the numbers the department attaches to placental circulation are numbers about the basalis.

Arterial blood reaches the intervillous spaces through 80 to 100 decidual arterioles. A full-term placenta contains about 150 ml of maternal blood, and that volume is changed three to four times per minute. After exchange, blood in the intervillous spaces flows back towards the decidual venules.

The decidua basalis also supplies the cores of the placental septa. These are incomplete septa running from the decidual plate into the cavity of the intervillous spaces; they appear during the fourth and fifth gestational months, and each is a core of decidua basalis covered by cytotrophoblast and syncytiotrophoblast. Opposite each septum there is a groove on the maternal surface of the placenta, and those grooves are what separate the cotyledons. The lobulated maternal surface a student is asked to describe is therefore a decidual structure, not a fetal one.

The fate of the capsularis has its own consequence. Once it has fused with the parietalis at the end of the third month there is no uterine cavity left — a fact that explains why, from the second trimester onwards, the sac occupies the uterus completely.

### Common misconceptions
Swapping basalis and capsularis is the error the concept record names, and it is the one the parts are worth over-learning against. Basalis is beneath and becomes placenta. Capsularis is the covering and disappears. The mnemonic in the words themselves is that a capsule covers and a base lies under.

The decidua is not the whole endometrium in a general sense. It is the endometrium after implantation. Before implantation there is no decidua, which is why every part of the definition depends on a blastocyst having arrived.

Degeneration of the capsularis is not a failure. It is the ordinary consequence of a sac growing across a cavity, and it happens in every normal pregnancy at the same time as the obliteration of the uterine cavity.

## lose_the_mark
Swapping basalis and capsularis. Basalis is beneath the conceptus and becomes placenta; capsularis is the covering and disappears.
Writing only "they degenerate" for the capsularis and parietalis. The marked fate is that they fuse, obliterate the uterine cavity, degenerate, and are expelled after delivery.
Giving parts and fates but no definition and no features. Dr. Galal sets the topic out under four headings, and the exam question asks for the first three of them by name.
Dating the obliteration of the uterine cavity to the fourth month. The department book says the end of the third month, and the beginning of the third month is when the amnion meets the chorion.
Calling the decidua a tissue that appears in every menstrual cycle. It is the endometrium after implantation; without implantation there is no decidua.
Attributing the grooves on the maternal surface of the placenta to the fetal side. The septa that make them are cores of decidua basalis.

## conflicts

The department book states the end of the third month, and separately dates the amnion meeting the chorion to the beginning of the third month. This article follows the department book. Where the topic sits in the curriculum. The concept and this article place the decidua under Fetal Membranes, which is where the exam paper's question sat. ## evidence_gaps
No source in this corpus describes the decidual reaction at the cellular level, nor names decidual cells as glycogen-laden and lipid-laden. The features stated here are the three the books give and no more.
Neither source states what hormone maintains the decidua, nor connects it to the corpus luteum. The sentence in Definition linking the exaggerated secretory phase to a persisting corpus luteum is general knowledge and is flagged in field_notes.
Neither source describes the decidua in ectopic pregnancy, nor the decidual cast. Abnormal sites of implantation are covered by both, but without reference to what the decidua does there.
Neither source names decidua basalis as the plane of separation at delivery, or describes retained placenta or placenta accreta.
