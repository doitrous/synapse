## title
Anatomy of the pituitary gland and its relations
## subject
endo
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Anatomy
## summary
The pituitary gland sits in the hypophyseal fossa of the sphenoid body, roofed by the diaphragma sellae, with the hypothalamus above, the sphenoid air sinus below and the cavernous sinus (carrying the internal carotid artery) on each side. Because it lies directly beneath the optic chiasma, an enlarging adenoma compresses the crossing nasal fibres and produces a bitemporal hemianopia. Its anterior lobe is supplied indirectly through the hypothalamo-hypophyseal portal circulation, while the posterior lobe is fed directly by the inferior hypophyseal artery; venous blood drains to the cavernous sinuses.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the pituitary gross-anatomy facts tested by the Dr Medhat endocrine bank (Q2-4): the bitemporal hemianopia of a chiasmatic mass, the portal blood supply of the anterior lobe, and the relation of the gland to the diaphragma sellae, sphenoid body and hypothalamus.
### Mechanism
The visual defect follows from the vertical relation of the gland to the chiasma above it; the anterior-lobe supply follows from the two-capillary-bed portal system that also delivers hypothalamic releasing hormones; the dural and bony relations follow from the gland sitting in the sellar fossa below the diaphragma sellae.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: bitemporal (chiasmatic) versus homonymous (retrochiasmatic) field loss; portal (indirect) versus direct arterial supply of the adenohypophysis; and the gland lying below, not above, the diaphragma sellae.
### Clinical significance
These relations underlie the reading of pituitary tumour visual fields, the trans-sphenoidal surgical route, and the vulnerability of the cavernous internal carotid artery beside the gland.

## title
Anatomy of the thyroid and parathyroid glands
## subject
endo
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Anatomy
## summary
Each thyroid lobe is moulded medially on the trachea, oesophagus, larynx and inferior constrictor, while its posteromedial surface carries the inferior thyroid artery, the recurrent laryngeal nerve and the parathyroid glands. The isthmus crosses the second to fourth tracheal rings and is crossed above by the anastomosing superior thyroid arteries. Blood comes from the superior thyroid artery (first branch of the external carotid, running with the external laryngeal nerve) and the inferior thyroid artery (from the thyrocervical trunk, running with the recurrent laryngeal nerve). These nerve relations dictate the ligation rules and the preservation of the posterior thyroid remnant to protect the parathyroids in thyroidectomy.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the thyroid and parathyroid gross-anatomy facts tested by the Dr Medhat endocrine bank (Q5-10): the medial and posterior relations of the lobe, the relations of the isthmus, the origins of the two thyroid arteries, the superior thyroid artery and external laryngeal nerve, and preservation of the parathyroids in thyroidectomy.
### Mechanism
The surgical rules follow from vessel-nerve companionship: the superior thyroid artery runs with the external laryngeal nerve so is ligated close to the gland, while the inferior thyroid artery runs with the recurrent laryngeal nerve so is ligated away from the gland; the parathyroids lie posteriorly on the inferior thyroid arterial supply, so the posterior remnant is spared.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: trachea/oesophagus as medial (not the middle constrictor) relations; the inferior thyroid artery as a posterior relation; the isthmus over the 2nd-4th (not 6th-8th) rings; superior thyroid from the external carotid versus inferior thyroid from the thyrocervical trunk; and the constant superior versus variable inferior parathyroid.
### Clinical significance
These relations underlie safe thyroidectomy (voice-sparing artery ligation and avoidance of post-operative hypocalcaemic tetany) and the interpretation of neck imaging.

## title
Anatomy of the suprarenal (adrenal) glands
## subject
endo
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Anatomy
## summary
The two suprarenal glands cap the upper poles of the kidneys: the right is pyramidal (triangular) and tucked behind the inferior vena cava with the bare area of the liver in front, while the left is semilunar (crescentic) and reaches towards the renal hilum. Each gland is supplied by three arteries (superior from the inferior phrenic, middle from the aorta, inferior from the renal artery) but is drained by a single vein, and this venous drainage is asymmetrical: the right suprarenal vein is short and enters the IVC directly, whereas the left drains into the left renal vein.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the suprarenal gross-anatomy facts tested by the Dr Medhat endocrine bank (Q12-14, 17): the shapes of the right and left glands, the anterior relations of the right gland, and the arterial supply and asymmetrical venous drainage.
### Mechanism
Shape and relations follow from each gland moulding onto its neighbours (IVC and liver on the right, pancreas and stomach on the left); the asymmetrical veins follow from the side-specific drainage of a single suprarenal vein into the nearest large vein.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: pyramidal right versus semilunar left; the IVC and liver as anterior relations of the right gland; three arteries but one vein; and the left suprarenal vein ending in the left renal vein rather than the IVC.
### Clinical significance
These relations underlie right adrenalectomy hazards (the short right vein on the IVC) and the reading of adrenal cross-sectional imaging.

## title
Anatomy of the pancreas and its ducts
## subject
endo
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Anatomy
## summary
The pancreas lies transversely across the posterior abdominal wall, its head, neck and body in the epigastrium and its tail passing into the left hypochondrium to reach the hilum of the spleen in the splenorenal ligament. The common bile duct runs behind the head before opening into the second part of the duodenum, so carcinoma of the head causes obstructive jaundice; the body lies in front of the aorta, and the portal vein begins behind the neck. Near its end the main pancreatic duct joins the common bile duct to form the hepatopancreatic ampulla, which opens at the major duodenal papilla under the sphincter of Oddi.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the pancreatic gross-anatomy facts tested by the Dr Medhat endocrine bank (Q19-21, 24): the surface location, the bile duct behind the head, the relation of the tail to the spleen, and the ductal termination at the hepatopancreatic ampulla.
### Mechanism
The clinical picture follows from posterior relations: the CBD grooving the head explains obstructive jaundice; the intraperitoneal tail in the splenorenal ligament explains its risk in splenectomy; and the shared ampulla explains combined biliary-pancreatic outflow at the major papilla.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: epigastrium plus left hypochondrium (not right hypochondrium); the CBD behind the head; the tail at the splenic hilum; the portal vein beginning behind the neck; and the main duct (major papilla, 2nd part) versus the accessory duct (minor papilla).
### Clinical significance
These relations underlie the presentation of pancreatic head carcinoma, ERCP cannulation of the ampulla, and the hazard to the pancreatic tail in splenectomy.
