## title
Histology of the endocrine glands
## subject
endo
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Histology
## summary
The endocrine glands share the endocrine plan of secretory cells set among sinusoids with no ducts. The pituitary pars distalis holds acidophils and PAS-positive basophils (thyrotrophs, gonadotrophs, corticotrophs) plus pale chromophobes, while the pars nervosa stores oxytocin and ADH in Herring bodies among pituicytes. Thyroid follicular cells are rER- and Golgi-rich protein secretors bordering colloid, and the pale parafollicular (C) cells secrete calcitonin from basal granules. The adrenal cortex runs glomerulosa, fasciculata (lipid- and sER-rich spongiocytes) and reticularis, while the medullary chromaffin cells are modified sympathetic neurons giving a positive chromaffin reaction. Pinealocytes are melatonin-secreting cells under unmyelinated sympathetic control, and parathyroid chief cells raise calcium through PTH.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the endocrine-histology facts tested by Q1-8; each linked concept carries the atomic claim, and this overview ties them into one picture. Read the individual concepts for the precise statement and the pitfalls the exam options probe.
### Mechanism
Each gland matches its cell ultrastructure to its product: protein/glycoprotein secretors (pituitary chromophils, thyroid follicular cells) are rER- and Golgi-rich, while steroid secretors (adrenal fasciculata spongiocytes) are packed with smooth ER and lipid droplets. Storage-and-transport specialisations appear as Herring bodies in the pars nervosa and basally placed calcitonin granules in C cells. Neural links show as the unmyelinated sympathetic supply to pinealocytes and the neuron-derived chromaffin cells of the medulla.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: Herring bodies are axon-terminal neurosecretion; follicular cells become columnar when hyperactive (not hypothyroid); thyrotrophs are basophils; fasciculata cells are wide-zone lipid-rich spongiocytes; pinealocytes take an unmyelinated sympathetic supply; C cells are pale calcitonin cells not reaching the lumen; medullary cells give the chromaffin reaction; and parathyroid hyperfunction raises calcium and fractures bone.
### Clinical significance
These features underlie reading of endocrine biopsies and cytology, the calcium disturbances of hyper- and hypoparathyroidism, phaeochromocytoma of chromaffin cells, and the follicular changes seen in thyroid over- and underactivity.

## title
Histology of the male reproductive system
## subject
androl
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Histology
## summary
The testis is a compound tubular gland whose seminiferous tubules are the exocrine sperm-producing units and whose interstitial Leydig cells are the endocrine (testosterone) part, recognised by their smooth-ER-rich steroid ultrastructure. Within the tubule, spermatogenesis proceeds from basal spermatogonia through the large primary spermatocyte to the short-lived, rarely seen secondary spermatocyte and then spermatids; spermiogenesis differentiates the spermatid over four phases, with spermiation released in the maturation phase. Tall Sertoli cells with a well-developed cytoskeleton support the germ cells and, through tight junctions, form the blood-testis barrier that prevents autoimmunity against sperm. In the excurrent system, benign prostatic hyperplasia arises in the transitional (periurethral) zone.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the male reproductive histology facts tested by Q9-16 (Q10 excluded as a double-marked key); each linked concept carries the atomic claim, and this overview ties them together.
### Mechanism
Function follows structure: Leydig cells are steroid secretors (sER, lipid, tubular mitochondria); Sertoli cells are structural and barrier cells (cytoskeleton and tight junctions); germ cells progress in a fixed sequence whose kinetics explain why secondary spermatocytes are scarce in sections. Spermiogenesis remodels the spermatid without division, ending in spermiation. The blood-testis barrier partitions basal and adluminal compartments to protect and nurture haploid cells.
### Key determinants
Discriminators: Leydig cells are sER-rich (not protein secretors) and do not form the barrier; the testis is a compound tubular gland with the seminiferous tubule as its exocrine unit; secondary spermatocytes are transient; spermiation occurs in the maturation phase of a four-phase spermiogenesis; Sertoli cells have a well-developed cytoskeleton and indistinct borders and secrete ABP/inhibin/AMH (not LH); the barrier prevents anti-sperm autoimmunity; and BPH is a transitional-zone lesion.
### Clinical significance
These features underlie the interpretation of testicular biopsy in infertility, the sparing of Leydig cells in cryptorchidism, autoimmune orchitis after barrier breach, and the obstructive urinary symptoms of transitional-zone prostatic hyperplasia.

## title
Histology of the female reproductive system
## subject
gyn
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Histology
## summary
The ovarian follicle matures through a secondary (antral) stage, defined by granulosa secretion of liquor folliculi, to the Graafian follicle; after ovulation the granulosa-lutein cells of the corpus luteum secrete progesterone and inhibin. The genital tract shows region-specific epithelia and walls: the fallopian tube moves the ovum chiefly by muscular peristalsis; the endometrium is divided into a shed spiral-artery-supplied functional zone and a retained straight-artery-supplied basal (reserve) zone, with straight narrow glands in the proliferative phase; the cervix carries mucus-secreting endocervical glands and does not shed; and the vagina keeps an acidic lumen through lactobacillus fermentation of epithelial glycogen. The placental villus has an inner mononuclear cytotrophoblast that fuses into the outer syncytiotrophoblast, PCOS arrests many secondary follicles, and the resting mammary gland is mainly ducts of cuboidal cells with myoepithelium in a fatty stroma.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the female reproductive histology facts tested by Q17-26; each linked concept carries the atomic claim, and this overview ties them into one picture.
### Mechanism
Ovarian and endometrial events are hormone-driven: oestrogen builds straight proliferative glands and vaginal glycogen, progesterone from granulosa-lutein cells makes the endometrium secretory, and follicular fluid from granulosa cells creates the antrum. Transport and barrier functions are structural: tubal peristalsis moves the ovum, the basalis regenerates the functional layer, and the trophoblast differentiates from mononuclear stem cells (cytotrophoblast) into an invasive syncytium.
### Key determinants
Discriminators: the secondary follicle is defined by antral liquor folliculi with a still-primary oocyte; granulosa-lutein cells secrete progesterone and inhibin; tubal transport is mainly peristaltic; the basal zone is the retained reserve; proliferative glands are straight and narrow; the cervix has mucous glands and is not shed; vaginal acidity comes from lactic acid; the cytotrophoblast is mononuclear with clear boundaries; PCOS arrests many follicles; and the resting breast is duct-and-myoepithelium in fat.
### Clinical significance
These features underlie the histological dating of the endometrium, the pathology of ectopic (tubal) pregnancy, Nabothian cysts of the cervix, the protective vaginal flora, placental villous pathology, the polycystic ovary in PCOS, and the reading of breast tissue.
