## title
Coeliac-trunk vasculature of the stomach, spleen and liver, portal vein tributaries, and portosystemic anastomoses
## subject
gi
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Anatomy
## summary
The coeliac trunk, the artery of the foregut, divides into the left gastric, common hepatic and splenic arteries, each feeding a further arcade -- right gastric from the hepatic, gastroepiploic vessels from the splenic and gastroduodenal, and short gastric arteries specifically to the fundus -- so the whole stomach's supply traces back to one trunk even though individual regions (fundus versus body) have their own named branch. The splenic vein and superior mesenteric vein unite behind the neck of the pancreas to form the portal vein, and only vessels that join at that exact point (the splenic vein itself) are direct tributaries; every other gastrointestinal vein reaches the portal system one step further upstream. Where the portal and systemic venous systems anastomose -- most clinically important at the lower oesophagus -- portal hypertension produces dilated collaterals, of which oesophageal varices are the one that kills through massive haematemesis rather than through cosmetic or minor bleeding.
## sections
### Definition
The coeliac trunk arises from the abdominal aorta at T12 and is the single ventral, unpaired artery of the foregut, giving three branches: left gastric (smallest, to the lower oesophagus, cardia and upper lesser curvature), common hepatic (giving the proper hepatic artery onward to the liver, and the gastroduodenal artery, which itself gives the right gastroepiploic and superior pancreaticoduodenal arteries), and splenic (the largest, running a tortuous course along the superior border of the pancreas, giving pancreatic branches, the left gastroepiploic artery and multiple short gastric arteries before reaching the spleen).

### Mechanism
Two arcades cover the stomach: the lesser-curvature arcade (left gastric plus right gastric, the latter from the proper hepatic artery) and the greater-curvature arcade (left gastroepiploic from the splenic artery, plus right gastroepiploic from the gastroduodenal artery). The fundus sits outside both arcades and is supplied instead by the short gastric arteries, direct splenic artery branches -- a frequently tested exception, since it is easy to assume the greater-curvature gastroepiploic vessels cover the whole stomach including the fundus. On the venous side, the portal vein forms behind the neck of the pancreas from the union of the splenic vein and the superior mesenteric vein; the right gastroepiploic and inferior pancreaticoduodenal veins drain into the SMV, and the short gastric veins drain into the splenic vein, so all three reach the portal vein indirectly, one vessel removed from the union point itself. The abdominal aorta's own branch pattern follows a fixed sequence useful for level-based recall: coeliac trunk at T12, superior mesenteric artery at L1, renal arteries around L1-L2, gonadal arteries below the SMA (around L2), inferior mesenteric artery at L3, and finally the median sacral artery -- a small, unpaired posterior branch and embryological remnant of the dorsal aorta -- immediately before the aortic bifurcation into the common iliac arteries at L4.

### Key determinants
Every question on this material turns on one of three axes: which named artery is the *direct* coeliac trunk branch versus a second-order branch of one of the three (right gastric and left gastroepiploic are both second-order); which vein is a *direct* portal-vein tributary versus one that joins the SMV or splenic vein first; and the fixed vertebral level of each unpaired ventral aortic branch (T12, L1, L3) plus the paired/unpaired status of each branch along the way.

### Clinical significance
Portal hypertension (commonly from cirrhosis) raises pressure throughout the portal system, and the four classic sites where the portal and systemic venous systems anastomose -- lower oesophagus, upper anal canal/lower rectum, paraumbilical region, and the bare-area retroperitoneal veins of Retzius -- each develop dilated collaterals. Only the oesophageal site (varices) routinely produces severe, sometimes fatal, upper GI haemorrhage, because those thin-walled submucosal veins sit directly in the path of swallowed food and reflux acid; the other three sites are clinically important (haemorrhoids, caput medusae) but far less often catastrophic.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming the gastroepiploic arcade supplies the whole stomach including the fundus (the fundus is short-gastric territory specifically); calling the right gastric or left gastroepiploic artery a 'coeliac trunk branch' rather than the second-order branch it actually is; and treating every vein that eventually empties into the portal vein as a 'direct tributary,' when only the splenic vein and SMV meet exactly at the point of formation.

ART-GIT-206DIG-PERITONEUM-EMBRYOLOGY-POSTERIOR-WALL
## title
Peritoneal spaces of the upper abdomen, foregut/midgut boundaries, and the posterior abdominal wall's nerves and coverings
## subject
gi
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Anatomy
## summary
The lesser sac (omental bursa) sits behind the stomach and lesser omentum and only communicates with the rest of the peritoneal cavity through the narrow epiploic foramen, which is why a posterior gastric perforation floods the lesser sac rather than the general peritoneal cavity an anterior perforation would reach. The three primitive gut divisions -- foregut, midgut, hindgut -- are defined by fixed anatomical boundaries and each supplies its own dedicated artery and, for the midgut, a single venous outflow route through the superior mesenteric vein; the midgut's true distal limit is the transverse-colon junction, not the ileocaecal valve, since the caecum and appendix are themselves midgut structures. On the posterior abdominal wall, most lumbar-plexus branches emerge from the lateral border of psoas major, except the genitofemoral nerve, which is unusual in piercing the muscle itself; and the spermatic cord's three coverings map one-to-one onto the three musculofascial layers of the anterior abdominal wall the descending testis passed through.
## sections
### Definition
The greater peritoneal sac and the omental bursa (lesser sac) are the two divisions of the peritoneal cavity, connected only by the epiploic (omental) foramen of Winslow, bounded posteriorly by the inferior vena cava. The three primitive gut divisions are: foregut (pharynx to mid-second part of duodenum, at the bile duct's entry -- giving the oesophagus, stomach, proximal duodenum, liver, gallbladder, biliary tree and pancreas), midgut (mid-second part of duodenum to the junction of the proximal two-thirds and distal one-third of the transverse colon -- giving the rest of the duodenum, jejunum, ileum, caecum, appendix, ascending colon and proximal transverse colon), and hindgut (distal third of transverse colon to the upper anal canal -- descending colon, sigmoid colon, rectum).

### Mechanism
Because the posterior wall of the stomach forms the anterior wall of the lesser sac, a posteriorly perforating gastric ulcer spills directly into the omental bursa, whereas an anterior perforation reaches the greater sac's subhepatic, hepatorenal (Morison's pouch) or subphrenic recesses instead. The duodenum's four parts show a mixed peritoneal relationship: only the proximal ~2.5 cm of the first part (the duodenal cap) is intraperitoneal, the rest is retroperitoneal; the bile duct and main pancreatic duct both open together at the major duodenal papilla on the second part, which lies anterior to the right kidney's hilum; and the third (horizontal) part is crossed anteriorly by the superior mesenteric artery and vein together with the root of the mesentery -- compression here causes superior mesenteric artery syndrome. Within the midgut mesentery, the SMA lies to the left of the SMV, and the SMA itself branches from the aorta at L1 -- one level above the inferior mesenteric artery's L3 origin, a pairing this exam tests across two separate questions. On the posterior abdominal wall, the ilioinguinal, iliohypogastric and lateral cutaneous nerve of the thigh all emerge from the lateral border of psoas major; the genitofemoral nerve (L1, L2) is the exception, passing through the substance of the muscle and emerging on its anterior surface before dividing into genital and femoral branches. As the testis descends through the anterior abdominal wall, it picks up one covering from each layer it passes: external spermatic fascia from external oblique's aponeurosis, cremasteric muscle and fascia from internal oblique, and internal spermatic fascia from transversalis fascia -- dartos fascia is a separate scrotal-wall layer, not a fourth cord covering.

### Key determinants
Three recurring axes: anterior versus posterior perforation determines which peritoneal space is flooded; the fixed embryological boundary (transverse-colon junction, not the ileocaecal valve) defines the midgut regardless of how far distally its derivatives (caecum, appendix) extend; and which structures emerge from psoas major's lateral border versus pass through its substance (genitofemoral nerve alone) separates one exam distractor from the rest.

### Clinical significance
A posteriorly perforating peptic ulcer can be radiologically and clinically silent in the greater sac while still producing a localized lesser-sac collection, changing the expected exam findings; SMA compression of the duodenum's third part causes a recognised (if uncommon) cause of postprandial vomiting and weight loss; and the spermatic cord's three-layer covering sequence is exactly what a surgeon divides, in order, during inguinal hernia repair or orchidopexy.

### Common misconceptions
Ending the midgut at the ileocaecal valve rather than at the transverse-colon junction; assuming the whole duodenum is retroperitoneal; swapping the SMA/SMV lateral relationship or the SMA/IMA vertebral levels; and listing dartos fascia as a spermatic cord covering rather than a scrotal wall layer.

# Item
## id
ART-GIT-206DIG-LIVER-INTESTINE-APPENDIX
## title
Liver surface topography, jejunum-versus-ileum gross distinguishing features, and appendix anatomy
## subject
gi
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Anatomy
## summary
The liver occupies most of the right hypochondrium and epigastrium, almost but not completely covered by the costal margin, with the ligamentum teres fissure on its inferior (visceral) surface and the ligamentum venosum fissure on its posterior surface -- two adjacent fissures in the same sagittal plane but on two different named surfaces, a distinction exam distractors love to swap. The jejunum and ileum are told apart by four paired gross features (wall thickness, mesenteric fat, vascular arcade number, lymphoid follicle density), all of which trend in the same direction from proximal to distal small bowel. The vermiform appendix opens into the caecum about 2 cm below the ileocaecal valve at the taenial confluence, carries its own short mesentery, and is most often retrocaecal in position -- with its pain referred to McBurney's point, not to Murphy's point, which is a separate, gallbladder-specific sign.
## sections
### Definition
The liver's two principal surfaces are the diaphragmatic (anterosuperior) surface and the visceral (inferior) surface; a further, more granular six-surface scheme (superior, anterior, right, posterior, inferior/visceral, and the porta-hepatis region) is commonly taught to localise specific fissures precisely. The jejunum begins at the duodenojejunal flexure and the ileum ends at the ileocaecal valve, with no sharp anatomical boundary between them -- only a gradient of gross features. The vermiform appendix is a blind-ended tube arising from the posteromedial caecal wall.

### Mechanism
On the visceral (inferior) surface, the fissure for the ligamentum teres (the obliterated left umbilical vein) runs in the left sagittal fissure between the quadrate lobe and the anatomical left lobe; the fissure for the ligamentum venosum (the obliterated ductus venosus) continues this same sagittal plane onto the posterior surface, running alongside the groove for the inferior vena cava and separating the caudate lobe from the left lobe -- so the two fissures that together form one continuous groove sit on two different named surfaces of the liver. The liver is not completely hidden by the costal margin: a small triangular area in the epigastrium lies in direct contact with the anterior abdominal wall, unprotected by the rib cage. Moving along the small intestine from jejunum to ileum, wall thickness decreases, mesenteric fat content increases (making jejunal mesentery visibly more translucent when held to light), vascular arcades become more numerous but smaller with shorter vasa recta, and lymphoid (Peyer's patch) density increases, most markedly in the distal ileum. The appendix arises about 2 cm below the ileocaecal valve at the point where the three taeniae coli of the caecum converge -- a landmark useful for finding its base at surgery even when its tip is retrocaecal and obscured. It carries its own short mesentery (the mesoappendix, containing the appendicular artery), and its most common anatomical position is retrocaecal, with pelvic position second most frequent.

### Key determinants
The recurring test axis for the liver is inferior (visceral) versus posterior surface placement for the two adjacent sagittal-fissure structures; for the small intestine it is which of the four paired gross features increases versus decreases from jejunum to ileum; and for the appendix it is the correct clinical eponym (McBurney's point, not Murphy's point) and the correct majority anatomical position (retrocaecal, not subcaecal).

### Clinical significance
Knowing which liver surface a lesion or fissure sits on matters for imaging-plane interpretation and surgical approach; the jejunum/ileum gross distinguishing features (especially mesenteric fat translucency) are exactly what a surgeon uses intraoperatively to identify a segment of exposed small bowel without preoperative marking; and the appendix's variable position (retrocaecal most often, but also pelvic, subcaecal, pre-ileal or post-ileal) explains why appendicitis can present with atypical pain patterns despite a fixed base location relative to the ileocaecal valve.

### Common misconceptions
Swapping the ligamentum teres and ligamentum venosum fissures between the inferior and posterior liver surfaces; assuming the ileum has thicker walls or fewer, larger arcades than the jejunum (the reverse is true); attributing appendicitis pain to Murphy's point (a cholecystitis sign); and assuming the appendix has no mesentery or is usually subcaecal rather than retrocaecal.
