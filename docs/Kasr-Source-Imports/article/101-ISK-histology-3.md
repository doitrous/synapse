## summary
Four organelles, one route. A protein is made on a ribosome, and where that ribosome is sitting decides everything that follows: free in the cytosol and the protein stays in the cell, bound to the endoplasmic reticulum and the protein is packed, sent to the Golgi and exported. Every light-microscopic finding the exam asks for is a side effect of that route — the basophilia is the ribosomes, the acidophilia is their absence, and the pale patch beside the nucleus is a Golgi that H&E cannot stain.
## sections
### Definition
The endoplasmic reticulum is a membranous network extending from the nucleus to the cell membrane, enclosing intercommunicating channels and sacs called cisternae. It is of two types, rough and smooth, and the difference between them is one thing only: whether ribosomes are bound to the membrane.

A ribosome is a non-membranous particle formed of ribosomal RNA and proteins. The rRNA is formed in the nucleolus and the proteins in the cytoplasm; both unite in the nucleolus into a small and a large subunit, which return to the cytoplasm and join one another only during protein synthesis. Ribosomes are of two types by position — free and attached — and the position is what the question is about.

The Golgi apparatus is a membranous organelle concerned with secretion. It is well developed in secretory cells, and it is sited apically in them and perinuclearly in nerve cells.

These three are sorted differently in the chapter's opening classification, and it is worth holding. The endoplasmic reticulum and the Golgi apparatus are membranous organelles, listed with the plasma membrane, the mitochondria, the lysosomes and the peroxisomes. The ribosome is not: it is a non-membranous organelle, listed with the cytoskeleton.

### Mechanism
The route runs in one direction, and each organelle does one step of it.

Synthesis begins on the ribosome. A free ribosome sits in the cytosol, singly or as a polyribosome — several ribosomes linked by a strand of messenger RNA into rosettes or spiral chains — and the protein it makes is used inside the cell, the book's example being the glycolytic enzymes. An attached ribosome is bound to the membrane of the rough endoplasmic reticulum by its large subunit, at proteins of the membrane called ribophorins, and the protein it makes is one the cell secretes: an enzyme or a hormone. Nothing else distinguishes the two. It is the same particle, and its address decides its product's destination.

The rough endoplasmic reticulum then does five further things to that protein and one thing for the cell. It synthesises it, segregates it inside the cisterna, performs its initial glycosylation, packs it into transfer vesicles addressed to the Golgi apparatus, protects the cytoplasm from the hydrolytic enzymes it is carrying, and serves as an intracellular pathway. Segregation and protection are the same fact seen from two sides: once a hydrolytic enzyme is inside a cisterna it cannot digest the cell that made it.

The Golgi apparatus receives those transfer vesicles. Each stack has an entry face, the cis face, which receives transfer vesicles from the rough endoplasmic reticulum, and an exit face, the trans face, which buds off secretory vesicles or lysosomes. That is the pathway's fork: the same stack dispatches the product the cell exports and the enzyme packet the cell keeps.

The smooth endoplasmic reticulum runs off this route rather than along it. Its membranes carry no ribosomes, so it makes no protein. What it does is lipid work and clearance: synthesis of phospholipid, synthesis of the steroid hormones — the book names cortisone and testosterone — breakdown of glycogen to glucose, detoxification of drugs, alcohol and hormones, release of calcium in muscle contraction, and service as an intracellular pathway. It is continuous with the rough endoplasmic reticulum, which is why the two are one organelle in two states rather than two organelles.

### Key determinants
The department book prints this as a four-column table — site, light microscopy, electron microscopy, function — and the paper asks it back column by column. Answer in those columns.

**Rough endoplasmic reticulum.** Its site is the protein-forming cell, and the book's example is the plasma cell. By light microscopy it is not seen as an object; what is seen is the cytoplasmic basophilia it causes, and the basophilia belongs to the RNA of the ribosomes on it, not to the membrane. By electron microscopy it is parallel flattened cisternae, continuous with the outer nuclear envelope, studded on their outer surface with ribosomes attached by their large subunit at the ribophorins. Its functions are the six listed above.

**Smooth endoplasmic reticulum.** Its site is the lipid-forming cell, and the book's example is the liver cell. By light microscopy it is not demonstrated as such; where it is abundant the cytoplasm is acidophilic, and that acidophilia is the whole of the light-microscopic answer. By electron microscopy it is a network of branching and anastomosing tubular cisternae of variable shape, continuous with the rough endoplasmic reticulum, carrying no ribosomes. Its functions are the six listed above.

The two light-microscopic rows are a matched pair and the examiner sets them against each other. A cell packed with rough endoplasmic reticulum is basophilic; a cell packed with smooth endoplasmic reticulum is acidophilic; and neither organelle is itself visible.

**Golgi apparatus.** Its site is apical in secretory cells and perinuclear in nerve cells. Its stains are the examinable point, because there are two answers and they are opposite. In haematoxylin and eosin the Golgi is not stained: it appears only as a pale unstained area beside the nucleus, the negative Golgi image. Silver impregnation demonstrates it directly, as a network of brown granules and fibrils. By electron microscopy it is a stack of three to ten interconnected parallel flat curved saccules lying above one another, with a cis entry face and a trans exit face.

**Ribosomes.** By electron microscopy a free ribosome is a single dense particle, or a polyribosome — a rosette or a spiral chain of particles strung on messenger RNA. An attached ribosome is a particle on the outer surface of a rough endoplasmic reticulum cisterna, held by its large subunit at a ribophorin. Free ribosomes form protein used within the cell; attached ribosomes form protein secreted by the cell. Ribosomes are also what makes cytoplasm basophilic, because of the acidity of the phosphate group in RNA, and the book describes that basophilia as focal, diffuse or localised.

### Normal values
The Golgi stack is three to ten saccules. The endoplasmic reticulum has two types. The ribosome has two subunits, small and large, and two positions, free and attached. Cytoplasmic basophilia is described in three patterns: focal, diffuse and localised. The book gives six functions for the rough endoplasmic reticulum and six for the smooth.

### Clinical significance
The book states the consequences through the functions rather than through named diseases, and two of them carry directly.

Detoxification of drugs, of alcohol and of hormones is a stated function of the smooth endoplasmic reticulum, and the book sites that organelle in the liver cell. Steroid hormone synthesis — cortisone and testosterone are the book's own examples — is a second stated function of the same organelle, which is why a steroid-secreting cell is built the way it is rather than the way a plasma cell is.

The rough endoplasmic reticulum and the Golgi apparatus together are where the lysosomal enzymes are made and packaged: they are synthesised in the rough endoplasmic reticulum, carried by transfer vesicles to the Golgi apparatus, and released from it inside lysosomes. The book's applied note on lysosomes is therefore a note on this route. A lack of lysosomal enzymes such as the sulfatases results in intracellular accumulation of sulfated compounds, which interfere with the normal function of nerve cells.

The negative Golgi image also has a use at the bench rather than in a clinic. It is one of the two findings that identify a plasma cell in a routine section — deeply basophilic cytoplasm from rough endoplasmic reticulum, and a pale unstained Golgi beside an eccentric cart-wheel nucleus — so a student who understands why H&E leaves the Golgi blank can name the cell.

### Common misconceptions
The basophilia is not the membrane. It is the RNA of the ribosomes bound to the membrane, and RNA takes a basic dye because of the acidity of the phosphate group. The proof is the smooth endoplasmic reticulum: the same membrane without ribosomes gives acidophilia instead.

The negative Golgi image is not an absent Golgi. The organelle is there; H&E simply does not take to it, and a silver preparation shows it as a network of brown granules and fibrils in the same place.

Free and attached ribosomes are not two organelles. They are one particle in two positions, and the position is the answer to the question "where does the protein go".

The smooth endoplasmic reticulum is not the rough one with its ribosomes knocked off. The two are continuous and the book treats them as two types of one organelle, but they sit in different cells, look different on electron microscopy — flattened parallel cisternae against branching anastomosing tubules — and do different work.

## summary

The mitochondrion is asked as a description with the marks sitting on the small structures — the elementary particles on the cristae, the granules in the matrix — rather than on the two membranes everyone remembers. ## sections
### Definition
Mitochondria are membranous organelles containing the enzymes for aerobic respiration and energy production. The book calls them the power-house of the cell and sites them in the cell's most active areas, which is the first fact about them: their position is not fixed by the cell's anatomy but by where work is being done.

The cytoskeleton is a complex network of microtubules, microfilaments and intermediate filaments together with linking proteins, forming what the book calls a microtrabecular lattice. It is one of the two non-membranous organelles, the other being the ribosome.

The three cytoskeletal elements are a closed list of three, and each is defined by a diameter and a protein. Microtubules are twenty-four nanometres across and made of tubulin. Microfilaments are five to seven nanometres across and made of actin. Intermediate filaments are eight to ten nanometres across, and their protein is not one protein but six, named by the tissue each belongs to.

### Mechanism
**The mitochondrion.** It is bounded by two unit membranes, and the two are not variants of one another. The outer membrane is smooth, carries porins, and is permeable to small molecules. The inner membrane is selectively permeable and is thrown into folds — the cristae — and on those cristae sit elementary particles with ATP synthase activity. That last clause is where the marks are. Folding the inner membrane multiplies the surface, and the surface is worth multiplying because it carries the particles that make the ATP.

Inside is the matrix, and the book gives it three contents. It holds the oxidative enzymes of the citric acid cycle. It holds mitochondrial DNA, with messenger, transfer and ribosomal RNA. And it holds dense granules rich in calcium, which act as catalysts.

Mitochondria increase in number by simple division — not by assembly from parts made elsewhere — which is consistent with their carrying their own DNA and their own RNAs. They stain dark blue with iron haematoxylin and green with Janus green.

**The cytoskeleton.** A microtubule is a hollow cylinder. Its wall is thirteen parallel protofilaments built of alpha and beta tubulin, and its assembly is directed by the microtubule organising centre, the MTOC, which contains a third tubulin, gamma. It determines cell shape and elongation, transports organelles and macromolecules, forms the mitotic spindle, and forms the centrioles, cilia and flagella.

A microfilament is a fine strand of two chains of globular G actin coiled together into filamentous F actin. It lies beneath the plasmalemma and inside microvilli. It changes the cell's shape in endocytosis, exocytosis and amoeboid movement, transports organelles, cleaves the cell in division, forms the core of the microvillus, and acts in muscle contraction.

An intermediate filament is formed by polymerisation of chemically differing tetrameric subunits, and its role is supportive. The six proteins the book names each belong to a tissue: cytokeratin in epithelium, vimentin in connective and muscular tissue, desmin in muscular tissue, neurofilaments in neurons, glial fibrillar acidic protein in glial cells, and lamins in the nuclear envelope.

Read the two filament systems against each other and their division of labour is plain. The microtubule holds a shape and moves things along a line; the microfilament changes a shape and pinches things off. Which is why the microtubule builds the spindle that separates chromosomes and the microfilament builds the ring that cleaves the cell.

### Key determinants
**Electron microscopy of the mitochondrion**, which is how the paper phrases it. Answer in four parts and in this order.

Two unit membranes bound it. The outer is smooth, carries porins and is permeable to small molecules. The inner is selectively permeable and folded into cristae. On the cristae are elementary particles carrying ATP synthase activity. The matrix within holds the oxidative enzymes of the citric acid cycle, mitochondrial DNA with its mRNA, tRNA and rRNA, and dense calcium-rich granules acting as catalysts.

An answer that names the two membranes and the cristae and stops has given the shape without the machinery, and the machinery is what the organelle is for.

**Microtubules against microfilaments**, which the book prints as a table and the resit paper asks under three named headings: diameter, protein subunit and light-microscopic picture.

Diameter: microtubule twenty-four nanometres, microfilament five to seven nanometres. The intermediate filament, at eight to ten nanometres, sits between them, which is where its name comes from.

Protein subunit: microtubule, alpha and beta tubulin, arranged as thirteen parallel protofilaments forming the wall of a hollow cylinder, with gamma tubulin in the organising centre that directs them. Microfilament, two chains of globular G actin coiled into filamentous F actin.

Light-microscopic picture: neither is seen. The book states that all three cytoskeletal elements are difficult to see by light microscopy except by immunofluorescence, and that is the answer the row wants. Writing a description of what a microtubule looks like under the light microscope is answering a question the slide cannot answer.

Location and function fill the remaining rows. Microtubules lie throughout the cell and build the centrioles, cilia and flagella; microfilaments lie beneath the plasmalemma and in the microvilli, and build the microvillus core.

### Normal values
Microtubule diameter is 24 nm and its wall is 13 parallel protofilaments. Microfilament diameter is 5–7 nm. Intermediate filament diameter is 8–10 nm. A liver cell may hold up to 2000 mitochondria. The book names three cytoskeletal elements, six intermediate filament proteins, three tubulins — alpha, beta and gamma — and three structures formed by microtubules: centrioles, cilia and flagella.

### Clinical significance
The book's applied notes for this part of the chapter are three, and all three follow from the structures above.

A defect in the mitochondrial enzymes means failure to produce the ATP that every vital activity requires. In muscle, the book says, this presents as muscular weakness — the tissue whose work is most obviously energy is the tissue where the failure is first seen.

Cancer chemotherapy is used to arrest cell proliferation in tumours by preventing microtubule formation. The link is the mitotic spindle: a cell that cannot build microtubules cannot separate its chromosomes and cannot divide.

Identifying the intermediate filament proteins by immunocytochemical methods matters for the diagnosis and treatment of tumours, because it reveals the cell the tumour came from. This is the clinical pay-off of the six-protein list: cytokeratin says epithelium, vimentin says connective tissue, desmin says muscle, neurofilaments say neuron, glial fibrillar acidic protein says glia. A tumour that has lost its shape has not lost its filaments.

### Common misconceptions
Describing the cristae as folds and stopping. The elementary particles on them carry the ATP synthase, and they are the reason the folding is worth having. The mark is on the particles.

Treating the two mitochondrial membranes as one thing described twice. They differ in three stated ways: the outer is smooth, porin-bearing and permeable to small molecules, the inner is selectively permeable and folded.

Answering the light-microscopy row with a description. Neither microtubules nor microfilaments are resolvable by ordinary light microscopy, and the honest answer — visible only by immunofluorescence — is the one the book gives.

Reading "intermediate filament" as a filament halfway through assembly, or as a stage between the other two. It is a separate, third element, named for a diameter that falls between the other two, and it is built from a different kind of subunit.

Assuming an organelle with its own DNA must make all its own protein. The book states only that the matrix holds DNA and the three RNAs, and that mitochondria multiply by simple division. It does not say how much of the mitochondrion is made from that DNA.
## hold_these
The mitochondrion is bounded by two unit membranes: the outer smooth, carrying porins and permeable to small molecules, the inner selectively permeable and folded into cristae.
The cristae bear elementary particles with ATP synthase activity.
The mitochondrial matrix contains the oxidative enzymes of the citric acid cycle, mitochondrial DNA with mRNA, tRNA and rRNA, and dense calcium-rich granules acting as catalysts.
Mitochondria increase in number by simple division, and stain dark blue with iron haematoxylin and green with Janus green.
A liver cell may hold up to 2000 mitochondria.
Microtubules are 24 nm across, hollow cylinders whose wall is 13 parallel protofilaments of alpha and beta tubulin, directed by the microtubule organising centre which contains gamma tubulin.
Microfilaments are 5-7 nm across, two chains of globular G actin coiled into filamentous F actin.
Intermediate filaments are 8-10 nm across and are supportive, with six named proteins: cytokeratin, vimentin, desmin, neurofilaments, glial fibrillar acidic protein and lamins.
None of the three cytoskeletal elements is seen by ordinary light microscopy; they are demonstrated only by immunofluorescence.
Microtubules determine cell shape and elongation, transport organelles and macromolecules, form the mitotic spindle, and form centrioles, cilia and flagella.
Microfilaments change cell shape in endocytosis, exocytosis and amoeboid movement, transport organelles, cleave the cell in division, form the microvillus core and act in muscle contraction.
The ribosome and the cytoskeleton are the two non-membranous organelles.
## lose_the_mark
Naming the cristae and stopping. The elementary particles with ATP synthase activity are what the cristae are for, and the mark is on them.
Describing both mitochondrial membranes as smooth and permeable. Only the outer is; the inner is selectively permeable and folded.
Leaving the matrix as "enzymes". The book names three contents: citric acid cycle enzymes, DNA with its three RNAs, and dense calcium-rich granules acting as catalysts.
Giving a light-microscopic description of a microtubule or a microfilament. Neither is resolvable; the answer is immunofluorescence.
Swapping the diameters. The microtubule is the widest at 24 nm and the microfilament the narrowest at 5-7 nm, with the intermediate filament between them at 8-10 nm.
Giving actin as the microtubule protein or tubulin as the microfilament protein. Tubulin builds the tube, actin builds the filament.
Naming only alpha and beta tubulin. Gamma tubulin is in the microtubule organising centre, and the organising centre is what directs assembly.
Listing five intermediate filament proteins. The book names six, and lamins in the nuclear envelope are the one usually dropped.
Saying mitochondria are made by the Golgi or the endoplasmic reticulum. They increase by simple division.

## evidence_gaps
The book does not state the size or shape of a mitochondrion, nor how many cristae a mitochondrion carries, so no figure is given here.
The book does not name the enzyme complexes of the respiratory chain, nor describe oxidative phosphorylation beyond attributing ATP synthase activity to the elementary particles.
The book does not say that mitochondrial DNA is maternally inherited, nor name any mitochondrial disease. The applied note is generic: a defect in mitochondrial enzymes causing muscular weakness.
The book does not give the length of a microtubule or a microfilament, only diameters, and gives no polarity, no plus and minus end, and no named motor protein for the transport it attributes to both.
The book states that intermediate filaments are formed by polymerisation of chemically differing tetrameric subunits but does not describe that subunit further.
The book does not state where in the cell the microtubule organising centre lies in relation to the centrosome in this passage, although the centriole passage on printed page 19 describes the centrosome as the organising centre during mitosis. The two statements are not reconciled in the text.
