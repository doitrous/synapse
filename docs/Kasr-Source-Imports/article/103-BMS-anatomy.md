## summary
The adductor canal is the tunnel that carries the femoral vessels from the front of the thigh to the back of the knee. It is triangular in cross section, lies under sartorius on the medial side of the middle third of the thigh, and has three walls: a fibrous antero-medial roof, a floor of adductor longus above and adductor magnus below, and an antero-lateral wall of vastus medialis. Four structures pass through it — the femoral artery, the femoral vein, the saphenous nerve and the nerve to vastus medialis. Knowing the walls and the contents is the whole of what the 2025 paper asked for six marks.

## sections
### Overview and position
The adductor canal is an intermuscular tunnel on the medial aspect of the middle third of the thigh, under cover of the sartorius muscle. It begins at the apex of the femoral triangle and ends at the opening in adductor magnus, the adductor hiatus. It is the corridor that connects the femoral triangle on the front of the thigh to the popliteal fossa on the back of the knee, and the femoral artery uses it to change compartments: the upper half of the artery is superficial and lies in the femoral triangle, the lower half is deep and lies here.

### Structure
The canal is triangular in cross section, having the following walls. The antero-medial wall is a fibrous roof extending between vastus medialis and adductor magnus, covered by sartorius. The posterior wall, which is also called the floor, is formed by adductor longus above and adductor magnus below. The antero-lateral wall is formed by vastus medialis.

Its contents are four: the femoral artery, the femoral vein, the saphenous nerve and the nerve to vastus medialis. Note that the roof is a fibrous sheet and not a muscle; sartorius lies on top of it rather than forming it, which is why the canal is also called the subsartorial canal.

### Relations
Inside the canal the femoral artery has a fibrous roof and sartorius in front of it, adductor longus and adductor magnus behind it, and the femoral vein behind it in the upper part of the canal. Laterally lie the saphenous nerve, in the upper part, together with vastus medialis and its nerve; medially lies the saphenous nerve in the lower part.

That crossing is worth holding: the saphenous nerve enters the canal lateral to the artery, crosses in front of it, and finishes medial to it before leaving by piercing the roof. Adductor longus is the muscle that separates the femoral artery from the profunda femoris vessels.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
The canal is a space rather than an organ, so it has no supply of its own; what matters is what it carries. The femoral artery traverses it and leaves through the adductor hiatus to become the popliteal artery. The saphenous nerve, the longest cutaneous nerve in the body, is a branch of the femoral nerve that arises in the femoral triangle, runs the length of the canal and leaves it by piercing the fibrous roof, giving its infra-patellar branch there. The nerve to vastus medialis, also from the femoral nerve, is motor. The department book records no lymphatic content for the canal, and none is asserted here.

### Development
The department book treats limb development as a single chapter for the whole lower limb rather than region by region, so it prints nothing specific to this canal. What it does record, and what makes the arrangement here intelligible, is that the lower limb bud rotates approximately 90 degrees medially during the seventh week, placing the extensor muscles on the anterior surface and the big toe medially. The adductor group and its intermuscular tunnel therefore end up on the medial side of the thigh.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
The canal lies deep to sartorius over the middle third of a line drawn from the mid-inguinal point to the adductor tubercle. Because the femoral artery is deep here, its pulse is not palpable in the canal the way it is in the femoral triangle — that is the practical consequence of the artery changing from superficial to deep at the apex of the triangle. No imaging appearance is described in the department book, and none is claimed.

### Clinical correlations
The canal is the standard site for the adductor canal block, which anaesthetises the saphenous nerve, and it is where the femoral artery is exposed surgically in the thigh. Its clinical logic follows from its structure: a fibrous roof and three muscular walls make it a rigid tunnel, so a mass or a haematoma inside it compresses its contents rather than displacing them, and the saphenous nerve — being the only sensory content — is the one that declares the problem.

## summary
Peroneus longus is the larger of the two muscles of the lateral compartment of the leg. It arises from the upper two thirds of the lateral surface of the fibula, and its tendon runs behind the lateral malleolus, crosses the sole transversely and inserts into the medial cuneiform and the base of the first metatarsal. It is supplied by the superficial peroneal nerve. Because its tendon passes behind the ankle and across the sole, it everts the foot at the subtalar joint and plantar-flexes it at the ankle, and it supports the lateral longitudinal and transverse arches.

## sections
### Overview and position
The lateral compartment lies on the lateral surface of the fibula and contains two peroneal muscles, peroneus longus and peroneus brevis. The tendon of peroneus longus lies superficial to that of peroneus brevis. This is the eversion compartment of the leg: neither muscle dorsiflexes, and both are supplied by the same nerve.

### Structure
Peroneus longus arises from the upper two thirds of the lateral surface of the shaft of the fibula. Its tendon crosses the sole transversely, from lateral to medial, to become inserted, in the fourth layer of the sole, into the lateral aspect of the medial cuneiform bone and the lateral aspect of the base of the first metatarsal bone.

Peroneus brevis, alongside it, arises from the lower two thirds of the same surface and inserts into the tuberosity on the base of the fifth metatarsal. The pair are easy to separate on their insertions: longus crosses the sole to the medial side, brevis stops on the lateral side of the foot.

### Relations
The muscle wraps the fibula, so the common peroneal nerve is its most important relation: the nerve curves round the neck of the fibula and then pierces peroneus longus, dividing inside the substance of the muscle into the superficial and deep peroneal nerves. The superficial peroneal nerve then runs in the substance of peroneus longus, and afterwards between peroneus longus and brevis. Distally the tendon passes behind the lateral malleolus with that of peroneus brevis and then turns into the sole.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
Peroneus longus is supplied by the musculo-cutaneous nerve, which is the superficial peroneal nerve. That is the point the exam turns on: the muscle that everts the foot is supplied by the superficial peroneal branch, while the muscles that dorsiflex it are supplied by the deep peroneal branch, and a lesion of the parent common peroneal nerve takes out both. The department book does not state the arterial supply of this muscle individually; the peroneal artery is described as the main supply of the back of the leg, and no separate claim is made for the lateral compartment here.

### Development
The department book has no development section for individual muscles. What it does record is that limb musculature is derived from the dermomyotomes of the somites, that the muscle tissue first splits into flexor and extensor components, and that the lower limb rotates approximately 90 degrees medially in the seventh week, which places the extensor muscles on the anterior surface. That rotation is why the peroneal muscles, which are neither flexors nor extensors of the foot in the simple sense, end up on the lateral side.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
The tendon of peroneus longus can be followed behind the lateral malleolus when the foot is actively everted against resistance, and the muscle belly is palpable over the upper lateral leg. The neck of the fibula, just below the head, is palpable, and it is the point at which the common peroneal nerve is against bone before it enters this muscle. The department book describes no imaging appearance for the muscle and none is claimed.

### Clinical correlations
Peroneus longus is the muscle whose paralysis abolishes eversion. When the common peroneal nerve is injured at the fibular neck, the three peroneal muscles are denervated and the foot loses eversion, while the anterior compartment loses dorsiflexion; together those produce talipes equinovarus. The tendon's long transverse course across the sole also makes it one of the two active supports of the transverse arch, so its loss contributes to the flattening seen after peroneal palsy.

## summary
The hip is a polyaxial ball-and-socket synovial joint between the head of the femur and the acetabulum, so it moves in three planes. Each movement has one prime mover and named assistants: iliopsoas flexes, gluteus maximus extends, gluteus medius and minimus abduct, the adductors adduct, the anterior fibres of gluteus medius and minimus rotate medially, and the six small rotators rotate laterally. Circumduction is a combination of the others. Learning the joint as seven movements, each with a prime mover and its helpers, is exactly the structure the 2025 paper's six-mark question rewarded.

## sections
### Overview and position
The hip joint is a synovial, polyaxial joint of the ball-and-socket variety. Its articulating bones are the head of the femur, the rounded ball, and the acetabulum of the hip bone, the cup-like socket. It is a very stable joint, and the book gives three reasons: a bony factor, in that the femoral head fits well into the deep socket; a ligamentous factor, in that a strong capsule and ligaments surround the joint, especially the ilio-femoral ligament; and a muscular factor, in that many strong muscles surround it. That stability is what makes its range smaller than the shoulder's and its dislocations rarer and more violent.

### Structure
A strong fibrous capsule surrounds the joint completely and is attached to the margins of the articulating bones. Three extra-capsular ligaments strengthen it. The ilio-femoral ligament is an inverted Y whose stem is attached to the anterior inferior iliac spine and whose two limbs reach the two ends of the intertrochanteric line; it is the strongest ligament of the joint and it limits over-extension. The pubo-femoral ligament is triangular, attached to the superior pubic ramus and the ilio-pubic eminence, and it limits over-abduction. The ischio-femoral ligament is attached to the body of the ischium below the acetabulum, supports the back of the capsule and limits excessive medial rotation.

Inside the joint the round ligament of the head of the femur, the ligamentum teres, runs from the fovea on the head to the acetabular notch and the transverse acetabular ligament, and it carries an artery to the head. The transverse acetabular ligament converts the notch into a foramen, and the labrum acetabulare is a fibrocartilaginous rim that deepens the socket.

### Relations
Each of the three capsular ligaments limits one movement, which is why the ligaments and the movements have to be learned together: ilio-femoral against over-extension, pubo-femoral against over-abduction, ischio-femoral against excessive medial rotation. The muscles that produce the movements lie in three groups around the joint — the flexors in front, the gluteal group behind and laterally, and the adductors medially — and the six small lateral rotators lie deep to gluteus maximus in the gluteal region.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
The nerve supply of the hip joint is from branches of the femoral nerve, the anterior division of the obturator nerve, the sciatic nerve and the nerve to quadratus femoris. Its arterial supply comes from the gluteal, circumflex and obturator arteries. Some fibres arising from the capsule, called retinacula, extend along the neck of the femur towards its head; they carry the blood supply to the head and neck and keep the segments of a fractured neck in position. The department book records no lymphatic drainage for the joint, and none is asserted here.

### Development
The department book gives no development section for the hip joint specifically. For the limb as a whole it records that by the sixth week of development joints are formed by arresting chondrogenesis to form a joint cavity, with the surrounding cells differentiating into a joint capsule, and that the lower limb rotates approximately 90 degrees medially in the seventh week. The medial rotation is what puts the extensor muscles of the lower limb on the front, which is why the hip flexors and the knee extensors share the anterior compartment of the thigh.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
The greater trochanter is palpable on the lateral side of the hip and is one of the two landmarks between which the sciatic nerve descends. The joint line itself is not palpable, as it lies deep to a thick muscular envelope. The department book asks students to identify the articular surfaces of the joint on skeletons, cadavers and radiographs, but describes no radiographic appearance in text, so none is reproduced here.

### Clinical correlations
Fractures of the neck of the femur are very common in old age, especially in women, because of osteoporosis, and the capsular retinacula matter because they carry the blood supply the fractured head depends on. Dislocation of the hip may be congenital or traumatic; traumatic posterior dislocation occurs in car accidents and may injure the sciatic nerve, which is the anatomical link between this article and the sciatic nerve article. Loss of a single movement is a useful localiser at the bedside: isolated weakness of abduction points at gluteus medius and minimus and their nerve, while weak lateral rotation points at the deep gluteal group.

## summary
The posterior tibial artery is the larger of the two terminal branches of the popliteal artery. It runs down the back of the leg between the superficial and deep flexor groups, becomes superficial in the lower leg where its pulse is felt, and ends under the flexor retinaculum by dividing into the medial and lateral plantar arteries. Along the way it gives the circumflex fibular artery, the peroneal artery — its largest branch and the main supply of the leg — muscular branches, a nutrient artery to the tibia, and medial malleolar and medial calcanean branches.

## sections
### Overview and position
The posterior tibial artery begins as the larger of the two terminal branches of the popliteal artery, at the lower border of popliteus. It passes downwards and medially between the superficial and deep flexor groups of the posterior compartment. In the lower part of the leg it becomes superficial and its pulsations can be felt. It ends under the flexor retinaculum, midway between the medial tubercle of the calcaneus and the medial malleolus, by dividing into the medial and lateral plantar arteries.

### Structure
The book lists seven branches. The circumflex fibular artery winds around the neck of the fibula. The peroneal artery is the largest branch and the main supply of the leg; it runs obliquely downwards and laterally, then vertically along the back of the fibula, and ends by dividing into terminal calcanean branches on the back of the inferior tibio-fibular joint. Muscular branches go to the muscles of the back of the leg. A nutrient artery goes to the tibia. The medial malleolar branch ramifies on the medial malleolus supplying the ankle, and the medial calcanean branch ramifies on the medial side of the calcaneus supplying the ankle. The medial and lateral plantar arteries are the terminal branches and enter the sole of the foot.

The peroneal artery has its own branch list, and it is a common second half of the question. It gives muscular branches, which are the main supply to the muscles of the back of the leg; a nutrient artery to the fibula; a lateral malleolar branch; a perforating branch, which pierces the interosseous membrane to reach the front of the leg and joins the anastomosis around the lateral malleolus, and which may be so large as to replace the dorsalis pedis artery itself; and a lateral calcanean terminal branch, which shares in the anastomosis around the ankle.

### Relations
The artery runs with the posterior tibial nerve, which descends between the superficial and deep flexors alongside it. In the upper two thirds of the leg both are deep, lying on tibialis posterior and covered by soleus and gastrocnemius; in the lower third both are superficial, lying directly on the back of the tibia and covered by skin and fascia only. Both then pass deep to the flexor retinaculum together, which is why one injury behind the medial malleolus takes out an artery and a nerve at once.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
This article is about a vessel, so its supply is what it gives rather than what it receives. Its medial malleolar and medial calcanean branches join the anastomosis around the medial malleolus, alongside the anterior medial malleolar branch of the anterior tibial artery, the medial tarsal branch of the dorsalis pedis and branches from the medial plantar artery. Its peroneal branch contributes the lateral malleolar and perforating branches to the anastomosis on the other side. The department book records no lymphatic relations for the artery and none is claimed.

### Development
The department book has no development section for individual vessels. What it records for the limb as a whole is that the mesenchymal core of the limb bud is derived from lateral plate mesoderm and forms the skeleton and connective tissue of the limb, and that the lower limb bud rotates about 90 degrees medially in the seventh week. The medial position of the posterior tibial artery at the ankle, and the fact that the pulse is felt behind the medial malleolus rather than the lateral one, follow from that rotation.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
The posterior tibial pulse is felt behind and below the medial malleolus, where the artery is superficial and lies against bone before it passes deep to the flexor retinaculum. It is one of the two foot pulses examined routinely, the other being the dorsalis pedis. The department book describes no imaging appearance and none is claimed here.

### Clinical correlations
Two clinical points follow directly from the branch list. First, the perforating branch of the peroneal artery may be large enough to replace the dorsalis pedis, so an absent dorsalis pedis pulse is not by itself evidence of arterial disease, and the posterior tibial pulse must be checked before concluding anything. Second, the posterior tibial nerve travels with the artery under the flexor retinaculum, and its entrapment there is tarsal tunnel syndrome, which causes pain, tingling and numbness of the sole.

## summary
The sciatic nerve is the thickest nerve in the body. It arises from the sacral plexus in the pelvis from L4, L5, S1, S2 and S3, leaves through the greater sciatic foramen below piriformis, descends on the back of the ischium and enters the back of the thigh midway between the greater trochanter and the ischial tuberosity, and divides a little below the middle of the thigh into the tibial and common peroneal nerves. ## sections
### Overview and position
The sciatic nerve is the thickest nerve in the body. It arises from the sacral plexus, from the anterior and posterior divisions of L4, L5, S1, S2 and S3, in the pelvis. Within a common fascial sheath it carries two nerves side by side from the start: a tibial part from the ventral divisions and a common peroneal part from the dorsal divisions, which is why its division later is a separation rather than a branching.

### Structure
The nerve leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen below the piriformis to enter the gluteal region. It descends on the back of the ischium and enters the back of the thigh midway between the greater trochanter and the ischial tuberosity. It ends a little below the middle of the thigh by dividing into its two terminal branches, the common peroneal and the tibial nerve.

Its branches are four. The tibial, or medial popliteal, nerve is the larger of the two terminal divisions and leaves the thigh to enter the popliteal fossa. The common peroneal, or lateral popliteal, nerve is the smaller and enters the fossa lateral to the tibial nerve. The muscular branches come from the tibial part to the long head of biceps femoris, semitendinosus, semimembranosus and the ischial part of adductor magnus, and from the common peroneal part to the short head of biceps femoris alone. The articular branches go to the hip joint.

### Relations
In the gluteal region the nerve is one of the structures that leave the pelvis below piriformis, alongside the inferior gluteal nerve and vessels, the nerve to quadratus femoris, the posterior cutaneous nerve of the thigh, the nerve to obturator internus, the internal pudendal vessels and the pudendal nerve. The nerve to quadratus femoris lies deep to the sciatic nerve and the posterior cutaneous nerve of the thigh lies superficial to it — a pair of relations worth holding, because a needle that reaches the sciatic nerve has passed one and stopped short of the other.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
The sciatic nerve is itself an innervating structure. Its motor territory in the thigh is the hamstring group and the ischial part of adductor magnus; below the knee, through its two terminal divisions, it supplies all the muscles of the leg and foot. Its sensory territory is the skin below the knee except the medial side, which is saphenous, and the back of the calf, which is posterior cutaneous nerve of thigh. The department book records no separate vascular or lymphatic description for the nerve and none is claimed.

### Development
The department book gives no development section for individual nerves. It records for the limb as a whole that the lower limb buds lie opposite the lower four lumbar and upper two sacral segments, and that the nerve supply of the limb depends on the somites from which the skeletal muscles and dermatomes are derived. That is why the sciatic nerve carries exactly L4 to S3, and why its root value can be read off the level of the bud rather than memorised as an arbitrary list.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
The nerve is represented by a broad band about one centimetre thick extending between three points: midway between the posterior superior iliac spine and the ischial tuberosity; midway between the tip of the greater trochanter and the ischial tuberosity; and the middle of the back of the thigh in the midline. Those three points are what make the upper outer quadrant the safe quadrant for a gluteal injection, and the lower medial quadrant the dangerous one.

### Clinical correlations
The book gives two causes of sciatic nerve injury: posterior dislocation of the hip joint with fracture in the acetabulum, and faulty intramuscular injection, which happens in infants and in the elderly. Injury paralyses the hamstrings and all the muscles of the leg and foot, causes loss of cutaneous sensation below the knee except on the medial side and the back of the calf, and produces drop foot. Sciatica is different: it is the pain felt along the sensory distribution of the nerve, caused by prolapse of an intervertebral disc pressing on one or more nerve roots, or by an intrapelvic tumour, or by inflammation of the nerve or one of its terminal branches.

## summary
The common peroneal nerve is the smaller terminal division of the sciatic nerve. It crosses the popliteal fossa, curves behind the head of the fibula and lies against the lateral aspect of its neck, covered only by skin and fascia, before piercing peroneus longus and dividing into the superficial and deep peroneal nerves. That superficial course against bone is why a fracture of the neck of the fibula, or pressure from a cast, injures it. The result is loss of dorsiflexion and of eversion — foot drop with inversion, which is talipes equinovarus — and loss of sensation over the upper lateral leg and the dorsum of the foot.

## sections
### Overview and position
The common peroneal nerve, also called the lateral popliteal nerve, is the smaller of the two terminal branches of the sciatic nerve. It begins at the lower third of the back of the thigh, enters the popliteal fossa through its upper angle and runs downwards and laterally along the medial border of biceps femoris. It leaves the fossa through its lateral angle by crossing superficial to plantaris and the lateral head of gastrocnemius.

### Structure
The nerve curves behind the head of the fibula and then lies close to the lateral aspect of its neck. It ends by piercing the peroneus longus muscle and dividing inside its substance into its two terminal branches, the superficial and deep peroneal nerves.

Its branches in the popliteal fossa fall into three groups. The cutaneous branches are two: the sural communicating nerve, which arises in the upper part of the fossa and runs inferomedially to join the sural nerve, and the lateral cutaneous nerve of the calf, which arises on the lateral head of gastrocnemius and supplies the upper third of the anterolateral side of the leg. The articular branches to the knee joint are the superior and inferior lateral genicular and the recurrent genicular nerves. The terminal branches are the superficial peroneal, or musculo-cutaneous, nerve and the deep peroneal, or anterior tibial, nerve.

### Relations
Inside the popliteal fossa the nerve lies lateral to the tibial nerve and follows the medial border of biceps femoris, which is the muscle a surgeon uses to find it. At the fibular neck its only coverings are skin and fascia, so it can be rolled under the finger against the bone. It then enters peroneus longus, which means the muscle it will later paralyse is also the muscle that hides its division.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
The nerve's own supply is not described in the department book. What matters for the exam is what it innervates. Through the deep peroneal nerve it supplies the extensor muscles of the front of the leg, which dorsiflex the foot. Through the superficial peroneal nerve it supplies the two peroneal muscles of the lateral compartment, which evert the foot, and the skin of the lower two thirds of the anterior and lateral leg and most of the dorsum of the foot. Its own two cutaneous branches in the fossa supply the upper third of the anterolateral leg and contribute to the sural nerve.

### Development
The department book has no development section for individual nerves. For the limb as a whole it records that the lower limb rotates approximately 90 degrees medially during the seventh week, placing the extensor muscles on the anterior surface. That rotation explains an arrangement that would otherwise look arbitrary: the nerve of the extensor and peroneal compartments has to travel round the lateral side of the leg to reach the front of it, and it is that journey round the fibular neck that leaves it exposed.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
The head of the fibula is palpable at the upper lateral corner of the leg, and the nerve can be rolled against the bone one to two centimetres below and behind it. A plain radiograph that shows a fracture of the fibular neck is, in practice, a radiograph of a nerve at risk, and the 2025 examination case is built on exactly that reading. The department book describes no other imaging appearance and none is claimed.

### Clinical correlations
The book gives the causes of injury as fracture of the head or neck of the fibula, and pressure from casts or splints. The motor effects are foot drop, due to paralysis of all the extensor muscles of the front of the leg, and loss of eversion of the foot, due to paralysis of the three peroneal muscles; the resulting deformity is called talipes equinovarus. The sensory effects are loss of skin sensation on the upper lateral side of the leg, supplied by the lateral cutaneous nerve of the calf, and on the front of the lower part of the leg and the dorsum of the foot, supplied by the superficial and deep peroneal nerves.

The mirror image is worth learning at the same time. Injury of the tibial nerve paralyses the muscles of the back of the leg and the sole, leaving the foot dorsiflexed and everted, and that deformity is called talipes calcaneo-valgus. Equinovarus is down and in and belongs to the peroneal nerve; calcaneo-valgus is up and out and belongs to the tibial nerve.

## hold_these
The nerve lies against the lateral aspect of the neck of the fibula, covered by skin and fascia only, which is why a fracture there injures it.
It pierces peroneus longus and divides inside it into the superficial and deep peroneal nerves.
Its cutaneous branches in the popliteal fossa are the sural communicating nerve and the lateral cutaneous nerve of the calf.
The deformity of common peroneal palsy is talipes equinovarus; the deformity of tibial nerve palsy is talipes calcaneo-valgus.

## lose_the_mark
Answering "sciatic nerve" to a fibular neck fracture. The sciatic has already divided in the mid-thigh and never reaches the fibula.
Giving the sural nerve as a cutaneous branch. The common peroneal gives the sural communicating nerve, which joins the sural; the sural itself is tibial.
Listing the superficial and deep peroneal nerves as cutaneous branches in the fossa. They are the terminal branches, given after the nerve has left it.
Swapping talipes equinovarus and talipes calcaneo-valgus between the two nerves.
