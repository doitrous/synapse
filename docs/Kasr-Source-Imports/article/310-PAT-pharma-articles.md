## title
Clinical pharmacokinetics: age, sex and food factors in dosing
## subject
pharm
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Pharmacology
## summary
Drug handling changes with age, sex, renal function and food. Neonates and infants have proportionally more total body water and a larger extracellular compartment than adults, which raises the volume of distribution of water-soluble drugs, together with lower plasma albumin and a more permeable blood-brain barrier. The elderly have reduced glomerular filtration, so renally cleared drugs accumulate and need dose reduction. Sex differences in lean mass and body composition, and the practice of giving gastric-irritant drugs after meals, are further dosage-modifying factors tested in this section.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the clinical-pharmacokinetics concepts tested by Q1-2 of the bank: how paediatric, geriatric, sex and food factors modify drug dose.
### Mechanism
Higher body water in children enlarges the distribution volume of hydrophilic drugs; a more permeable neonatal blood-brain barrier increases CNS exposure; declining GFR in the elderly slows renal clearance; food buffers gastric-irritant drugs.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: children have MORE body water and a MORE permeable BBB (not less); neonates have LESS fat and LOWER albumin; GFR falls with age; irritant drugs are given after meals; women may need modestly smaller doses.
### Clinical significance
These principles drive weight-based paediatric dosing, renal dose adjustment in the elderly, and simple administration advice that reduces gastrointestinal adverse effects.
### Common misconceptions
The commonest trap is the reversed paediatric statement (less body water / less permeable BBB); each linked concept's pitfalls field states the exact reversal the distractors exploit.

## title
Autonomic pharmacology: cholinergic agonists, anticholinesterases and antimuscarinics
## subject
pharm
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Pharmacology
## summary
Cholinergic agonists and anticholinesterases raise muscarinic and nicotinic tone; antimuscarinics oppose it. Direct agonists (bethanechol, pilocarpine) and anticholinesterases (neostigmine) treat paralytic ileus, urinary retention, glaucoma and myasthenia gravis, and are contraindicated in asthma and peptic ulcer. Lipid-soluble tertiary agents (pilocarpine, hyoscine) cross the cornea and blood-brain barrier, whereas quaternary agents (neostigmine) do not. Atropine is the muscarinic antidote for organophosphate poisoning but cannot reverse the nicotinic skeletal-muscle effects, and it is co-given with neostigmine in myasthenia to cover muscarinic side effects. Because blood vessels are largely uninnervated by parasympathetic nerves, circulating acetylcholine from an anticholinesterase can cause a vasodilator hypotension that nerve stimulation cannot.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the autonomic-pharmacology concepts tested by Q3-10 of the bank: cholinergic agonists, anticholinesterases and antimuscarinics, their uses, contraindications and antidotal roles.
### Mechanism
Muscarinic stimulation contracts gut and bladder muscle, constricts pupils and slows the heart; nicotinic stimulation drives the skeletal neuromuscular junction. Anticholinesterases amplify both by raising acetylcholine; antimuscarinics block only the muscarinic arm. Lipid solubility (tertiary vs quaternary structure) governs corneal and CNS penetration.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: cholinergic uses = ileus, retention, glaucoma, myasthenia (not asthma or peptic ulcer); pilocarpine is the lipid-soluble miotic; atropine is preferred for organophosphate poisoning; hyoscine is best for motion sickness; atropine does NOT reverse nicotinic muscle effects; atropine covers neostigmine's muscarinic side effects; hypotension from anticholinesterases reflects uninnervated endothelial muscarinic receptors.
### Clinical significance
These agents underpin the emergency management of organophosphate poisoning, the maintenance therapy of myasthenia gravis and glaucoma, and the safe treatment of postoperative ileus and retention.
### Common misconceptions
The frequent traps are treating muscle paralysis with atropine, using a quaternary agent as a miotic, and giving a cholinomimetic for retention before excluding obstruction; each linked concept's pitfalls field names the exact error the distractors exploit.
