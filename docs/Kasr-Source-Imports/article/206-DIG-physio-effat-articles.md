## summary
Gastrointestinal physiology links motility, absorption and endocrine control. Meals distend the stomach and duodenum and evoke the gastrocolic and duodenocolic reflexes, which drive colonic mass movements and the post-prandial urge to defecate. Absorption is regionally specialised: vitamin B12 is taken up almost exclusively in the terminal ileum after binding intrinsic factor, so ileal disease or resection selectively causes B12 deficiency. Endocrine control runs through the gut hormones, among which GIP is distinctive as the incretin released by all three macronutrients — fat, carbohydrate and protein — whereas CCK, gastrin and motilin each respond to a narrower stimulus.

### Mechanism
Gastric and duodenal distension trigger vagovagal and enteric reflexes (gastrocolic, duodenocolic) that raise colonic motility; rectal filling then sets off the defecation reflex. Vitamin B12 binds intrinsic factor from gastric parietal cells and is absorbed by cubam receptors of the terminal-ileal brush border. Gut hormones are released from mucosal endocrine cells: GIP from K cells by fat, carbohydrate and protein; CCK from I cells by fat and protein; gastrin from G cells by protein; motilin mainly in the interdigestive period.
### Key determinants
Each item turns on one discriminator: the duodenocolic (not enterogastric or rectosphincteric) reflex explains post-prandial defecation; the terminal ileum (not duodenum/jejunum) is the B12 absorption site; and GIP (not CCK, gastrin or motilin) is the hormone released by all three macronutrients.
### Clinical significance
Loss of the terminal ileum (Crohn disease, surgery) or of intrinsic factor (pernicious anaemia, gastrectomy) causes B12 deficiency and megaloblastic anaemia; exaggerated gastrocolic reflex activity contributes to post-prandial urgency in irritable bowel syndrome; and incretin (GIP/GLP-1) physiology underlies the enhanced insulin response to oral versus intravenous glucose.
