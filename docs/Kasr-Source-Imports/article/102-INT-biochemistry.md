## summary
Twenty amino acids build the body's proteins, and the department classifies them four separate ways: by chemical structure, by the polarity of the side chain, by whether the diet has to supply them, and by their metabolic fate. The examiner tests two of those axes at once, so a single amino acid has to be placed twice in one answer — glycine is neutral aliphatic and non-essential, lysine is basic aliphatic and essential. Hold the two lists apart in your head and the matching question answers itself.
## sections
### Definition
Proteins are organic compounds with a high molecular weight, and twenty different amino acids are commonly required to build them. All twenty are α-amino acids except proline, which is an imino acid. An amino acid is an organic acid carrying one or more amino groups, written in general as R–CH(NH2)–COOH; at physiological pH it exists in the ionisable form R–CH(NH3+)–COO−.

The department book classifies amino acids on four separate axes, and they are independent of one another. Chemical classification goes by chemical structure. Polarity classification goes by the polarity of the side chain. Nutritional classification goes by whether the amino acid has to be supplied in the diet. Metabolic classification goes by metabolic fate.

The 2025 paper asked for two of those axes in the same line of a matching question, which is why they are taught together here rather than as four separate lists.

### Mechanism
The chemical classification asks what the carbon skeleton looks like, and it splits three ways: aliphatic, aromatic and heterocyclic.

The aliphatic amino acids subdivide again. Neutral aliphatic amino acids are the short-chain ones, glycine and alanine; the branched-chain ones, valine, leucine and isoleucine; the hydroxyl-containing ones, serine and threonine; the sulfur-containing ones, cysteine and methionine; and the amides of the acidic amino acids, asparagine and glutamine. Acidic aliphatic amino acids are aspartic acid and glutamic acid. Basic aliphatic amino acids are arginine and lysine.

The aromatic amino acids are phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan. The heterocyclic amino acids are histidine and proline.

Four notes travel with that chart and the book prints them as an N.B. Hydroxylysine and hydroxyproline are formed by hydroxylation of lysine and proline after the protein has been synthesised, so they are not separate members of the twenty. Tyrosine is an aromatic hydroxyl-containing amino acid. Tryptophan can be counted as both aromatic and heterocyclic. Histidine is a basic heterocyclic amino acid.

Cystine is not a twenty-first amino acid either. Two molecules of cysteine are conjugated by a disulfide bond formed by the removal of two hydrogen atoms, and the dimer is cystine. It is therefore a sulfur-containing amino acid, and it inherits cysteine's nutritional status rather than methionine's.

The polarity classification asks what the side chain does in water, and it splits three ways. Amino acids with nonpolar or hydrophobic R groups are glycine, alanine, valine, leucine, isoleucine, methionine, phenylalanine, tryptophan and proline. Amino acids with uncharged polar R groups are serine, threonine, tyrosine and hydroxyproline, which carry a hydroxyl group; cysteine, which carries an SH group; and asparagine and glutamine, which carry an amide group. Amino acids with charged R groups are the basic ones — lysine, arginine and histidine, the diamino-monocarboxylic acids — and the acidic ones, aspartic acid and glutamic acid, the monoamino-dicarboxylic acids.

### Key determinants
The nutritional classification is the second half of the marked answer, and it has three groups.

Essential amino acids are not formed in the body. Their deficiency decreases the rate of growth and of protein synthesis, producing a negative nitrogen balance, so they have to be supplied in the diet. There are nine members: valine, leucine, isoleucine, threonine, methionine, lysine, phenylalanine, tryptophan and histidine.

Half-essential, or semi-essential, amino acids are formed in the body at a rate that is enough for adults but not for growing children and adolescents. Arginine is the only half-essential amino acid.

Non-essential amino acids are all the rest. They are formed in the body, mostly from carbohydrates, at a rate that is enough for adults and for growing children and adolescents alike.

Read the two lists against each other and one pattern falls out that the paper tests directly. Of the sulfur-containing pair, methionine is essential and cysteine is not — so cystine, being two cysteines, is not essential either. Of the acidic aliphatic pair, neither aspartic acid nor glutamic acid is essential, and there is no essential acidic amino acid anywhere on the list. Of the basic aliphatic pair, lysine is essential and arginine is half-essential. Glycine, the smallest amino acid, is neutral aliphatic and non-essential. Isoleucine is branched-chain and essential.

The metabolic classification is the fourth axis and also has three groups. Pure glucogenic amino acids give glucose inside the body and include every amino acid except the members of the other two groups. Pure ketogenic amino acids give ketone bodies and are leucine and lysine. Glucogenic and ketogenic, or mixed, amino acids give both, and are phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan and isoleucine.

### Normal values
The isoelectric point of all monoamino-monocarboxylic amino acids is pH 6.02. At that pH the amino acid carries equal negative and positive charges, exists as a dipolar ion or zwitterion, and cannot migrate in an electric field.

Chain length is defined by number of residues. Oligopeptides contain from 2 to 10 amino acids. Polypeptides contain from 11 to 49. A protein molecule is formed of 50 or more.

### Clinical significance
Nutritional status is what the essential list is for. Proteins that contain all the essential amino acids are of high biological value — milk and egg proteins are the book's examples. Proteins deficient in one or more essential amino acid are of low biological value, and the book's example is zein of maize, which is deficient in tryptophan. A diet built on maize therefore supplies protein without supplying complete protein, which is the practical reason the essential list is memorised rather than looked up.

Two chemical properties of amino acids carry into later chapters. The amphoteric property means an amino acid reacts with both acids and bases: positively charged as R-NH3+ in acidic medium, negatively charged as R-COO− in alkaline medium, and zwitterionic at the isoelectric point. Amino acids therefore exist in three forms according to the pH of the solution, and the uncharged form is not present at any pH.

Peptide bond formation is the other. A dipeptide is formed by condensation of the carboxylic group of one amino acid with the amino group of a second, losing one molecule of water. Every level of protein structure, and every point mutation that changes a protein, is built on top of that single bond.

### Common misconceptions
Cystine is not essential. The trap is real and the paper set it deliberately: cysteine and methionine are both sulfur-containing, methionine is the essential one, and cysteine — and therefore its disulfide-linked dimer cystine — is made in the body.

There is no essential acidic amino acid. A matching question that offers "essential acidic amino acid" as an option is offering a distractor with no correct partner, because aspartic acid and glutamic acid are both non-essential.

The chemical group and the nutritional group are independent facts about the same molecule. Being branched-chain does not make an amino acid essential in itself; it happens that all three branched-chain amino acids are. Being basic does not make one essential either — lysine is, arginine is only half-essential.

## summary
A protein has up to four orders of structure, and only the first is held together by peptide bonds. Denaturation breaks every bond above the first and leaves the peptide backbone untouched — which is why a denatured protein is easier to digest rather than already digested. Six effects follow from that one sentence, and the department marks them as a list.
## sections
### Definition
Proteins in their native state are characterised by their three-dimensional structure. A protein made of one polypeptide chain has primary, secondary and tertiary structure. A protein made of two or more polypeptide chains has an additional quaternary structure.

Primary structure is the amino acid sequence of the polypeptide chain, held together by peptide bonds made during protein biosynthesis. The sequence is specific to that protein. The chain starts on the left with amino acid number 1, which has a free terminal amino group and is called the N-terminus; it ends on the right with an amino acid carrying a free terminal carboxylic group, the C-terminus. Synthesis runs from the N-terminus towards the C-terminus, with the sequence determined by the genetic information in the DNA.

Secondary structure has two regular forms, the α-helix and the β-pleated sheet, with loop regions, β bends and disordered regions also occurring.

Tertiary structure is the folding of the polypeptide chain into a specific higher three-dimensional globular structure.

Quaternary structure is shown only by proteins with two or more polypeptide chains. Each subunit has its own primary, secondary and tertiary structure, and the subunits combine to form the quaternary order.

Denaturation is a specific property of proteins. It is the change of the natural or native state of a protein, due to rupture of the chemical bonds that stabilise the secondary, tertiary and quaternary structures, leaving the protein in its primary structure.

### Mechanism
Each order is held together by a different set of bonds, and knowing which bonds hold which order is what makes the effects of denaturation predictable.

The α-helix is a folding of the polypeptide chain along its long axis into a coiled structure held together by hydrogen bonds. A hydrogen bond is a weak interaction between the hydrogen of one molecule, carrying a partial positive charge, and a more electronegative acceptor atom carrying a partial negative charge. Folding can be right-handed or left-handed; most proteins use a right-handed helix and collagen uses a left-handed one. The backbone is tightly coiled around the long axis, and the helix is stabilised by intra-chain hydrogen bonds formed between the NH group of one peptide bond and the C=O group of another. Every peptide bond takes part in hydrogen bonding, which is what gives the α-helix maximum stability. The R groups project outwards from the helix. Some R groups disrupt the helix, either by forming ionic bonds — histidine, lysine, arginine, aspartic acid and glutamic acid — or because their ring structure disturbs the helical arrangement, as in proline and tryptophan.

The β-pleated sheet can form within a single polypeptide chain or between several. Two or more segments of one chain lying side by side give a sheet stabilised by intra-chain hydrogen bonds; several separate chains give one stabilised by inter-chain hydrogen bonds. The R groups project above or below the plane of the sheet. Segments running in the same direction from N to C terminus give a parallel sheet, and adjacent segments running in opposite directions give an anti-parallel sheet. One protein may contain both types of secondary structure.

Tertiary structure is maintained by five kinds of interaction. Hydrophobic interactions are the tendency of nonpolar compounds to cluster together in an aqueous medium, so the nonpolar side chains of neutral amino acids associate. Electrostatic bonds, also called salt bridges or ionic bonds, form between oppositely charged side chains — lysine, positively charged, against aspartic acid, negatively charged, at body pH. Disulfide bonds form when two cysteines are connected to give cystine, as in keratin and insulin. Hydrogen bonds form between side chains: the hydroxyl group of serine and threonine, the amide group of glutamine and asparagine, the carboxylic group of aspartic and glutamic acids, and the ring nitrogen of histidine. Van der Waals interactions are transient attractions between molecules at a distance called the Van der Waals distance, and these weak interactions also contribute to protein stability.

Quaternary structure is stabilised by the same set — hydrogen bonds, electrostatic interactions, hydrophobic interactions, Van der Waals interactions and disulfide bonds — acting between subunits rather than within one chain. This level of organisation may be essential for the activity of certain proteins, such as enzymes and haemoglobin.

Notice what is missing from all three of those lists: the peptide bond. Nothing above the primary order is held together by one, which is exactly why denaturation stops where it does.

### Key determinants
Two factors produce denaturation, and the department book gives both as lists.

Physical agents: heat, mechanical agitation, sonication, ultraviolet irradiation, X-ray and the like.

Chemical agents: acids, alkalis, alcohols, urea, and salts of heavy metals such as Pb2+, Ag2+ and Cu2+.

The effects of denaturation on proteins are six, and this is the marked list.

Loss of the secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure of the protein.

Increased viscosity.

Decreased solubility, due to exposure of nonpolar hydrophobic groups.

Increased digestibility by proteolytic enzymes, due to exposure of peptide bonds.

Loss of biological activity, for example inactivation of enzymes.

Loss of antigenic property — injection of a denatured protein cannot induce antibody formation in the body, in contrast to the action of native proteins.

Denaturation may be reversible or irreversible, and it may result in protein coagulation. The book's worked example is albumin coagulated by heat, where the irreversibility comes from the formation of disulfide cross-linkages.

### Clinical significance
Proteins are classified by conformation into two major classes. Fibrous proteins consist of polypeptide chains arranged in parallel along a single axis to yield long fibres or sheets — collagen, elastin and α-keratin. Globular proteins are tightly folded into compact spherical shapes — most enzymes, haemoglobin, myoglobin, many hormones, immunoglobulins and plasma proteins.

The book lists eight biological functions of protein, and they are worth reading as the reason the structure matters. Proteins provide the body with essential amino acids, nitrogen and sulfur. Enzymes are mainly protein in nature. Many hormones are peptides or proteins, such as glucagon and insulin. The antibodies, or immunoglobulins, that carry out the body's defensive mechanisms are protein in nature. Haemoglobin is a chromoprotein that carries oxygen from the lungs to the tissues. Plasma proteins are responsible for the most effective osmotic pressure of the blood, which plays a central role in water distribution and urine formation. Plasma proteins help transport lipids, hormones such as the thyroid hormones, and minerals such as calcium, iron and copper. And proteins form very important components of cell membranes, as receptors and transporters.

Folding is not automatic. Proteins must fold into defined three-dimensional structures to gain functional activity, and newly synthesised proteins are at great risk of abnormal folding and aggregation. A complex network of molecular proteins known as chaperones uses different mechanisms to promote efficient folding and prevent aggregation. An age-related decline in chaperones allows the manifestation of various protein-aggregation diseases, including Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease.

### Common misconceptions
Denaturation does not break peptide bonds. The primary structure survives intact, which is precisely why a denatured protein is more digestible rather than already digested: the peptide bonds are exposed to proteolytic enzymes, not cleaved.

Increased digestibility and decreased solubility are not contradictory. Both follow from the same event — the chain unfolds, so what was buried is now on the surface. Nonpolar groups on the surface cost the protein its solubility; exposed peptide bonds gain it digestibility.

Loss of antigenic property is not the same as loss of biological activity, and the book lists them separately. A denatured enzyme has lost its catalysis; a denatured protein injected into an animal has additionally lost its ability to raise antibodies.

Saying that denaturation breaks the peptide bonds. It leaves the primary structure untouched, and that is why a denatured protein is more digestible rather than already digested.
Writing "loses its structure" as the whole answer. The department marks six separate effects, and a single sentence scores one of them.
Giving increased solubility because the protein has unfolded. Solubility falls, because the nonpolar groups that were buried are now on the surface.
Leaving out loss of antigenic property. It is the sixth item on the book's own list and it is the one students most often omit.
Listing the denaturing agents when the question asked for the effects. They are two separate lists on the same page.

## summary
Two carbohydrates in this chapter do their job by not being metabolised. Cellulose is glucose joined the wrong way round for human amylase, so it never leaves the gut lumen and works as bulk. Glycosaminoglycans carry fixed negative charge, so they hold water into a gel that can be squeezed and springs back. Both answers are structural arguments, and both are marked as mechanisms rather than as lists.
## sections
### Definition
Carbohydrates are poly-hydroxy-alcohols with an aldehyde or a keto group. Their general formula Cn(H2O)n is why they are called hydrates of carbon. They provide a significant fraction of the dietary calories for most organisms, act as a storage form of energy, and serve as structural components either on their own or combined with other molecules.

Carbohydrates are classified by their hydrolysis products into four groups. Monosaccharides consist of one monosaccharide unit. Disaccharides consist of two units per molecule. Oligosaccharides consist of three to ten units. Polysaccharides consist of more than ten units.

Polysaccharides subdivide into homopolysaccharides, made entirely of one type of monosaccharide unit, and heteropolysaccharides, made of more than one type. The glucans — starch, glycogen and cellulose — are homopolysaccharides of glucose. The glycosaminoglycans are the heteropolysaccharides.

### Mechanism
Three glucans, one sugar, three different molecules. What separates them is the bond.

Starch is the chief storage form of carbohydrate in chlorophyll-containing plants, present in large amounts in cereals such as rice and wheat, in tubers such as potatoes and sweet potatoes, and in legumes such as beans. Its granules contain two forms. Amylose is a linear unbranched molecule with α1,4-glucosidic bonds connecting the glucose units. Amylopectin is branched, with α1,4-glucosidic bonds within the branches and α1,6-glucosidic bonds at the branching points.

Glycogen is the storage form of carbohydrate in animals, which is why it is called animal starch. It is present in most cells but especially in skeletal muscle and liver, as stores of excess glucose. It is a highly branched molecule, more branched than amylopectin, with α1,4-glucosidic bonds within the branches and α1,6-glucosidic bonds at the branching points.

Cellulose forms the principal part of the cell wall of plants. It is formed of a long non-branched chain of β-glucose units connected by β1,4-glucosidic linkage.

That single letter is the whole of the clinical answer. Cellulose is insoluble in water, and it is non-hydrolysable by the amylase digestive enzyme because amylase hydrolyses only α-linkages. Undigested cellulose in the diet increases the bulk of food, which stimulates intestinal contractions and thus prevents constipation. Cellulose in the diet also delays fat absorption.

Glycosaminoglycans work by charge rather than by bond. They are unbranched long chains, usually of more than fifty sugar units, mostly composed of repeating disaccharide units each made of an amino sugar and a uronic acid. They divide into sulfate-free glycosaminoglycans, which means hyaluronic acid, and sulfate-containing ones: chondroitin sulfate, keratan sulfate, dermatan sulfate, heparin and heparan sulfate. Most are covalently conjugated to a protein core, and that product is a proteoglycan, formed of 95% carbohydrate and only 5% protein. They sit mainly in the extracellular matrix, or ground substance, alongside other extracellular proteins.

The negatively charged carboxylate and sulfate groups of the proteoglycan help it to trap water molecules, creating a hydrated gel. That gel provides flexible mechanical support for the extracellular matrix, acts as a filter allowing the diffusion of ions such as Ca2+, acts as a lubricant in synovial fluid, and is compressible. Compressibility is the shock-absorbing property: when a GAG solution is compressed, water is squeezed out and the GAGs occupy a smaller volume; when the compression is released, their molecules regain their original hydrated size. That is why they act as shock absorbers in joints and make the eyeball resilient.

### Key determinants
The disaccharides are three, and each is defined by which two sugars and which linkage.

Maltose, malt sugar, is two molecules of glucose united by α1,4-glucosidic linkage. It is the main product of digestion of starch by amylase, and it is hydrolysed by maltase or by acid into two molecules of D-glucose.

Lactose, milk sugar, is galactose and glucose united by β1,4-galactosidic linkage. It is hydrolysed by lactase or by acid into glucose and galactose.

Sucrose, cane or table sugar, is present in plants such as sugar cane and beet. It is fructose and glucose united by α1,2-glucosidic linkage, which can equally be named β2,1-fructosidic. Maltose and lactose have free carbonyl groups, so both are reducing sugars; in sucrose both carbonyl carbons are involved in the linkage, so it is non-reducing.

The biomedical importance of the individual glycosaminoglycans is a six-item list.

Hyaluronic acid is found in the extracellular matrix, particularly connective tissue, and also in skin, the synovial fluid of joints, the umbilical cord, the vitreous body of the eye and embryonic tissues. In skin, ageing reduces production of hyaluronic acid and collagen, so less hydrating gel forms and facial volume is lost and wrinkles appear. In joints, osteoarthritis gradually lowers the concentration of hyaluronic acid, lowering the viscosity of the synovial fluid that protects joints from excess friction. In the eye, the vitreous substance is composed almost completely of hyaluronic acid, which gives it viscous gel-like characteristics, functions as a shock absorber and transports nutrients into the eye's delicate tissues.

Chondroitin sulfate is the most abundant GAG and the prominent component of cartilage, tendons, ligaments, bone and aorta. It is commonly used as an oral treatment for osteoarthritis.

Dermatan sulfate is present in skin, blood vessels and heart valves. It has anticoagulant activity, binding to heparin and augmenting its action, and it has been associated with the ability to increase resistance to infectious diseases.

Keratan sulfate is one of the major components of the cornea, and is suggested to have important roles in corneal development and in maintaining transparency.

Heparin proteoglycan is an important anticoagulant that prevents thrombus formation. It inactivates clotting factors IX and XI by binding to them, and it activates antithrombin III by acting as a catalyst for it. Heparin also helps remove and clear blood lipids by binding lipoprotein lipase and increasing its release from the capillary wall into the plasma.

Heparan sulfate is associated mainly with the plasma membrane of cells and plays an important role in cell membrane receptors and cell-cell interaction.

### Clinical significance
Hyaluronidase, the spreading factor, hydrolyses the hyaluronic acid present in the ground substance of connective tissue. Hyaluronidase secreted by some bacteria helps the spread of those bacteria through subcutaneous tissue. The same enzyme is present in the heads of sperm, where it helps the process of fertilisation of the ovum.

Mucopolysaccharidosis is the disease of the other direction. GAGs were formerly called mucopolysaccharides, and mucopolysaccharidosis is a group of more than forty genetic metabolic disorders caused by the absence or defect of the lysosomal enzymes needed to break GAGs down. GAGs collect in the cells, blood and connective tissues, which leads to permanent progressive cellular damage affecting appearance, physical abilities and system functioning.

Among the monosaccharide derivatives, three have clinical uses the book states. Sorbitol is glucose and fructose alcohol, a natural product that might be involved in the pathogenesis of diabetic complications, and it is also used as a sweetener. Mannitol is mannose alcohol, used as a low-calorie sweetener because it is poorly absorbed by the intestine, and — being non-metabolised and easily excreted by the kidney — used as a diuretic to reduce eye pressure in glaucoma and intracranial pressure in brain oedema, by osmosis. Amino sugars such as glucosamine, galactosamine and mannosamine are important constituents of glycosaminoglycans and of some glycolipids and glycoproteins.

### Common misconceptions
Humans do not have a cellulase. The benefit of dietary cellulose comes entirely from the cellulose that is never absorbed at all — nothing in the human gut hydrolyses a β1,4 bond, so the molecule passes through and does its work as bulk.

The shock absorption is not done by the protein core and it is not done by the sugar chains being physically springy. It is done by fixed negative charge holding water. Take the water away and the gel does nothing, which is why the mechanism has to be stated as charge, then water, then compressibility.

Starch and cellulose are both long chains of glucose. What makes one a food and the other a fibre is the anomeric form of the linkage, not the length of the chain or the presence of branches.

Chondroitin sulfate is the most abundant GAG, not hyaluronic acid. Hyaluronic acid is the only sulfate-free one, which is a different superlative.

## summary
One membrane phospholipid, one enzyme that cuts it, and everything downstream follows. Phospholipase A2 releases arachidonic acid, and where the acid goes next decides both the physiology and the pharmacology: prostaglandin H synthase makes the cyclic eicosanoids, lipoxygenase makes the acyclic ones. The same enzyme in snake venom, acting on a red cell instead of a signalling pathway, produces haemolysis. Three drug classes act at three separate points of that one diagram, and which point they act at is the whole examinable answer.
## sections
### Definition
Lipids are hydrophobic organic compounds, insoluble in water but soluble in fat solvents — benzene, alcohol, chloroform, acetone and ether. They provide the body with energy at 9.3 kcal per gram, supply the fat-soluble vitamins and the essential fatty acids, and carry many biological functions inside the body.

Lipids are classified into three main groups by composition. Simple lipids are esters of fatty acids with various alcohols. Compound lipids are esters of fatty acids with alcohol plus another group added. Derived lipids are the fat-soluble products of hydrolysis of the first two groups, or compounds present in association with them in nature.

Eicosanoids are physiologically active compounds formed from polyunsaturated fatty acids with twenty carbons, of which arachidonic acid is the example. Arachidonic acid is liberated from membrane phospholipids by phospholipase A2, or synthesised from the ω-6 essential fatty acid linoleate.

### Mechanism
Simple lipids are esters of fatty acid with alcohol, and the type of alcohol splits them in two. Neutral fats, or triacylglycerols, are esters of three fatty acids with glycerol; the fatty acids in a triacylglycerol are usually of different types, making a mixed triacylglycerol. Oils are liquid at room temperature because of their high content of unsaturated fatty acid; solid fats are solid because of their high content of long-chain saturated fatty acid. Waxes are esters of one fatty acid with a long-chain monohydroxy alcohol higher than glycerol, and the book's examples are ceramide, cholesteryl esters, vitamin A retinol esters and vitamin D calciferol esters.

Compound lipids contain fatty acid, alcohol and another group. Phospholipids carry a phosphate group; glycolipids carry a carbohydrate group. Phospholipids divide by their alcohol into glycerophospholipids, containing glycerol, and sphingomyelin, containing sphingosine.

Phosphatidic acid is the parent glycerophospholipid: on hydrolysis it gives one glycerol, one saturated fatty acid at position 1, one unsaturated fatty acid at position 2, and phosphoric acid at position 3. Its derivatives are named by what is attached to the phosphate. Lecithin is phosphatidic acid plus choline; it provides choline for the synthesis of acetylcholine, and it is important as lung surfactant. Cephalin is phosphatidic acid plus ethanolamine and increases thrombin formation, so it is essential for blood clotting. Phosphatidylserine carries serine. Phosphatidylinositol carries inositol and acts as a second messenger for many chemical transmitters, hormones and growth factors. Phosphatidylglycerol carries glycerol. Plasmalogens resemble lecithin and cephalin but carry a fatty alcohol instead of a fatty acid at position 1; choline plasmalogen provides platelet activating factor, and reduced plasmalogen levels have been associated with Alzheimer disease. Cardiolipins are two molecules of phosphatidic acid connected by one glycerol, so they contain four fatty acids, three glycerols and two phosphates, and they are found almost exclusively in the inner mitochondrial membrane.

Glycerophospholipids are hydrolysed by a group of enzymes called the phospholipases — PLA1, PLA2, PLC and PLD — each named for the bond it attacks. Phospholipase D is not present in humans and is present only in plants.

That last sentence is the whole of the snake venom answer. Snake venom toxins contain a lecithinase enzyme with phospholipase A2 activity. When injected into blood, it converts the phospholipids present in the cell membranes of red blood cells into lysophospholipids, causing cell membrane rupture, which is haemolysis. Snake venom toxins therefore cause death if they are not treated with antitoxins.

Sphingomyelin is built the other way. Sphingosine is an eighteen-carbon amino alcohol; a fatty acid linked to sphingosine by an amide bond gives ceramide, and ceramide connected to phosphocholine gives sphingomyelin. It sits in the cell membranes of the lungs and the brain, mainly in the myelin sheath. Glycolipids are ceramide plus a carbohydrate radical, and include the cerebrosides, sulfolipids and gangliosides; they are found mainly in brain tissue, myelin sheath and the cell membrane of red cells, act as components of cell membrane receptors for hormones and other ligands, and provide recognition properties that let a cell communicate with its extracellular environment.

### Key determinants
The eicosanoid pathway is one diagram with three enzymes on it, and the department examines it by taking the labels off.

The pathway starts at membrane phospholipids. Phospholipase A2 converts phospholipids to lysophospholipid and releases arachidonic acid. From arachidonic acid the pathway forks.

Prostaglandin H synthase makes the cyclic compounds — the prostaglandins, prostacyclins and thromboxanes. This enzyme has two catalytic activities: cyclooxygenase, abbreviated COX, and peroxidase. Its immediate product is prostaglandin H2, from which prostacyclin synthase makes prostacyclin PGI2 and thromboxane synthase makes thromboxane TXA2, with other prostaglandins made alongside.

Lipoxygenase makes the acyclic compounds — the leukotrienes and the lipoxins.

The effects of the individual eicosanoids are what make the fork matter. PGE2, made in most tissues, causes vasodilatation and smooth muscle relaxation and is a mediator of inflammatory reactions — hyperaemia, oedema, pain and fever. PGF2α, also made in most tissues, causes vasoconstriction and smooth muscle contraction. PGI2, prostacyclin, is made by the endothelium of blood vessels and causes vasodilatation and inhibits platelet aggregation. TXA2, thromboxane A2, is made by platelets and causes vasoconstriction and stimulates platelet aggregation; normally there is a balance between the action of prostacyclins and thromboxanes. Leukotrienes, made by leukocytes, platelets and mast cells, stimulate inflammatory reactions and contribute to severe allergic reactions that may result in bronchoconstriction, low blood pressure and shock. Lipoxins, made in arterial walls, are anti-inflammatory and decrease the immune response.

### Clinical significance
The department book closes the chapter with a section it titles Clinical Aspects, on anti-inflammatory and antiallergic drugs. Each class is located on the pathway above, and its effect follows from where it sits. The book states class and mechanism and no dose, and none is added here.

Steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, SAIDs, inhibit phospholipase A2 activity and thus decrease the availability of arachidonic acid. The book's examples are hydrocortisone, which is cortisol, prednisone and betamethasone. Because they act above the fork, they reduce the supply to the cyclic and the acyclic branch alike.

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, NSAIDs, inhibit cyclooxygenase activity and thus inhibit prostaglandin synthesis. The book's examples are aspirin and other NSAIDs such as indomethacin and ibuprofen. Because they act on one of the two catalytic activities of prostaglandin H synthase, they close the cyclic branch and leave the lipoxygenase branch running.

Singulair is a leukotriene receptor antagonist. It relieves bronchospasm and inhibits the allergic pathway, and it is used to treat severe bronchial asthmatic attacks and allergies. It is the only one of the three that does not inhibit an enzyme at all: it blocks the receptor that the leukotrienes act on, downstream of the whole pathway.

Two further clinical aspects of this chapter carry into other topics. Lung surfactant is naturally secreted by pneumocytes and is formed mainly of dipalmitoyl-lecithin; it reduces the tension between the air of the alveoli and the alveolar membrane to facilitate transmembrane passage of oxygen and carbon dioxide, without which the alveolar membrane collapses, producing respiratory distress syndrome, which develops particularly in premature infants. The lecithin to sphingomyelin ratio, the L/S ratio, is a marker of fetal lung maturity.

Cholesterol is the other. Plasma cholesterol normally ranges from 120 to 200 mg/dL, with the recommended level less than 200 mg/dL, about 30% free and 70% as cholesteryl ester. Every cell apart from the red cell can synthesise its own cholesterol from acetyl CoA, and plasma cholesterol is synthesised by the liver and the intestine. It is converted into bile acids and bile salts in the liver, is the precursor of all steroid hormones, can be oxidised in the liver into 7-dehydrocholesterol and then converted into vitamin D3 under the skin by ultraviolet rays, and is an important constituent of cell membranes that controls their fluidity. It is excreted mainly in bile as bile salts. An increased plasma level predisposes to atherosclerosis and coronary heart disease, and high levels may cause gall bladder stones and xanthelasmas, which are cholesterol deposits on the skin of the eyelid.

### Common misconceptions
Phospholipase D is not the venom enzyme. It is absent from humans and present only in plants; the venom's lecithinase has phospholipase A2 activity, which is why the product named in the answer is a lysophospholipid.

Cyclooxygenase and prostaglandin H synthase are not two enzymes at two points of the pathway. Cyclooxygenase is one of the two catalytic activities of prostaglandin H synthase, and peroxidase is the other. Drawing them as separate steps produces a pathway with a step that does not exist.

An NSAID is not a weaker steroid acting at the same place. A steroid shuts off arachidonic acid for both branches; an NSAID leaves the lipoxygenase branch running, which is why it does not relieve leukotriene-driven bronchospasm and why a leukotriene receptor antagonist is a separate drug rather than a stronger NSAID.

The eicosanoid pathway is not a cascade with one output. It is a fork with two, and every clinical statement in the chapter depends on which side of the fork the drug or the mediator sits on.

## summary
A haemoprotein is a protein with haem bound to it as a prosthetic group, and the chapter is built on two of them: myoglobin, one chain and one haem, and haemoglobin, four chains and four haems. The clinical test the department examines comes out of the same structure. Glucose binds the N-terminal valine of the β chain without any enzyme, so the amount of glycated haemoglobin measures how much glucose the red cell has met across its whole 120-day life — an average, not a reading.
## sections
### Definition
Haemoproteins are conjugated proteins containing haem as a tightly bound prosthetic group. The department book names four. Haemoglobin transports oxygen in blood. Myoglobin stores oxygen in muscle. The cytochromes transport electrons in the respiratory chain. Catalase and the peroxidases degrade hydrogen peroxide.

Haem is a ferrous protoporphyrin IX. Protoporphyrins contain the porphin ring, which is four pyrrole rings linked through methenyl bridges. The four pyrrole rings are labelled I, II, III and IV; the substituent positions on the rings are numbered 1 to 8; the methenyl bridges are lettered α, β, γ and δ. In haem, the substituted positions on the porphin ring of protoporphyrin IX carry four methyl groups, which are nonpolar, at positions 1, 3, 5 and 8; two vinyl groups, also nonpolar, at positions 2 and 4; and two propionate groups, which are polar, at positions 6 and 7.

Both myoglobin and haemoglobin are haem attached to a basic protein — apomyoglobin in myoglobin, globin in haemoglobin. Myoglobin is one haem attached to one polypeptide chain. Haemoglobin is four haem groups attached to four polypeptide chains.

Glycated haemoglobin, HbA1c, is the fraction of adult haemoglobin HbA1 that has bound blood glucose covalently.

### Mechanism
Myoglobin sits in cardiac and skeletal muscle. It works as a reservoir for oxygen and as an oxygen carrier that increases the rate of release of oxygen within the muscle cell during severe muscular exercise, when the partial pressure of oxygen falls to 5 mmHg. It has a greater affinity for oxygen than haemoglobin does, and it is 90% saturated at a pO2 of 20 mmHg.

Apomyoglobin has 153 amino acids in a single chain. That chain folds into eight α-helices, named A to H starting from the N-terminal end; each amino acid is given a letter for its helix and a number for its position from that helix's N-terminal end, so His E7 is amino acid number 7 on helix E, and the loops between helices take the letters of the two helices they connect, so EF connects helix E to helix F. The tertiary fold puts the polar amino acids towards the surface, which is why the protein is water-soluble, and the nonpolar amino acids inside — with two exceptions, histidine F8 and histidine E7, which have a role in oxygen binding.

The haem ring sits inside the hydrophobic core, in a pocket between helices E and F. It interacts with the chain through hydrophobic bonds between its nonpolar side chains and the nonpolar amino acids of apomyoglobin, and through its iron, which interacts with the proximal histidine F8.

Iron in the ferrous state forms six coordinate bonds. Four are formed with the four nitrogen atoms of the porphyrin ring, which lie in the plane of the ring. The fifth is for the proximal histidine F8. The sixth is for oxygen, which lies between the ferrous iron and the distal histidine E7 and is unoccupied in the absence of oxygen. The fifth and sixth coordinations lie on an axis perpendicular to the plane of the porphyrin ring.

Haemoglobin is found exclusively in red blood cells. It transports oxygen from the lungs to the capillaries of the tissues, removes 15% of the carbon dioxide produced at the tissues, and its oxyHb/Hb system acts as a buffer inside the red cell.

Adult haemoglobin A1 is a tetramer of two identical α chains and two identical β chains, each binding one haem ring. Each α chain has 141 amino acids and seven α-helices; each β chain has 146 amino acids and eight. Each chain folds globularly with polar amino acids at the surface and nonpolar amino acids inside. In the quaternary structure each α and β chain are held together to form two identical dimers, (αβ)1 and (αβ)2. The two chains within a dimer are held tightly by ionic and hydrophobic interactions that prevent their movement relative to each other, but the two dimers are linked to each other by weaker non-covalent bonds such as hydrogen bonds, so the two dimers can move freely during oxygenation and deoxygenation.

That mobility is what the T and R forms are. The deoxy form is the T, or tense, form, in which the two dimers interact through a network of ionic and hydrogen bonds that constrain the movement of the polypeptide chains; T is the low oxygen-affinity form. Binding of oxygen ruptures some of the non-covalent bonds between the two dimers, giving the R, or relaxed, form, in which the chains have more freedom of movement; R is the high oxygen-affinity form.

### Key determinants
Glycation is a separate process from all of that, and its whole examinable value comes from one word: non-enzymatic.

Part of adult haemoglobin HbA1 can bind with blood glucose to form glycated haemoglobin, HbA1c. Glucose forms a covalent bond with the N-terminal valine of the β chain. It is a non-enzymatic reaction that depends on the exposure of red cells to particular blood glucose levels throughout the life span of the red cell, which is 120 days.

Follow the consequences one at a time, because that chain is the three-mark answer. There is no enzyme, so there is nothing to saturate, nothing to induce and nothing to inhibit. The rate therefore depends only on how much glucose the haemoglobin has met. The bond is covalent, so what is formed stays formed for as long as that haemoglobin molecule exists. The haemoglobin molecule exists for as long as its red cell does, which is 120 days. So the fraction of haemoglobin that is glycated is an integral of glucose exposure across the life of the red cell population — roughly the preceding three months — rather than a reading of the glucose in the tube at the moment of sampling.

The other haemoglobins are named by their chains, and knowing which chains are present is what makes the glycation site meaningful. Embryonic haemoglobin, Hb Gower, is the first formed during the first few weeks after conception: Gower-1 is ζ2ε2 and Gower-2 is α2ε2. Fetal haemoglobin HbF is formed at the third month of pregnancy and remains up to six months after birth; it is α2γ2 and has a higher affinity for oxygen than adult haemoglobin, which allows more efficient transplacental transfer of oxygen from mother to fetus. Adult HbA1 is formed at the end of the late month of pregnancy and continues throughout life; it is α2β2 and forms about 98% of total adult haemoglobin. Adult HbA2 is α2δ2 and forms only 2%.

The globin genes sit in two families on two chromosomes. The α-gene cluster on chromosome 16 contains two genes for the α-globin chains, α1 and α2, and the zeta gene. The β-gene cluster on chromosome 11 contains the single β gene plus four more: the epsilon gene, expressed early in embryonic development; the δ gene, which codes for the chain found in the minor adult haemoglobin HbA2; and two gamma genes, expressed in fetal haemoglobin.

### Normal values
HbA1c represents 4 to 6.5% of HbA in normal individuals. Higher levels are present in cases of diabetes mellitus.

Adult HbA1 forms about 98% of total adult haemoglobin and HbA2 about 2%.

Myoglobin is 90% saturated at a pO2 of 20 mmHg, and it releases oxygen in muscle when the pO2 falls to 5 mmHg.

Haemoglobin removes 15% of the carbon dioxide produced at the tissues.

### Clinical significance
HbA1c is used as a test to determine the diabetic control of the patient during the last three months. That is the department book's own sentence, and it is the sentence the exam question quotes back.

Myoglobin has the reciprocal clinical use. It is present in cardiac and skeletal muscle, and during a heart attack or severe muscle damage it is released into the blood. It rises in blood 2 to 3 hours after the first symptoms of muscle damage and usually peaks about 8 to 12 hours later.

The protein part of haemoglobin is not inert packaging, and the book gives four reasons. Globin makes haem soluble, because of the surface polar amino acids. It prevents iron oxidation, because the nonpolar amino acids lining the haem pocket prevent oxidation of haem into haematin and prevent the formation of a haem-O2-haem complex. It prevents diffusion of haem into plasma, because of its large size. And it reduces the affinity of haem for carbon monoxide: carbon monoxide binds isolated haem in its preferred orientation, with iron, carbon and oxygen all perpendicular to the plane of the haem, but in myoglobin and haemoglobin the distal histidine sterically hinders that orientation, and binding at a less favoured angle reduces the strength of the haem-CO bond.

The haemoglobinopathies are a family of disorders caused by synthesis of either structurally abnormal globin chains, as in sickle-cell anaemia and HbS, or an abnormally reduced amount of α or β globin chains, as in the thalassaemias.

Sickle cell anaemia is a genetic disorder common among black populations all over the world. It is caused by a single nucleotide alteration, a point mutation, in the β-globin gene, which replaces polar glutamate at position six with nonpolar valine. The nonpolar amino acid generates a hydrophobic sticky patch on the surface of the β subunit of both oxy-HbS and deoxy-HbS. Both HbA and HbS carry a complementary hydrophobic sticky receptor on the β subunits, but only in the deoxygenated state. So at low pO2 deoxy-HbS molecules polymerise into long insoluble fibres, producing rigid mis-shapen erythrocytes; sickled cells frequently block flow in narrow capillaries, causing localised anoxia, pain and eventually infarction of the cells near the blockage. The spleen removes sickle cells faster than normal cells, shortening their life span and producing haemolytic anaemia.

Thalassaemia is a hereditary disease with defective production of either the α chain, in α-thalassaemia, or the β chain, in β-thalassaemia. Its severity depends on the number of genes affected, and symptoms vary from very mild to severe anaemia and even death of the fetus before birth or immediately after birth.

### Common misconceptions
Glycation is not enzymatic. Assuming an enzyme catalyses it invites the wrong conclusions — that it could be saturated, induced or inhibited. It is non-enzymatic, and that is precisely why the result is a faithful record of glucose exposure rather than a regulated quantity.

The three-month window is not a property of the test. It is a property of the red cell. HbA1c reports three months because the red cell lives 120 days; any condition that shortens red cell life shortens the window the test integrates over, although the book does not discuss that case.

HbA1c is not a measure of the current blood glucose. A patient with a normal glucose at the moment of sampling can have a high HbA1c, and the department's own wording — control during the last three months — is what a student should reproduce.

Glucose does not bind the haem iron. It binds the N-terminal valine of the β chain, which is a protein site; the sixth coordination of the iron is for oxygen.

## summary
An enzyme lowers the activation energy of a reaction without changing where it ends up, and five things change how fast it does so: substrate, enzyme, cofactor, temperature and pH. Each of the first three raises velocity until something else becomes the limiting factor, and each of the last two has an optimum with a fall on either side. Competitive inhibition is the same graph with a molecule that looks like the substrate in the way, which is exactly how a sulfonamide stops a bacterium building folate.
## sections
### Definition
Enzymes are biocatalysts that regulate the rate of biochemical reactions. The department book gives six common features. All are produced by living cells and can act outside those cells. They are needed in very small amounts. They accelerate the reaction without affecting its equilibrium point. They are mainly protein in nature, although some RNAs have catalytic activity — the ribozymes. They are not changed chemically by the end of the reaction. And they are highly specific, acting on a specific substrate or on inter-related substrates.

Enzymes are either simple proteins or conjugated proteins, and a conjugated enzyme is a holoenzyme. A holoenzyme is an apoenzyme, the protein part, plus a cofactor, the non-protein part. Cofactors are organic or inorganic molecules required for the activity of a holoenzyme: the apoenzyme alone is inactive, the cofactor is the activator, and the holoenzyme is active.

Cofactors are of two kinds. Coenzymes are small organic molecules, either a prosthetic group tightly bound to the apoenzyme or a co-substrate loosely bound to it. Metal ions are small inorganic molecules, either tightly bound in a metalloenzyme or loosely bound in a metal-activated enzyme.

An inhibitor is any substance that can diminish the velocity of an enzyme-catalysed reaction.

### Mechanism
The catalytic efficiency of an enzyme is explained two ways: by what happens at the active site, and by thermodynamics.

At the active site, the enzyme molecule contains a special pocket or cleft whose amino acid chains create a three-dimensional surface complementary to the substrate. The induced fit model states that when a substrate binds to the active site, forming an enzyme-substrate complex, it induces conformational changes in the enzyme that allow more fitting and catalysis. Enzyme specificity is due to the nature and arrangement of the chemical groups at the catalytic site, which is what lets the enzyme unite with and activate only one substrate or a small number of structurally related substrates.

Thermodynamically, a reaction needs a minimum amount of energy, the activation energy, for the reactants to convert into products. The enzyme accelerates the reaction by lowering the energy of activation. It does not affect the free energy change of the reaction, ΔG, and therefore does not change the equilibrium. Reactions come in three kinds by ΔG. Exothermic or exergonic reactions release free energy, have a negative ΔG and are irreversible. Endothermic or endergonic reactions gain free energy and have a positive ΔG; exergonic reactions are usually coupled to them, and ATP is the main source of energy for most endergonic biological reactions. Isothermic reactions have a ΔG of zero and are reversible.

Reversible inhibition has two common types, and the difference is where the inhibitor binds.

A competitive inhibitor is structurally similar to the substrate, so it competes with the substrate to bind reversibly at the active or catalytic site. The degree of inhibition depends on the ratio of the concentration of the inhibitor to that of the substrate, not on the absolute concentration of either, and on the relative affinity of substrate and inhibitor for the enzyme. There is no effect on Vmax: raising the substrate concentration far enough reverses the inhibition, and the reaction reaches the same Vmax as without inhibitor. Km rises: a competitive inhibitor increases the apparent Km, which means more substrate is needed to reach half Vmax.

An allosteric inhibitor is usually a small organic molecule that binds to a specific site away from the catalytic site. It produces conformational changes in the protein structure that decrease the activity of the enzyme, as ATP does for phosphofructokinase-1. Allosteric inhibitors decrease the affinity of the enzyme for its substrate, increasing Km, or decrease the maximal catalytic activity, decreasing Vmax, or both.

### Key determinants
Five factors affect the rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction, and the book insists that only one is varied at a time with all others held constant, and that the rate is always measured at the very beginning of the reaction — the initial velocity — to avoid the fall in substrate concentration, the accumulation of products, and equilibrium.

Substrate concentration. Velocity increases as substrate concentration increases, up to the point where the enzyme is saturated; at that point the enzyme concentration is the limiting factor. The measured initial velocity increases to a maximum, Vmax. The substrate concentration that produces half the maximal velocity is the Michaelis constant, Km. A smaller Km reflects a higher affinity of the enzyme for its substrate.

Enzyme concentration. Velocity is directly proportional to the concentration of the enzyme, up to a point at which further increase is not accompanied by an increase in velocity; at that point the substrate is the limiting factor.

Cofactor concentration. Velocity is directly proportional to the concentration of the cofactor if one is required, up to the point at which every enzyme molecule is associated with its cofactor; beyond that the enzyme concentration is the limiting factor.

Temperature. At 0 °C enzyme action virtually stops, because movement and collision between substrate and enzyme are inhibited. As temperature rises, velocity rises with the increased kinetic energy and increased collision, forming more enzyme-substrate complex. That continues to the optimum temperature, beyond which any further increase decreases the reaction rate. The optimum for most animal enzymes is about 37 °C and for most plant enzymes about 50 °C. For most enzymes activity virtually stops at about 70 °C, because the enzyme protein denatures and the organisation of the catalytic site is disrupted.

pH. Each enzyme has an optimum pH at which it shows maximal activity, and activity decreases away from it, virtually stopping about two pH units above or below. Slight changes in pH cause marked changes in activity, by altering the charges on the substrate and on the catalytic site. Extreme changes cause denaturation and irreversible inhibition. Most enzymes have an optimum pH between 5 and 9, with exceptions — pepsin, the stomach's digestive enzyme, is maximally active at pH 2, and enzymes designed to work at neutral pH are denatured by such an acid environment.

Notice the shape of the answer. The first three factors are limiting-factor arguments: velocity rises until something else runs out. The last two are optimum arguments: velocity rises to a peak and then falls, because the protein itself is being damaged.

### Clinical significance
The book gives three worked examples of competitive inhibition, and all three are drugs.

Allopurinol treats gout, which is excessive production of uric acid. Xanthine oxidase is the enzyme that forms uric acid from hypoxanthine. Allopurinol is a structural analogue of hypoxanthine and therefore inhibits xanthine oxidase.

Sulfonamides treat bacterial infections and are bacteriostatic agents. The argument runs in four steps. Bacteria require folic acid for their growth and multiplication. Bacteria synthesise folic acid using para-aminobenzoic acid, PABA. Sulfonamides are structurally similar to PABA. They therefore act as competitive inhibitors of the enzyme involved in the formation of folic acid using PABA as substrate. Folate synthesis stops, growth and multiplication stop, and the bacteria are held rather than killed — which is what bacteriostatic means. The book prints sulfanilamide beside PABA to show the structural similarity, and it names no dose.

Dicumarol and warfarin are used as anticoagulants because they are structurally similar to a vitamin K derivative, which is required for the activation of blood clotting factors.

Irreversible inhibition is the other half of the clinical picture, and it splits by what is inhibited. Inhibitors of cofactors include fluoride, which blocks enzymes requiring Ca2+ and Mg2+ by chelating those ions as salts — the book's example is chelation of Mg2+ in enolase, an enzyme of glucose oxidation — and cyanide and carbon monoxide, which inhibit cytochrome oxidase in the respiratory chain by blocking the iron of haem. Inhibitors of the apoenzyme either denature the protein or block chemical groups important for substrate binding or catalysis: antienzymes, such as antithrombin binding thrombin and inhibiting blood clotting; protein denaturants such as strong acids, alkalis, alcohols and salts of heavy metals; and enzyme poisons, which block either the sulfhydryl group — oxidising agents, against which intracellular enzymes are protected by reducing agents such as glutathione, and salts of heavy metals such as Hg2+, which combine with the negatively charged sulfur of the SH group — or the hydroxyl group. The book's example of the last is aspirin, which acetylates the hydroxyl group of the serine at the active site of cyclooxygenase, the enzyme responsible for prostaglandin synthesis, and that is what explains the anti-inflammatory and antipyretic actions of aspirin.

Enzyme activity is regulated on two time scales. Short-term regulation occurs within seconds by changing the activity of the enzyme: availability of cofactors or substrates, presence of inhibitors or activators, and covalent modification by reversible phosphorylation and dephosphorylation. Long-term regulation takes hours or days and changes the amount of enzyme: increasing or decreasing its degradation, and increasing or decreasing its synthesis by regulation of gene expression.

### Common misconceptions
Higher temperature is not simply faster. Above the optimum the enzyme protein denatures and the catalytic site is disorganised, so velocity falls rather than rises, and by about 70 °C most enzymes have stopped.

A sulfonamide is not bactericidal. It stops folate synthesis, so growth and multiplication stop, and the organism is held rather than killed. Nor does it act on human cells: humans take folate in the diet rather than building it from PABA, so the target enzyme is not there to inhibit, and that selectivity is the whole point of the drug.

A competitive inhibitor does not lower Vmax. It raises the apparent Km, so more substrate is needed to reach half maximal velocity, but enough substrate still reaches the same Vmax. An allosteric inhibitor is the one that can lower Vmax.

The enzyme does not change where the reaction ends up. It lowers the activation energy and speeds the approach to equilibrium; ΔG and the equilibrium position are unchanged.

Cofactor and coenzyme are not synonyms. A coenzyme is one kind of cofactor — the organic kind — and metal ions are the other.

## summary
Nucleotides build DNA and RNA, but the free ones do four other jobs, and the department examines the fourth. Four nucleotide-derived coenzymes carry hydrogen: NAD+ and NADP+, which are nicotinamide dinucleotides, and FMN and FAD, which are flavins. Each has an oxidised and a reduced form, and naming both halves of the pair is what the question is actually asking for. Coenzyme A is the fifth nucleotide coenzyme and carries something else entirely.
## sections
### Definition
Nucleotides are the building units of DNA and RNA molecules. Hydrolysis breaks a nucleotide into a nucleoside plus phosphate; hydrolysis of the nucleoside gives a nitrogenous base plus a pentose.

Each nucleoside is formed of a nitrogenous base and a pentose — ribose in RNA, deoxyribose in DNA — where C1' of the pentose forms an N-linked glycoside with N1 of a pyrimidine or N9 of a purine. Each nucleotide is formed of a nitrogenous base, a pentose and a phosphate, and the phosphate group is usually attached by an ester bond to the terminal alcohol group of the pentose, the 5' hydroxyl group. A deoxynucleoside or deoxynucleotide contains 2-deoxyribose, and its name is preceded by deoxy or by the letter d, as in dCMP.

The nitrogenous bases are of two kinds. The major pyrimidines are cytosine, present in both nucleic acids; uracil, present only in RNA; and thymine, present only in DNA and as a minor base in tRNA. The purine ring is two fused rings, a pyrimidine and an imidazole, and the two major purines are adenine and guanine, both present in the structure of RNA and DNA.

Free nucleosides and nucleotides, over and above their role as building blocks, play an important role in many biochemical functions. The book gives four groups: energy transduction, signalling second messengers, activated precursors in biosynthesis, and coenzyme components.

### Mechanism
Energy transduction is the first group. Adenosine mono-, di- and triphosphate — AMP, ADP and ATP — and guanosine mono-, di- and triphosphate — GMP, GDP and GTP — carry energy in the bonds between their phosphate groups. ATP is used as a source of energy in all mechanical or chemical processes: muscular exercise, nerve impulse, cell membrane transport, anabolic reactions and the phosphorylation of compounds. When energy is needed, ATP is converted to ADP or AMP. The bonds between the γ and β phosphate groups and between the β and α phosphate groups are high energy bonds. GTP is also an energy source in some metabolic processes.

Signalling second messengers are the second group: cyclic 3',5'-adenosine monophosphate, cAMP, and cyclic 3',5'-guanosine monophosphate, cGMP. cAMP is formed from ATP by adenylyl cyclase and converted to 5' AMP by phosphodiesterase. Hormone-receptor interaction activates G proteins, which activate adenylyl cyclase, which forms cAMP from ATP. Cyclic AMP activates protein kinase A, which phosphorylates proteins; converting a dephosphorylated enzyme to a phosphorylated one either activates it, as with glycogen phosphorylase kinase, or inactivates it, as with glycogen synthase. Inactive protein kinase is a tetramer of two regulatory and two catalytic subunits; binding of two molecules of cAMP to the regulatory subunits releases the two catalytic subunits, which are active. The process is reversed by a protein phosphatase. Glucagon and epinephrine activate adenylyl cyclase and increase cAMP, which in turn inhibits protein phosphatase; insulin activates protein phosphatase and phosphodiesterase and decreases cAMP, so it reverses the effects of glucagon and epinephrine. cGMP is formed from GTP by guanylyl cyclase and also acts as a second messenger; many vasodilators, nitric oxide among them, cause smooth muscle relaxation and vasodilatation by activating guanylyl cyclase and increasing cGMP.

Activated precursors in biosynthesis are the third group. S-adenosyl methionine, active methionine or SAM, is formed in the body from methionine and ATP and is the most important methyl donor in trans-methylation reactions. 3'-phosphoadenosine-5'-phosphosulfate, PAPS, is used for the synthesis of sulfur-containing compounds such as the sulfate-containing GAGs and the sulfolipids. CDP-choline, CDP-ethanolamine and CDP-diacylglycerol are used for phospholipid synthesis. UDP-glucose is used for the synthesis of glycogen, glycoproteins and glycolipids; UDP-galactose for lactose, glycoproteins and glycolipids; and UDP-glucuronic acid is the active form of glucuronic acid, used for the synthesis of GAGs and in conjugation reactions with steroids, bile pigments and some drugs.

### Key determinants
Coenzyme components are the fourth group, and this is the examined one. The book lists five, and describes each by its parts.

Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide, NAD+: nicotinamide, ribose, phosphate, phosphate, ribose, adenine.

Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate, NADP+: the same chain as NAD+ with an extra phosphate on the 2' position.

Flavin mononucleotide, FMN: flavin, ribitol, phosphate. The book adds the note that FMN does not contain adenine, and it is the only one of the four hydrogen carriers of which that is true.

Flavin adenine dinucleotide, FAD: flavin, ribitol, phosphate, phosphate, ribose, adenine.

Coenzyme A: thioethylamine carrying a free thiol group, pantothenic acid, phosphate, phosphate, ribose, adenine, with a further phosphate at the 3' position.

Four of those five are hydrogen carriers, and the book sets the oxidised and reduced forms out as a pair for each. NAD+ is reduced to NADH,H+. NADP+ is reduced to NADPH,H+. FMN is reduced to FMNH2. FAD is reduced to FADH2. The oxidised form is on the left of each pair and the reduced form on the right, and the conversion in each case is the addition of 2H.

Coenzyme A is the fifth, and it is not a hydrogen carrier. It is an acyl group carrier, written R-CO~S-CoA, and its examples are acetyl-CoA, propionyl-CoA and succinyl-CoA. The active thiol group is provided by the thioethylamine.

### Clinical significance
Two of the four hydrogen carriers are vitamin-derived, and the book states one of those derivations explicitly in an earlier chapter: ribitol, which is ribose alcohol, is a component of riboflavin, vitamin B2 — and ribitol is the sugar in both FMN and FAD.

The cAMP system is where several endocrine mechanisms in later modules are built. Glucagon and epinephrine raise cAMP; insulin lowers it by activating phosphodiesterase and protein phosphatase, and so reverses their effects. That opposition is the reason a single second messenger can carry two hormones with opposite jobs.

The cGMP system is the mechanism behind vasodilatation by nitric oxide, which activates guanylyl cyclase and raises cGMP, relaxing smooth muscle.

UDP-glucuronic acid conjugates steroids, bile pigments and some drugs, which is the biochemical basis of one of the body's main routes of drug handling. The book states the conjugation without naming an enzyme or a drug.

### Common misconceptions
Coenzyme A is a nucleotide coenzyme, but it is not a hydrogen carrier. It carries acyl groups on its thiol, and adding it to the list of hydrogen carriers is the commonest way to lose a mark on a two-item enumerate question.

FMN is not a dinucleotide. Its name says mononucleotide, and the book points out that it contains no adenine — the only one of the four hydrogen carriers of which that is true.

NADH,H+ is not the same as NADH. The department writes the reduced form of NAD+ as NADH,H+, and that is the notation a student should reproduce.

A nucleoside is not a nucleotide. A nucleoside is base plus pentose; a nucleotide adds the phosphate. Four of the five coenzymes above are named as dinucleotides or mononucleotides for exactly that reason.

## summary
Replication is one job split five ways, and the five DNA polymerases are named by which part of the job they do. Alpha makes the primers, beta repairs, gamma works in the mitochondrion, delta makes the lagging strand from many primers, and epsilon makes the leading strand from one. Learn them against the fork rather than as a list of letters, because the fork is what tells you why delta needs many primers and epsilon needs one.
## sections
### Definition
The central dogma of molecular biology is the process in which genetic information flows from DNA to RNA to make a functional protein product.

DNA replication is the process by which a double-stranded DNA molecule is copied to produce two identical daughter DNA molecules, catalysed mainly by the DNA polymerases. Its major function is to provide the genetic information that daughter cells require from their parent cell.

Each parental strand acts as a template for the formation of a complementary new strand, so each daughter molecule is composed of one original conserved strand and one newly synthesised strand. That is semi-conservative replication.

DNA polymerases are template-directed enzymes. They read the parental nucleotide sequence only in the 3' to 5' direction, and therefore synthesise the new strands in the 5' to 3' direction. They catalyse the formation of polynucleotide chains by adding complementary nucleotides joined by phosphodiester bonds and base-paired with the parental strand. They cannot initiate synthesis without an RNA primer.

### Mechanism
Replication runs in four stages, and the polymerases enter at the second.

Separation of the two DNA strands comes first. Many origins of replication across the chromosomes, rich in AT base pairs, serve as starting points; they are recognised by origin recognition complex proteins, which bind and produce local opening and unwinding of the double helix. Having many origins markedly decreases the time replication takes. Two replication forks then form at each origin: helicase molecules attach and unwind the DNA by breaking the hydrogen bonds between the base pairs, forming replication bubbles, and single strand binding proteins bind the unwound single strands and stabilise them — without SSB proteins the two strands would rewind, and SSB proteins also protect the single strand from nucleases that cleave single-stranded DNA. Helicase plus SSB proteins is what creates a replication fork.

Initiation is the formation of the RNA primers. An RNA primer is a short segment of RNA used as a binding site for DNA polymerase, formed by the DNA polymerase α-primase complex and base-paired to the parental strand. The 5' end of the primer is attached to the 3' end of the parental strand, and the 3' end of the primer accepts the first deoxynucleotide.

Elongation is where the two strands part company. The leading strand is synthesised by DNA polymerase ε, requires only one RNA primer, is copied in the direction of the advancing replication fork, and is synthesised continuously in the 5' to 3' direction. The lagging strand is synthesised by DNA polymerase δ, requires multiple RNA primers, is copied in the opposite direction to the advancing fork, and is synthesised discontinuously as short Okazaki fragments produced in a 5' to 3' direction, each 100 to 200 bases long. Both polymerases ε and δ have proof-reading activity: misreading the template would give a permanent mutation, so they remove the misplaced nucleotide, acting as an exonuclease, and replace it with the correct one. Supercoils formed as the DNA unwinds are removed by the DNA topoisomerases.

Termination removes the primers and joins the fragments. RNase H removes the RNA primers by its exonuclease activity. DNA polymerase δ fills the gaps between Okazaki fragments. DNA ligase joins the ends of adjacent fragments.

The reason ε needs one primer and δ needs many is the fork itself. Both new strands must be built 5' to 3', but the two templates run in opposite directions, so only one of them can be read continuously as the fork opens. The other has to wait for the fork to expose more template and then start again, and every restart needs its own primer.

### Key determinants
The five eukaryotic DNA polymerases and the job of each, in the order the department book's summary gives them.

DNA polymerase α, working as the α-primase complex, synthesises the RNA primers and the short stretch of DNA connected to them.

DNA polymerase β repairs DNA.

DNA polymerase γ synthesises mitochondrial DNA.

DNA polymerase δ synthesises the lagging strand.

DNA polymerase ε synthesises the leading strand.

The same summary lists the other five enzyme groups replication needs. DNA helicases unwind and separate the two strands. Topoisomerases remove the supercoils formed during replication. RNase H removes the RNA primers. DNA ligase joins the ends of adjacent DNA segments, and requires ATP. Telomerase elongates the 3' end of the telomeres.

Telomerase exists because of an asymmetry the fork cannot solve. The ends of eukaryotic linear chromosomes are the telomeres; the 3' end strand of a telomere contains a non-coding region with the sequence 5'-TTAGGG-3' repeated thousands of times, and it is longer than the complementary 5' strand by a few hundred nucleotides. That single-stranded overhang folds back on itself into a loop stabilised by protein, which prevents the stacking of chromosomes and protects the chromosome ends from being attacked and hydrolysed by nucleases. During replication the leading strand can be synthesised to the very end of the parent strand, but the lagging strand cannot: primase cannot act at the 3' end of the strand because the space is too narrow, and even if it did, removal of the RNA primer would leave a short gap. Either way the chromosome shortens with each successive cycle of replication, and telomerase is the enzyme that restores chromosomal length.

### Clinical significance
Replication is accurate but not perfect: an error can occur for every 30,000 bases. DNA is also constantly subjected to environmental factors such as radiation and chemicals that alter or remove nucleotide bases, and bases are altered or lost spontaneously and during replication. If the damage is not repaired a permanent mutation occurs, which may result in a hereditary disorder.

Repair has four broad steps, and one of the five polymerases appears in it. Recognition of the lesion is the function of an endonuclease, which cleaves the damaged strand to form a nick. Excision of the damaged DNA is done by an exonuclease. Filling the gap is catalysed by repair DNA polymerase β. Ligation is catalysed by DNA ligase.

Defects in the repair mechanism are responsible for several genetic disorders. The department book's example is xeroderma pigmentosum, a genetic disease due to defective DNA repair: a clinical syndrome characterised by hypersensitivity to sunlight and ultraviolet, resulting in an increased incidence of skin cancer and in premature ageing and death.

### Common misconceptions
Delta and epsilon are the pair to be careful with. Epsilon makes the leading strand and needs only one primer; delta makes the lagging strand and needs many. Swapping them inverts every other statement about the fork.

Delta appears twice, and that is not a mistake. It synthesises the lagging strand during elongation, and it also fills the gaps between Okazaki fragments after RNase H has removed the primers. Beta is the polymerase of repair, not of gap-filling at the fork.

Okazaki fragments are not made backwards. Every fragment is synthesised 5' to 3', like everything else; it is the direction of the fragments relative to the fork's advance that is opposite, not the direction of synthesis within a fragment.

The primer is RNA, not DNA. A DNA polymerase cannot start a chain, which is the whole reason the α-primase complex exists and the whole reason RNase H is needed later.

## summary
The RNA a ribosome reads is not the RNA that comes off the template. Four modifications happen in the nucleus first: a cap on the 5' end, a poly(A) tail on the 3' end, splicing to cut out the introns, and editing to change a codon outright. Splicing is done by the spliceosome, built of small nuclear ribonucleoproteins, and splicing the same transcript in different ways is how one gene makes several proteins.
## sections
### Definition
Transcription is the synthesis of RNA from DNA.

Only one strand of DNA is transcribed. The strand that is transcribed into RNA is the template strand; the other is the coding strand. The sequence of the RNA is complementary to the template strand and the same as the coding strand except that U replaces T.

The transcriptional unit has three parts. The promoter is the nucleotide sequence in the template strand at which RNA polymerase begins attachment; it lies upstream of the transcription region and marks the beginning. The transcription region is the sequence transcribed by RNA polymerase. The terminator lies downstream of the transcription region and marks the end. The first nucleotide at the 3' end of the transcription region of the template strand is the start site, designated +1; the 3' direction of the template strand before the start site is upstream and takes negative numbers, and the 5' direction after it is downstream and takes positive numbers.

Messenger RNA is synthesised as a primary transcript, immature, known as heterogeneous nuclear RNA or hnRNA. In the nucleus it undergoes post-transcriptional modification — capping, polyadenylation, splicing and mRNA editing — before the mature functioning mRNA combines with protein to form an mRNA-protein complex that leaves through a nuclear pore into the cytoplasm.

### Mechanism
There are three types of nuclear RNA polymerase. RNA polymerase I and RNA polymerase III synthesise the rRNAs and tRNAs mainly. RNA polymerase II synthesises the mRNAs, most small nuclear RNAs and the microRNAs.

The eukaryotic promoter carries two types of sequence element, called the basal expression elements. The TATA box defines where transcription is to start along the DNA; its sequence is TATAAA and it is usually located 20 to 30 base pairs upstream of the transcription start site. The CAAT box and the GC box define when transcription is to start, controlling how frequently the event occurs, and they are located 40 to 200 base pairs upstream.

RNA synthesis runs in four phases. In pre-initiation, TFIID binds to the TATA box, and it is the only one of the transcription factors independently capable of specific high-affinity binding to the promoter; the other factors TFIIA, B, F, E and H then bind, along with RNA polymerase II, and the preinitiation complex is formed of the TATA box, RNA polymerase II and the transcription factors. In initiation, TFIIH uses its helicase activity to separate the two strands of DNA and also activates RNA polymerase II; TFIIA, B, E and H are then released, and the RNA polymerase II-TFIIF complex leaves the promoter region and moves towards the +1 nucleotide. In elongation, that complex synthesises a complementary transcript of the template strand using ATP, GTP, CTP and UTP as substrates, releasing pyrophosphate each time a nucleoside monophosphate is added, and continues until a termination signal is reached. Termination occurs when the polymerase reaches a special termination sequence on the template strand.

### Key determinants
The four post-transcriptional modifications, in the department book's own order. This is the diagram the paper prints with its labels removed.

Capping at the 5' end. A methyl-guanosine cap is added at the 5' end. It stabilises the mRNA and protects it against attack by ribonucleases, and it facilitates protein synthesis by helping transport the mRNA to the cytoplasm and allowing its recognition by the ribosomes.

Polyadenylation at the 3' end. Poly(A) polymerase adds a poly(A) tail of up to 200 adenine residues at the 3' end of the pre-mRNA. It stabilises the mRNA and protects it against ribonucleases; the length of the poly(A) tail determines the half-life of the mRNA; and it increases the efficiency of protein synthesis by binding specific poly(A) tail-binding proteins.

Splicing. In the primary transcript, parts of the mRNA do not code for amino acids; these are the introns, and they lie between the amino-acid-coding regions, the exons. Splicing removes the introns, the non-expressed regions, and joins the exons, the expressed regions, to form functional mRNA. Splicing requires a spliceosome, which is small nuclear ribonucleoproteins, snRNPs, formed of special proteins and small nuclear RNAs, snRNAs.

Alternative splicing sits inside that third step and is examined separately. The primary transcript of some genes may be spliced differently to yield different proteins from the same gene. That is why the estimated number of proteins much exceeds the number of genes, and the department book's example is the antibody genes.

mRNA editing. The coding information of an mRNA can be changed by editing. In the liver, the single apolipoprotein B gene is transcribed into an mRNA that directs the synthesis of apoB-100 protein, 100 kDa. In the intestine, the same gene directs the synthesis of a primary transcript in which a cytidine deaminase converts a CAA codon into UAA, a termination codon, so translating the edited mRNA gives apoB-48 protein, 48 kDa. ApoB-100 and apoB-48 have different functions.

### Clinical significance
The clinical correlation the department attaches to splicing is autoimmune rather than genetic. Systemic lupus erythematosus is an autoimmune disease characterised by autoantibodies against many cellular components, and the small nuclear ribonucleoproteins are one of the targets of those antibodies. That is why "mention a disease related" follows "mention the ribonucleoproteins responsible" in the exam question — the disease is a disease of the splicing machinery being attacked, not of splicing going wrong.

mRNA editing has its own clinical consequence, and the book states it as a difference in product rather than as a disease: the same gene yields a 100 kDa apolipoprotein in the liver and a 48 kDa one in the intestine, and the two proteins have different functions.

Alternative splicing is the reason the protein count exceeds the gene count, and the antibody genes are the worked example. A student meeting immunoglobulin diversity in a later module is meeting the same mechanism.

### Common misconceptions
Alternative splicing is not a fault. It is a normal, regulated route by which one gene legitimately encodes several different products, and the department book's example — the antibody genes — is a physiological one.

The spliceosome is not an enzyme in the ordinary sense. It is small nuclear ribonucleoproteins, built of special proteins together with small nuclear RNAs, and it is those particles, not a protein enzyme, that lupus autoantibodies target.

Introns are removed, exons are kept. The word to hold on to is expressed: exons are the expressed regions and are joined together; introns are the non-expressed regions and are discarded.

The cap and the tail are not just protection. Both stabilise the mRNA against ribonucleases, but both also increase the efficiency of protein synthesis — the cap by allowing ribosome recognition and the tail by binding poly(A) tail-binding proteins.

Transcription copies one strand only. Naming the template strand as the coding strand inverts the whole convention, and the RNA's sequence is the same as the coding strand, not the template.

## summary
A chain that has just left the ribosome is usually not yet a working protein. Two things can be done to it: a piece can be cut out, which is trimming, or a functional group can be added to a residue, which is covalent modification — phosphate, carbohydrate, acetyl or methyl, hydroxyl or carboxyl, each with its own worked example. The same chapter carries the mutations that make the wrong chain in the first place, and a base substitution in a coding region is nonsense, missense or silent depending only on what the new codon means.
## sections
### Definition
Translation is the process by which a cell makes protein using the genetic information carried in mRNA.

The genetic code is the nucleotide sequence of mRNA representing the code words for amino acids; since those code words originate from the DNA genes, they are called the genetic code. Each three successive nitrogenous bases in mRNA make a codon. With three of four bases per codon there are 4^3 = 64 possible codons. One codon, AUG, acts as the initiation codon. Three codons — UAA, UAG and UGA — do not code for any amino acid and are the termination or stop codons. The remaining codons code for amino acids.

The code has four characteristics. Specificity: a particular codon always codes for only one amino acid, so UUU codes only for phenylalanine. Degeneracy: an amino acid may be coded by more than one codon, and those are synonym codons, so phenylalanine is coded by two. Universality: the code is nearly universal, the same in all organisms. Reading frame: the codons are read from a specific starting point, the initiating codon, as a continuous uninterrupted sequence of bases taken three at a time.

Mutations are permanent changes in a DNA sequence.

Post-translational modification is what converts the chain a ribosome releases into a functional protein. Most proteins emerging from ribosomes are not biologically active proteins, and the book divides the modifications into two: trimming, and covalent modification.

### Mechanism
Translation needs all three classes of RNA — mRNA as the template, tRNAs to carry activated amino acids into the ribosome as aminoacyl-tRNA, and ribosomes, which associate with the mRNA and carry peptidyl transferase activity. It also needs amino acids activated and carried on tRNA, a source of energy in ATP and GTP, and various protein factors. The rough endoplasmic reticulum is the site of protein formation in eukaryotes.

Aminoacyl-tRNA is formed first. In the cytosol, the twenty amino acids are carried on their corresponding tRNAs by twenty different aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, each specific for one amino acid and its tRNA. Formation occurs in two steps: an enzyme-AMP-amino acid complex forms with hydrolysis of ATP into AMP and pyrophosphate, then the aminoacyl-tRNA complex forms with release of the enzyme and AMP. Aminoacyl-tRNA is charged tRNA.

Codon recognition follows base pairing. For a given codon on mRNA there is an anticodon on tRNA; both are read 5' to 3', and they are antiparallel and complementary in base composition. Some tRNAs recognise more than one codon for a given amino acid, which the wobble hypothesis explains: the first two nucleotides of the codon are essential and the third is flexible, so the base pairing of the third nucleotide of the codon with the first nucleotide of the anticodon is less specific.

Synthesis of the chain runs in three phases. In initiation, IF-4 binds the 5' cap of the mRNA and facilitates the binding of mRNA to the 40S ribosomal subunit; the 40S subunit moves along the mRNA to reach the first AUG codon; the initiating methionyl tRNA binds to that AUG; and the 60S subunit joins the 40S to form the 80S initiation complex, with release of IF-4. The 80S ribosome has two important binding sites: the peptidyl site, the P site, where the initiating Met-tRNA first binds and where the growing peptide chain sits after the first peptide bond, and the aminoacyl site, the A site, which binds each new incoming tRNA carrying an activated amino acid. At the end of initiation the initiating Met-tRNA is on the P site and the A site is free.

Elongation is a three-step cycle repeated for each amino acid added after the initiator methionine, during which the ribosome moves 5' to 3' along the mRNA and the protein is built from amino to carboxyl terminus. The charged tRNA binds the A site, determined by the mRNA codon aligned with it. Peptidyl transferase, an enzyme which is part of the 60S subunit, forms the peptide bond between the amino group of the new aminoacyl-tRNA in the A site and the carboxylic group of the peptidyl chain carried by the tRNA in the P site, so the growing peptide is now attached to the tRNA in the A site. Translocation then moves the ribosome exactly three nucleotides — one codon — along the mRNA, which moves the peptidyl-tRNA from the A site to the P site and frees the A site for the next aminoacyl-tRNA.

In termination, the elongation steps repeat until a stop codon moves into the A site. Since no tRNA has an anticodon that can pair with a stop codon, a release factor binds the ribosome instead, causing peptidyl transferase to hydrolyse the bond between the peptide chain and the tRNA. The mRNA, ribosome, tRNA and factors can all be reused. Many ribosomes can work on the same mRNA at once; those aggregates are polyribosomes or polysomes, attached to the wall of the endoplasmic reticulum to form rough ER, with each ribosome about 80 to 100 nucleotides apart on the mRNA.

### Key determinants
Post-translational modification is of two kinds, and confusing them is what costs the mark.

Trimming is the first. Many proteins secreted from the cell are initially made as large precursor molecules that are not functionally active, and portions of the chain must be removed by specialised endoproteases, which releases an active molecule. Insulin is formed as pre-proinsulin, which is converted to proinsulin and then to insulin. Zymogens are inactive precursors of secreted enzymes — trypsinogen is activated to trypsin in the small intestine.

Covalent modification is the second, and it is the examined one. Amino acid residues within the peptide chain are modified enzymatically to achieve one or more of three functions: to alter the activity or stability of the protein, to direct it to a subcellular compartment, or to prepare it for secretion from the cell. Residues are modified by the addition of various types of functional group, and the department book gives five with an example each.

Phosphorylation and dephosphorylation. Phosphate groups bind covalently to serine, threonine or tyrosine residues, and adding and removing them regulates the activity of many proteins — the enzymes of glycogen metabolism and the regulators of gene transcription.

Glycosylation. The addition of carbohydrate groups is a common modification, occurring mainly on proteins destined to be secreted or incorporated into lysosomes or cellular membranes.

Acetylation or methylation. The N-terminal amino acid is sometimes acetylated or methylated, and these changes alter the charge on the protein; the example is chromatin remodelling by histone acetylation.

Hydroxylation. Prolyl and lysyl residues can be modified by hydroxylation, and in collagen hydroxylation leads to stabilisation of the protein.

Carboxylation. Carboxylation is important especially for the function of proteins involved in blood coagulation; formation of γ-carboxyglutamate allows these proteins to bind Ca2+, a step that occurs in clot formation.

Mutations are the other half of the chapter. Their causes are two. Errors in replication: if a base that is not complementary to the template is added during replication, that leads to a mutation if the error is not repaired. Damage to the nucleotides of DNA, which comes from chemical mutagens inhaled or absorbed from the environment, such as the carcinogens in cigarette smoke; from irradiation, ultraviolet light or ionising radiation such as X-rays and gamma rays; and from oxidative damage, which is implicated in diseases ranging from neurological disorders such as Alzheimer's disease to cancers, and which also plays a part in ageing.

Base substitution, or point mutation, is the commonest type. It has two subgroups by chemistry: transition, in which one purine is replaced by another purine or one pyrimidine by another pyrimidine, and transversion, in which a purine is replaced by a pyrimidine or a pyrimidine by a purine. When a base substitution occurs in the coding region of a gene, it produces one of three effects, and this is the marked three-item list.

A missense mutation leads to a changed codon and results in an amino acid change in the protein product of that gene. Depending on which amino acid is changed, missense mutations can have either no effect or very serious ones, as in sickle cell anaemia.

A nonsense mutation leads to the conversion of an amino acid codon to a stop or nonsense codon. Translation of the mRNA from that gene is prematurely terminated and the protein product is usually non-functional, as in thalassaemia.

A silent mutation leads to the formation of a synonym codon that codes for the same amino acid, so no change occurs in the amino acid sequence of the gene product.

The other type of mutation is deletion or insertion of one or more bases. A frameshift mutation occurs if the deletion or insertion is not a multiple of three — one or two bases; the reading frame of the translated mRNA is changed, and this usually results in a non-functional gene product. Deletion or insertion of three bases, or a multiple of three, produces no change in the reading frame and only deletes or inserts one or more amino acids, so it is less severe than a frameshift.

### Clinical significance
Each of the three point mutations is examined through the damage it can do, and the book supplies a worked case for two of them.

Sickle cell anaemia is the missense case. A single nucleotide alteration in the β-globin gene replaces polar glutamate at position six with nonpolar valine. That one substitution generates a hydrophobic sticky patch on the surface of the β subunit, which lets deoxy-HbS polymerise into insoluble fibres at low pO2, producing rigid mis-shapen erythrocytes, capillary blockage, localised anoxia, pain and infarction, and a haemolytic anaemia from the spleen removing sickled cells faster than normal ones. One amino acid, changed for one of the opposite polarity, does all of that.

Thalassaemia is the nonsense case. Translation stops early and the protein product is usually non-functional, so the affected globin chain is not made in adequate amount.

A silent mutation is the third, and its effect is that there is no effect: the codon is a synonym and the amino acid sequence is unchanged.

The covalent modifications carry their own clinical weight. Carboxylation to γ-carboxyglutamate is what lets clotting proteins bind calcium during clot formation, which is the biochemical basis for the vitamin K dependence taught elsewhere in this course. Hydroxylation of prolyl and lysyl residues stabilises collagen. Glycosylation is the tag that sends a protein to secretion, to a lysosome or to a membrane. And reversible phosphorylation is the switch on the enzymes of glycogen metabolism and on the regulators of gene transcription.

### Common misconceptions
Trimming is not a covalent modification. Offering pre-proinsulin becoming insulin as an example of covalent modification answers the wrong half of the chapter: trimming is proteolytic removal of a segment, and covalent modification is the addition of a functional group.

Not every missense mutation is harmless because only one amino acid changes. Whether it matters depends on which amino acid — swapping polar glutamate for nonpolar valine creates the sticky patch that produces sickle cell anaemia.

A silent mutation is a real base substitution. The DNA has changed; what has not changed is the amino acid, because the new codon is a synonym. Degeneracy of the code is why silent mutations are possible at all.

Nonsense does not mean meaningless. It means the codon has become a stop codon, and the consequence is a chain that ends too early rather than a chain with a wrong residue.

A three-base deletion is not a frameshift. Only a deletion or insertion that is not a multiple of three shifts the reading frame; three bases removes one amino acid and leaves the frame intact.

## summary

## sections
### Definition
The cell cycle is a series of events that take place in a cell as it grows and divides into two daughter cells.

It is divided into two main phases, the mitotic phase and interphase, and interphase is further divided into G1, S and G2. The cell spends most of its time in interphase, during which it grows, replicates its chromosomes and prepares for division; it then leaves interphase, undergoes mitosis and completes its division. G1 is the stage where the cell grows by increasing in size. S is the stage where the cell copies all its DNA, and during it all the genetic material is duplicated. G2 is the stage where the cell grows more and prepares to divide. M is the stage of mitosis, where the cell separates the two copies of genetic material into two identical daughter cells. G0 is what follows mitosis for a cell that stops dividing: after passing through mitosis into G1 the cell either continues through another division or enters the quiescent G0 phase, where it is in the resting state performing its function without dividing. G0 is a permanent state for some cells, such as most adult neuronal cells.

Apoptosis is programmed, genetically directed cell death — a physiological cell suicide program. Many cells can precisely control the time of their own death by apoptosis; the word comes from the Greek for dropping off, as leaves drop in the autumn.

Tumour suppressor genes encode proteins with at least one of three functions: proteins that inhibit progression through a specific stage of the cell cycle or induce apoptosis, such as p53 and p21; proteins that promote apoptosis, such as Bax; and DNA repair enzymes. They are termed anti-oncogenes, and their mutations remove certain controls on growth and proliferation, which plays a major role in the development of cancer.

### Mechanism
The cell cycle is controlled by regulatory proteins and by checkpoints.

The regulatory proteins are the cyclins and the cyclin-dependent kinases, CDKs. The cycle is initiated by binding of a growth factor to a growth factor receptor on the plasma membrane, which mediates an intracellular signalling cascade that induces the cyclin genes. Cyclins complex with and activate certain CDKs, and those complexes produce the regulatory effects essential for the passage of the cell from one phase to another. There are at least ten different cyclins, designated A, B and so forth, and at least eight CDKs, CDK1 through CDK8, acting in various combinations at specific points. CDK4-cyclin D and CDK6-cyclin D drive progression past the restriction point at the G1/S boundary. CDK2-cyclin E and CDK2-cyclin A initiate DNA synthesis in early S phase. CDK1-cyclin A and CDK1-cyclin B drive the transition from G2 to M. The restriction point itself is located in late G1, and cells that pass it enter S phase and complete the entire cycle independently of the presence of growth factors.

The checkpoints are three, and they exist to ensure that DNA and chromosomal structure are intact before the cycle is completed. The G1 checkpoint checks for cell size, nutrients, growth factors and DNA damage. The G2 checkpoint checks for cell size and DNA damage. The spindle assembly checkpoint checks for proper attachment of the chromosomes to the spindle.

If there is a defect in any of the regulatory proteins or checkpoints, or if the cell suffers irreparable DNA damage before S phase, it may destroy itself in response to programmed steps — and that is apoptosis.

Apoptosis has two pathways. The extrinsic pathway is initiated through binding of a ligand to the tumour necrosis factor, TNF, or FAS receptors. That binding activates several caspases, which are intracellular cysteine proteases. Activated caspases activate caspase-activated DNase, CADase, the enzyme that cuts genomic DNA between the nucleosomes, generating DNA fragments of approximately 200 base pairs or multiples of it — which is responsible for the characteristic DNA ladder pattern seen after DNA electrophoresis. The intrinsic or mitochondrial pathway is used extensively in response to different forms of cellular stress; those stressors promote the release of cytochrome c from mitochondria into the cytosol, and released cytochrome c activates caspases, leading to activation of CADase and induction of apoptosis.

The intrinsic pathway is regulated by the Bcl-2 family. The anti-apoptotic modulators — Bcl-2, Bcl-x and others — possess anti-apoptotic activity. The apoptotic modulators — Bax, Bak and Bok — exert their apoptosis-modulating effects by controlling the release of cytochrome c from mitochondria to cytosol. Under physiological conditions there is a balance between the two groups.

### Key determinants
The department examines this chapter by naming a protein and asking what it does. Each of the four families answers a different question.

Cyclin-dependent kinases are the regulatory proteins of the cell cycle. Complexed with their cyclins, they carry the cell from one phase to the next.

p53 is the guardian of the genome, and the book also calls it the molecular policeman. The gene encodes a nuclear phosphoprotein of molecular weight 53 kDa. Its behaviour depends on how badly the DNA is damaged. In moderate DNA damage, certain protein kinases phosphorylate p53 and make it active; active phosphorylated p53 serves as a transcription factor that stimulates the synthesis of the protein p21; p21 inhibits the protein kinase activity of the CDK2-cyclin E complex, and the cell cycle is arrested in G1. That gives the cell time to repair its DNA before entering S phase, avoiding the transfer of a defective genome to one or both daughter cells. In severe DNA damage, the same phosphorylation occurs, but active phosphorylated p53 acts through activation of the Bax gene and triggers apoptosis, so the cell dies.

Bax is an apoptotic member of the Bcl-2 family, alongside Bak and Bok, and the group controls cytochrome c release from the mitochondria.

Bcl-2 is an anti-apoptotic member of the same family, alongside Bcl-x.

The TNF and FAS receptors initiate the extrinsic pathway of apoptosis when their ligands bind.

### Clinical significance
Cancer arises when the homeostatic balance between cell division and apoptosis is altered. Decreased apoptosis in cancer cells may result from altered expression of apoptosis regulatory proteins.

Mutation in the p53 gene leads to genomic instability and decreased apoptosis, and leads to cancer. About 50% of human cancers have a mutated p53 gene. The book names two specific mutagens and the cancer each is linked to. Aflatoxin, a fungal toxin, causes mutation of p53 and may lead to hepatic cancer. Benzopyrene, from smoking, causes mutation of p53 and may lead to lung cancer.

The biological significance of apoptosis is threefold, and it explains why losing it matters. Apoptosis eliminates individual cells whose survival would be harmful to the organism — a cell damaged beyond repair, infected with a virus, starving, or under the effects of ionising radiation or toxins that cause irreparable DNA damage. It is used during development of the embryo. And it maintains homeostasis: in normal healthy adults the number of cells is kept relatively constant by a balance between cell division and cell death, and if that equilibrium is disturbed, abnormal growth and tumours, or abnormal cell loss, can result.

### Common misconceptions
Bcl-2 is not pro-apoptotic. The whole family is named after it, which is exactly why students read the name as meaning apoptotic. Bcl-2 itself is the anti-apoptotic member; Bax is the apoptotic one, and under physiological conditions the two are in balance.

p53 does not have one action. Moderate damage produces arrest in G1 through p21; severe damage produces apoptosis through Bax. Giving only one of the two answers half the question.

The TNF and FAS receptors start the extrinsic pathway, not the intrinsic one. The intrinsic pathway is started by intracellular stress and runs through cytochrome c; no receptor initiates it.

Checking cell size and DNA damage is what a checkpoint does, not what a CDK does. The CDK-cyclin complexes drive the transitions; the G1 and G2 checkpoints are the surveillance.

G0 is not a phase the cell passes through on its way round. It is a state the cell leaves the cycle for, and for some cells — most adult neurones — it is permanent.

## summary
A nucleic acid is a polymer of nucleotides, and the chapter builds DNA in three storeys: the primary structure is one chain held by phosphodiester bonds, the secondary structure is the Watson and Crick double helix held by hydrogen bonds, and the tertiary structure is the supercoiling that packs two metres of DNA into a ten-micron nucleus. The chapter then adds the one DNA molecule that breaks every rule — mitochondrial DNA, which is circular, codes for the machinery of oxidative phosphorylation, and comes only from the mother — and closes on the three RNAs.
## sections
### Definition
Nucleic acids are polymers of nucleotides. There are two main types, deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) and ribonucleic acid (RNA), and they are responsible mainly for storage and transfer of the information needed for the production of proteins.

DNA consists of two strands of polynucleotides and has a primary, a secondary and a tertiary structure. The chapter is organised on those three levels, and so is any answer about it.

The primary structure is the sequence and the backbone. Four nucleotides mainly form it: dAMP, dGMP, dCMP and dTMP. In each strand the nucleotides are linked by phosphodiester bonds between the 3'-hydroxyl group of one nucleotide and the 5'-hydroxyl group of the next through a phosphate group, and the alternating sugar-phosphate units form the backbone of each strand, written 5'-P-S-P-S-3'.

The nitrogenous bases are linked to the pentoses and project to the inside of the two strands at right angles, and the sequence of bases is what carries the genetic information. Each strand has two different ends: one carries a free phosphate group on the 5'-hydroxyl of the terminal pentose, the other a free 3'-hydroxyl group. The order of nucleotides in any DNA strand is always written 5' to 3', because that is the direction of synthesis.

### Mechanism
The secondary structure is the double helix, and the book gives it five numbered characteristics.

Watson and Crick proposed a structure for DNA in the form of a double helix, which is the most common physiological form.

First, two antiparallel strands form a right-handed helix. The two strands of DNA run antiparallel and are paired to each other and coil around a common axis to form a right-handed helix.

Second, complementary base pairing holds them together. The two strands are held together by complementary base pairing through specific hydrogen bonds. Adenine pairs with thymine through two hydrogen bonds, and guanine pairs with cytosine through three hydrogen bonds.

Third, base stacking stabilises the helix. The base pairs inside the helix are stacked above each other by Van der Waals forces and hydrophobic interactions, and those forces provide the stability of the double helix.

Fourth, the whole thing looks like a spiral staircase, in which the base pairs form the steps and the phosphate-sugar backbones form the handrails.

Fifth, the dimensions. The DNA is 2 nm in width, and from outside the helix two grooves are apparent, a major groove of 2.2 nm and a minor groove of 1.2 nm. Through these grooves many drugs and proteins can contact the nitrogenous bases without any need to open the helix.

Now put the two bond types side by side, because that pairing is the whole point of the section. Along each strand the nucleotides are held by phosphodiester bonds, which are covalent. Across the two strands they are held only by hydrogen bonds. That is why heating alone separates the strands and does not destroy them.

Heating DNA produces rupture of hydrogen bonds and separation of the two strands, which is DNA denaturation. Cooling denatured DNA results in reformation of the double helix, called renaturation or reannealing. Denaturation is the first step of the in-vitro amplification technique, the polymerase chain reaction.

The tertiary structure is packing. The length of human DNA per cell is approximately 2 metres and the diameter of the nucleus is 10 microns, so supercoiling is what makes the DNA fit. Each DNA molecule is condensed into a compact structure through different levels of supercoiling.

The double helix in the eukaryotic nucleus is packaged by special proteins, and the protein-DNA complex is called chromatin. Chromatin is supercoiled in the form of chromosomes, and each chromosome is formed of two identical chromatids.

Each chromosome contains three things. A DNA molecule. Histone proteins. And non-histone proteins.

The histones are basic proteins rich in lysine and arginine, so they are positively charged, and they are united to DNA, which is acidic and negatively charged because of its phosphate groups. Five major types are present in eukaryotic chromosomes — H1, H2A, H2B, H3 and H4 — and they interact with the minor grooves of DNA. Their main function is to condense DNA into structural units called nucleosomes.

Each nucleosome is formed of eight histones, the histone octamer or histone core, that is two copies each of H2A, H2B, H3 and H4. Around the octamer is wrapped one and three quarters of a supercoiled DNA segment containing an average of 140 base pairs. Linker or spacer DNA, an average of 60 base pairs, connects the different nucleosomes, and H1 is attached to the linker DNA. Connection of nucleosomes by linker DNA makes chromatin look like beads on a string, and nucleosomes are further arranged into more complex structures that condense the long DNA molecule into chromosomes.

The non-histone proteins interact with the major grooves. They share in the packing of DNA, are important for regulation of gene expression, and are involved in replication and transcription.

Notice which protein sits in which groove. Histones take the minor groove and do the packing; non-histone proteins take the major groove and do the regulating. That division is the reason the major groove is the one drugs and regulatory proteins use.

### Key determinants
Mitochondrial DNA is the exception the chapter builds a section around, and it is examined as a set of four facts: shape, amount, coding content and inheritance.

DNA is mainly linear but sometimes circular, as in the DNA of mitochondria, of chloroplasts in plants, and of bacteria. Mitochondrial DNA represents 0.3 to 1% of the total cellular DNA. Human mitochondrial DNA is present in the form of a small double-stranded circular supercoil.

It is responsible for controlling the synthesis of 2 ribosomal RNAs, 22 transfer RNAs and 13 proteins, most of which are involved in oxidative phosphorylation — the electron transport chain and ATP synthesis.

Mitochondria are provided by the ovum, so mitochondrial DNA molecules are maternally inherited and are used as maternal lineage. Mutations of mitochondrial DNA are associated with certain types of myopathies.

Read those two sentences together and the clinical pattern follows without being memorised separately. A mitochondrion carries the genes for its own energy machinery, and it arrives in the zygote inside the ovum. So a disease of that machinery is passed down the maternal line and shows itself first in the tissues that spend the most ATP.

The chapter's other half is RNA, and three types participate in protein synthesis: ribosomal RNA, transfer RNA and messenger RNA. Minor types found in eukaryotic cells are small nuclear RNAs, micro RNAs and small interfering RNAs.

Transfer RNA is a single strand folded into a cloverleaf stabilised by hydrogen bonds, with four main arms and one extra arm. The acceptor arm terminates at its 3'-OH end in the specific sequence CCA, and amino acids are carried as an aminoacyl group connected to that 3'-hydroxyl. The D-arm contains the unusual base dihydrouracil and is important for recognition of the tRNA by aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase, the enzyme that binds a specific amino acid to its specific tRNA. The anticodon arm carries three bases, the anticodon, which pair with the complementary codon of mRNA. The T-psi-C arm contains an unusual sequence of thymine and pseudouridine bases and binds the tRNA to the ribosome during protein synthesis. The extra arm contains 3 to 12 unpaired bases and is the major site of variation between tRNAs.

Messenger RNA carries genetic information from nuclear DNA to the cytosol, where it is the template for protein synthesis, each three bases or codon designating one amino acid. Its coding region starts with a specific initiation codon and ends with a specific termination codon. The untranslated regions before the initiation codon and after the termination codon — the 5' UTR and the 3' UTR — are transcribed with the coding region and are present in the mature mRNA, and the book attributes mRNA stability, translational efficiency and regulation of gene expression to them.

Ribosomal RNA is found with several proteins as a component of the ribosomes and serves as the site of protein synthesis. The fully active 80S ribosome is formed mainly of two nucleoprotein subunits of unequal size, 60S and 40S. The Svedberg unit is a measure of sedimentation velocity in a high-speed centrifuge, which depends on molecular weight as well as on the size of the particles.

The other forms are noncoding. Small nuclear RNAs are involved in mRNA processing, micro RNAs play an important role in regulation of gene expression, and small interfering RNAs inhibit gene expression through RNA interference.

### Normal values
DNA width: 2 nm. Major groove 2.2 nm, minor groove 1.2 nm.

Nucleosome: 8 histones, with an average of 140 base pairs of DNA wrapped one and three quarter turns around them; linker DNA averages 60 base pairs.

Length of human DNA per cell: approximately 2 metres, against a nuclear diameter of 10 microns.

Mitochondrial DNA: 0.3 to 1% of total cellular DNA; codes for 2 rRNAs, 22 tRNAs and 13 proteins.

Share of total cellular RNA: transfer RNA about 15%, messenger RNA about 5%, ribosomal RNA about 80%.

Ribosome: 80S, made of a 60S and a 40S subunit. The tRNA extra arm carries 3 to 12 bases.

### Clinical significance
The grooves are a drug target. The book says directly that through the major and minor grooves many drugs and proteins can contact the nitrogenous bases without any need to open the helix, which is why a molecule can act on DNA without denaturing it.

Denaturation is a laboratory tool before it is a disease. Heating separates the strands, cooling reanneals them, and that cycle is the first step of the polymerase chain reaction; the book refers the student to the practical book for the technique itself.

Mitochondrial DNA carries a named clinical consequence and the book gives one: mutations of mitochondrial DNA are associated with certain types of myopathies. It also states the forensic and genealogical use — mitochondrial DNA is used as maternal lineage.

Regulation lives on the packing. The non-histone proteins that interact with the major grooves are described as important for regulation of gene expression and involved in replication and transcription, so how tightly a stretch of DNA is packed is not a storage detail; it is part of whether that stretch is read.

### Common misconceptions
The bond between the two strands is not covalent. The phosphodiester bonds run along each backbone and are covalent; the two strands are held to each other only by hydrogen bonds, two for A-T and three for G-C. If the join were covalent, heat alone could not separate them.

Maternal inheritance is not X-linked inheritance. It has nothing to do with the sex chromosomes. The zygote gets its mitochondria from the ovum, so an affected father passes nothing to any child, and an affected mother passes to all of hers.

Circular DNA is not only bacterial. The book names three sites — mitochondria, chloroplasts in plants, and bacteria — and human mitochondrial DNA is a small double-stranded circular supercoil.

Thymine is not absent from RNA in this book's account. The comparison table gives thymine to RNA as a minor base in tRNA, and the T-psi-C arm is named for it. Uracil is what DNA has none of.

Histones and non-histone proteins do different jobs in different grooves. Histones are basic, sit in the minor groove and condense DNA into nucleosomes; non-histone proteins sit in the major groove and regulate expression.

## summary
This chapter is off the 2025/2026 written paper and was a four-mark question on the 2024 one, so read it knowing which exam you are sitting. What it teaches is one idea done twice: a fibrous protein's job is written into its structure. Collagen is built for tightness at every level — three residues per turn, glycine every third position, left-handed chains in a right-handed superhelix, hydroxyproline hydrogen bonds, covalent cross-links — and is therefore supportive. Elastin is built from a single chain with desmosine cross-links in every direction and is therefore elastic. Cartilage and bone are then read as collagen plus a named non-collagenous protein each.
## sections
### Definition
Extracellular matrix is present in the connective tissue surrounding the tissues and organs.

Connective tissue is made of two things. Cells — fibroblasts, macrophages, mesenchymal cells, chondrocytes and osteocytes. And the extracellular matrix itself, which has four components: fibrous proteins, which are collagen, elastin, fibrillin, fibronectin and laminin; proteoglycans, a core of proteins, 5%, attached to glycosaminoglycans; water; and minerals.

Collagen is the most abundant protein in the body, forming 25% of total body proteins. It has a supportive function and is present in tendons, skin, cartilage, bones, lungs, liver, vessels and cornea. It is synthesised by fibroblasts, mesenchymal cells, chondrocytes and osteocytes — three of which are the connective tissue cells just listed, which is the point: the matrix is made by the cells sitting in it.

### Mechanism
Collagen has a building unit, and everything about the protein follows from it.

Tropocollagen is the building unit. It is a long thin protein. It consists of three coiled alpha-chains, each of which is a left-handed helix, and the presence of proline prevents right-handed helix formation in the individual chain. The three left-handed peptide chains are then tightly twisted to form a right-handed superhelix, which is the tropocollagen molecule.

The amino acid composition is as tightly specified as the shape. Each alpha-chain contains approximately 1000 amino acids with the repeating triplet sequence Gly-X-Y, where Gly is glycine, present in every third position, X is proline and Y is hydroxyproline. The X position is sometimes occupied by lysine and the Y position sometimes by hydroxylysine, and the number of hydroxylysine residues varies between collagen types. Glucose and galactose are attached to hydroxylysine residues, which is why collagen is considered a glycoprotein.

The collagen superfamily includes more than twenty types. The two most common are type I, which contains two identical alpha-1 chains and one alpha-2 chain, and type II, which contains three identical alpha-1 chains.

Synthesis begins inside a specialised cell and finishes outside it, in five stages.

On the ribosomes of the rough endoplasmic reticulum, preprocollagen alpha-chains are formed, each with one N-terminal signal peptide and two extension peptides, one at each terminus.

In the lumen of the rough endoplasmic reticulum, the signal peptide directs the chain in and is then removed by signal peptidase, converting preprocollagen to procollagen. Some proline and some lysine residues are hydroxylated by hydroxylase enzymes, which require vitamin C as a cofactor. Some hydroxylysine residues are glycosylated with glucose or galactose. Three procollagen alpha-chains then adhere into the triple helix procollagen molecule, by formation of disulfide bonds in the C-terminal extension peptides through oxidation of cysteine residues, and by initiation of folding.

In the Golgi apparatus the procollagen molecules are packaged into secretory vesicles, and from the excretory vesicles they are secreted outside the cell by exocytosis.

Outside the cell, procollagen peptidases remove the two extension peptides from the N- and C-terminals, and the tropocollagen molecule is formed. Newly formed tropocollagen molecules are assembled in a parallel and staggered array into collagen fibrils stabilised by covalent cross-links, and the fibrils are packed together into collagen fibres stabilised by more covalent cross-links.

The book adds one line about time: the cross-links increase with age, and collagen becomes less flexible.

Follow the vitamin C. It is needed by the hydroxylase enzymes in the lumen of the rough endoplasmic reticulum, which is where proline becomes hydroxyproline. Hydroxyproline is what forms the hydrogen bonds between the chains. So the strength of the finished fibre is decided at a step that depends on a vitamin, and this chapter is where that dependency is stated.

### Key determinants
The 2024 paper asked why collagen has a strong structure, and the book prints the answer as seven numbered reasons. Learn them as seven, because each is a mark.

One. Each turn contains 3 amino acid residues, which makes a tight helix, where normal proteins contain 3.6 amino acids per turn.

Two. The presence of glycine, with its short side chain, makes the polypeptide chains very close to each other.

Three. The 3 left-handed coiled peptide chains form a right-handed superhelix.

Four. The high content of hydroxyproline forms hydrogen bonds between chains.

Five. The formation of covalent cross-linkages between adjacent polypeptide chains is responsible for the tensile properties of collagen, so it can be stretched without being broken.

Six. The specific arrangement into fibril and fibres.

Seven. The staggered array, overhanging, gives marked flexibility to collagen.

Read the seven as a ladder from the smallest scale to the largest. Reasons one to three are about a single turn and a single chain; four and five are about what holds chains to each other; six and seven are about how whole molecules are stacked. An answer that stays at one scale is an answer that has found two or three of the seven.

Elastin is the same argument run for a different job. It is an insoluble rubber-like protein, present in the elastic connective tissue of ligaments, lungs and the walls of blood vessels. Tropoelastin is the building unit, and it is a monomer — one polypeptide chain — rich in glycine, valine, alanine, proline and lysine, but containing little hydroxyproline and no hydroxylysine.

Tropoelastin monomers are secreted into the extracellular space where they are cross-linked into elastin fibres immediately. Some of the lysyl residues are oxidatively deaminated by lysyl oxidase to allysine, and three allysines plus one unmodified lysine form a desmosine cross-link. That makes elastin extensively interconnected, a rubbery network that can stretch and bend in any direction when stressed, giving connective tissue its elasticity. Elastin is fibrous when extended and globular when relaxed.

The book's own comparison sets them out in four rows. By chemical nature collagen is a glycoprotein and elastin is not, because elastin has no hydroxylysine. By common site, collagen is in skin, bone, cartilage, tendon, liver, lung, vessels and cornea, and elastin is in ligament, lung and blood vessels. By structure, collagen is tropocollagen, three polypeptide chains stabilised by hydrogen bonds and covalent cross-links, and elastin is tropoelastin, one polypeptide chain, its monomers stabilised by desmosine cross-links. By function, collagen is supportive and elastin is elastic.

One chain against three, and cross-links in every direction against cross-links along an axis. That is the whole difference, and both consequences follow from it.

### Clinical significance
Cartilage and bone are where the chapter turns the chemistry into two tissues, and each is taught as collagen plus one named non-collagenous protein.

Cartilage is an avascular connective tissue, nourished by diffusion and osmosis. Its collagen is type II, which forms 90 to 98% of the total collagen present, is formed of three alpha-1 chains, and contains more hydroxylysine and more glycosylation; minor forms present are types V, VI, IX, X and XI. Its non-collagenous proteins are aggrecan, other proteoglycans, and chondronectin, which binds type II collagen to the chondrocytes.

Aggrecan is the major proteoglycan of cartilage. It has a bottle-brush shape: a long chain of hyaluronic acid attached noncovalently to link proteins, which are attached noncovalently to core proteins, with several types of glycosaminoglycan — hyaluronic acid, chondroitin sulfate and keratan sulfate — projecting from the core proteins.

That shape explains how cartilage takes load. The glycosaminoglycan side chains bind electrostatically to collagen fibrils; being acidic they are negatively charged, so they repel each other and attract water in between, and the molecule forms a gel. That gel is what makes cartilage compressible.

And it explains why height falls with age. With age the structure of aggrecan changes: keratan sulfate and protein content increase, chondroitin sulfate content decreases, the hyaluronic acid chain becomes shorter, and the average size decreases. So cartilage compressibility decreases and human stature shortens.

Bone is a mineralised connective tissue. Its collagen is type I, which forms 90% of bone proteins and is formed of two alpha-1 and one alpha-2 chains, with type V as the minor form. Its non-collagenous proteins are osteocalcin, osteonectin and different types of proteoglycan important in bone development and mineralisation.

Osteocalcin is bone specific, synthesised by osteoblasts, and is the major non-collagenous protein in bone. It contains about 3 glutamate residues that are gamma-carboxylated by a vitamin K dependent enzyme to gamma-carboxyglutamate, which binds hydroxyapatite and so mineralises bone. Its synthesis is induced by calcitriol, the active form of vitamin D3, and its serum level is used as a marker of bone formation.

Osteonectin is a glycoprotein that plays a role in bone mineralisation, because it binds collagen and has a high affinity for calcium and for hydroxyapatite.

Two vitamins appear in this chapter and they act at different steps. Vitamin C is the cofactor for the hydroxylases that make hydroxyproline and hydroxylysine during collagen synthesis. Vitamin K is required by the enzyme that carboxylates osteocalcin so that it can bind hydroxyapatite. One builds the fibre, the other mineralises around it.

### Common misconceptions
Proline is not what makes collagen strong. Proline prevents a right-handed helix forming in the individual chain, which is why each alpha-chain is left-handed. Strength comes from glycine at every third position letting the chains lie close, from the hydroxyproline hydrogen bonds, and from the covalent cross-links.

Glycine is not one amino acid among a thousand. It is in every third position, and the reason is its short side chain: nothing bulkier would let three chains pack that closely.

The right-handed superhelix and the left-handed chain are not a contradiction. Three left-handed chains twist together into a right-handed superhelix, and the book counts that opposition as one of the seven reasons for strength.

Elastin is not collagen with fewer cross-links. It has one chain rather than three, contains little hydroxyproline and no hydroxylysine, is therefore not a glycoprotein, and its cross-link is desmosine formed from three allysines and one lysine.

Ageing does not weaken collagen in the way students expect. The book says cross-links increase with age and collagen becomes less flexible — stiffer, not looser. The loss of height with age is the aggrecan story, not the collagen one.

Tropocollagen is not what leaves the cell. Procollagen leaves the cell; the extension peptides are removed outside it by procollagen peptidases, and only then is there tropocollagen. Getting that boundary wrong puts fibril assembly inside the cell, where it does not happen.

## lose_the_mark
Attributing collagen's strength to proline. Proline prevents a right-handed helix in the single chain; glycine, hydroxyproline and the covalent cross-links do the strengthening.
Giving three or four reasons when the book prints seven. The 2024 question was worth four marks for "explain on biochemical basis", and a partial list is a partial answer.
Saying 3.6 residues per turn for collagen. That is the normal protein figure; collagen has 3, which is what makes the helix tight.
Reversing the handedness. The individual chains are left-handed, the superhelix they form is right-handed.
Confusing covalent cross-links with hydrogen bonds. Hydroxyproline makes the hydrogen bonds; the covalent cross-links are what let collagen be stretched without breaking.
Putting fibril assembly inside the cell. The extension peptides are cut off outside the cell, and only tropocollagen assembles into fibrils.
Calling elastin a glycoprotein. It has no hydroxylysine, so nothing is glycosylated on it; that is the first row of the book's own comparison table.
Naming lysine as the desmosine cross-link on its own. Three lysyl residues are first oxidatively deaminated by lysyl oxidase to allysine, and three allysines plus one unmodified lysine make desmosine.
Swapping the collagen types between the two tissues. Type II is cartilage; type I is bone.
Explaining loss of height with age by collagen. It is the change in aggrecan that reduces cartilage compressibility; ageing collagen becomes less flexible, not shorter.
