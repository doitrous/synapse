# Item
## id
ART-GIT-206DIG-RETROPERITONEUM-VASCULATURE-NERVES
## title
Retroperitoneal vasculature, renal/ureteric relations, diaphragmatic openings and posterior abdominal wall nerves
## subject
gi
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Anatomy
## summary
The great retroperitoneal vessels set a fixed pattern of relations that exam distractors routinely reverse: the aortic hiatus (T12) carries the aorta, azygos vein and thoracic duct, not the vagus and oesophagus, which belong to the higher oesophageal hiatus (T10); the IVC has structures directly anterior to it (the third part of the duodenum) that are at risk from an anterior mass, while posterior structures like the sympathetic trunk are shielded; and the right kidney sits lower than the left because of the liver above it. On the posterior abdominal wall, the femoral and obturator nerves emerge from opposite (lateral and medial) borders of psoas major, the ureter passes deep to the gonadal vessels as both descend, and quadratus lumborum's only real action is lateral trunk flexion, not any hip-joint movement.
## sections
### Definition
The abdominal aorta gives three unpaired branches (coeliac trunk T12, superior mesenteric artery L1, inferior mesenteric artery L3) and several paired branches (inferior phrenic, middle suprarenal, renal, gonadal, lumbar arteries) before bifurcating at L4; the suprarenal glands receive a triple direct supply from the aorta, inferior phrenic and renal arteries. The IVC ascends just to the right of the aorta, crossed anteriorly by the third part of the duodenum (among other structures). The right kidney lies roughly one vertebral level below the left, its hilum related anteriorly to the second part of the duodenum; at each renal hilum the vein lies anterior to the artery, which lies anterior to the renal pelvis. The ureter descends on psoas major, passing deep to the gonadal vessels, and is crossed anteriorly by the root of the small bowel mesentery together with the abdominal aorta, IVC and right gonadal vessels.
### Mechanism
The aortic hiatus, at T12 behind the median arcuate ligament, is unlike the oesophageal (T10) and caval (T8) openings in having no surrounding muscular sphincter, and transmits the aorta, azygos vein and thoracic duct — an aneurysm here threatens those three structures, not the vagus nerve or oesophagus, which pass through the separate, higher oesophageal hiatus. On the posterior wall, most lumbar plexus branches (iliohypogastric, ilioinguinal, lateral cutaneous nerve of thigh, femoral) emerge from psoas major's lateral border, while the obturator nerve is the exception from its medial border, and the genitofemoral nerve (root value L1-L2, not L2-3) is the further exception that pierces through the muscle's substance entirely. The sympathetic trunks enter the abdomen behind the medial (not lateral) arcuate ligaments. Quadratus lumborum, running iliac crest to the 12th rib and upper lumbar transverse processes, laterally flexes the trunk unilaterally and helps fix the 12th rib in bilateral contraction — it has no hip-joint action. A duplicated ureter follows premature splitting of the ureteric bud (the mesonephric duct outgrowth that induces the metanephric blastema and becomes the ureter/pelvis/calyces/collecting system) before or during its penetration of the metanephric tissue.
### Key determinants
Three recurring axes: which diaphragmatic opening (T8 caval, T10 oesophageal, T12 aortic) carries which structures; anterior-versus-posterior position relative to the IVC (only anterior structures are compressed by an anterior mass); and which lateral-versus-medial psoas major border a given lumbar plexus nerve emerges from (femoral lateral, obturator medial, genitofemoral through the substance).
### Clinical significance
An aortic hiatus aneurysm can compress the thoracic duct (causing chylous effusions) and azygos vein without touching the vagus or oesophagus, a distinction that matters for interpreting the resulting clinical picture; an anteriorly sited retroperitoneal mass causing duodenal (third part) obstruction versus a posteriorly sited one causing sympathetic chain symptoms reflects the same anteroposterior IVC-relation logic; and recognising the femoral/obturator nerve split at psoas major's two borders underlies expected sensory and motor deficits in retroperitoneal or psoas-compartment pathology.
### Common misconceptions
Assigning the vagus nerve or oesophagus to the aortic hiatus rather than the oesophageal hiatus; assuming any nearby retroperitoneal structure is at risk from a mass regardless of its anteroposterior IVC relation; reversing the femoral/obturator nerve-to-psoas-border pairing or citing the lateral (rather than medial) arcuate ligament for the sympathetic trunks; and crediting quadratus lumborum with a hip-joint action it does not perform.

# Item
## id
ART-GIT-206DIG-ANTEROLATERAL-WALL-INGUINAL
## title
Anterolateral abdominal wall layers, rectus sheath composition and the inguinal canal's two rings
## subject
gi
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Anatomy
## summary
The three flat anterolateral abdominal wall muscles (external oblique, internal oblique, transversus abdominis) each contribute in a fixed, testable way to the inguinal canal and rectus sheath: external oblique's rolled-under lower border becomes the inguinal ligament and its aponeurosis alone covers rectus abdominis above the costal margin; transversalis fascia (deep to all three muscles) forms the deep inguinal ring, distinct from the external-oblique-aponeurosis-derived superficial ring; and the spermatic cord's three fascial coverings map one-to-one onto the three layers a descending testis passes through, with the iliohypogastric nerve a close but genuine exception that never enters the cord itself.
## sections
### Definition
External oblique arises by eight fleshy digitations from the lower eight ribs, interdigitating with serratus anterior above and latissimus dorsi below; its fibres run obliquely downwards and forwards to a broad aponeurosis whose lower border rolls under to form the inguinal ligament, and whose medial part opens as the superficial inguinal ring. Internal oblique lies deep to external oblique, its aponeurosis splitting around rectus abdominis in the sheath's mid-zone. Transversus abdominis, the deepest of the three, arises from the lower six costal cartilages (interdigitating with the diaphragm), the thoracolumbar fascia, iliac crest and inguinal ligament, inserting via its aponeurosis into the linea alba and pubic crest. Transversalis fascia, deep to transversus abdominis, opens as the deep inguinal ring.
### Mechanism
Above the costal margin, rectus abdominis lies directly on the costal cartilages and only the external oblique aponeurosis passes anterior to it; below the costal margin, in the sheath's mid-zone, the internal oblique aponeurosis splits to add an anterior lamina alongside the external oblique aponeurosis. Quadratus lumborum (posterior wall, not anterolateral) laterally flexes the trunk. The deep inguinal ring, an opening in transversalis fascia lateral to the inferior epigastric vessels, is where the testis carries a tubular evagination of that fascia (becoming the internal spermatic fascia) during descent; the superficial ring, by contrast, opens in the external oblique aponeurosis. The three spermatic cord coverings (external spermatic fascia from external oblique, cremaster from internal oblique, internal spermatic fascia from transversalis fascia) wrap the cord's true contents — testicular artery, vas deferens, pampiniform plexus, genital branch of genitofemoral nerve, cremasteric vessels, lymphatics — but not the iliohypogastric nerve, which runs a separate course above the cord after piercing internal oblique.
### Key determinants
Which flat-muscle aponeurosis forms which ring (external oblique aponeurosis → superficial ring; transversalis fascia → deep ring) and which forms the anterior rectus sheath wall at which vertical level (external oblique alone above the costal margin, plus internal oblique's anterior lamina in the mid-zone below it); and which nerve genuinely enters the spermatic cord (genitofemoral's genital branch) versus which merely passes nearby (iliohypogastric).
### Clinical significance
The indirect/direct hernia distinction depends on exactly this ring anatomy — an indirect hernia traverses the deep ring lateral to the inferior epigastric vessels and follows the same fascial coverings as the spermatic cord, while a direct hernia bulges medial to those vessels through the posterior wall (transversalis fascia) directly; and correctly excluding the iliohypogastric nerve from the cord's contents matters for surgical nerve-sparing during hernia repair or orchidopexy.
### Common misconceptions
Crediting pectoralis major (rather than latissimus dorsi) as external oblique's lower interdigitation partner, or citing an upwards fibre direction; reversing transversus abdominis's costal cartilage relation from origin to insertion; confusing the deep and superficial inguinal rings' parent layers; and assuming the iliohypogastric nerve travels within the spermatic cord because of its proximity in the inguinal region.

# Item
## id
ART-GIT-206DIG-HEPATOBILIARY-SPLENIC-CLINICAL
## title
Hepatobiliary and splenic gross anatomy, portosystemic anastomoses and gastrointestinal clinical landmarks
## subject
gi
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Anatomy
## summary
The gallbladder, spleen and colon each have a recognised set of relations that exam distractors reverse with a single swapped word: Hartmann's pouch bulges from the gallbladder's neck, its body rests on the transverse (not ascending) colon, and the common bile duct grooves the head (not body) of the pancreas; the spleen sits in the left hypochondrium, supplied by the coeliac trunk, with its diaphragmatic surface (not visceral) facing the diaphragm; and the caecum, fully peritonealised with three taeniae coli, depends on the superior (not inferior) mesenteric artery. Two clinical landmarks recur across this cluster — Murphy's point (right 9th costal cartilage, gallbladder fundus) and the referred right-shoulder pain of cholecystitis (via the phrenic nerve's shared C3-C5 territory with the diaphragm) — alongside the vascular logic of which bowel segment an isolated superior mesenteric artery injury threatens (the midgut, e.g. ascending colon) and which venous pair genuinely forms a portosystemic anastomosis (superior with middle/inferior rectal veins, not any two veins from the same system).
## sections
### Definition
The gallbladder has a fundus, body and neck; Hartmann's pouch is a mucosal outpouching of the neck near the cystic duct junction, a common site for stone impaction. Murphy's point, the gallbladder fundus's surface marking, sits at the tip of the right 9th costal cartilage where rectus abdominis's lateral border crosses the costal margin. The spleen lies in the left hypochondrium opposite the 9th-11th ribs, its diaphragmatic surface facing the diaphragm and its visceral surface facing the stomach, left kidney and pancreatic tail. The caecum, in the right iliac fossa, is completely invested by peritoneum, usually without its own mesentery, and bears three taeniae coli. The quadrate lobe occupies the liver's anterior visceral surface between the ligamentum-teres fissure and the gallbladder fossa, related to the transverse colon and pyloroduodenal region.
### Mechanism
The common hepatic artery (a coeliac trunk, foregut branch) continues as the proper hepatic artery, ascending in the free edge of the lesser omentum anterior to the portal vein (bile duct to its right), and divides into right and left terminal branches at the porta hepatis — the cystic artery typically arises from the right terminal branch, usually within Calot's triangle. The common bile duct descends behind the first part of the duodenum and grooves the posterior surface of the pancreatic head before opening at the major duodenal papilla. The superior mesenteric artery, the artery of the midgut, supplies structures from the distal duodenum through the proximal two-thirds of the transverse colon (including the ascending colon and caecum, via its ileocolic branch) — an isolated SMA injury spares foregut structures (like the first part of the duodenum, coeliac trunk territory) and hindgut/pelvic structures (sigmoid colon, rectum). At the anorectal junction, the superior rectal vein (portal, via the inferior mesenteric vein) anastomoses with the middle and inferior rectal veins (systemic, via internal iliac/pudendal veins) — one of four classic portosystemic sites. Referred gallbladder pain reaches the right shoulder because the gallbladder and central diaphragm share phrenic nerve (C3-C5) afferents, the same dermatomal territory as the phrenic nerve's cutaneous distribution over the shoulder.
### Key determinants
Three recurring axes: which named vessel or landmark sits on the "correct" side or level when a exam option shifts it by one structure (9th vs 10th costal cartilage; head vs body of pancreas; transverse vs ascending colon; left vs right hypochondrium); which of the three gut-artery territories (foregut/coeliac, midgut/SMA, hindgut/IMA) a given bowel segment belongs to; and which venous pairing genuinely spans the portal-systemic divide versus staying within one system.
### Clinical significance
Murphy's sign (pain and inspiratory arrest on palpating Murphy's point) remains a bedside test for acute cholecystitis; the referred right-shoulder pain pattern explains a recognised atypical presentation of gallbladder disease; and knowing the SMA's precise midgut territory underlies the classic presentation and localisation of acute mesenteric ischaemia after embolic or traumatic SMA occlusion.
### Common misconceptions
Shifting Murphy's point from the 9th to the 10th costal cartilage, or confusing it with McBurney's point (appendicitis); resting the gallbladder body on the ascending rather than transverse colon; grooving the bile duct against the pancreatic body rather than head; placing the spleen in the right hypochondrium or swapping its diaphragmatic/visceral surface relations; and assigning caecal blood supply to the inferior rather than superior mesenteric artery.
