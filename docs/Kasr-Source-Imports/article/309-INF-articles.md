Parasitology: host-parasite general principles
## subject

## status

## summary
This article groups the general parasitology principles tested by the paper (Q1, Q13): the definition of a facultative parasite (one able to exist independently of a host, unlike an obligate parasite) and the definition of the definitive host (the host harbouring the adult or sexually reproducing stage, in contrast to the intermediate host that carries larval or asexual stages). These host-parasite definitions frame the trematode life-cycle facts covered in the companion article.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the introductory host-parasite concepts tested by the paper; each linked concept carries one atomic definition (facultative parasite; definitive host) that underpins the parasite life cycles studied in the module.
### Mechanism
The facts rest on how a parasite relates to its hosts: a facultative parasite retains the ability to live free of any host, while the definitive host is defined by supporting the adult/sexual phase and the intermediate host by supporting larval/asexual multiplication.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: a facultative parasite is defined by the ability to live independently of a host (not merely within one); the definitive host is the one with the adult or sexual stage (man for Schistosoma and Taenia), whereas larval/asexual stages mark the intermediate host and a transmitting insect is a vector.
### Clinical significance
These definitions are the vocabulary for reading every parasite life cycle: identifying which host is definitive versus intermediate determines where transmission is interrupted and how a diagnosis (adult, egg, larva) maps onto a host.

See each concept's pitfalls field for the exact reversal each distractor exploits (facultative confused with obligate; definitive host confused with the intermediate host or vector).

## title
Trematodes and food-borne flukes: Fasciola, Schistosoma, Heterophyes
## subject
inf
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Parasitology
## summary
This article groups the trematode and food-borne-fluke facts tested by the paper (Q2-Q11, Q14-Q19). It covers Fasciola (the sheep liver fluke: Lymnaea snail host, encysted metacercaria on vegetation as infective stage and mode of infection, immature-fluke destruction of liver parenchyma, obstructive jaundice from adults in the bile ducts, and spurious infection from eating infected liver), Heterophyes (Pirenella-conica snail and fish two-intermediate-host cycle, infection by undercooked Tilapia/Mugil, the lophocercous cercaria, and egg emboli causing granulomatous brain and cardiac lesions), Schistosoma (pathology from eggs trapped in tissues, and Bulinus truncatus as the snail host of S. haematobium) and the pentastome Linguatula serrata as a cause of halzoun.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the trematode/food-borne-fluke concepts tested by the paper; each linked concept carries one atomic fact about a fluke's snail (and fish) host, infective stage, mode of infection or pathology.
### Mechanism
The facts follow trematode life cycles: cercariae leave a snail intermediate host and either encyst on vegetation (Fasciola) or in fish (Heterophyes) or penetrate skin (Schistosoma); disease arises from immature-fluke tissue migration (acute fascioliasis), adult residence (biliary obstruction), egg embolisation and granuloma formation (heterophyiasis, schistosomiasis) rather than from the tolerated adult worms.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: Fasciola infective stage = metacercaria on plants (Lymnaea snail), drug of choice triclabendazole; Heterophyes needs snail (Pirenella) then fish, has a lophocercous cercaria, and its eggs embolise to brain; Schistosoma disease = granuloma around trapped eggs, S. haematobium snail = Bulinus truncatus; spurious fascioliasis = transit eggs in stool; Linguatula serrata = halzoun.
### Clinical significance
These facts underlie diagnosis and control of the common Egyptian trematode infections: interrupting the snail (or fish) stage, recognising obstructive jaundice and hepatic disease in fascioliasis, urinary and hepatosplenic disease in schistosomiasis, and avoiding the pitfall of transit (spurious) eggs on stool microscopy.
### Common misconceptions
See each concept's pitfalls field for the exact reversal each distractor exploits (Fasciola egg/cercaria versus metacercaria infectivity; snail-host mix-ups Lymnaea/Bulinus/Biomphalaria/Pirenella; egg emboli versus larva migrans; adults versus eggs as the cause of schistosomal disease).

## title
Cestodes: tapeworms of Taenia, Diphyllobothrium, Echinococcus and Hymenolepis
## subject
inf
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Parasitology
## summary
This article groups the cestode (tapeworm) facts tested by the Cestoda section of the paper (Q1-28), including the tail items on Hymenolepis nana's indirect arthropod cycle, the Casoni immediate-hypersensitivity test and anaphylaxis from hydatid-cyst rupture, coenurosis (Taenia multiceps) and cestode transmission by ingestion of an infected flea. It covers Taenia (Cysticercus bovis as the larval stage of the beef tapeworm T. saginata, the single cattle intermediate host, the acid-fast/Ziehl-Neelsen-positive taeniid egg, and how T. solium is more dangerous than T. saginata because it causes human cysticercosis — with man a blind dead-end intermediate host and corticosteroids used to cover the inflammation of cyst disintegration), Diphyllobothrium latum (the operculated egg, fish-borne infection, stool diagnosis by operculated eggs without segments, bears as paratenic hosts, and vitamin-B12-deficiency megaloblastic anaemia with neuropathy), Echinococcus granulosus (man as accidental intermediate host, the dog definitive host, the cartwheel daughter-cyst ultrasound sign, and PAIR treatment of hydatid disease) and Hymenolepis nana (eggs directly infective to man, and toxic/allergic — not CNS-invasive — nervous manifestations).
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the cestode concepts tested by the paper; each linked concept carries one atomic fact about a tapeworm's larval stage, egg, host role, mode of infection, pathology or treatment.
### Mechanism
The facts follow cestode life cycles: taeniid eggs develop into a cysticercus in one intermediate host (cattle for T. saginata, pig/man for T. solium), Diphyllobothrium passes through a copepod then a fish, and Echinococcus cycles between the dog (adult worm) and an intermediate host (man, livestock) that forms hydatid cysts; disease arises from larval tissue cysts (cysticercosis, hydatidosis), worm-induced vitamin B12 deficiency (Diphyllobothrium) or toxic/allergic effects (Hymenolepis) rather than from CNS invasion by the worm itself.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: Cysticercus bovis = T. saginata larva; only T. solium causes human cysticercosis (man a blind intermediate host); Diphyllobothrium has the operculated egg, is fish-borne, uses bears as paratenic hosts and causes B12-deficiency anaemia/neuropathy; Echinococcus has the dog as definitive host, man as accidental intermediate host, the cartwheel daughter-cyst sign and PAIR treatment; Hymenolepis nana eggs are directly infective; taeniid eggs are acid-fast; Taenia saginata needs only one intermediate host.
### Clinical significance
These facts underlie diagnosis and control of the medically important tapeworms: recognising fish- versus meat-borne acquisition, diagnosing Diphyllobothrium by operculated eggs and its megaloblastic anaemia, imaging and treating hydatid disease (cartwheel sign, PAIR, dog contact as the risk factor), and managing neurocysticercosis (anti-helminthics under corticosteroid cover).
### Common misconceptions
See each concept's pitfalls field for the exact reversal each distractor exploits (Cysticercus bovis vs cellulosae; taeniasis vs cysticercosis; operculated Diphyllobothrium egg vs non-operculated taeniid/Hymenolepis eggs; B12-deficiency anaemia vs iron-deficiency/haemolysis; dog as definitive vs man as intermediate host of Echinococcus).
