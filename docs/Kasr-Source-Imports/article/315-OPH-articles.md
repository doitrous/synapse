## summary
This article groups the anatomy facts of the eye and its adnexa tested by Chapter 1 of the question bank (Q1-15, Q18, Q19, Q23-26). It covers the three coats of the eye (the outer fibrous cornea and sclera, the middle vascular uvea of iris, ciliary body and choroid, and the inner neurosensory retina), the cornea (five layers, non-keratinized epithelium, lamellar stroma, avascularity and endothelial pump that keep it transparent), aqueous humour (secreted by the ciliary body into the posterior chamber, draining at the anterior chamber angle), the chambers and the drainage angle (trabecular meshwork, scleral spur, Schwalbe line), the crystalline lens (about 20 D of refractive power, suspended by zonules), the conjunctiva (palpebral, bulbar and fornices), and the orbit and adnexa (four-walled pyramid related to the paranasal sinuses, the nasolacrimal duct draining to the inferior meatus, and the superior orbital fissure transmitting cranial nerves III, IV, V1 and VI).
## sections
### Definition
The eye is a three-coated globe (fibrous, vascular/uveal and neurosensory) sitting in a bony orbit with its adnexa (lids, conjunctiva and lacrimal apparatus). Each linked concept states one atomic anatomical fact; this overview ties them into the coats, the transparent media, the drainage pathway and the orbit.
### Mechanism
The anatomy explains function: the avascular, non-keratinized, lamellar cornea with an active endothelial pump stays transparent and refracts ~43 D; aqueous secreted by the ciliary body into the posterior chamber flows through the pupil and drains at the angle to maintain intraocular pressure; the zonules transmit ciliary-muscle action to the lens for accommodation; and the close relation of the orbital walls to the paranasal sinuses is the route by which sinus infection reaches the orbit.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: aqueous is secreted by the ciliary body but drained at the angle; the uvea (not the retina) is the vascular middle coat; the cornea has five layers (Bowman, not Bruch), is non-keratinized and avascular; the posterior chamber lies between iris and lens; the tarsus is eyelid, not conjunctiva; the nasolacrimal duct opens into the inferior meatus; the lens is suspended by zonules; the RPE is not an angle structure; and the superior orbital fissure (not the optic canal) transmits CN III, IV, V1 and VI.
### Clinical significance
These facts underlie clinical ophthalmology: aqueous dynamics explain glaucoma, corneal transparency explains why oedema or vascularisation blinds, the orbit-sinus relation explains orbital cellulitis, and lens/zonule anatomy underlies cataract surgery and lens subluxation.
### Common misconceptions
See each concept's pitfalls field for the exact reversal each distractor exploits (production versus drainage of aqueous, uvea versus retina, Bowman versus Bruch, tarsus as conjunctiva, optic canal versus superior orbital fissure).

Ophthalmology: basic clinical examination of the eye

## summary
This article groups the examination facts tested by Chapter 1 of the question bank (Q16, Q17, Q20, Q21, Q22). It covers the abnormal pupillary (fundal) reflex colours (white = mature cataract, black = vitreous haemorrhage, yellow = endophthalmitis, and no blue reflex for glaucoma), the Hirschberg corneal light reflex test for estimating the angle of strabismus (about 15 prism diopters per millimetre of reflex displacement, temporal reflex meaning esotropia), the methods of fundus examination (direct and indirect ophthalmoscopy and slit-lamp biomicroscopy, distinguished from retinoscopy which measures refraction), confrontation visual-field testing (detecting gross defects such as hemianopia but not small scotomas), and the place of OCT as an ancillary imaging investigation rather than a routine bedside examination.
## sections
### Definition
The basic ophthalmic examination is a sequence of bedside tests of vision, pupils, ocular alignment, fields and the fundus, supplemented by ancillary investigations. Each linked concept states one atomic examination fact.
### Mechanism
Each test probes a specific function: the fundal reflex colour reflects the clarity and pathology of the media and retina; the Hirschberg reflex position measures ocular alignment geometrically (~15 PD/mm); ophthalmoscopy and slit-lamp lenses image the fundus optically; and confrontation crudely maps the visual field against the examiner's own.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: there is no blue reflex for glaucoma; a 2 mm temporal Hirschberg reflex is ~30 PD of esotropia; retinoscopy is refraction, not fundus examination; confrontation detects hemianopia but not paracentral scotoma or an enlarged blind spot; and OCT is an investigation, not a routine examination step.
### Clinical significance
These bedside skills triage ophthalmic disease: leukocoria demands urgent exclusion of retinoblastoma and cataract, the Hirschberg test screens for strabismus in children, and confrontation fields detect neurological visual loss such as hemianopia at the bedside.
### Common misconceptions
See each concept's pitfalls field for the exact reversal each distractor exploits (inventing a glaucoma reflex colour, mistaking retinoscopy for fundoscopy, or expecting confrontation to reveal small scotomas).
