# Item

## title
GI hormones of the fasting and fed states: gastrin, secretin, GIP, VIP and motilin
## subject
gi
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Physiology
## summary
Four peptide hormones and one paracrine mediator coordinate GI secretion and motility across the fed and fasting states. Gastrin (G cells, antrum) drives gastric acid secretion and is switched off by acid itself -- destroy the acid-secreting parietal cells, as in pernicious anaemia, and gastrin rises unchecked. Secretin and GIP are both released by duodenal contents (acid for secretin, glucose and fat for GIP) and both act as enterogastrones that dampen gastric acid and emptying, while GIP additionally potentiates insulin release as an incretin. VIP, released from enteric "cholinergic VIP-releasing" inhibitory neurons rather than circulating in blood as a classical hormone, relaxes GI smooth muscle and augments pancreatic bicarbonate secretion. Motilin, cycling only between meals, triggers the migrating motor complex and has no role in stimulating gastric acid secretion -- a fasting-state motor hormone, not a fed-state secretagogue.
## sections
### Definition
Gastrin is secreted by antral G cells in response to luminal peptides/amino acids, gastric distension, and vagal (GRP-mediated) stimulation; it is the most potent physiological stimulant of parietal-cell acid secretion and of chief-cell pepsinogen release, and its own secretion is suppressed once antral/duodenal luminal pH falls below about 2 -- a direct acid-mediated negative-feedback loop. Secretin is released from duodenal S cells specifically by acid chyme (pH below about 4.5) entering the duodenum. Glucose-dependent insulinotropic peptide (GIP) is released from duodenal/jejunal K cells by luminal glucose and fat. Vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) is not secreted into the bloodstream from a discrete endocrine cell type the way the other three are; it is released as a neurotransmitter from cholinergic VIP-releasing enteric neurons. Motilin is released cyclically from duodenal/jejunal Mo cells specifically during fasting.

### Mechanism
Gastrin's acid-mediated negative feedback is the key to its clinical vignettes: anything that removes gastric acid (pernicious anaemia's autoimmune destruction of parietal cells, or a proton pump inhibitor) removes the brake and raises gastrin (secondary hypergastrinaemia), while anything that adds unregulated gastrin secretion despite normal or high acid (a gastrinoma, Zollinger-Ellison syndrome) is a primary hypergastrinaemia with the opposite acid-secretion direction. Secretin and GIP are the two classical "enterogastrones": secretin inhibits parietal-cell HCl secretion and inhibits gastric emptying, while stimulating pancreatic duct cells to secrete bicarbonate; GIP inhibits gastric acid secretion and motility while its incretin action potentiates glucose-stimulated insulin release and protects beta cells from apoptosis. VIP relaxes GI and vascular smooth muscle (vasodilation, not vasoconstriction) and augments secretin-stimulated pancreatic bicarbonate output; a VIP-secreting tumour (VIPoma) produces watery diarrhoea through this same relaxant, pro-secretory profile. Motilin's action is purely motor: it triggers phase III of the migrating motor complex, and its secretion (and the MMC it drives) is suppressed by feeding and reduced by the smooth-muscle-relaxant effect of pregnancy's high progesterone, contributing to pregnancy dyspepsia/constipation.

### Key determinants
Distinguish each hormone by its trigger (acid → secretin; glucose/fat → GIP; peptides/distension/vagal → gastrin; fasting state → motilin; enteric neural firing, not a blood-borne trigger → VIP) and by its net effect on gastric acid (gastrin stimulates; secretin and GIP inhibit; motilin has no direct secretory effect at all). Pernicious anaemia versus Zollinger-Ellison syndrome is the classic gastrin discriminator: both raise gastrin, but acid output is low in the former and inappropriately high in the latter.

### Clinical significance
Recognising which hormone is "the exception" in an "all of the following except" question depends on knowing each hormone's defined trigger and action precisely -- e.g., GIP's own secretion is stimulated (not inhibited) by duodenal fat and glucose, despite GIP's separate inhibitory effect on the stomach; motilin plays no role in stimulating gastric secretion despite being a gut peptide hormone; and gastrin, unlike the enterogastrones, raises rather than lowers gastric pH resistance (i.e., it stimulates acid, lowering pH) in a way that is easy to invert when reading quickly.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming every gut peptide hormone stimulates gastric acid secretion (only gastrin, among these five, does); assuming GIP's "inhibitory" name means duodenal fat/glucose inhibit its own release (they stimulate it -- the inhibition targets the stomach, downstream); assuming VIP circulates as a classical blood-borne hormone rather than acting largely as an enteric neurotransmitter; and assuming motilin, like gastrin, promotes gastric secretion, when its role is exclusively about fasting-state motility.

ART-GIT-206DIG-SALIVA-GASTRIC-PANCREATIC-SECRETION
## title
Salivary, gastric acid and pancreatic exocrine secretion: composition, buffering and drug targets
## subject
gi
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Physiology
## summary
Saliva, gastric juice and pancreatic juice are each two-stage secretions -- an initial acinar/parietal output modified by a duct or a basolateral exchange step -- and each has a distinct, testable composition. Ductal modification makes final saliva hypotonic and rich in bicarbonate/potassium (not sodium), with resting oral pH close to neutral rather than fixed high. Gastric acid secretion by the parietal cell H+/K+ ATPase is paired with basolateral bicarbonate efflux into gastric venous blood (the alkaline tide) and is the target of proton pump inhibitors, which irreversibly block the pump itself. Pancreatic duct cells secrete a bicarbonate-rich fluid under secretin stimulation via active transport, lowering (not raising) pancreatic venous blood pH -- the mirror image of the gastric alkaline tide -- while pancreatic acinar cells secrete the digestive enzymes, of which lipase loss specifically produces the steatorrhoea of pancreatitis.
## sections
### Definition
Salivary acinar cells produce an isotonic primary secretion that striated-duct cells then modify by reabsorbing Na+/Cl- and secreting K+/HCO3-, yielding a final hypotonic, bicarbonate/potassium-rich fluid; besides lubrication and buffering, saliva protects the oral mucosa against pathogenic bacteria (lysozyme, IgA, mechanical washing) and cools hot food. Gastric parietal cells secrete H+ apically via the H+/K+ ATPase while a basolateral Cl-/HCO3- exchanger secretes carbonic-anhydrase-derived HCO3- into gastric venous blood (the alkaline tide). Pancreatic exocrine tissue has two functional cell types: acinar cells secrete the digestive enzymes (amylase, lipase, trypsin/chymotrypsin), while duct (centroacinar/intercalated) cells secrete the bicarbonate-rich alkaline fluid that neutralises duodenal acid chyme, driven by secretin and by active, not passive, bicarbonate transport.

### Mechanism
Because ductal Na+/Cl- reabsorption exceeds K+/HCO3- secretion in the salivary duct, final saliva is hypotonic to plasma even though the primary acinar secretion is isotonic -- this ductal modification (and the resulting hypotonicity) is reduced only at very high flow rates. In the stomach, the alkaline tide is a direct consequence of active H+ secretion, not of the proton pump's structure or of its blockade -- proton pump inhibitors instead act by covalently and irreversibly binding the H+/K+ ATPase itself (after being protonated and converted to a reactive sulphenamide in the acidic canalicular space), giving more complete and durable acid suppression than H2-receptor or muscarinic-receptor blockade. In the pancreas, secretin-stimulated duct-cell bicarbonate secretion is active (transcellular, not passive diffusion), and because bicarbonate is taken up from blood before net apical secretion into the duct lumen, pancreatic venous blood pH falls during active secretion -- opposite in direction to the gastric alkaline tide. Loss of pancreatic acinar lipase output specifically (more than amylase or the proteases) in pancreatitis produces steatorrhoea, since lipase alone hydrolyses dietary triglycerides for micellar solubilisation and absorption.

### Key determinants
Every question in this cluster turns on getting a direction right: is saliva's final composition rich or poor in a given ion relative to plasma (rich in K+/HCO3-, poor in Na+/Cl-, hypotonic overall); does gastric venous blood pH rise or fall during active secretion (rises -- alkaline tide) versus pancreatic venous blood pH (falls, the mirror image); is a given enzyme deficiency (lipase specifically) responsible for steatorrhoea; and is pancreatic/salivary bicarbonate secreted by active transport (yes) or passive diffusion (no, a common distractor).

### Clinical significance
Proton pump inhibitors are first-line therapy for duodenal ulcer and reflux precisely because irreversible H+/K+ ATPase blockade gives more complete acid suppression than reversible H2-blockade; recognising the alkaline tide explains why large meals can transiently alkalinise systemic blood. Steatorrhoea as an early/prominent sign of exocrine pancreatic insufficiency (chronic pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis) reflects the disproportionate vulnerability of fat digestion to lipase loss compared with protein/carbohydrate digestion, which have more reserve capacity from proteases and amylase respectively.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming saliva is isotonic and Na+-rich like plasma (ductal modification specifically makes it hypotonic and K+/HCO3--rich); assuming salivary buffering keeps oral pH fixed at a high value rather than close to neutral at rest; confusing the alkaline tide (a consequence of acid secretion) with the proton pump itself or with pump inhibition; and assuming pancreatic venous blood becomes more alkaline (like the stomach's) during active bicarbonate secretion, when it in fact becomes more acidic.

ART-GIT-206DIG-MOTILITY-SWALLOWING-VOMITING
## title
GI motility: slow waves, swallowing, gastric accommodation, peristalsis and the migrating motor complex
## subject
gi
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Physiology
## summary
GI smooth muscle contraction is organised by two independent layers of control: an intrinsic electrical rhythm set by pacemaker interstitial cells of Cajal, and reflex programmes -- swallowing, receptive relaxation, peristalsis and the migrating motor complex -- that are superimposed on it. Swallowing's pharyngeal phase requires transient relaxation, not contraction, of the upper oesophageal sphincter, and briefly inhibits respiration to protect the airway. Lower oesophageal sphincter tone (reduced in pregnancy) prevents reflux, while receptive relaxation of the proximal stomach, not distal peristalsis, accommodates an incoming meal. Small-intestinal peristalsis follows the "law of the gut" -- contraction behind, relaxation ahead of the bolus -- and the fasting-state migrating motor complex sweeps residual debris and bacteria through the gut between meals. Vomiting, a medullary reflex, empties the stomach and drives hypochloraemic metabolic alkalosis through loss of gastric H+ and Cl-.
## sections
### Definition
Interstitial cells of Cajal generate the basal electrical rhythm (slow waves) that sets the maximum possible contraction frequency of each GIT segment (about 3/min in the stomach, up to 12/min in the duodenum); an actual contraction only occurs if a slow wave reaches threshold, typically via added neural or hormonal excitation producing superimposed spike potentials. Swallowing begins voluntarily (oral phase) but is completed by a reflex coordinated by the medullary swallowing centre; during its pharyngeal phase, the upper oesophageal sphincter relaxes to admit the bolus, the larynx elevates under the epiglottis, the soft palate seals the nasopharynx, and respiration is briefly inhibited (deglutition apnoea). Receptive relaxation is a vagally-mediated fall in fundic/upper-body gastric tone that accommodates an incoming meal without raising intragastric pressure, distinct from the antrum's peristalsis, segmentation and mixing movements. Small-intestinal peristalsis follows the peristaltic reflex ("law of the gut"): ascending excitation (contraction) behind the bolus paired with descending inhibition (relaxation) ahead of it. The migrating motor complex is a cyclical, multiphasic, motilin-driven pattern of fasting-state motility that sweeps from stomach to ileocaecal valve roughly every 90-120 minutes.

### Mechanism
Lower oesophageal sphincter tone, myogenically maintained and higher than that of the adjacent stomach/mid-oesophagus, is the principal anti-reflux barrier; it is reduced by pregnancy's high progesterone (explaining pregnancy heartburn) and is only transiently relaxed by NO/VIP signalling specifically to permit a swallow, not as part of its resting anti-reflux function. Vomiting is coordinated by the medullary (not pontine) vomiting centre, involves stomach-wall relaxation (not contraction) combined with abdominal/diaphragmatic contraction, and produces hypochloraemic metabolic alkalosis because vomited gastric fluid is HCl-rich, causing net H+/Cl- loss from the body -- the opposite acid-base direction from diarrhoea, which typically loses bicarbonate. The migrating motor complex's housekeeping role (clearing debris and limiting bacterial colonisation of the small intestine) depends on it being a fasting-state phenomenon suppressed by feeding; loss of MMC activity predisposes to small-intestinal bacterial overgrowth.

### Key determinants
Each item in this cluster hinges on a specific reversal a hurried reader might miss: BER sets a ceiling, it does not guarantee contraction; the UES relaxes (not contracts) during the pharyngeal phase; LES tone falls (not rises) in pregnancy; receptive relaxation is a proximal- (not distal-) stomach function; peristalsis pairs contraction behind with relaxation ahead (not contraction on both sides); the MMC is fasting-state (not fed-state) and multiphasic (not a single wave); and vomiting causes alkalosis (not acidosis) with stomach-wall relaxation (not contraction).

### Clinical significance
Failure of pharyngeal-phase UES relaxation produces cricopharyngeal dysphagia; loss of LES tone (surgical, pharmacological, or pregnancy-related) produces gastro-oesophageal reflux disease; impaired receptive relaxation contributes to early satiety and dyspepsia; loss of the migrating motor complex (e.g. in diabetic gastroparesis or after vagotomy) predisposes to small-intestinal bacterial overgrowth; and protracted vomiting (e.g. pyloric stenosis, hyperemesis gravidarum) is a classic cause of hypochloraemic, hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming a GIT slow wave always produces a contraction; assuming the UES stays contracted throughout swallowing because it is normally tonically closed; assuming pregnancy raises LES tone (it lowers it); attributing meal accommodation to antral peristalsis rather than proximal-stomach receptive relaxation; describing peristalsis as contraction on both sides of the bolus; describing the MMC as a single wave triggered by food; and assuming vomiting causes metabolic acidosis rather than alkalosis.
