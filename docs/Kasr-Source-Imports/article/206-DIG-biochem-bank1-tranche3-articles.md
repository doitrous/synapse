Lipases, amino-acid absorption and hepatic detoxification

## summary
Pancreatic lipase is a hydrolase that splits dietary triacylglycerol at the lipid-water interface, while lipoprotein lipase on capillary endothelium clears circulating triacylglycerol, is induced by insulin, requires apo C-II, and when deficient causes type I hyperlipoproteinaemia. Protein digestion yields L-amino acids that are absorbed by sodium-coupled active transport, and one product of amino-acid metabolism, glutathione, is the tripeptide of glutamate, cysteine and glycine. Hepatic biotransformation of xenobiotics runs in two stages: phase I functionalisation (oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis, including hydroxylation) and phase II conjugation (methylation, sulfation, glucuronidation, acetylation, glutathione conjugation).

### Mechanism
Pancreatic lipase, activated by colipase and bile salts, hydrolyses triacylglycerol ester bonds; lipoprotein lipase, activated by apo C-II and induced by insulin, hydrolyses lipoprotein triacylglycerol and is released into plasma by heparin. L-amino acids enter the enterocyte against their gradient on sodium-dependent carriers. Glutathione is assembled from glutamate (gamma-linked), cysteine (the reactive thiol) and glycine. In the liver, phase I enzymes (chiefly cytochrome P450) add or expose a reactive group by oxidation/reduction/hydrolysis, and phase II enzymes then conjugate that group to a polar partner for excretion.
### Key determinants
Each item turns on one discriminator: pancreatic lipase is a hydrolase (not a peptidase/carboxylase/dehydrogenase); lipoprotein lipase is increased by insulin and needs apo C-II; L-amino acids use active transport; glutathione lacks serine; phase I is oxidation/reduction/hydrolysis (methylation is not phase I); and phase II is conjugation (hydroxylation is not phase II).
### Clinical significance
Lipoprotein lipase or apo C-II deficiency produces type I hyperlipoproteinaemia; pancreatic lipase failure causes fat malabsorption and steatorrhoea; defective amino-acid transporters cause aminoacidurias (e.g. Hartnup, cystinuria); glutathione depletion underlies paracetamol hepatotoxicity; and phase I/II capacity governs drug and toxin clearance and drug interactions.
