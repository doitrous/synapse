Carbohydrate digestion and absorption: intestinal transporters and insulin

## summary
Dietary monosaccharides cross the enterocyte by distinct transporters. Glucose and galactose enter the apical membrane against their gradient by SGLT1, a secondary-active, insulin-independent cotransporter powered by the inward sodium gradient; fructose enters by facilitated diffusion through the fructose-specific GLUT5. Both leave the enterocyte basolaterally through GLUT2. In muscle and adipose tissue, by contrast, glucose enters by facilitated diffusion through GLUT4, whose recruitment to the plasma membrane is enhanced by insulin. SGLT1 is confined to intestine and kidney and is not found in muscle or fat.

### Mechanism
SGLT1 couples the uphill movement of glucose (or galactose) to the downhill entry of sodium, whose gradient is maintained by the basolateral Na+/K+-ATPase; this makes intestinal and renal glucose uptake secondary-active and insulin-independent. GLUT5 moves fructose down its concentration gradient with no energy or sodium requirement. GLUT4 in muscle and fat likewise moves glucose down its gradient, but insulin increases the transport rate by translocating GLUT4-laden vesicles to the cell surface.
### Key determinants
Each item turns on one discriminator: SGLT1 is sodium-coupled, insulin-independent and restricted to intestine and kidney (not muscle or fat); fructose absorption is by the facilitative, sodium-independent GLUT5; and muscle glucose uptake is facilitated diffusion through GLUT4 that insulin enhances by recruiting transporters to the membrane rather than by changing the gradient.
### Clinical significance
These transporters underlie glucose-galactose malabsorption (SGLT1 loss), hereditary and dietary fructose handling (GLUT5), and the insulin resistance of type 2 diabetes, where impaired GLUT4 translocation blunts muscle and adipose glucose uptake. Renal SGLT2/SGLT1 inhibition is the basis of the gliflozin class of antidiabetic drugs.
