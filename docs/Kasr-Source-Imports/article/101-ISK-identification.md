## summary
Four of the department's plates show a nucleus, and no two ask the same question. Two are light micrographs where the whole answer is pale against dark; two are electron micrographs where the answer is a proportion — how much of the nucleus is condensed chromatin — and then a list of named parts under coloured arrows. The order to check them in never changes: which microscope, then how pale, then what is arrowed. Getting the order wrong is what turns a nucleus you can see into a mark you did not get.
## sections
### Definition
Every nucleus plate in this module is one of three preparations, and naming the preparation first decides which vocabulary the rest of the answer is allowed to use.

A light micrograph of a stained section shows nuclei as rounded basophilic bodies inside cytoplasm, at a magnification where no internal structure resolves beyond a nucleolus. On such a plate the department's terms are **open-face** (or vesicular) and **closed-face**. These are light-microscopic words. They describe how pale or dark a whole nucleus looks.

An electron micrograph of a nucleus shows the nucleus filling most of the field, bounded by a double line, with the nucleolus as a discrete very dense rounded mass and chromatin distributed as dark material against a pale nuclear sap. On such a plate the department's terms are **euchromatic** and **heterochromatic**. These are electron-microscopic words. They describe the proportion of condensed chromatin.

An electron micrograph taken at higher magnification of the nuclear boundary shows the envelope itself: two parallel unit membranes with a space between them, interrupted where the two fuse. The book prints this as a labelled plate naming an outer nuclear layer and an inner nuclear layer.

At low power, before anything is identified, the practical question is only this: is the field grainy and grey with no colour — an electron micrograph — or is it coloured, with pink cytoplasm and blue nuclei — a light micrograph? Nothing else can be settled until that is.

### Mechanism
One physical fact produces all three appearances: chromatin is either extended or condensed, and the two states scatter light and electrons differently.

Extended chromatin is uncoiled DNA at work. It is spread thinly through the nuclear sap, so it binds little basic dye and stops few electrons. A nucleus full of it is pale in both microscopes, and pallor therefore means the cell is transcribing — an active, protein-forming cell such as a nerve cell. Condensed chromatin is coiled and packed. It binds basic dye heavily and it is electron dense. A nucleus full of it is dark in both microscopes, and it belongs to a cell doing little synthetic work, of which the department's example is the small lymphocyte.

The same fact explains why the two microscopes use different words for the same thing. The light microscope cannot resolve where the condensed chromatin sits, so it can only report the summed effect over the whole nucleus: open-face or closed-face. The electron microscope resolves the distribution, so it can report where the condensed chromatin lies — against the envelope as **peripheral heterochromatin**, free in the sap as a **chromatin island**, or as a shell around the nucleolus as **nucleolus-associated chromatin** — and it names the state by which of the two dominates.

The envelope is a separate matter. It is two unit membranes, each trilaminar in its own right, separated by the **perinuclear space** and interrupted where the two fuse to leave a **nuclear pore**. The outer membrane carries ribosomes and is continuous with rough endoplasmic reticulum, which is why the cytoplasm immediately outside a nucleus so often looks granular.

### Key determinants
Check in this order. Each step closes off the answers the next step is allowed to give.

**1. Which microscope.** Grey and grainy with no colour is EM; coloured is LM. If it is LM, the answer words are open-face and closed-face and nothing else. If it is EM, they are euchromatic and heterochromatic.

**2. On a light micrograph: how pale, and compared with what.** The department's plate at page 27 of the practical book prints both states in one field precisely so the comparison is available: a large pale nucleus beside a small dark one. The pale one is the open-face nucleus and belongs to the more active cell; the small darkly basophilic one is closed-face. If a stem asks which cell is more active, the pale nucleus is the answer.

**3. On an electron micrograph: the proportion, not the presence.** Both nuclei carry every component — peripheral heterochromatin, chromatin islands, nucleolus-associated chromatin, a nucleolus, an envelope and pores. What separates them is how much of the nucleus the condensed material occupies. Mostly dark condensed chromatin with only patches of pale sap is **heterochromatic**, the nucleus of an inactive cell. Mostly pale sap with a discrete dense nucleolus and only a thin rim of condensed chromatin against the envelope is **euchromatic**, the nucleus of an active cell.

**4. Then read the arrows, by position.** The department marks the components with coloured arrows and the answer depends on where each sits, not on what it looks like in isolation:

- condensed chromatin lining the inside of the envelope — **peripheral heterochromatin**
- a clump of condensed chromatin lying free in the sap — **a chromatin island**
- a shell of condensed chromatin around the nucleolus — **nucleolus-associated chromatin**
- the pale background between them — **nuclear sap**, which is euchromatin
- the boundary of the nucleus — **the nuclear membrane**
- a gap in that boundary — **a nuclear pore**
- a discrete very dense rounded mass inside the nucleus — **the nucleolus**

**5. On the envelope plate.** Two parallel membranes with the space between them: outer nuclear membrane, perinuclear space, inner nuclear membrane. Where they fuse and the line breaks, that is a pore. The outer membrane is the one with ribosomes on it and the one continuous with rough ER.

**The single discriminator that decides the commonest confusion** — nucleolus against nuclear membrane — is position, not density. Both are dense. The nucleolus is *inside* the nucleus, discrete, rounded and with no limiting membrane of its own. The nuclear membrane is the *boundary*, a line that goes all the way round. The department arrows both on the same plate for exactly this reason.

### Common misconceptions
**Pallor is a diagnosis, not an artefact.** A student who reads a pale nucleus as a badly stained one has thrown away the only information the plate carries. The paleness is the uncoiled chromatin, and the uncoiled chromatin is the cell's activity.

**"Nucleus" alone is not an answer when the stem says be specific.** The department's stem on both EM plates reads *identify the structure (be specific)*, and it is worded that way because a nucleus named without its chromatin state earns nothing. Say heterochromatic, or say euchromatic.

**The pale middle layer of a membrane is not a space between cells or between membranes.** Each unit membrane is dark–pale–dark in its own right; the pale layer is inside one membrane. The perinuclear space lies between the two membranes of the envelope, and it is a different thing again from the intercellular space between two cells.

**Open-face and euchromatic are not interchangeable on a plate.** They describe the same underlying state but belong to different microscopes, and answering the EM plate with "open-face nucleus" answers a question the examiner did not ask.

### Clinical significance
The chromatin state is the histologist's readout of how hard a cell is working, and it is the reason the same feature is used in pathology to grade cells. A large open-face nucleus with a prominent nucleolus is the picture of high synthetic and proliferative activity; the small closed-face nucleus of a resting lymphocyte is the picture of a cell holding still. Within this module the point is narrower and worth keeping narrow: the department teaches the nerve cell as its example of the active nucleus and the small lymphocyte as its example of the inactive one, and those two examples are what a stem asking for an example expects back.

The nuclear pore matters for the same practical reason. It is the route by which messenger RNA leaves and nuclear proteins enter, so a nucleus with many pores is a nucleus in traffic — which is the euchromatic one.

## summary
Nine of the department's spot plates are the same preparation, and every one of them opens with the same sentence: this is a ..., stained with ... . The marks for that sentence are given before any cell is named, and they are the marks most often left on the table. This article is about the first ten seconds at the microscope — recognising a blood film as a film, naming Leishman's, and then identifying the cell that fills nine tenths of the field, the erythrocyte, by the two features the answer page actually asks for.
## sections
### Definition
A blood film is a drop of blood spread thin on a slide, dried, fixed and stained. The stain is **Leishman's**, a neutral stain: methylene blue, which is basic, and eosin, which is acidic, dissolved together in methyl alcohol. Because it carries both an acid and a basic dye, one preparation colours acidophilic and basophilic structures at once, which is what lets a single film show red cells, all five leucocytes and platelets together.

At low power the field is unmistakable and unlike any section in the module. There is no tissue architecture — no epithelium, no matrix, no fibres, no boundaries of any kind. Instead a single layer of separate rounded pink cells lies spread across the whole field, evenly distributed, not touching in sheets and not embedded in anything. That monolayer of pink discs *is* the diagnosis. Scattered through it, at perhaps one in a hundred, sit larger cells with a visible nucleus; between them lie specks far smaller than any cell.

Three populations, then, and the department's plates use exactly these three:

- **erythrocytes** — the pink discs that make up almost the whole field, with no nucleus
- **leucocytes** — occasional larger cells, each with a stained nucleus
- **platelets** — small non-nucleated fragments, much smaller than a red cell

Recognising the preparation is a separate answer from recognising a cell in it, and it is asked first.

### Mechanism
The appearance follows from what the stain is and from what the red cell is shaped like.

Leishman's neutral stain works because tissue components differ in charge. Haemoglobin is a basic protein and takes up the acidic dye eosin, so the red cell is **acidophilic** — pink. Nucleic acid is acidic and takes up the basic dye methylene blue, so any nucleus in the field is blue-purple. Platelet granules take up the dye enough to look faintly purple. This is why the nucleated cells stand out against a pink background rather than having to be hunted for.

The red cell's central pallor is not a stain effect at all: it is geometry. The mature erythrocyte is a **biconcave disc**, thick at the rim and thin at the centre. Light passes through the thin centre with less haemoglobin in its path, so the middle of each cell is paler than its edge. The pale zone is about **one third of the cell's diameter**, and the constancy of that fraction is what makes it usable as an identifying feature rather than an impression.

Crenation is a second geometric effect. Placed in a hypertonic medium, the cell loses water, shrinks, and its surface throws up regular notches or spikes at the edge. It is the same cell, the same size class, altered by its environment — not a different cell type and not a diseased one.

### Key determinants
Answer in this order. The department's stem is built in this order and the marks are allotted along it.

**1. Name the preparation and the stain, before naming anything in it.** *A blood film, stained with Leishman's stain.* Where a stem adds a third blank — *which is a ... stain* — the answer is that Leishman's is a **neutral** stain. On the one plate where that third blank is answered *supravital*, the preparation is not a Leishman film at all and the whole reading changes; see the article on the reticulocyte and bone marrow.

**2. Say what fills the field.** Erythrocytes. Nearly the whole field is them, and a stem asking which element predominates is asking for that word.

**3. Identify the erythrocyte by its two marking features, in this order:** it is **non-nucleated**, and it has a **central pallor** of about a third of its diameter. Non-nucleated is the character the answer page asks for most often, and it is the one that cannot be argued with. Add the size — about 7.5 micrometres — where the stem asks for a dimension, and note that the red cell is the module's yardstick: everything else on the film is measured against it.

**4. Then read the arrows outward from the red cell.** A structure much smaller than a red cell with no nucleus is a **platelet**. A cell larger than a red cell with a stained nucleus is a **leucocyte**, and which one is settled by its granules and its nucleus. A cell the same size as a red cell but notched and shrunken at its edge is a **crenated red cell**.

**The single discriminator for the commonest confusion on this plate** — central pallor against a nucleus — is *colour, not shape*. A nucleus is stained: it is blue or purple and darker than the cytoplasm around it. A central pallor is unstained: it is paler than the rim around it. Both are round and central; only one is dark. Applied the other way, this is also the discriminator between a red cell and a leucocyte: if you can see a nucleus, it is not a red cell.

**What the same field looks like under a different stain.** Leishman's is applied to a film that has been dried and fixed, so every cell in it is dead and only what is already there is coloured. A supravital stain such as cresyl blue is applied to living cells, and it precipitates ribosomal remnants in the youngest red cells into a visible blue network — a reticulum that a Leishman film never shows, however carefully it is searched. Two preparations, the same blood, different information.

### Normal values
The erythrocyte is about 7.5 micrometres in diameter, and the central pallor about one third of that. Reticulocytes do not exceed one per cent of the red cells in peripheral blood, and are invisible on a Leishman film in any case. Leucocytes are the occasional cells of the field, so a plate showing several nucleated cells close together is a plate the examiner has chosen for them rather than a normal sample of the field.

### Common misconceptions
**The central pallor is not a hole, a vacuole or a nucleus.** It is thinning. The mature red cell has extruded its nucleus during development and has none at all, and that absence is the character the examiner is asking about.

**A crenated red cell is not a different cell.** It is a normal erythrocyte shrunken in a hypertonic medium, and the department arrows it on the same plate as a normal one so the two can be told apart in one field.

**"Blood film" is not a complete answer to the opening stem.** The stem has two blanks, sometimes three, and each carries a mark. Naming the preparation without naming the stain answers half a question.

**Neutral does not mean weak.** Leishman's is called neutral because it contains both an acidic and a basic dye, not because it stains faintly.

### Clinical significance
The film is the oldest and still the most informative test in haematology, and everything a clinician reads from it is read in the terms this article uses. Red cell size, the width of the central pallor and the shape of the cell are the whole of the morphological description: a pallor wider than a third of the diameter is hypochromia, which points to iron deficiency, and cells smaller than 7.5 micrometres are microcytes. Because bilharziasis and hookworm are common causes of chronic blood loss in Egypt, this is not an abstract exercise in a Cairo first year.

Crenation matters in the opposite direction: it is usually an artefact of preparation or of a hypertonic medium rather than a finding about the patient, and a student who reports it as disease has misread a slide fault as a diagnosis.

## summary
The department puts a lymphocyte and a monocyte on the same plate, arrows both, and asks for a character of each. It does that because these are the two cells students swap. Size will not separate them reliably, because a large lymphocyte is as big as a small monocyte; nuclear pallor will not, because both can look pale and indented. One feature separates them every time, and it is the one students look at last: the quality of the cytoplasm.
## sections
### Definition
Non-granular leucocytes are the white cells with no specific granules — the lymphocyte and the monocyte. Both appear on a routine Leishman film, and on the department's plates both are arrowed in the same field.

Before either is named the field has to be read as a blood film: a monolayer of pink non-nucleated discs with occasional larger nucleated cells among them. The nucleated cells are the leucocytes, and the first cut is between those with visible specific granules and those without. A cell whose cytoplasm carries obvious granules — fine and pale, coarse and pink, or coarse and dark — is a granulocyte and is out of this comparison. What is left is a cell whose cytoplasm looks smooth or evenly textured, and that is one of the two here.

The **small lymphocyte** is the smaller cell. Its nucleus is dark, rounded and heterochromatic, and it fills almost the whole cell, leaving a thin rim of pale basophilic cytoplasm around it.

The **monocyte** is the largest leucocyte on the film, thirteen to twenty micrometres across. Its nucleus is large, pale and **kidney-shaped** or indented, and it sits eccentrically. Its cytoplasm is abundant and has a **frosted-glass** appearance, produced by fine azurophilic granules too small to be resolved individually.

### Mechanism
Both appearances follow from what the cell is doing.

The small lymphocyte is a resting cell in transit. Its chromatin is condensed, which is why the nucleus is dark and why the department's own example of a closed-face nucleus is this cell. It carries very little cytoplasm because it is not synthesising or storing anything at that moment; when it is activated it enlarges, its chromatin opens, and it acquires cytoplasm that is **deeply basophilic** because the cell has filled with ribosomes for antibody-directed protein synthesis. That is the large lymphocyte, and it is where the confusion starts.

The monocyte is a macrophage in transit. It spends about three days in the blood before entering connective tissue and becoming a macrophage, and it is already equipped for the job: its azurophilic granules are lysosomes, and there are many of them, each far below the resolution of the light microscope. What the eye receives is not granularity but an even fine haze over the whole cytoplasm — the frosted-glass look. Its nucleus is indented because the cell is motile and amoeboid rather than because of any staining property.

Leishman's stain shows both because it carries a basic and an acidic dye at once, and because the azure component — oxidised methylene blue — is what the monocyte's azurophilic granules have an affinity for. The word azurophilic names that affinity, not a colour that identifies a cell.

### Key determinants
Check in this order. Steps one and two narrow the field; step three settles it.

**1. Size, against the red cells around it.** The red cell is 7.5 micrometres and is the ruler on every plate. A leucocyte barely larger than a red cell is a small lymphocyte. A leucocyte two to three red cells wide is a monocyte — or a large lymphocyte, which is why this step narrows rather than decides.

**2. The nucleus.** Dark, rounded and filling the cell is a small lymphocyte and you are already finished. Pale and indented does not finish anything: go to step three. Where the nucleus is pale, note whether the indentation is a definite **kidney shape** with an eccentric position, which favours the monocyte, or a shallower notch.

**3. The cytoplasm — this is the discriminator.** Two possibilities and only two:

- **deeply basophilic**, evenly blue, no texture — a **large lymphocyte**
- **frosted glass**, pale grey-blue with a fine even haze over it — a **monocyte**

Nothing else needs to be checked. A student who reaches step three and reads the cytoplasm correctly cannot get this pair wrong, and a student who stops at step one or two frequently does.

**The single discriminator, stated as the department states it:** the cytoplasm is deeply basophilic in the large lymphocyte and frosted-glass in the monocyte. When the answer page asks for the monocyte with two characters, those characters are *kidney-shaped nucleus* and *frosted-glass cytoplasm*; when it asks for the lymphocyte with two, they are *dark nucleus* and *thin rim of cytoplasm*.

**What the stem will look like.** The department's wording on this plate is *blue arrow points to ..., characterized by ... nucleus and the cytoplasm shows ... appearance*, with a second arrow on the lymphocyte and a third on a red cell. The three blanks are worth answering in the order printed: cell, nucleus, cytoplasm.

### Normal values
Lymphocytes are the second commonest leucocyte and monocytes the least common of the two here; the small lymphocyte is six to eight micrometres and the monocyte thirteen to twenty. The monocyte is the largest leucocyte in the blood, which is a useful thing to hold, and the megakaryocyte at fifty to seventy micrometres — a marrow cell, not a blood cell — is not a counter-example because it never appears on a film.

Because the monocyte is uncommon on a normal film, a plate showing one has been chosen for it. That is a reason to expect the question, not a reason to doubt the identification.

### Common misconceptions
**A large lymphocyte is not a monocyte.** It has the two features that make students reach for monocyte — a pale indented nucleus and plenty of cytoplasm — and it is the trap this plate is built around. Its cytoplasm is deeply basophilic, not frosted-glass, and its nucleus is notched rather than frankly kidney-shaped.

**Frosted glass is not granularity.** A cell with granules you can count is a granulocyte. The monocyte's granules are below resolution; what you see is an even haze, and describing it as granular puts the cell in the wrong class.

**Azurophilic is an affinity, not a diagnosis.** All the leucocytes have azurophilic granules. Only in the monocyte are they numerous and fine enough to produce the frosted-glass effect, and only there is that effect the identifying feature.

**Size is a hint, not an answer.** The overlap between a large lymphocyte and a monocyte is exactly where the plate is set.

### Clinical significance
The two cells move in opposite clinical directions and the differential count is where that is read. Lymphocytosis accompanies chronic infection and viral illness; the monocyte's rise accompanies chronic infection too, and in Egypt the chronic infections that matter are common ones. What makes the morphological discrimination clinically real rather than academic is that a laboratory differential count is produced by a human being naming these cells one at a time down a microscope, and a count is only as good as the discrimination in this article.

The monocyte's destination is the other half of its clinical meaning. It leaves the blood after about three days and becomes the tissue macrophage — the same cell described in the connective tissue articles, with a different name because of where it now lives.

## summary
Two of the department's blood plates are traps because they open with the same sentence as the nine that are ordinary Leishman films, and the sentence has a different answer. One is a tissue section — bone marrow, crowded and unlike any film — and the stem asks first for the tissue. The other looks like a film, and is one, but was stained on living cells with cresyl blue, and the third blank in the stem wants the word supravital. Both are settled before any cell in them is named, and both are lost by answering out of habit.
## sections
### Definition
Every other blood plate in this module is a dried, fixed film stained with Leishman's. These two are not, and each announces itself at low power in a way that is worth learning as a shape rather than as a list.

**Bone marrow** is a *section of a tissue*, not a smear. At low power there is no monolayer of separate cells: the field is crowded, cellular and dense, packed with developing blood cells of many sizes lying against one another, with wide irregular blood spaces — sinusoids — running through it, and scattered through the crowd large rounded cells that look empty. Nothing about that field resembles a blood film, and the department's stem accordingly opens *identify the tissue* rather than *this is a ..., stained with ...*.

Within that field the **megakaryocyte** is unmistakable once you know to look for size. It is fifty to seventy micrometres across — several times any other cell in the marrow — with one very large, dark, deeply lobed nucleus and abundant basophilic cytoplasm from which platelets detach at the margins. The large empty-looking cells are **fat cells**, or adipocytes: their lipid dissolved out during processing, leaving a clear space with a thin rim of cytoplasm and a flattened nucleus pushed to one edge.

**The supravital preparation** looks like a blood film, because it is one, and this is what makes it dangerous. The field is a monolayer of red cells as usual. What is different is the colour — the cells take a blue-green rather than a pink cast, because cresyl blue has been applied to living cells and no eosin is present — and the presence, in about one cell in a hundred, of a fine blue **reticulum**, a network of granules and threads inside an otherwise ordinary red cell. That cell is a **reticulocyte**.

### Mechanism
Each appearance is produced by something the preparation does rather than by the cells being different.

**Why marrow looks crowded.** Marrow is the site of haemopoiesis, so what fills the field is a production line at every stage at once, held in a stroma rather than spread out. The sinusoids are the wide thin-walled vessels through which finished cells enter the circulation, which is why they are irregular and why they run through the crowd rather than round it. The fat cells are stroma, and they are large because a lipid droplet is large; the lipid is dissolved by the solvents of routine processing, so what is left on the slide is the space it occupied. That is why an adipocyte on an H&E section looks empty — an artefact of processing that has become an identifying feature.

**Why the megakaryocyte has one nucleus and not several.** It undergoes repeated nuclear division without cytoplasmic division, so the DNA multiplies inside a single nuclear envelope and the nucleus becomes very large and deeply lobed. The lobes are connected. This is a single multilobed nucleus, and the cell is not multinucleated. Its cytoplasm then fragments at its margins, and the fragments are platelets — which is the whole reason a first-year sees this cell at all.

**Why the reticulum needs a living cell.** The reticulocyte is the last stage before the mature erythrocyte. It has lost its nucleus but still holds remnants of ribosomes and polysomes. These are dispersed in the living cell and invisible; a **supravital** stain — one applied to cells that are still alive, of which the department's example is **cresyl blue** — precipitates them into an aggregated network large enough to see. Fix and dry the film first, as Leishman's requires, and the material is no longer precipitable in that way: a Leishman film shows no reticulum in any cell, however hard it is searched. The reticulum is not a structure that is hidden by the wrong stain; it is a structure the right stain creates from dispersed material in a living cell.

### Key determinants
**For a crowded cellular field with no monolayer — check in this order.**

1. **Is it a smear or a section?** Separate cells lying in one plane is a smear. Cells packed against each other with vessels running through is a section. This decides which answer to the opening stem is even available.
2. **Name the tissue: bone marrow.** The department asks for this first and it carries its own mark.
3. **Then take the arrows by size.** The very large cell with one big dark multilobed nucleus is the **megakaryocyte**. The large cells that look empty, with a thin rim of cytoplasm and a peripheral flattened nucleus, are **fat cells**, or adipocytes. Those are the two things the department arrows.

**For a monolayer of red cells — check in this order.**

1. **What colour is the field?** Pink cells with purple nuclei is Leishman's. A blue-green cast with no pink is a supravital preparation.
2. **Complete all three blanks.** *This is a blood film, stained with cresyl blue, which is a supravital stain.* Where a stem gives three blanks, the third is asking for the word supravital, and it is a separate mark.
3. **Name the marked cell.** A red cell containing a fine blue network is a **reticulocyte**.

**The single discriminator for the marrow plate** — megakaryocyte against anything else large — is *one nucleus, many lobes, all connected*. An osteoclast, which is the other very large cell a student may reach for, is genuinely multinucleated: separate nuclei, separate envelopes. And the marrow stem asks for the tissue first, which osteoclasts would not have identified anyway.

**The single discriminator for the supravital plate** is *the stain, not the cell*. The reticulum can only be there if the stain was supravital, so the question about the preparation and the question about the cell answer each other. If you have written Leishman's, you cannot then write reticulocyte.

**What the same blood looks like under the other stain.** A Leishman film of the very same patient shows reticulocytes — up to one in a hundred red cells are present — as perfectly ordinary erythrocytes, indistinguishable from the rest. Nothing is missing from the film; the information simply is not recoverable from it.

### Normal values
Reticulocytes do not exceed one per cent of the red cells in peripheral blood, so the marked cell on the plate has been sought out. The megakaryocyte is fifty to seventy micrometres in diameter, several times the size of anything else in the marrow field and roughly seven to nine times the diameter of a red cell.
### Common misconceptions
**A megakaryocyte is not multinucleated.** The lobes belong to one nucleus. Writing "multinucleated cell" describes an osteoclast, which is a different cell in a different tissue.

**The fat cell is not empty.** It held a lipid droplet that was dissolved out in processing, and the thin rim of cytoplasm with its flattened peripheral nucleus is the remaining evidence of the cell.

**"Largest" is ambiguous in marrow and should be avoided as an answer.** The departmental teaching describes fat cells as the largest cells of the marrow stroma; the megakaryocyte is named by its measured diameter of fifty to seventy micrometres and by its single multilobed nucleus, not by being the largest cell present. Identify it by the nucleus and the number, and the ambiguity never arises.

**A supravital preparation is not a special microscope or a special film.** It is an ordinary film of blood stained while the cells were alive. What is unusual is the timing of the stain, and that is what the word means.

### Clinical significance
Both plates matter clinically for the same reason: they are the two places a clinician looks when the peripheral film raises a question it cannot answer.

The reticulocyte count is the standard measure of how fast the marrow is making red cells. It rises after haemorrhage and in haemolysis, where the marrow is working; it stays low in marrow failure and in untreated iron deficiency, where the marrow cannot. Because the count requires a supravital stain, ordering the right preparation is part of asking the right question — the reason a first-year is taught that the reticulum exists only in a living cell is that the laboratory request depends on it.

Bone marrow examination is the investigation of last resort in a blood disorder, and the megakaryocyte is one of the cells specifically counted: platelets come from it, so a low platelet count with plentiful megakaryocytes means the platelets are being destroyed in the periphery, while a low count with few megakaryocytes means they are not being made.

## summary
Three fibres, and only one useful question about each: does it run in bundles, does it branch, and does it show at all in the stain you are looking at. Colour answers none of these in an H&E section, where collagen and elastic fibres are both pink and reticular fibres are invisible. This article gives the two characters the department's answer pages actually accept for each fibre, in the order that separates them, and the one thing that is true of reticular fibres and of nothing else in the module.
## sections
### Definition
The connective tissue matrix carries three fibres. All three are protein, all three are laid down by fibroblasts, and the whole of the practical skill is telling them apart in a field where two of them lie side by side.

**Collagen fibres** run as **thick, wavy bundles** of condensed fibres. They do **not branch**. Individual fibres are gathered into bundles, and a bundle keeps its thickness along its course. In the fresh, unstained state collagen is **white**, which is why the tissues built mainly of it are called white fibrous.

**Elastic fibres** run **singly** — never gathered into bundles — and are **thin**, with a **zigzag** or wavy course, and they **branch** and rejoin. In the fresh state elastic tissue is **yellow**, which is why the tissue built mainly of them is called yellow elastic.

**Reticular fibres** are type III collagen with a high sugar content. They are **thin**, they **branch and anastomose** into a fine network, and they are **invisible in H&E**. They exist on a slide only when a stain has been chosen to show them.

At low power, before any arrow is read, a loose areolar section shows a pale open field with pink fibrous material running through it in two obviously different ways: some of it thick and gathered, some of it fine and scattered. Those are collagen and elastic respectively, and the department arrows one of each on the same plate. A silver preparation looks nothing like it: a brown web on a pale ground, with no pink anywhere.

### Mechanism
Each appearance follows from the chemistry of the fibre and the chemistry of the stain, and the two must be kept separate in the mind or the traps in this topic are unavoidable.

**Why collagen bundles and elastic does not.** Collagen is laid down as fibrils that aggregate into fibres and fibres into bundles, and the resulting cable resists tension along its length — hence its arrangement into parallel bundles in tendon. Elastin is laid down as a branching network in which each fibre must be able to recoil independently, so gathering it into bundles would defeat it. The bundling is therefore not a staining artefact but the mechanical design of each fibre, which is why it is reliable as an identifying feature and why the department accepts *condensed* and *bundles* as characters of collagen and *singly*, *thin* and *zigzag* as characters of elastic.

**Why both are pink in H&E.** Both are acidophilic proteins, so both take up eosin. Eosin is not selective between them. This is the single most important fact about this topic for exam purposes: **in a stained section, colour does not discriminate collagen from elastic**, and a student who tries to use it will guess.

**What the selective stains do.** Orcein has an affinity for elastin and stains elastic fibres **brown**, leaving collagen essentially unstained; the department's own plate of elastic connective tissue is an orcein preparation and the answer page says *orcein (stains brown)*. Silver impregnation deposits metallic silver on reticular fibres and stains them **brown**; PAS stains them **red**, because of the sugar they carry. Neither silver nor PAS is a colour test: both are ways of making visible a fibre that H&E leaves blank.

**Why fresh colour is a separate question.** White for collagen and yellow for elastic are the colours of the unstained tissue in the fresh state — what a dissector sees, not what a microscopist sees. The department asks for them explicitly on the connective tissue plates, and they are the only place colour is a legitimate answer.

### Key determinants
**When two fibres lie in the same pink field, check in this order.**

1. **Bundled or single?** Thick and gathered into a bundle is collagen. Thin and running alone is elastic. This alone settles most plates.
2. **Wavy or zigzag?** Collagen bundles are wavy — broad gentle curves. Elastic fibres are zigzag — sharper, finer changes of direction.
3. **Does it branch?** Collagen does not. Elastic does.

**When the field is brown on a pale ground, check in this order.**

1. **Is there a network, or are there separate fibres?** A fine web whose fibres branch *and rejoin one another* is reticular. Separate brown fibres running individually in a zigzag, not rejoining, are elastic on orcein.
2. **Name the stain by what it was for.** Brown fibres forming an anastomosing network — **silver**. Brown fibres running singly — **orcein**.
3. **Give the two characters the answer page asks for.** For reticular fibres: *brown*, *thin*, *branching and anastomosing* — the department accepts these.

**The single discriminator for each pair.**

- Collagen against elastic: **bundling**. Collagen is bundled and does not branch; elastic runs singly and branches. Never colour, in a stained section.
- Elastic against reticular, both brown: **anastomosis**. A brown fibre that branches and *rejoins* to form a net is reticular; a brown fibre that runs singly in a zigzag is elastic. Colour is identical, and colour is therefore no help.
- Collagen against reticular: both are collagen chemically, but reticular fibres are type III, thin, networked, and **not visible in H&E at all**. If you can see it in H&E and it is thick, it is not reticular.

**What the same tissue looks like under a different stain.** This is the point the department returns to on plate after plate, and it is worth holding as a table in the head:

- **H&E** — collagen pink, elastic pink, reticular invisible
- **Orcein** — elastic brown, collagen essentially unstained
- **Silver** — reticular brown, forming a net
- **PAS** — reticular red
- **Fresh, unstained** — collagen white, elastic yellow

A single tissue looks like a different tissue in each of these, and the examiner's question about the stain is always answerable from what is visible: if you can see a net, silver did it.

### Common misconceptions
**Colour is not the discriminator in a stained section.** Both collagen and elastic are acidophilic and both are pink in H&E. Students who have learned "collagen is white and elastic is yellow" reach for those words at the microscope, where they are wrong: white and yellow are fresh-state colours.

**Brown does not mean elastic.** Orcein gives brown and silver gives brown. Two selective stains, two different fibres, one colour. What separates them on the slide is that reticular fibres anastomose into a network and elastic fibres do not.

**Reticular fibres are not absent from H&E sections; they are invisible in them.** They are present in the tissue — they are the stroma of spleen, lymph node and liver — and they simply take no dye. Answering "there are no reticular fibres in this section" is a different claim from "they cannot be seen".

**Branching is not the same as bundling.** Elastic fibres branch and are still single fibres. Collagen fibres are gathered together and are still unbranched. A student who conflates the two ends up describing a bundle as a branch.

### Clinical significance
The three fibres fail in three characteristic ways, and each failure is recognisable because of the properties this article uses to identify them.

Collagen's tensile strength is what a scar is made of, and defective collagen makes tissues that tear — the basis of the inherited disorders of collagen, and the reason vitamin C deficiency, which blocks collagen synthesis, produces bleeding gums and poor wound healing. Elastic fibre loss is why skin sags and why elastic arteries dilate; where elastin is abnormal from birth the aorta is the tissue that shows it. Reticular fibres are the scaffold on which the lymphoid organs and the liver are built, which is why silver staining is a pathologist's routine for seeing whether that architecture has survived a disease process — the collapse of a reticulin framework being visible when the cells that filled it are not.

## summary
Six plates, one stem: identify the tissue, be specific. Every one of them is connective tissue, so the word connective earns nothing and the mark is entirely in the adjective. The decision is made at low power and made in one order — how much of the field is matrix, then which fibre dominates, then how that fibre is arranged. This article is that order, with the fresh-state colour and the stain each plate then asks for, and the four discriminations that separate the types students actually swap.
## sections
### Definition
Connective tissue proper is matrix, fibres and cells in varying proportions, and the five types the department sets are five different proportions of the same three things. Each has a low-power appearance that is recognisable before any arrow is read.

**Loose areolar connective tissue** — the commonest type. An open, pale field. Both fibre types are present and both are loose: thick pink collagen bundles and thin single zigzag elastic fibres run through an abundant matrix without filling it, with fibroblast nuclei scattered between them and open spaces — the areolae — holding tissue fluid. Nothing is packed; you can see between the fibres everywhere.

**Regular white fibrous connective tissue** — a dense field. Thick acidophilic collagen bundles run **parallel**, filling the field, with few cells; fibroblast nuclei lie in **rows** between the bundles. White in the fresh state.

**Irregular white fibrous connective tissue** — the same dense thick collagen bundles and the same few cells, but the bundles run **in every direction** and the fibroblasts are scattered among them rather than in rows. White in the fresh state.

**Yellow elastic connective tissue** — a dense field in which the predominating fibres are **thin, single and zigzag** rather than bundled, running broadly parallel. **Yellow** in the fresh state; **orcein** stains it **brown**.

**Reticular connective tissue** — a field with no pink fibres at all, because it is shown by **silver**: a fine **brown network** of thin fibres that branch and anastomose, with reticular cells along it. It is the stroma of spleen, lymph node and liver rather than a tissue that stands alone.

**Mucoid connective tissue** — a pale, jelly-like field in which the **ground substance predominates** over everything else. Few scattered fibroblasts, delicate fibres running through it, and matrix filling the space between them. It is Wharton's jelly of the umbilical cord, the vitreous humour, and the pulp of a tooth.

### Mechanism
Each type is a mechanical solution, and the arrangement you see is the solution.

Where tissue must be filled and kept flexible — under skin, around vessels, between organs — the answer is **loose areolar**: enough collagen for strength, enough elastic for recoil, and abundant matrix in between to hold fluid and let cells move. Its openness is functional, not incidental, and the areolae are what a swelling fills.

Where a pull comes always from one direction, as in a tendon or a ligament, collagen bundles are laid down **parallel to that pull**, and fibroblasts are compressed into rows between them: **regular white fibrous** tissue. Where pulls come from many directions, as in a dermis or an organ capsule, the same bundles are laid down **in all directions** and the fibroblasts are scattered: **irregular white fibrous** tissue. The two are the same material solving two different problems, which is precisely why the department asks a student to be specific about which one is on the slide.

Where tissue must stretch and recoil repeatedly — the ligamentum flavum between vertebrae, the ligamentum nuchae of the neck — elastin predominates and the fibres run singly so each can recoil independently: **yellow elastic** tissue.

Where a soft organ must be held open by a scaffold that does not obstruct it, the answer is a fine anastomosing network of **reticular** fibres, and the tissue is a stroma. Where the requirement is to resist compression and cushion — the umbilical cord, the vitreous — the answer is ground substance itself, rich in hyaluronic acid, which binds water and behaves as a gel: **mucoid** tissue.

The fresh-state colours follow from the fibre, not from the tissue. Collagen is white and elastin is yellow, so tissues built mainly of collagen are white in the fresh state and the tissue built mainly of elastin is yellow. This is why the department asks the fresh colour on both plates: the answer is the difference between them.

### Key determinants
**The decision, in order, at low power.**

**1. Is the field brown on a pale ground, with no pink anywhere?** Then it is a silver preparation and the tissue is **reticular connective tissue**. Say the stain in the same breath — the stem asks for the type *and* the stain — and give the characters: brown, thin, branching and anastomosing.

**2. Does matrix fill the field, with only a few scattered cells and delicate fibres in it?** Then it is **mucoid connective tissue**. Name a site: Wharton's jelly of the umbilical cord, the vitreous humour, or the pulp of a tooth.

**3. Is the field open, with both fibre types visible and loose, and cells scattered between them?** Then it is **loose areolar connective tissue**. The department will then arrow the collagen bundle, the elastic fibre, a fibroblast nucleus and the matrix; be ready for all four.

**4. Is the field dense and full of fibres? Then ask which fibre.**

- **Thick bundles, acidophilic** — white fibrous. Now ask **how they are arranged**: parallel with fibroblast nuclei in rows is **regular**; running in every direction with fibroblasts scattered is **irregular**. Fresh colour: **white**. Two characters: thick bundles and parallel, or thick bundles and irregularly arranged.
- **Thin single zigzag fibres, not bundled** — **yellow elastic**. Fresh colour: **yellow**. Stain: **orcein**, which gives brown. Two characters: singly and thin, or thin and zigzag.

**The four discriminations students actually get wrong.**

- **Regular against irregular white fibrous** — the discriminator is **the direction of the bundles**, and nothing else. Same fibre, same thickness, same colour, same fresh colour, same cells. Parallel or not parallel.
- **Yellow elastic against white fibrous** — the discriminator is **bundled or single**. Both are dense fields of pink fibres. Collagen is gathered into thick bundles; elastic fibres run singly and thin. The fresh colours then follow, and they are opposite.
- **Mucoid against loose areolar** — the discriminator is **what fills the field**. Both are pale and open. In loose areolar tissue there are two visible fibre populations and many cells; in mucoid tissue the matrix is what you are mostly looking at.
- **Reticular tissue against reticular fibre** — the discriminator is **what the stem asked for**. On one plate the department asks for the type of connective tissue and the answer is reticular connective tissue; on another it points an arrow at a fibre and the answer is reticular fibres. Both answers use the same word for different things, and reading the stem is the whole of the skill.

**What the same tissue looks like under a different stain.** Reticular tissue in H&E shows nothing of its framework — the fibres take no dye — which is why the department only ever shows it on silver. Yellow elastic tissue in H&E shows pink fibres that must be identified by shape; on orcein the same fibres are brown and unmistakable, and the collagen beside them disappears. White fibrous tissue is pink on H&E and is not improved by any special stain, which is why the department never asks for one on those plates.

### Normal values
The department's plates do not measure anything on these sections, and no dimension is asked for. The quantities that matter are proportions and they are judged by eye: matrix abundant in loose areolar and dominant in mucoid; fibres dominant and cells few in both white fibrous types and in yellow elastic.

### Common misconceptions
**"Connective tissue" is not an answer.** Every plate in this block is connective tissue. The stem says be specific because the mark is on the adjective, and regular and irregular are marked separately from each other.

**White is not the default fresh colour.** It is the fresh colour of the collagen-based tissues. Yellow elastic tissue is yellow in the fresh state, and the department asks the fresh colour on both plates precisely so that the two cannot be answered alike.

**A pale open field is not automatically loose areolar.** Mucoid tissue is pale and open too. Count what is there: two fibre populations and many cells is areolar; matrix filling the field is mucoid.

**Reticular connective tissue is a stroma, not a free-standing organ tissue.** It is what holds spleen, lymph node and liver together, and a section of it is a section of one of those organs treated to show its framework.

**The tissue and the fibre are not the same answer.** Reticular fibres are a component; reticular connective tissue is a tissue built of them with reticular cells. Which one the examiner wants is written in the stem.

### Clinical significance
These five arrangements are where a surgeon operates and where several common problems live.

Loose areolar tissue is the plane a surgeon separates along and the space an oedema or an infection spreads through, because its areolae hold fluid — cellulitis is the clinical name for that spread. Regular white fibrous tissue is tendon and ligament, and its parallel bundles are why a tendon tears along its length and heals slowly, being poorly cellular and poorly vascular. Irregular white fibrous tissue is dermis and organ capsule, and its multidirectional bundles are what a scar attempts and fails to reproduce. Yellow elastic tissue keeps the ligamentum flavum taut and the great arteries recoiling; its loss with age is why arteries stiffen. Reticular tissue's silver-stained framework is what a pathologist reads to decide whether the architecture of a lymph node has been destroyed. And mucoid tissue is Wharton's jelly, which is what keeps the umbilical vessels from kinking — its deficiency being a recognised cause of cord compression.
## hold_these
Every plate in this block is connective tissue, so the mark is entirely in the adjective; the department's stem says be specific for that reason.
Loose areolar tissue has both fibre types running loosely in abundant matrix, with scattered fibroblasts and open areolae.
Regular and irregular white fibrous tissue differ only in the direction of the same thick collagen bundles: parallel with fibroblasts in rows, or every direction with fibroblasts scattered.
Both white fibrous types are white in the fresh state; yellow elastic connective tissue is yellow in the fresh state and is stained brown by orcein.
Yellow elastic tissue is a dense field of thin, single, zigzag fibres — not bundles.
Reticular connective tissue is shown by silver as a fine brown network of thin fibres that branch and anastomose, and it is the stroma of spleen, lymph node and liver.
Mucoid connective tissue is recognised by ground substance predominating over cells and fibres, and its sites are Wharton's jelly, the vitreous humour and the pulp of a tooth.
The discriminator between mucoid and loose areolar tissue is what fills the field: matrix in mucoid, two fibre populations and many cells in areolar.
The stem decides whether reticular means the tissue or the fibre.
## lose_the_mark
Answering "connective tissue" and stopping. The stem says be specific and the adjective is where the mark is.
Answering white for the fresh colour of yellow elastic tissue out of habit. White belongs to the collagen-based types.
Naming white fibrous connective tissue without saying regular or irregular. They are marked separately and the arrangement of the bundles is the whole difference.
Calling a dense field of thin zigzag fibres white fibrous because it is pink. Bundled means collagen; single and thin means elastic.
Reading a pale open field as loose areolar without checking what fills it. Mucoid tissue is pale and open and its matrix dominates.
Answering "reticular fibres" when the stem asked for the type of connective tissue, or the reverse. The same word answers two different questions on two different plates.
Naming orcein on the reticular plate. Orcein is for elastic fibres; silver is what shows a reticular network.
Giving one visible character when the stem asks for two.

## evidence_gaps
No image exists in the repository for any of these six plates. Every identification in this article is a low-power judgement about what fills a field, and that is the single thing a written description is least able to supply; the article cannot fully do its job until the plates above exist.
The mucoid connective tissue plate is printed in the practical book as a titled teaching plate with no marked question and no model answer. What the examiner would accept as a visible character for it is therefore taken from the caption and from Prof. Dalia El Marakby's Connective tissue handout, not from a marked answer page, and it is the least certain identification in this article.
The practical book does not state a stain for its loose areolar or white fibrous plates and the slide catalogue records none. H&E is named in the image requests as sourcing direction to a fulfiller, not as taught content; a student should not answer a stain question on those plates from this article.
Fibroblast nuclei lying in rows in regular fibrous tissue, as against scattered in the irregular form, is drawn from the departmental handout. The department's own answer pages give only the arrangement of the bundles, so the row-versus-scatter character has not been confirmed against a model answer.
The mechanical explanations in Mechanism and the whole of the clinical section are general histology and pathology rather than statements from the department's sources, and none of it is examinable from these materials.
No independent citation has been attached to any statement in this article.

## notes
Five concepts are grouped into one article because they are five answers to one question. The department's stem on every one of these plates is identify the tissue, be specific, and a student does not know in advance which of the five is under the microscope; the skill is the ordered elimination, not five separate recognitions. Splitting them would give a student five articles and no way to choose between them, which is exactly the position they are in at the microscope.

Adipose tissue is set on the same block of plates and is deliberately not covered here: it is already taught elsewhere in the library and is not on the untaught list this batch exists to close.
