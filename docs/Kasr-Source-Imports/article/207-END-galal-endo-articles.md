## title
Anatomy of the thyroid gland and thyroidectomy relations
## subject
endo
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Anatomy
## summary
The thyroid gland is enclosed by the pretracheal fascia, which binds it to the larynx so that it (and thyroid swellings) rise with swallowing. It is drained by three paired veins (superior, middle and inferior thyroid), the inferior veins lying in the infra-isthmic midline where a low tracheostomy endangers them. Behind the gland the recurrent laryngeal nerve accompanies the inferior thyroid artery, so the artery is ligated away from the gland to protect the nerve, whose injury causes hoarseness. The gland develops from the endodermal floor of the pharynx at the foramen cecum, and a persistent thyroglossal duct forms a midline cyst that rises on tongue protrusion.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the thyroid gross-anatomy, surgical and developmental facts tested by the Dr Galal endocrine bank: venous drainage by three paired veins, mobility on swallowing, recurrent-laryngeal-nerve hoarseness, midline tracheostomy bleeding from the inferior thyroid veins, safe ligation of the inferior thyroid artery, the endodermal (floor-of-pharynx) origin of the gland, and the thyroglossal cyst.
### Mechanism
The mobility on swallowing follows from the pretracheal fascia and Berry ligament tethering the gland to the larynx; the surgical rules follow from the constant relation of the recurrent laryngeal nerve to the inferior thyroid artery behind the gland; the developmental facts follow from the median thyroid diverticulum descending from the tongue base along the thyroglossal duct.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: three paired veins versus a single vein; recurrent laryngeal (hoarseness) versus external laryngeal (pitch) injury; inferior thyroid artery tied far from the gland versus superior artery tied close; endodermal versus mesodermal follicles; and a thyroglossal (midline, moves with tongue) versus a branchial (lateral) cyst.
### Clinical significance
These points underlie thyroidectomy technique (nerve preservation, arterial ligation), the emergency-tracheostomy approach, and the clinical recognition of thyroglossal cysts.

## title
Anatomy and development of the pancreas
## subject
endo
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Anatomy
## summary
The pancreas is retroperitoneal except for its mobile tail. The splenic artery runs along the upper border of the body while the splenic vein lies behind it, joining the superior mesenteric vein behind the neck to form the portal vein. The superior mesenteric vessels pass in front of the uncinate process, which is supplied mainly from the superior mesenteric artery, and the head is crossed anteriorly by the transverse colon. Pancreatic lymph drains to pancreaticosplenic, coeliac and superior mesenteric (not inferior mesenteric) nodes. Developmentally the main duct forms from the distal dorsal duct and the whole ventral duct, and an annular pancreas obstructs the second part of the duodenum.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the pancreatic facts tested by the Dr Galal endocrine bank: the superior mesenteric vessels anterior to the uncinate process, the splenic artery (not vein) along the upper border, the SMA supply of the uncinate, the transverse-colon relation of the head, pancreatic lymphatic drainage, the formation of the main duct, and the duodenal obstruction of annular pancreas.
### Mechanism
The vascular relations follow from the uncinate hooking behind the superior mesenteric vessels and the splenic vessels running along/behind the body; the ductal and annular-pancreas facts follow from the fusion and migration of the dorsal and ventral pancreatic buds during development.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: superior mesenteric vessels anterior (not posterior) to the uncinate; splenic artery (upper border) versus splenic vein (behind body); SMA versus coeliac supply of the uncinate; anterior (transverse colon) versus posterior (IVC) relations of the head; superior mesenteric nodes yes but inferior mesenteric no; and the second part of the duodenum as the annular-pancreas obstruction site.
### Clinical significance
These relations underlie the resectability of uncinate/head tumours, the interpretation of obstructive jaundice and duodenal obstruction, and the pattern of pancreatic lymphatic spread.

## title
Anatomy and zonation of the suprarenal glands
## subject
endo
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Anatomy
## summary
The left suprarenal gland is semilunar and larger than the right, extending down the medial border of the left kidney to reach the renal hilum, and is related anteriorly to the stomach bed and pancreas. The cortex is arranged in three zones from the capsule inward - glomerulosa, fasciculata and reticularis - overlying the medulla. The cortex is mesodermal in origin whereas the medulla is ectodermal, formed by neural-crest chromaffin cells. The left suprarenal vein drains into the left renal vein and the right into the inferior vena cava.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the suprarenal facts tested by the Dr Galal endocrine bank: the medial extent of the left gland to the renal hilum, and the superficial-to-deep zonation of the cortex together with the germ-layer origins of cortex and medulla.
### Mechanism
The shape and extent of the left gland follow from its position on the medial border of the kidney; the zonation reflects the functional cortical layers (mineralocorticoid, glucocorticoid, androgen) overlying the sympathetic-derived medulla.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: the left gland reaching the renal hilum; glomerulosa-fasciculata-reticularis from superficial to deep; cortex mesoderm versus medulla ectoderm; and the asymmetric venous drainage (left to renal vein, right to IVC).
### Clinical significance
These points underlie the surgical approach to the suprarenal glands, the interpretation of cortical versus medullary tumours, and adrenal venous sampling.

## title
Blood supply of the pituitary gland
## subject
endo
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Anatomy
## summary
The pituitary gland sits in the hypophyseal fossa below the optic chiasma. Its posterior lobe (neurohypophysis) is supplied directly by the inferior hypophyseal artery, a branch of the internal carotid artery within the cavernous sinus. The anterior lobe (adenohypophysis), which is the larger lobe, is supplied indirectly through the hypothalamo-hypophyseal portal system fed by the superior hypophyseal arteries, the route by which hypothalamic releasing hormones reach it. An enlarging adenoma classically compresses the chiasma to cause a bitemporal (not binasal) hemianopia.
## sections
### Definition
This article covers the pituitary blood-supply fact tested by the Dr Galal endocrine bank: the direct arterial supply of the posterior lobe by the inferior hypophyseal artery, contrasted with the portal supply of the anterior lobe.
### Mechanism
The posterior lobe receives systemic arterial blood directly from the inferior hypophyseal artery, whereas the anterior lobe depends on a two-capillary-bed portal system that also delivers hypothalamic releasing and inhibiting hormones.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: direct (posterior lobe, inferior hypophyseal artery) versus portal (anterior lobe, superior hypophyseal arteries) supply; the anterior lobe as the larger lobe; and bitemporal versus binasal hemianopia in chiasmatic compression.
### Clinical significance
This supply pattern underlies pituitary apoplexy risk, the delivery of hypothalamic hormones to the adenohypophysis, and the reading of tumour visual fields.
