## summary
The thoracic cage is a cone of bone and cartilage, narrower above than below, and its two named openings are the whole of what the department book asks about it: a narrow inlet at the top that connects to the neck, and a broad outlet at the bottom that the diaphragm closes. Three boundaries define each, and getting the front, back and sides of both right is the entire content of this short chapter.

## sections
### Overview and position
The thoracic cage is the osteocartilaginous skeleton of the thorax. Its front wall is shorter than its back wall, which gives it a conical shape overall, and it is built from three components: the sternum and costal cartilages in front, the thoracic vertebrae, intervertebral discs and the shafts of the twelve pairs of ribs behind, and the ribs themselves on each side. Its two functions are to support and protect the thoracic and upper abdominal viscera, and to take part in the movements of respiration.

### Structure
The cavity the cage encloses has three parts: a narrow inlet, continuous with the root of the neck; a cavity proper, holding the lungs and heart; and a broad outlet, closed off from the abdominal cavity by the diaphragm. The thoracic inlet's plane is oblique, sloping downwards and forwards, bounded by the upper border of the manubrium sterni in front, the body of the first thoracic vertebra behind, and the inner border of the first rib on each side. The thoracic outlet's plane slopes the opposite way, downwards and backwards, bounded by the xiphoid process in front, the body of T12 and the eleventh and twelfth ribs behind, and the costal cartilages of the seventh to tenth ribs on each side.

### Relations
At the inlet, the structures that pass between thorax and neck — trachea, oesophagus, the great vessels of the neck, the phrenic and vagus nerves and the apex of each lung — all cross this oblique plane, which is why a mass at the thoracic inlet (a retrosternal thyroid, a Pancoast tumour) can compress several of them at once. At the outlet, the diaphragm's peripheral attachment follows the same bony margin the outlet is defined by, so every structure that pierces the diaphragm does so within, or just at the edge of, this boundary.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
The thoracic cage itself is not usefully described by a single blood or nerve supply; its ribs, sternum and vertebrae take the segmental supply of the region they belong to, covered with the intercostal spaces and the vertebral column elsewhere in this book. What matters for this chapter is purely the shape the cage's bony boundaries create.

### Development
The department book's thorax section gives no developmental account of the ribs, sternum or vertebral column as such; that content belongs to general osteology rather than to this thorax-specific chapter, and none is invented here to fill the gap.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
The two boundaries are landmarks a student is expected to reproduce as lines on a diagram or a cadaver: the inlet as an oblique plane from the manubrium's upper border to T1, the outlet as a wider oblique plane from the xiphoid to T12 and the lower two ribs. On a chest radiograph the first ribs mark the inlet and the costal margin marks the outlet, though the book itself does not describe the radiographic view.

### Clinical correlations
A mass at the thoracic inlet can compress the trachea, oesophagus, great vessels or brachial plexus roots that cross this narrow plane — the anatomical basis of thoracic outlet syndrome and of the compressive syndromes produced by an apical (Pancoast) lung tumour. The outlet's closure by the diaphragm is the reason a rise in intra-abdominal pressure and a rise in intrathoracic pressure are coupled through the same muscular sheet.

## summary
Each intercostal space is one repeating unit: three muscle layers doing the mechanical work of breathing, a neurovascular bundle riding in the costal groove with the vein above the artery above the nerve, and — since the internal thoracic artery is the front-line supply and the nerve is what a needle must dodge — the whole space is really one clinical structure the wall's mechanics, its blood supply and its sensation are all built around. This article covers everything the venous-drainage companion article does not: the muscles, the typical nerve's own course, the internal thoracic artery, and the dermatome map the intercostal nerves lay onto the skin.

## sections
### Overview and position
There are eleven intercostal spaces, the gap below each numerically corresponding rib; the upper nine have anterior and posterior parts, the lowest two are incomplete anteriorly because the eleventh and twelfth ribs are free. Each space holds three muscle layers and a neurovascular bundle, and is the unit both of chest-wall mechanics and of the somatic sensation and vasculature of the trunk.

### Structure
The three muscle layers run superficial to deep: the external intercostal, fibres obliquely downwards and forwards from the tubercle of the rib above to the costochondral junction, continuing as the anterior intercostal membrane; the internal intercostal, fibres obliquely downwards and backwards from the sternum to the rib angle, continuing as the posterior intercostal membrane; and the innermost intercostal, same direction as the internal but separated from it by the neurovascular bundle. An incomplete fourth layer, transversus thoracis, is formed of three separate slips — sternocostalis, innermost intercostal and subcostalis — supporting the sternocostal and costotransverse joints. All are supplied by the corresponding intercostal nerve; the external layer elevates the ribs for inspiration, while some authors treat the internal and innermost as expiratory depressors, and all three brace the space against being sucked in or bulged out during breathing.

The internal thoracic artery arises from the first part of the subclavian artery in the root of the neck, descends behind the upper six costal cartilages half an inch from the sternal margin, and ends opposite the sixth space by dividing into the superior epigastric and musculophrenic arteries. Along the way it gives the pericardiacophrenic artery (with the phrenic nerve, to pericardium, pleura and diaphragm), six pairs of anterior intercostal branches, perforating branches to the skin and the medial breast, and pericardial, sternal and mediastinal twigs.

### Relations
The neurovascular bundle occupies the costal groove of the rib above, between the internal and innermost intercostal muscles, arranged from above down as vein, artery, nerve — VAN. A typical intercostal nerve, the anterior primary ramus of T2 to T6, starts posteriorly between parietal pleura and the posterior intercostal membrane, behind the sympathetic chain; runs forward in the costal groove within the neurovascular bundle, below the vessels; then crosses in front of the internal thoracic vessels near the sternum before piercing the wall as the anterior cutaneous branch. The first nerve mostly joins the brachial plexus; the twelfth (subcostal) runs entirely in the abdominal wall; the second to sixth are typical; the seventh to eleventh are non-typical, running part of their course in the thorax and part in the abdomen.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
Each intercostal muscle layer takes the corresponding intercostal nerve and its collateral branch. The typical intercostal nerve itself gives ganglionic rami communicantes to the sympathetic chain, a collateral branch along the rib below, muscular branches, a lateral cutaneous branch (which in the second space becomes the intercostobrachial nerve to the axillary floor and medial arm instead of dividing locally), the terminal anterior cutaneous branch, articular branches to the rib joints, and pleural branches to the adjacent parietal pleura.

### Development
The department book's thorax section gives no separate embryological account of the intercostal muscles, nerves or internal thoracic artery; they follow the general somite and body-wall mesoderm pattern covered elsewhere in the curriculum, and none of that general material is repeated here.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
Skin above the sternal angle is supplied by C3 and C4 through the supraclavicular nerves; below it, by the thoracic nerves from T2 down, each laying an oblique dermatome band that sits one segment above the vertebral level behind it — the classic example being T6 skin over a T9 vertebra. C5 to T1 are absent from this trunk sequence because those segments were carried away by the developing upper limb as the brachial plexus. The lower five intercostal nerves and the subcostal nerve continue their bands onto the abdominal wall, and the back is supplied separately by the posterior primary rami.

### Clinical correlations
Irritation of an intercostal nerve causes severe pain referred along its cutaneous distribution, which is the rationale for an intercostal nerve block using local anaesthetic placed around the nerve. Pus tracking along the neurovascular bundle from a vertebral source can present at any of the three sites the nerve's cutaneous branches emerge. Herpes zoster classically infects one intercostal nerve, producing a burning pain and a vesicular rash confined to that nerve's dermatome band — a direct clinical demonstration of the segmental map this article describes. The perforating branches of the internal thoracic artery in the second, third and fourth spaces must be deliberately ligated in radical mastectomy, or they retract and cause internal haemorrhage.

Three muscle layers, superficial to deep: external (fibres down-and-forward, inspiratory), internal (down-and-back), innermost (same direction as internal, separated from it by the neurovascular bundle).

## lose_the_mark
Reversing the VAN order, or forgetting the nerve is lowest — this is exactly what makes the lower border of the rib above (not the upper border of the rib below) the dangerous edge for a needle.
Treating the second intercostal nerve like the others. Its lateral cutaneous branch does not divide locally; it becomes the intercostobrachial nerve and crosses into the upper limb instead.
Matching a dermatome level to the vertebra beside it. The book's own example, T6 skin over the T9 vertebra, shows the two are genuinely offset.
Forgetting to ligate the second-, third- and fourth-space perforating branches of the internal thoracic artery in radical mastectomy — an omission that causes internal bleeding, not a cosmetic slip.

## summary
The pleura is a closed sac with two layers and four named regions, and two of the spaces it leaves unoccupied by lung — the costomediastinal and costodiaphragmatic recesses — are exactly where a collapsed lung has room to re-expand. This companion to the nerve-supply article maps the pleura's own divisions, gives its surface projection, and covers the four abnormal fillings of the pleural cavity and the one safe site to drain them.

## sections
### Overview and position
Each pleural sac is a closed serous sac, invaginated from its medial side by the lung, forming a visceral (pulmonary) layer closely adherent to the lung and a parietal layer lining the thoracic wall, mediastinum and diaphragm, separated by a potential cavity holding only a thin film of fluid that lets the lung slide against the wall with minimal friction.

### Structure
The parietal pleura is named by the wall it lines: cervical pleura, or the dome of the pleura, over the lung apex; costal pleura against the ribs, costal cartilages and intercostal spaces; mediastinal pleura against the sides of the mediastinum, sleeving the root of the lung and continuing below it as the pulmonary ligament, a loose empty fold that gives the pulmonary veins room to distend when venous return rises; and diaphragmatic pleura over the diaphragm, continuous at its edge with the costal and mediastinal pleura. Two recesses in each sac hold no lung except in forced inspiration: the costomediastinal recess, between costal and mediastinal pleura behind the sternum, and the costodiaphragmatic recess, between costal and diaphragmatic pleura at the thoracic outlet. The suprapleural membrane (Sibson's fascia), triangular with its apex at C7's transverse process and its base at the first rib and costal cartilage, protects the cervical pleura and apex of the lung and stops both inward suction at the neck root during inspiration and upward bulging of the apex during forced expiration.

### Relations
Costal pleura is continuous with mediastinal pleura at the costomediastinal reflection and with diaphragmatic pleura at the costodiaphragmatic reflection — the two lines that bound the two named recesses. The pleura's own surface projections run about two ribs lower than the lung's at every reference point, which is exactly the margin the costodiaphragmatic recess occupies and the reason it can be aspirated safely without touching lung tissue in quiet breathing.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
The visceral pleura shares the lung's own supply throughout: autonomic (parasympathetic-predominant) nerve supply via the pulmonary plexuses, arterial supply from the bronchial vessels, and lymphatic drainage to the bronchopulmonary and tracheobronchial nodes — and, matching that autonomic nerve supply, it is not sensitive to somatic pain or temperature. The parietal pleura shares the thoracic wall, mediastinum and diaphragm's own supply: nerve territorially, by region — cervical pleura from the first intercostal nerve, costal pleura segmentally from the corresponding intercostal nerves, mediastinal and central diaphragmatic pleura from the phrenic nerve, peripheral diaphragmatic pleura from the lower intercostal nerves; arterial supply from the internal thoracic and intercostal arteries; and lymphatic drainage to the parasternal, intercostal, posterior mediastinal and diaphragmatic nodes.

### Development
The department book's thorax section gives no separate embryological account of the pleura beyond its adult structure; the general principle that visceral serous layers derive from splanchnic mesoderm and parietal layers from somatic mesoderm is standard embryology outside the scope of what this chapter states.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
The cervical pleura is a curved line, convex upwards, from the junction of the medial and middle thirds of the clavicle to the sternoclavicular joint, its highest point about 3 cm above the medial third of the clavicle. The anterior border is identical on both sides down to the sternal angle; below that the right descends vertically to the xiphisternal junction while the left deviates at the fourth costal cartilage to make room for the heart before rejoining the sternal margin at the sixth cartilage. The inferior border runs obliquely backwards, crossing the eighth rib in the midclavicular line, the tenth in the midaxillary line and the vertebral column at T12; the posterior border ascends vertically from T12 to T1 alongside the vertebral column.

### Clinical correlations
Dry, roughened pleural surfaces produce friction and pain on breathing. Irritation of the costal pleura or the peripheral diaphragmatic pleura refers pain to the thoracic or abdominal wall according to the intercostal nerve involved; irritation of the mediastinal pleura or the central diaphragmatic pleura refers pain to the tip of the shoulder, because the phrenic nerve (C3–5) shares cervical segments with the supraclavicular nerves (C3–4) supplying that skin. The pleural cavity can fill abnormally with air (pneumothorax, collapsing the lung), blood (haemothorax), pus (pyothorax or empyema) or excess serous fluid (pleural effusion); fluid is aspirated with a wide-bore needle in the seventh intercostal space in the midaxillary line — not lower, to avoid the diaphragm — placed midway between the space's upper and lower boundaries to avoid the neurovascular bundle above and the collateral vessels below.

## summary
Everything that makes the right and left lung different — three lobes against two, two fissures against one, a straight anterior border against one with a cardiac notch and a lingula, a shorter but heavier and broader shape — traces back to two neighbours: the liver pushing up on the right, and the heart bulging left. This article covers the external shape of each lung, its hilum and root, its fissures and lobes, its mediastinal surface impressions, and the full comparison between the two sides.

## sections
### Overview and position
Each lung is the organ of gaseous exchange, shaped like half a cone, with an apex, a base, two surfaces (costal and medial) and three borders (anterior, posterior, inferior). Before birth the lung is firm and sinks in water; after the first breath it becomes spongy and floats, a finding with medico-legal importance in determining whether a stillbirth breathed. The apex is rounded and projects through the thoracic inlet to about an inch above the medial third of the clavicle, grooved in front by the subclavian artery and separated behind from the first rib's neck by three structures crossing there; the base is concave and rests on the diaphragm, separated by it from the liver on the right and the liver, stomach and spleen on the left.

### Structure
The costal surface is wide, smooth and convex, against the ribs and intercostal spaces. The medial surface carries the hilum and divides into a vertebral part, related identically on both sides to the vertebral bodies, intercostal nerves and vessels and the sympathetic chain, and a mediastinal part, carrying the hilum and a set of grooves and impressions that differ between the two lungs. The anterior border is thin and sharp, notched above by the first rib, straight on the right but carrying the cardiac notch and the tongue-like lingula on the left; the inferior border encircles the base; the posterior border is thick and rounded.

The oblique fissure, present in both lungs, cuts from the posterior border 6 cm below the apex down to the inferior border, as far as the hilum. The horizontal fissure exists only in the right lung, running from the anterior border to meet the oblique fissure at the sixth rib in the midaxillary line, and separates the upper from the middle lobe. The right lung therefore has three lobes — upper, middle and lower, the lower larger than the other two together — and the left has only an upper lobe, equivalent to the right's upper and middle combined and carrying the cardiac notch and lingula, and a lower lobe identical in extent to the right's.

### Relations
The hilum is the area on the mediastinal surface through which the root's structures pass; the root is the group of structures sleeved by a downward prolongation of mediastinal pleura continuing as the pulmonary ligament. In both lungs the superior pulmonary vein is most anterior in the hilum, the inferior pulmonary vein most inferior, and the principal bronchus most posterior; the pulmonary artery lies in front of the bronchus, above and in front of it on the left, and between the bronchus and superior pulmonary vein on the right. The right principal bronchus divides before the hilum into an eparterial branch to the upper lobe and a hyparterial branch to the middle and lower lobes.

On the mediastinal surface of the right lung, in front of the hilum lie the cardiac impression (related to the right atrium and auricle and the right phrenic nerve), the SVC groove continuing up into the right brachiocephalic vein groove, the triangular impression for the ascending aorta, and the IVC groove; above the hilum, the groove for the azygos arch and the tracheal and oesophageal grooves, the oesophageal groove continuing behind the hilum. On the mediastinal surface of the left lung, the cardiac impression relates mainly to the left ventricle and atrium and the left phrenic nerve; above the hilum, the broad groove for the aortic arch — separated from the lung by the left phrenic and vagus nerves, the superficial cardiac plexus and the left superior intercostal vein — and the grooves for the left common carotid and subclavian arteries, with the oesophageal groove behind the subclavian groove; behind the hilum, the groove for the descending thoracic aorta.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics

### Development
The department book's respiratory-development chapter describes the lung bud and bronchial tree's formation but does not attach the fissure or hilar-relation content of this article to a developmental account; that connection is covered separately in the development article.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
The apex's surface line is the same curve as the pleura's, its summit 3 cm above the medial third of the clavicle. The anterior border descends from the sternoclavicular joint to the sternal angle identically on both sides; below that the right continues to the sixth sternocostal junction while the left deviates at the fourth costal cartilage — the cardiac notch and lingula — before rejoining at the sixth. The inferior border, two ribs above the pleura's own line, crosses the sixth rib in the midclavicular line, the eighth in the midaxillary line and reaches T10 posteriorly, where it meets the posterior border, which ascends to T1. The oblique fissure runs from 3 cm off the T3 spine, opposite the root of the scapular spine, down to the sixth rib in the midaxillary line and forward to the sixth costal cartilage — corresponding to the medial scapular border when the arm is raised overhead. The horizontal fissure runs from the fourth right costal cartilage to the oblique fissure at the sixth rib in the midaxillary line.

### Clinical correlations
The hilar and mediastinal-surface relations described here are what a mediastinal mass, an enlarged lymph node or an aortic aneurysm will indent or displace on imaging, and are the anatomical basis for reading a chest radiograph's silhouette sign. The right lung's shorter, broader shape (from the liver and the leftward heart respectively), its extra lobe and fissure, and its different hilar arrangement (eparterial bronchus, artery position, single bronchial artery) are all summarised together in the differences article and are a recurring source of single-best-answer questions asking which side a described feature belongs to.

## summary
Ten segments on the right, eight on the left, each with its own bronchus, its own artery and its own drainage — a bronchopulmonary segment is the surgical unit of the lung precisely because it is also the anatomical unit, wedge-shaped and separated from its neighbours along the intersegmental planes the pulmonary veins run in. This article covers the segments, the lung's two separate circulations (one functional, one nutritive), and the lymphatic route from an alveolar wall that has no lymphatics of its own to the veins at the root of the neck.

## sections
### Overview and position
A bronchopulmonary segment is a functionally independent unit of lung tissue, supplied by its own segmental (tertiary) bronchus and an accompanying end-artery branch of the pulmonary artery, with its own lymphatics and autonomic innervation. Each is wedge-shaped, apex at the hilum and base at the lung surface, and the trachea's division into main, then lobar, then segmental bronchi produces ten segments in the right lung and eight in the left.

### Structure
The trachea bifurcates into two main bronchi; the right divides into three lobar bronchi and the left into two, matching the lobar pattern; each lobar bronchus then gives segmental bronchi — ten on the right (three in the upper lobe, two in the middle, five in the lower), eight on the left (four in the upper lobe, including the two lingular segments, and four in the lower). The left has two fewer than the right because its first and second segmental bronchi share a common apicoposterior stem and its seventh, medial basal, segment is frequently absent — though the numbering is kept identical on both sides for comparison. Each segment's three constituents are a segmental bronchus with its respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts and alveoli; a segmental branch of the pulmonary artery, an end artery with no anastomoses to neighbouring segments; and its own nerves, bronchial vessels and lymphatics, running with the bronchial tree. The intersegmental connective-tissue septa between segments carry the radicles of the pulmonary veins — the one component of a segment's supply that is not itself segmental.

### Relations
Because segmental pulmonary artery branches are end arteries that do not anastomose across segment boundaries, and the draining veins run in the septa between segments rather than within them, a single diseased segment — tuberculosis is the book's own example — stays confined to it, and a surgeon can excise one segment (segmentectomy) along its venous planes while leaving the neighbouring lung tissue intact, a smaller operation than a lobectomy or a pneumonectomy.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
The lung receives two independent circulations. The pulmonary vessels are functional: one pulmonary artery per lung carries deoxygenated blood, branching alongside the bronchial tree as end arteries down to the alveolar capillary bed, and two pulmonary veins per lung, superior and inferior, return oxygenated blood to the left atrium. The bronchial vessels are nutritive, supplying the bronchial tree's walls, the lung stroma and the visceral pleura: the right lung by one bronchial artery from the third right posterior intercostal artery (or the upper left bronchial artery), the left by two bronchial arteries from the descending thoracic aorta, with bronchial veins draining to the azygos vein on the right and the superior hemiazygos vein on the left.

The alveolar walls have no lymphatics of their own; interstitial lymph drains centripetally, from the visceral pleura at the periphery towards the hilum, starting in the bronchiole walls and running alongside the segmental and lobar bronchi through intrapulmonary lymph follicles to the bronchopulmonary nodes at the hilum, then the tracheobronchial nodes at the tracheal bifurcation, then the paratracheal nodes, drained in turn by the mediastinal lymph trunk — to the right brachiocephalic vein on the right side, to the thoracic duct on the left.

### Development
The bronchial tree's segmental branching pattern, established prenatally through seventeen generations of division and continuing through six more after birth, is covered in the development article; it is the embryological basis for the adult segment count given here.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
The department book gives no separate surface-anatomy account of the individual segments beyond the fissure and lobe lines already covered in the companion article; segmental anatomy is instead read from bronchoscopic or CT correlation, which the book does not describe.

### Clinical correlations
Because a diseased segment stays confined by its end-artery and intersegmental-vein boundaries, segmentectomy removes it while sparing the rest of the lobe — a far smaller operation than removing a whole lobe or lung. Knowing the direction each segmental bronchus takes lets a clinician choose the correct postural drainage position for a lung abscess. Cancer, unlike infection, is not confined by segment boundaries and can spread from one segment to another. At birth, the first breath opens the alveoli and the lung becomes spongy and floats — pink at first, darkening later with inhaled carbon and dust — the buoyancy test with medico-legal use already noted for the lung as a whole.

A bronchopulmonary segment = its own segmental bronchus + its own end-artery branch of the pulmonary artery + its own nerves and lymphatics; the pulmonary vein is the one component that is intersegmental, not segmental.

Treating 'blood supply of the lung' as answered by the pulmonary artery and veins alone. The bronchial arteries are the nutritive half of the answer and are what an examiner is testing when the question turns to the lung's own tissue viability.

Assuming lymphatics exist in the alveolar wall because the lung is so vascular. The book states plainly that the alveolar walls are devoid of lymph vessels.

## summary
The diaphragm has no bony insertion at all — every fibre from its three origins converges on one central tendon — and its three major openings sit stacked at three vertebral levels, T8, T10 and T12, each named for what passes through it. This article covers where the diaphragm arises and inserts, its three major and several minor openings, its sole motor nerve, and the three named hernias that follow directly from the small gaps left between its areas of origin.

## sections
### Overview and position
The diaphragm is a large, dome-shaped fibromuscular partition separating the thoracic from the abdominal cavity, convex upwards, with its right cupola higher than its left because the liver sits beneath it. It has a wide origin from the whole circumference of the thoracic outlet and the vertebral column, and, uniquely for a muscle of this size, no bony insertion at all: every fibre converges on a central aponeurosis.

### Structure
The diaphragm arises from three areas. The sternal origin is two fleshy slips from the back of the xiphoid process. The costal origin is fleshy slips from the deep surface of the lower six costal cartilages, interdigitating with the origin of transversus abdominis. The vertebral origin is by two crura and five arcuate ligaments: the right crus, larger because it contracts against the liver, arises from the upper three lumbar vertebrae and its fibres surround the lower oesophagus as a physiological sphincter; the left crus, smaller, arises from the upper two. The median arcuate ligament, one only, arches over the aorta between the two crura; the two medial arcuate ligaments each arch over psoas major, from the crus to the tip of L1's transverse process; the two lateral arcuate ligaments each arch over quadratus lumborum, from that same transverse process to the last rib. All fibres insert into the central tendon, a strong, semilunar, trifoliate aponeurosis whose median leaf relates to the pericardium and heart. Small gaps between the areas of origin, filled with loose areolar tissue, are the foramen of Morgagni, between the sternal and costal origins, transmitting the superior epigastric vessels, and the foramen of Bochdalek (vertebrocostal triangle), between the costal and vertebral origins, mainly on the left, where the costodiaphragmatic pleura is separated from the kidney only by areolar tissue — the remnant of the fetal pleuroperitoneal canal.

### Relations
On its superior surface the right cupola relates to the right pleura and lung, the left cupola to the left pleura and lung, and the central tendon to the base of the heart and pericardium. On its inferior surface, the right side relates to the right lobe of the liver and the right kidney and suprarenal gland; the left side to the left lobe of the liver, the fundus of the stomach and spleen, and the left kidney and suprarenal gland.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
Motor supply is entirely from the right and left phrenic nerves (C3, 4, 5), which supply the diaphragm's inferior (abdominal) surface because folding of the embryo turns what was its cranial surface to face caudally. Sensory supply is mainly to the pleura and peritoneum related to the diaphragm, with only a few proprioceptive fibres from the muscle itself: the phrenic nerves are sensory to the central part, the lower six thoracic spinal nerves to the peripheral parts. Arterial supply is from five sources: the superior phrenic and lower intercostal arteries from the thoracic aorta, the inferior phrenic artery from the abdominal aorta, and the musculophrenic and pericardiacophrenic arteries from the internal thoracic artery.

### Development
The department book's development chapter derives the diaphragm from four fused mesodermal sources — the septum transversum with the third to fifth cervical myotomes, forming the central tendon and carrying the phrenic nerve down with it; the two pleuroperitoneal membranes, closing the pericardioperitoneal canals and forming the dorsolateral parts; the body-wall mesoderm, forming the periphery; and the oesophageal mesentery and periaortic mesoderm, forming the crura — and attributes each named hernia to the failure of one of these sources, covered in full in the development article.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
The three major openings sit at three distinct vertebral levels in the midline plane. The aortic opening, at T12, lies behind the median arcuate ligament and transmits the aorta, the azygos vein to its right and the thoracic duct between them. The oesophageal opening, at T10, lies one inch left of the midline in the right crus and transmits the oesophagus, the two vagi and the oesophageal branches of the left gastric vessels. The caval opening, at T8, lies one inch right of the midline in the central tendon and transmits the IVC, the right phrenic nerve and lymphatics. Minor openings transmit the musculophrenic artery (between the 7th and 8th costal slips), the superior epigastric artery (between sternal and costal origins), the lower five intercostal nerves and vessels (between costal digitations), the subcostal nerve, vessels and quadratus lumborum (behind the lateral arcuate ligament), the hemiazygos vein (piercing the left crus), the sympathetic chain and psoas major (behind the medial arcuate ligament), the greater and lesser splanchnic nerves (through the crura) and the left phrenic nerve (through the left cupola).

### Clinical correlations
The diaphragm is the chief muscle of respiration and is also recruited to raise intra-abdominal pressure for parturition, coughing, defecation and micturition. Diaphragmatic hernia takes three forms, each named for the gap it uses: a hiatus hernia, the stomach passing through a widened oesophageal opening; a hernia of Bochdalek, through the costovertebral gap, mainly on the left; and a parasternal hernia of Morgagni, through the sternocostal gap. Because the phrenic nerve alone supplies motor function to each half of the diaphragm, a unilateral phrenic nerve injury paralyses that side; an accessory phrenic nerve, formed occasionally from C5 fibres and usually joining the main nerve at the first rib, can mean that cutting the visible phrenic nerve in the neck fails to achieve complete paralysis.

## summary
One oblique plane, from the sternal angle to the T4/T5 disc, is all that separates the superior mediastinum from the inferior — and the inferior mediastinum then splits into three more compartments around the pericardium. This article introduces all four subdivisions and works through the contents of the superior and middle compartments; the posterior compartment, already covered alongside the pleura, and the pericardium itself, covered in its own article, are cross-referenced rather than repeated.

## sections
### Overview and position
The mediastinum is the thick median partition of the thoracic cavity, extending from the sternum to the vertebral column and from the thoracic inlet to the diaphragm, holding the pericardium and heart, the great arteries and veins of the thorax, the vagus and phrenic nerves, the trachea and oesophagus, and the lymph nodes and thoracic duct.

### Structure
An imaginary plane from the sternal angle in front to the T4/T5 disc behind divides the mediastinum into a superior part, above the plane, and an inferior part, below it. The inferior mediastinum is further subdivided into an anterior mediastinum, a narrow space in front of the pericardium containing the sternopericardial ligaments, the lower part of the thymus and a few lymph nodes; a middle mediastinum, occupied by the pericardium and everything it encloses — the heart, ascending aorta, pulmonary trunk, the lower half of the SVC, the uppermost IVC, and the terminal parts of the four pulmonary veins; and a posterior mediastinum, behind the pericardium and in front of the vertebral column.

### Relations
The superior mediastinum lies between the manubrium in front, the upper four thoracic vertebrae behind, the thoracic inlet above and the sternal-angle plane below, flanked on each side by mediastinal pleura and the mediastinal surface of the lung. Retrosternally it holds the two brachiocephalic veins, the left superior intercostal vein, the upper half of the SVC (receiving the azygos arch on its posterior aspect) and the thymus; more centrally, the aortic arch — its concavity occupied by the pulmonary trunk's bifurcation — and its three branches, the two vagi, the two phrenic nerves and the left recurrent laryngeal nerve; and most posteriorly the trachea, the oesophagus behind it, and the thoracic duct ascending behind the oesophagus's left border. The anterior mediastinum lies between the sternum and the pericardium, from the plane above to the diaphragm below. The middle mediastinum lies between the anterior and posterior compartments, from the superior mediastinum above to the diaphragm below, flanked by pleura and lung.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
Not a single system for the compartments as a whole; each named content of the superior and middle mediastinum carries its own supply, covered in the articles on the individual vessels, nerves and the heart itself.

### Development
The department book's thorax section gives no separate embryological account of the mediastinum's own subdivisions; the heart, great vessels and diaphragm that come to occupy these compartments each have their own developmental account elsewhere in this book.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
The dividing plane between superior and inferior mediastinum is a standard radiographic landmark, running from the sternal angle to the T4/T5 disc, though the department book itself does not describe a radiographic view of it.

### Clinical correlations
Mediastinal syndrome — compression of the superior mediastinum's contents by a space-occupying lesion such as a tumour, enlarged lymph nodes or a retrosternal thyroid — produces effects that map directly onto the three groups of structures listed above: venous congestion or upper-limb and head-and-neck ischaemia from vessel compression, dyspnoea or dysphagia from tracheal or oesophageal compression, and hoarseness from compression of the left recurrent laryngeal nerve. The full clinical picture of an aortic-arch aneurysm producing exactly this triad is covered in the companion article on the arch of the aorta.

## summary
Two tube-like extensions of the visceral pericardium — one around the arterial end of the heart, one around the venous end — leave a through-passage between them, the transverse sinus, and a blind pocket behind the left atrium, the oblique sinus, and a surgeon uses both: the transverse sinus to clamp the great vessels in open-heart surgery, and the safe drainage window near the sternum to aspirate an effusion without ever touching either.

## sections
### Overview and position
The pericardium is a conical fibroserous sac surrounding the heart and the roots of its great vessels, lying in the middle mediastinum from the sternal-angle plane above to the diaphragm below, extending in front from the second to the sixth costal cartilages and behind opposite the fifth to seventh thoracic vertebrae. It has two coats: an outer fibrous pericardium and an inner serous pericardium.

### Structure
The fibrous pericardium is a strong, conical sac with an apex directed upwards, blending at the level of the sternal angle with the great vessels' outer coats, and a base directed downwards, adherent to the central tendon of the diaphragm — the reverse orientation of the heart itself. Its anterior surface is fixed to the sternum by the superior and inferior sternopericardial ligaments. The serous pericardium is a closed sac invaginated from above and behind by the heart and the roots of its great vessels: a parietal layer lines the fibrous pericardium and reflects onto the heart, and a visceral layer covers the heart as the epicardium, the two separated by the pericardial cavity, a potential space holding only a thin film of fluid.

### Relations
The visceral layer sends two tube-like extensions over the great-vessel roots: an anterior tube enclosing the ascending aorta and pulmonary trunk together, and a posterior tube enclosing parts of the SVC, IVC and the four pulmonary veins. Between them lies the transverse sinus, connecting the right and left sides of the pericardial cavity between the arterial end of the heart (bounded in front by the ascending aorta and pulmonary trunk) and the venous end (bounded behind by the SVC and the two atria, mainly the left, and above and behind by the right pulmonary artery); a finger passed from the right, behind the lower SVC and in front of the ascending aorta and pulmonary trunk, traverses it and appears at its left end between the pulmonary trunk and the left auricle. The oblique sinus is a blind recess extending upwards between the back of the left atrium in front and the fibrous pericardium behind — separating it from the descending aorta and oesophagus — closed above by the pericardial reflection onto the left atrium, bounded on the right by the reflections onto the SVC, the two right pulmonary veins and the IVC (from above down), on the left by the reflections onto the two left pulmonary veins, and open below into the main pericardial cavity between the IVC and the lower left pulmonary vein; three fingers placed behind the lifted heart's apex, to the left of the IVC, reach its blind upper end, separated by the left atrium's upper border from a finger in the transverse sinus just above and in front.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
The fibrous pericardium and the parietal layer of serous pericardium are supplied by the phrenic nerves, carrying somatic sensation and making this pericardium pain-sensitive, as in pericarditis; their arterial supply is from the internal thoracic arteries and their pericardiacophrenic and musculophrenic branches and from the descending aorta, with venous drainage through the azygos system. The visceral layer, the epicardium, shares the heart's own autonomic (sympathetic and parasympathetic) nerve supply and is insensitive to pain but sensitive to ischaemia; its blood supply is from the right and left coronary arteries, draining via the coronary sinus.

### Development
The department book's development chapter derives the epicardium, along with the myocardium, from the splanchnic mesoderm surrounding the primitive endocardial heart tube; the fibrous and parietal serous pericardium's own separate embryological origin is not stated in this chapter.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
The pericardium's own surface projection follows the heart's borders described in the companion article on the heart's surface anatomy; the book gives no separate pericardial outline beyond the costal-cartilage and vertebral-level extent stated above.

### Clinical correlations
The fibrous pericardium protects the heart, maintains its position and prevents overdistension, and keeps the mouths of the great vessels open. The oblique sinus, acting as a bursa behind the left atrium, allows that chamber's movement and lets the descending thoracic aorta pulsate and the oesophagus expand during swallowing without pericardial constraint; the transverse sinus allows the ascending aorta and pulmonary trunk to distend during diastole and is used surgically as the site to clamp or encircle the great vessels during open-heart surgery. Fluid collecting in the pericardial cavity (pericardial effusion) hinders the heart's movement and causes severe distress; it is drained by needle paracentesis in the fifth or sixth left intercostal space close to the sternum, a site chosen to avoid the pleura and the internal thoracic artery.

## summary
The heart is one third to the right and two thirds to the left of the midline, its apex entirely left ventricle, its base entirely atria — and inside, the right side's rough atrium and coarse-trabeculated ventricle contrast with the left side's smooth atrium and fine-trabeculated ventricle at almost every point of comparison the department book draws. This article covers the heart's external shape and the interior of all four chambers.

## sections
### Overview and position
The heart is a hollow muscular pump lying inside the pericardium in the middle mediastinum, one third of its bulk to the right of the median plane and two thirds to the left, behind the sternum and adjoining costal cartilages. It is about the size of its owner's closed fist, 12 cm long from base to apex, 8 cm side to side, 6 cm front to back, weighing about 300 g in males and 250 g in females.

### Structure
The heart is conical, with a base (posterior surface, formed mainly by the left atrium and, to a lesser extent, the right, lying opposite T5–T8 and related behind to the two right pulmonary veins, the oblique vein of the left atrium and the posterior mediastinum's own contents), an apex (formed wholly by the left ventricle, the lowest and leftmost point of the heart, opposite the left fifth intercostal space, separated from the chest wall by pleura and lung), a sternocostal surface (an atrial part above-right and a ventricular part below-left, separated by the coronary sulcus) and a diaphragmatic surface (limited behind by the coronary sulcus, formed mainly by the left ventricle), and four borders — superior (left atrium mainly), inferior (right ventricle mainly), right (right atrium only, separated from the sternocostal surface by the sulcus terminalis) and left (left ventricle mainly, left atrium above).

### Relations
Functionally the heart is a double pump of two atrium-ventricle halves, the right receiving and pumping deoxygenated blood, the left oxygenated, kept from mixing by complete septa. The right atrium, quadrangular and forming the heart's right border, has its cavity split by the crista terminalis — a muscular ridge between the SVC and IVC openings, corresponding externally to the sulcus terminalis — into an anterior rough atrium proper, roughened by the comb-like musculi pectinati extending from the crista into the auricle, and a posterior smooth sinus venarum, receiving the SVC (no valve), the IVC (a rudimentary valve) and the coronary sinus (a small valve), plus two to four anterior cardiac veins and numerous venae cordis minimi. Its septal wall shows the fossa ovalis, above and left of the IVC opening, and its raised margin the annulus ovalis, deficient below. The tricuspid orifice leads to the right ventricle, whose inflow part has coarse trabeculae carneae, three papillary muscles (anterior — continuous with the moderator band, chordae to the anterior and posterior cusps; posterior — chordae to the posterior and septal cusps; septal — several small ones, chordae to the anterior and septal cusps) and the septomarginal trabecula (moderator band), which prevents overdistension and carries the right bundle branch; its smooth outflow part, the infundibulum, leads through the pulmonary orifice (guarded by a three-cusp pulmonary valve, two anterior and one posterior) to the pulmonary trunk.

The left atrium, smaller than the right and forming most of the base and upper border, is concealed anteriorly by the aortic root and pulmonary trunk and separated posteriorly from the descending aorta and oesophagus by the oblique sinus; its cavity is smooth except for a few musculi pectinati in the auricle, and it receives the four pulmonary veins besides the left atrioventricular orifice. The left ventricle, wall three times thicker than the right's at 27 mm, has a rough inflow part with fine trabeculae carneae and two papillary muscles (anterior and posterior, chordae to both mitral cusps) and a smooth outflow part, the aortic vestibule, fibrous-walled to stay patent through systole; blood turns nearly a right angle between inflow and outflow in the right ventricle, an acute angle in the left.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
Covered in full, alongside the fibrous skeleton and conducting system, in the two companion articles on the heart's coronary vessels, veins, nerve supply and conducting system.

### Development
The right and left atria and ventricles of the adult heart derive from the primitive atrium and primitive ventricle of the embryonic heart tube, each divided by septation; the full mechanism, including the crista terminalis's own embryonic origin from the right sinus venosus valve, is covered in the development article.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
Covered fully, together with the auscultation areas, in the companion article on the heart's vessels, nerves and fixation.

### Clinical correlations
Because the right atrium's tricuspid orifice admits three fingers against the left atrioventricular orifice's two, and the tricuspid valve has three cusps against the mitral's two, the two sides are distinguishable by these counts alone on a specimen or in an operative field. The moderator band's dual role — bracing the right ventricle and carrying the right bundle branch — means damage to it can produce both a structural change and a conduction abnormality together. The fossa ovalis marks the site of the fetal foramen ovale and is the anatomical landmark used for transseptal catheterisation to reach the left atrium from a right heart approach.

## summary
A dense collagenous ring at the base of the ventricles holds the four valves in place and keeps atrial and ventricular muscle electrically apart everywhere except one bridge — the AV bundle — which is precisely why the conducting system needs to exist at all as a distinct set of specialised muscle fibres, not nerve tissue, carrying the impulse from the SA node down to a Purkinje network arranged so both ventricles contract from the apex up.

## sections
### Overview and position
The fibrous skeleton sits in the plane of the atrioventricular sulcus at the base of the two ventricles, built of dense collagenous bundles. The conducting system is the set of specialised cardiac muscle fibres — not nerve tissue — responsible for initiating and propagating the rhythmic impulse that makes the heart beat in order.

### Structure
The fibrous skeleton has four parts: the right and left atrioventricular rings, together forming an 8-shaped structure, the right larger but weaker; the fibrous rings of the pulmonary and aortic orifices, giving attachment to the bases of those valve cusps; the right and left fibrous trigones, masses of tissue between the aortic ring and each atrioventricular ring, the right the larger; and the tendon of the infundibulum, connecting the infundibulum to the aortic ring.

The conducting system has five named components. The sino-atrial (SA) node, in the right atrial wall behind the SVC opening, crescentic, 10-20 mm by 8 mm, is the pacemaker that initiates each impulse. The atrioventricular (AV) node, oval, sits in the lower posterior interatrial septum, 10 mm above the coronary sinus opening. The atrioventricular (AV) bundle, or bundle of His, is the sole muscular bridge across the fibrous skeleton, arising from the AV node, descending behind the tricuspid ring's fibrous margin to the interventricular septum, then along its membranous part to the muscular part, where it divides into right and left branches. The right branch runs beneath the endocardium of the right side of the septum to the moderator band, through it to the anterior papillary muscle's base, and breaks into Purkinje fibres; the left branch runs beneath the septum's left-side endocardium towards the apex. In each ventricle, Purkinje fibres encircle the papillary muscle bases then ascend beneath the endocardium towards the base, so the ventricles contract from below upwards, pumping blood out through the great arteries rather than trapping it.

### Relations
The fibrous skeleton's third function — after providing stable attachment for the valve cusps and for the atrial and ventricular musculature — is to keep the atrial and ventricular myocardium electrically discontinuous everywhere except where the AV bundle deliberately crosses it, which is the entire reason a distinct conducting bundle is necessary at all.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
The conducting system's own blood supply is covered as part of the coronary arteries in the companion article: the SA node from the right coronary artery in 60% of people and the left in 40%; the AV node and bundle from the right coronary artery in 80% and the left in 20%.

### Development
The interatrial and interventricular septa, the atrioventricular canal and cushions, and the sinus venosus's own contribution to the crista terminalis and atrioventricular valves are covered in the development article; the conducting tissue's specific embryological origin is not stated in this chapter of the department book.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
Neither the fibrous skeleton nor the conducting system has a surface projection the department book describes separately from the heart's own valve and border projections, covered in the companion article on the heart's surface anatomy.

### Clinical correlations
Because the AV bundle is the only muscular connection between atria and ventricles, damage anywhere along it — including its right and left branches — produces heart block, a conduction abnormality rather than a mechanical one. The right coronary artery's supply to most of the conducting system in most people is why occlusion of that artery carries a disproportionate risk of arrhythmia, noted again in the coronary arteries article.

## summary
The right coronary artery supplies the whole conducting system in most people and the right side of the heart; the left coronary artery's two branches supply the left atrium, most of the left ventricle and the front two thirds of the septum — a division of labour with a poor anastomosis between the two that is exactly why a sudden coronary occlusion causes infarction rather than a painless reroute. This article covers both coronary arteries, the coronary sinus and cardiac veins, the heart's autonomic nerve supply and plexuses, its surface projections and auscultation areas, and the sling mechanism that fixes it in place.

## sections
### Overview and position
The heart's own tissue is supplied by two coronary arteries arising from the aortic sinuses just above the aortic valve, drained mostly through the coronary sinus into the right atrium, innervated by autonomic fibres that modulate but do not initiate its beat, and fixed in the mediastinum by a combination of pericardial attachment and vessel-hooking around the bronchi.

### Structure
The right coronary artery arises from the anterior aortic sinus, passes between the pulmonary trunk and right auricle to the anterior coronary sulcus, descends to the junction of the right and inferior borders — giving the marginal artery there — then turns back along the posterior sulcus to give the posterior (inferior) interventricular artery, which also supplies the AV node and bundle, before anastomosing with the circumflex artery; small branches include the right conus artery and, in 60% of people, the artery to the SA node. It supplies the whole right atrium, the whole right ventricle except a strip beside the anterior interventricular groove, a strip of the left ventricle's diaphragmatic surface beside the posterior interventricular groove, the posterior third of the septum, and — in most people — the whole conducting system (SA node in 60%, AV node and bundle branches in 80%); when its posterior interventricular branch is present, the pattern is called right-dominant, the commonest.

The left coronary artery, larger, arises from the left posterior aortic sinus, passes between the pulmonary trunk and left auricle to the top of the anterior interventricular groove, and divides into the anterior interventricular artery — descending with the great cardiac vein to the inferior border, turning round it to anastomose posteriorly with the right coronary artery, supplying the left ventricle's sternocostal surface, a strip of the right ventricle and the anterior two thirds of the septum — and the circumflex artery, running the coronary sulcus to anastomose with the right coronary artery posteriorly, supplying the left atrium and, through its marginal branch, the left ventricle to the apex, and the SA node in 40% and AV node in 20% of people.

The coronary sinus, a wide, short channel (3–4 cm) in the posterior coronary sulcus, begins as the continuation of the great cardiac vein and opens into the right atrium between the IVC opening and the tricuspid orifice, guarded by a small valve. Its tributaries are the great cardiac vein (ascending in the anterior interventricular groove), the middle cardiac vein (in the posterior interventricular groove), the small cardiac vein (along the inferior border with the marginal artery) and the oblique vein of the left atrium. Anterior cardiac veins and venae cordis minimi bypass the sinus, opening directly into the right atrium.

### Relations
The anastomosis between the right and left coronary arteries is poor, especially in youth, and although it improves with age it remains inadequate to provide efficient collateral flow if a large branch is suddenly occluded, because the anastomosis opens too slowly to compensate. The transverse sinus of the pericardium lies immediately behind the aortic root the coronary arteries arise from, and the coronary sinus itself is accompanied throughout its course by the circumflex artery, the two vessels' blood flowing in the same direction.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
The heart's rate and contractile strength are set by autonomic fibres — sympathetic increasing both, parasympathetic decreasing them — with neither responsible for initiating or propagating the impulse itself, which is the conducting system's job; the same plexuses also carry afferent sympathetic fibres conveying cardiac pain. Parasympathetic preganglionic fibres come from the vagal nuclei via cervical and thoracic cardiac branches of the vagi and the left recurrent laryngeal nerve, relaying in ganglia in the heart wall. Sympathetic preganglionic fibres arise in the lateral horn of the upper five thoracic segments, relay in the cervical and upper five thoracic ganglia, and travel as postganglionic cardiac branches. The superficial cardiac plexus, in the concavity of the aortic arch on the ligamentum arteriosum, is formed from the left sympathetic chain's superior cervical cardiac branch and the left vagus's inferior cervical cardiac branch, and sends branches to the deep plexus, the right coronary plexus and the left anterior pulmonary plexus. The deep cardiac plexus, in front of the tracheal bifurcation deep to the aortic arch, is formed from all cardiac branches of both sympathetic chains and both vagi, and sends branches to both atria directly and to both coronary and anterior pulmonary plexuses.

### Development
The coronary arteries' embryological origin from the aortic sinuses of the developing aortic root is covered, alongside the truncus arteriosus and conotruncal septation that produce those sinuses, in the development article on the bulbus cordis.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
The heart's four borders project as: an upper border, from the lower second left costal cartilage (4 cm from the midline) to the upper third right costal cartilage (3 cm from the midline); a right border, vertical and gently convex, to the sixth right costal cartilage (3 cm from the midline); a lower border, oblique, to the left fifth intercostal space (9 cm from the midline, the apex); and a left border, oblique and convex, back to the upper border's start. The coronary sulcus runs from the third left to the sixth right sternocostal junction; the anterior interventricular groove from the third left sternocostal junction to 1.5 cm right of the apex. All four valves project along or below the coronary sulcus, in the order PAMT from upper right to lower left: pulmonary (behind the third left costal cartilage at the sternum), aortic (behind the left sternal margin, third left intercostal space), mitral (behind the left half of the sternum, fourth left sternocostal junction) and tricuspid (behind the sternal centre, fourth intercostal space). Because sound propagation does not follow the valve's own anatomical position, each valve is auscultated at a separate site: pulmonary at the second left sternocostal junction, aortic at the second right, mitral at the apex, tricuspid at the xiphisternal junction.

### Clinical correlations
A narrowed coronary branch causes myocardial ischaemia, presenting as angina pectoris — agonising retrosternal pain referred to the left shoulder and the medial arm — while occlusion of a coronary artery or its main branch causes myocardial infarction, and because the right coronary artery supplies most of the conducting system, its occlusion carries a particular risk of arrhythmia. The heart is fixed in position by pericardial attachment (to the sternum by the sternopericardial ligaments, to the skull base indirectly via the pretracheal fascia and carotid sheath, to the diaphragm's central tendon below, to the mediastinal pleura laterally) together with the cardiac sling — the aortic arch and left pulmonary artery hooking round the left principal bronchus, the azygos arch and right pulmonary artery round the right principal bronchus, and the subclavian vessels hanging across the first rib at the thoracic inlet — a mechanism that fixes the base while leaving the ventricular mass free to contract.

## summary
Four great arteries — the pulmonary trunk and the three parts of the aorta — carry the whole output of both ventricles, and every one of their relations in this article exists because of one shared fact: the pulmonary trunk and ascending aorta both lie entirely inside the fibrous pericardium, wound around each other by the same spiral septum that split them apart in development.

## sections
### Overview and position
The pulmonary trunk carries deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle to the lungs; the aorta, the largest artery in the body, carries oxygenated blood from the left ventricle to the whole body and is divided in the thorax into ascending aorta, arch of the aorta and descending thoracic aorta.

### Structure
The pulmonary trunk, 5 cm long and lying wholly within the fibrous pericardium, begins at the pulmonary orifice behind the third left costal cartilage, runs up, back and left winding round the ascending aorta, and ends at the sternal-angle level, below the aortic arch and in front of the trachea, by dividing into right and left pulmonary arteries, the right longer and wider. The ligamentum arteriosum, the obliterated fetal ductus arteriosus, links the left pulmonary artery to the arch's concavity, with the superficial cardiac plexus on its right and the left recurrent laryngeal nerve on its left.

The ascending aorta, also 5 cm and wholly pericardial, begins at the aortic orifice behind the left sternal border opposite the third left intercostal space and ends behind the second right sternocostal junction as the arch; at its origin it shows three dilatations, the aortic sinuses, from which the right and left coronary arteries arise as its only branches. The arch of the aorta continues from there, passing up, back and left in front of the trachea then down its left side, ending on the left of the T4/T5 disc as the descending thoracic aorta, giving from its upper convexity the brachiocephalic, left common carotid and left subclavian arteries in that order. The descending thoracic aorta, about 20 cm and the longest thoracic segment, continues from the arch to the lower border of T12, where it passes through the diaphragm's aortic opening to become the abdominal aorta, giving nine pairs of posterior intercostal arteries (third to eleventh space), one pair of subcostal arteries, two left bronchial arteries, four or five oesophageal branches, and small twigs to the pericardium, mediastinum and diaphragm.

### Relations
The arch's lower concave aspect overlies the pulmonary trunk's bifurcation, the left principal bronchus, the ligamentum arteriosum, the superficial cardiac plexus and the left recurrent laryngeal nerve; its left anterior aspect is crossed by the left phrenic and vagus nerves, cardiac branches and the left superior intercostal vein, separating it from the left pleura and lung; its right posterior aspect relates to the trachea, the deep cardiac plexus at the tracheal bifurcation, the oesophagus, the left recurrent laryngeal nerve and the thoracic duct. The descending thoracic aorta lies anteriorly against the left principal bronchus, pericardium, oesophagus and diaphragm in sequence from above down; posteriorly against the lower five vertebral bodies and the two hemiazygos veins crossing at T8 and T9; on its right against the oesophagus above and the thoracic duct and azygos vein throughout; on its left against the pleura and lung.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
As arteries, these four vessels are themselves a blood supply rather than recipients of one; their own wall nutrition and innervation are not described separately in this chapter of the department book beyond the small pericardial, sternal and mediastinal twigs already noted for the internal thoracic artery elsewhere in the corpus.

### Development
The pulmonary trunk and ascending aorta arise together from the truncus arteriosus, split by the spiral aorticopulmonary septum — the reason the pulmonary trunk winds anterior, then left, then posterior around the ascending aorta as an adult. The arch of the aorta itself derives from four sources: the aortic sac's stem and left horn (proximal part), the left fourth aortic arch (middle part) and the left dorsal aorta down to the seventh cervical intersegmental artery (distal part). The ligamentum arteriosum is the fetal ductus arteriosus, the persisting dorsal segment of the left sixth aortic arch. Full coverage of the six pharyngeal arch arteries and their fates is in the development article.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
The department book gives no separate surface-marking table for these four vessels distinct from the heart's own border and valve projections already covered in the heart articles; the pulmonary trunk's origin, for example, is stated by its relation to the third left costal cartilage rather than by an independent surface line.

### Clinical correlations
Coarctation of the aorta, a narrowing that may involve the segment from the left subclavian artery to the ductus arteriosus, leaves the lower body's circulation dependent either on a still-patent ductus arteriosus or, if the narrowing lies distal to the ligamentum arteriosum, on collaterals between the subclavian and descending-aorta branches. An aortic aneurysm, a localised dilatation, can compress the superior mediastinum's contents to produce mediastinal syndrome, and rupture of a weakened aneurysm wall causes severe, often fatal haemorrhage without prompt surgery. Embolism of the pulmonary trunk by a blood clot is a common cause of sudden death.

## summary
Two brachiocephalic veins, one nearly vertical and one markedly oblique across the aortic arch's own branches, unite behind the first right costal cartilage to form the superior vena cava — a vessel with exactly one named tributary, the azygos vein, and one clinical lesson: which collateral route opens depends entirely on where along its length an obstruction sits relative to that single tributary.

## sections
### Overview and position
The two brachiocephalic veins and the superior vena cava together drain the upper half of the body — both upper limbs, the head and neck, and the thoracic wall — into the right atrium.

### Structure
The right brachiocephalic vein descends almost vertically in the superior mediastinum from the medial end of the right clavicle to the lower border of the first right costal cartilage, receiving the right internal thoracic vein, the right first posterior intercostal vein and the right lymphatic duct. The left brachiocephalic vein runs obliquely down and to the right behind the upper half of the manubrium, along the upper aspect of the aortic arch and in front of the origins of its three branches, receiving the left internal thoracic vein, the left first posterior intercostal vein, the left superior intercostal vein and the thoracic duct. The two unite behind the lower border of the first right costal cartilage, close to the sternum, to form the superior vena cava, about 5 cm long, its upper half in the superior mediastinum and its lower half in the middle mediastinum inside the fibrous pericardium, which it pierces at the second right costal cartilage. It ends by opening into the right atrium behind the third right costal cartilage.

### Relations
The superior vena cava's only tributary is the azygos vein, entering from behind at the second right costal cartilage, just before the cava pierces the pericardium — everything else that drains the upper body reaches the SVC indirectly, through the brachiocephalic veins upstream of this point.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
As large systemic veins, these vessels are not usefully described by their own blood or nerve supply in this chapter; their clinical significance lies entirely in what they drain and where an obstruction along their length redirects that flow.

### Development
The department book's thorax section gives no separate embryological account of the brachiocephalic veins or the superior vena cava; their formation from the embryonic common cardinal and anterior cardinal venous system is standard developmental material outside what this chapter states.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
The right brachiocephalic vein's surface line runs from the medial end of the right clavicle to the first right costal cartilage's lower border, close to the sternum; the superior vena cava's own surface line is a vertical line from that same point to the third right costal cartilage, close to the sternum's right margin.

### Clinical correlations
Obstruction of the superior vena cava is read differently depending on where it sits relative to the azygos vein's entry. If the obstruction lies above the azygos entry, venous drainage from the upper half of the body is diverted to the heart via the azygos system, which is still open below the block. If the obstruction lies below the azygos entry, blood from the SVC's territory instead returns via anastomotic channels between the superior and inferior venae cavae — the classic collateral pathways of SVC obstruction syndrome, which the book frames purely by this one anatomical rule.

## summary
Four nerves run the whole length of the thorax and each does so on a different plane: the phrenic nerve down the venous side on the right and the arterial side on the left; the vagus crossing the aortic arch on the left but never touching it on the right; the left recurrent laryngeal nerve hooking under the arch itself; and the sympathetic chain running lateral to the azygos vein on the right and the two hemiazygos veins on the left. This article covers all four together with the branches — cardiac, pulmonary, oesophageal, splanchnic — that make them the source of most of the thorax's autonomic supply.

## sections
### Overview and position
The four major nerves of the thorax are the two phrenic nerves, the two vagi, the left recurrent laryngeal nerve (a branch of the left vagus) and the two sympathetic trunks, together supplying the diaphragm's motor function, the parasympathetic and much of the sympathetic innervation of the thoracic viscera, and the splanchnic outflow to the abdomen.

### Structure
The phrenic nerve, from C3, 4 and 5, is mixed — motor to the diaphragm, sensory to the pleura, pericardium and peritoneum. Each vagus, the tenth cranial nerve and the longest, is mixed too — motor to pharyngeal, laryngeal and palatal muscles, sensory from the respiratory and digestive mucosa, and parasympathetic to the heart and to the smooth muscle and glands of those two systems. The left recurrent laryngeal nerve branches from the left vagus on the aortic arch. The thoracic sympathetic chain has eleven or twelve ganglia, the first often fused with the inferior cervical ganglion as the stellate ganglion, each connected to its spinal nerve by white and grey rami communicantes.

### Relations
Each phrenic nerve enters the thorax in front of the subclavian artery and behind the start of the brachiocephalic vein, crosses the internal thoracic artery laterally to medially, and descends in front of its lung's root. The right nerve, shorter because the right cupola is higher and its own course more vertical, runs down the right side of the right brachiocephalic vein, the SVC, the pericardium over the right atrium and the IVC, leaving through the vena caval opening; the left runs down the left side of the left common carotid and subclavian arteries, crossing in front of the left vagus, then the aortic arch (crossed there by the left superior intercostal vein), then the pericardium over the left ventricle, leaving by piercing the left cupola directly.

The right vagus crosses in front of the subclavian artery's first part, giving its recurrent laryngeal branch there, then descends on the trachea's right side behind the right brachiocephalic vein and upper SVC, crossed on its right by the azygos arch, to the back of the right bronchus. The left vagus descends between the left common carotid and subclavian arteries, behind the left brachiocephalic vein, crossed by the left phrenic nerve, then crosses the aortic arch's left anterior aspect (crossed there by the left superior intercostal vein) to the back of the left bronchus. Behind each lung root the nerve forms the posterior pulmonary plexus, with filaments to the anterior plexus; below the root, oesophageal plexus branches — right vagus mainly posterior, left mainly anterior; near the diaphragm, the two reform as the posterior and anterior gastric nerves.

The left recurrent laryngeal nerve hooks below the arch, behind and to the left of the ligamentum arteriosum, then ascends deep to the arch in the left tracheo-oesophageal groove to the neck; the right nerve's equivalent hook, by contrast, is around the right subclavian artery in the neck, not in the thorax at all.

The thoracic sympathetic chain continues the cervical chain from in front of the first rib's neck, lying against the rib necks above and the vertebral bodies below, the right chain lateral to the azygos vein, the left lateral to the two hemiazygos veins, covered throughout by costal pleura, ending behind the medial arcuate ligament to become the lumbar chain.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
The phrenic nerve's own branches are motor to the diaphragm and sensory to the pericardium, mediastinal and diaphragmatic pleura and the peritoneum on the diaphragm's abdominal surface. The vagus's thoracic branches are cardiac, pulmonary and oesophageal, plus the left recurrent laryngeal nerve, all carrying parasympathetic fibres to their corresponding plexuses; the recurrent laryngeal nerve itself gives cardiac branches to the deep cardiac plexus and tracheal and oesophageal twigs. The sympathetic chain's upper five ganglia send vascular and visceral branches to the aortic and oesophageal plexuses, the second to fourth to the cardiac and pulmonary plexuses; its lower ganglia give the greater splanchnic nerve (fifth to ninth ganglia, ending in the coeliac ganglion), the lesser splanchnic nerve (ninth and tenth, ending in the aorticorenal ganglion) and the lowest splanchnic nerve (the last ganglion, ending in the renal plexus).

### Development
The department book's thorax section gives no separate embryological account of these four nerves; the phrenic nerve's origin in the neck, tracking the diaphragm's own descent from the cervical region, is the one developmental fact stated, and is covered together with the diaphragm's development in that article.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
None of these four nerves has a surface projection described in this chapter; their courses are described entirely by their relations to named vessels, the trachea and the bronchi.

### Clinical correlations
An occasional accessory phrenic nerve, formed from C5 fibres, usually rejoins the main phrenic nerve at the first rib; where it exists, cutting the main phrenic nerve in the neck fails to achieve complete diaphragmatic paralysis. The left recurrent laryngeal nerve, hooking beneath the aortic arch, can be compressed by an aortic aneurysm or by enlarged tracheobronchial lymph nodes, producing hoarseness or a persistent cough — a mediastinal-syndrome presentation covered in full in the aortic arch article. Because the sympathetic chain's splanchnic nerves carry preganglionic efferent and visceral afferent fibres to the abdominal viscera, thoracic sympathectomy at these levels is used to interrupt visceral pain pathways.

## summary
Three named structures constrict the trachea, one at each of three separate causes; two of the four oesophageal constrictions are produced not by the oesophageal wall itself but by the aortic arch and the left bronchus pressing on it from outside — and the right bronchus's combination of being both wider and more in line with the trachea is the whole reason an inhaled foreign body so reliably ends up on that side.

## sections
### Overview and position
The trachea continues the larynx from the lower border of C6 in the neck to the sternal angle in the thorax, where it divides into the two principal bronchi; the oesophagus continues the pharynx from the same C6 level to the stomach at the lower border of T10, running through the neck, thorax and abdomen in series.

### Structure
The trachea, 10–12 cm long and 1.5–2 cm wide, is kept patent by sixteen to twenty C-shaped cartilage rings, their open backs joined by the trachealis muscle to let the oesophagus distend on swallowing; its lowest ring projects up between the two bronchi as the carina. The right principal bronchus is shorter (about 1 inch), wider and more in line with the trachea (25° from the midline), dividing into three lobar bronchi with the superior (eparterial) bronchus arising outside the hilum; the left is longer (about 2 inches), narrower and more horizontal (45° from the midline), dividing into only two lobar bronchi on entering the hilum. The thoracic oesophagus, part of a tube about 25 cm long overall, shows four constrictions along its length: at its beginning (17 cm from the incisor teeth), where crossed by the aortic arch (22 cm), where crossed by the left principal bronchus (27 cm), and at the diaphragm (42 cm).

### Relations
The trachea lies in the midline except at its lower end, where the aortic arch pushes it slightly right; it may be constricted at its upper end by the thyroid gland, in its middle by the brachiocephalic artery on the right, and at its lower end by the aortic arch on the left. Anteriorly it relates to the manubrium and the left brachiocephalic vein; posteriorly, the oesophagus separates it from the vertebral column, with the left recurrent laryngeal nerve between the two; on its right, the right vagus and paratracheal nodes, then the brachiocephalic artery, the right brachiocephalic vein and upper SVC, and the azygos arch below; on its left, the left subclavian and common carotid arteries above and the aortic arch below; at its bifurcation, the deep cardiac plexus in front and tracheobronchial nodes at the three angles between trachea and bronchi.

The oesophagus enters the thorax between the trachea in front and the vertebral column behind, running as the superior mediastinum's most posterior structure, to the right of the aortic arch; at the sternal angle it passes behind the left principal bronchus into the posterior mediastinum, descending along the right side of the descending thoracic aorta, behind the pericardium and the left atrium, to T7, then deviating forward and left, crossing in front of the aorta, to leave through the diaphragm's oesophageal opening at T10, one inch left of the midline.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
The trachea and bronchi take arterial supply from the bronchial arteries and venous drainage via the bronchial veins to the azygos system; lymphatics drain to the tracheobronchial and paratracheal nodes; autonomic supply is from the two vagi and their recurrent laryngeal branches and from the sympathetic chains — identical for the two bronchi despite their structural asymmetry. The thoracic oesophagus's arterial supply is by oesophageal branches from the descending aorta and the bronchial arteries, its venous drainage to the azygos and hemiazygos veins, and its lymphatics to the tracheobronchial, brachiocephalic and posterior mediastinal nodes; its nerve supply forms anterior and posterior oesophageal plexuses below the lung roots, mainly parasympathetic from the vagi with a few sympathetic fibres from the upper five thoracic ganglia — the right vagus contributing mainly to the posterior plexus (giving the posterior gastric nerve), the left mainly to the anterior (giving the anterior gastric nerve).

### Development
The trachea and bronchial tree's origin from the respiratory diverticulum, and the trachea's separation from the oesophagus by the tracheoesophageal septum, are covered in the development article; that same septum's incomplete partitioning is the origin of tracheoesophageal fistula, also covered there.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
The trachea's surface line is a 2 cm-wide band in the midline from the lower border of the larynx to the sternal angle, just right of the median plane. Oesophagoscopy measurements are taken from the upper incisor teeth, and the three constrictions at 7, 11 and 17 inches (17, 22 and 27 cm respectively, before the fourth at 42 cm) mark both the narrowest points, where swallowed foreign bodies most often lodge, and the sites most prone to stricture after a corrosive injury.

### Clinical correlations
A foreign body inhaled past the carina passes more often into the right bronchus, because it is both wider and more in line with the trachea than the left. The carina may widen or distort — visible on bronchoscopy — when the inferior tracheobronchial nodes are involved by cancer, and the trachea itself may be compressed or displaced by enlarged nodes or the aortic arch. The oesophagus's close relation to the base of the heart, particularly the left atrium, means an enlarged left atrium in mitral stenosis displaces the oesophagus backwards, a finding classically shown on a barium swallow.

## summary
One long duct on the left drains everywhere in the body except the right upper quadrant; one short, one-centimetre duct on the right drains exactly that remaining quadrant — the thoracic duct and the right lymphatic duct between them account for the whole of the body's lymph, and the six named groups of thoracic lymph nodes are simply the local stations lymph passes through on its way to one duct or the other.

## sections
### Overview and position
Lymph from almost the entire body converges on two vessels at the root of the neck: the thoracic duct, by far the longer, and the much shorter right lymphatic duct — together the final common pathway for lymph from six named groups of thoracic nodes as well as from the abdomen, pelvis and lower limbs.

### Structure
The thoracic duct, the largest lymphatic vessel in the body at about 45 cm, is a beaded, valved tube beginning in the abdomen at the lower border of T12 as the continuation of the cisterna chyli. The right lymphatic duct, by contrast, is only about 1 cm long, formed in the root of the neck by the union of the right jugular, right subclavian and right bronchomediastinal lymph trunks.

Six groups of nodes drain the thorax. Parasternal nodes, alongside the internal thoracic artery in the upper six spaces, drain the deep anterior wall and the medial breast. Intercostal nodes, at the posterior ends of the spaces, drain the deep posterolateral wall; together with paratracheal and brachiocephalic efferents they form the bronchomediastinal trunk. Diaphragmatic nodes (anterior, posterior, and two lateral phrenic groups) drain the diaphragm and, anteriorly, the liver's upper right surface. Brachiocephalic (anterior mediastinal) nodes, along the left brachiocephalic vein, drain the pericardium, heart and oesophagus. Posterior mediastinal nodes, near the oesophagus and descending aorta, drain the oesophagus and pericardium. The lung, bronchi and trachea drain through pulmonary nodes (within the lung), bronchopulmonary nodes (at the hilum), tracheobronchial nodes (inferior, at the bifurcation; superior, in the angle between trachea and each bronchus) and paratracheal nodes (alongside the trachea).

### Relations
The thoracic duct enters the thorax through the aortic opening of the diaphragm between the aorta on its left and the azygos vein on its right, ascends in the posterior mediastinum in front of the lower vertebral bodies, behind the oesophagus's right border, then at T5 crosses behind the oesophagus from right to left, continuing up in the superior mediastinum behind the oesophagus's left border and the aortic arch, to end at the root of the neck by opening into the start of the left brachiocephalic vein, at the junction of the left internal jugular and subclavian veins.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
The thoracic duct's own tributaries, besides the three abdominal trunks it indirectly receives from the cisterna chyli (intestinal, right and left lumbar), are in the thorax the posterior intercostal and posterior mediastinal nodes, and at its termination the left jugular, left subclavian and left bronchomediastinal trunks. Its field of drainage is therefore the two lower limbs, the abdomen (except the liver's upper right surface), and the left side of the thorax, upper limb, head and neck. The right lymphatic duct's field, correspondingly, is the right side of the head and neck, the right upper limb, the right side of the thorax, and the liver's upper right surface — opening into the angle between the right internal jugular and right subclavian veins. Every group's efferents feed one of these two ducts, most via the bronchomediastinal trunk (left half to the thoracic duct, right half to the right lymphatic duct, or in either case sometimes directly to the corresponding brachiocephalic vein), and the posterior mediastinal group directly to the thoracic duct.

### Development
The department book's thorax section gives no separate embryological account of the thoracic duct, right lymphatic duct or thoracic lymph nodes; lymphatic system development is standard embryology outside what this chapter states.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
The thoracic duct's termination at the junction of the left internal jugular and subclavian veins is the anatomical basis for its surgical exposure in the root of the neck; the department book gives no further radiographic or surface account of the duct or nodes.

### Clinical correlations
Because the thoracic duct's field of drainage covers the whole body except the right upper quadrant, injury to it in the thorax or neck — from trauma or surgery — can produce a chylothorax, and its termination at the neck's venous angle is the classic site of a palpable Virchow's node when malignancy spreads via this route from the abdomen. The bronchopulmonary and tracheobronchial nodes are the stations enlarged lymph-node disease in the chest — reactive, tuberculous or malignant — most often presents at, producing the widened carina or mediastinal shadow seen on imaging.

## summary
One outgrowth from the foregut, in the fourth week, becomes the whole respiratory tree down to seventeen prenatal generations of branching; one septum splits that outgrowth from the oesophagus running beside it, and when that septum's partitioning fails, the result is one of the named components of the VACTERL association. ## sections
### Overview and position
The respiratory system and the diaphragm develop from separate embryonic sources that meet at the thorax: the lung bud from an endodermal outgrowth of the foregut in the fourth week, and the diaphragm from four fused mesodermal components.

### Structure
The respiratory diverticulum (lung bud) appears in the fourth week from the ventral wall of the foregut; its epithelium — lining the larynx, trachea, bronchi and lungs — is endodermal, while its cartilage, muscle and connective tissue come from the surrounding splanchnic mesoderm. Initially it opens freely into the foregut; as it expands caudally, two tracheoesophageal ridges appear and fuse into the tracheoesophageal septum, dividing the foregut into a dorsal oesophagus and a ventral trachea and lung buds, the two remaining connected only through the laryngeal orifice.

As the laryngotracheal tube separates from the foregut, it forms the trachea and two bronchial buds; the right bud gives three secondary (lobar) bronchi, the left two, matching the adult lobar pattern; further division gives ten tertiary (segmental) bronchi on the right and eight on the left, establishing the bronchopulmonary segments — seventeen generations of subdivision occurring before birth and six more after. The larynx's lining is endodermal, from the pharyngeal gut floor; its cartilages and muscles come from the fourth and sixth pharyngeal-arch mesenchyme, whose rapid proliferation reshapes the laryngeal orifice from a sagittal slit to its adult T-shape, with temporary epithelial occlusion and subsequent recanalisation producing the laryngeal ventricles.

The diaphragm is entirely mesodermal, built from four fused sources. The septum transversum, formed in the neck and invaded by the third to fifth cervical myotomes (which is why the phrenic nerve, the nerve of these somites, follows it down), descends to the lower thoracic cavity and becomes the central tendon, initially leaving an incomplete septum — the pericardioperitoneal canal — on each side. The two pleuroperitoneal membranes grow medially to close those canals, becoming the dorsolateral parts. The body-wall mesoderm grows in to form the periphery. The oesophageal mesentery and the periaortic mesoderm form the crura.

### Relations
The septum transversum's cranial region becomes the central tendon of the diaphragm; its caudal region (ventral mesogastrium) becomes the lesser omentum and the falciform, coronary and triangular ligaments of the liver, and its central mesenchyme becomes the liver's own haematopoietic cells — three quite different adult structures from one embryonic layer, differentiated by region.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
The diaphragm's motor and sensory innervation is entirely from the phrenic nerves, a direct consequence of its cervical origin in the septum transversum; because the diaphragm's periphery is contributed by thoracic body-wall mesoderm rather than the septum transversum, some of the lower intercostal nerves also contribute sensory fibres to that peripheral part, matching the adult pattern given in the diaphragm article.

### Development
Covered throughout this article; see Structure and Relations above.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
The department book gives no surface or imaging account of these embryonic structures; their adult surface projections are covered in the companion articles on the trachea, bronchi and diaphragm.

### Clinical correlations
Respiratory distress syndrome follows failure of the alveoli to ventilate through absence of surfactant. Ectopic lung lobes may arise from the trachea or oesophagus, and congenital lung cysts from dilation of terminal or larger bronchi. Oesophageal atresia, with or without tracheoesophageal fistula, results from abnormal partitioning by the tracheoesophageal septum and is a recognised component of the VACTERL association — vertebral anomalies, anal atresia, cardiac defects, tracheoesophageal fistula, oesophageal atresia, renal anomalies and limb defects — so its presence prompts a search for the other six. Diaphragmatic anomalies map onto the four developmental sources: the parasternal hernia of Morgagni follows failure of a small part between the sternal and costal components; congenital hiatus hernia follows widening of the oesophageal opening with a short oesophagus; and the congenital diaphragmatic hernia of Bochdalek follows failure of the pleuroperitoneal membranes to close the pericardioperitoneal canal.

## summary
A single tube bends into an S shape in the third week and never straightens out again — every adult chamber relation, left ventricle at the apex, right atrium on the right border, traces back to that one looping event. Septation follows the same logic throughout: a new opening is deliberately made before the old one closes, in the atria as much as at the tricuspid valve's own developmental history, so a right-to-left shunt is never fully interrupted until the moment of birth itself closes it for good.

## sections
### Overview and position
The cardiovascular system is the first major system to function in the embryo, appearing in the middle of the third week. Mesenchymal cells in the cardiogenic area, cranial to the buccopharyngeal membrane, form two endocardial tubes that fuse into a single primitive heart tube, which comes to lie ventral to the foregut once the head fold forms.

### Structure
The heart tube itself becomes the endocardium; the surrounding splanchnic mesoderm becomes the myocardium and the epicardium. Three constrictions divide it, caudal to cranial after folding, into the sinus venosus, primitive atrium, primitive ventricle and bulbus cordis, with the narrow atrioventricular canal between atrium and ventricle. As the tube elongates it bends first C-shaped then S-shaped: the bulbus cordis moves inferiorly, ventrally and to the right; the primitive ventricle moves ventrally and to the left; the primitive atrium moves posteriorly and superiorly. Leftward looping instead of rightward produces dextrocardia, a full mirror-image reversal.

The sinus venosus, the tube's most caudal chamber, has a small median part and two horns, each receiving a vitelline, an umbilical and a common cardinal vein, opening into the primitive atrium through a sino-atrial orifice guarded by right and left venous valves. A left-to-right shunt directs most blood into the right horn, which enlarges to receive the SVC and IVC while the left horn shrinks and is pulled left to become the coronary sinus. After the interatrial septum forms, the right horn and median part are absorbed into the right atrium as the sinus venarum; the two venous valves fuse cranially into the septum spurium, which joins the interatrial septum on the left, while the right valve stretches and subdivides into the crista terminalis, the valve of the IVC and the valve of the coronary sinus.

The interatrial septum is built from three parts acting in sequence. Ventral and dorsal atrioventricular cushions fuse across the atrioventricular canal to form the septum intermedium, dividing it into right and left canals. Septum primum, sickle-shaped, grows down from the atrial roof towards the septum intermedium; the gap beneath it, the ostium primum, closes as the two fuse — but before it closes, the upper part of septum primum breaks down to open a new gap, the ostium secundum, so a new shunt opens before the old one shuts. Septum secundum then grows down to the right of septum primum, overlapping the ostium secundum and leaving an oblique passage, the foramen ovale, between septum primum's free edge and septum secundum's lower margin — permitting right-to-left flow but not the reverse.

The interventricular septum has a muscular part, a sickle-shaped ridge growing up from the ventricular floor towards the fused atrioventricular cushions, its concave edge leaving a crescent-shaped interventricular foramen; and a membranous part, closing that foramen, formed from an outgrowth of the ventral atrioventricular cushion together with the lowermost part of the conus septum.

### Relations
The right atrium's adult components are the right half of the primitive atrium (forming the anterior rough part and the auricle) plus the absorbed sinus venosus and the absorbed right atrioventricular canal; the left atrium's are the left half of the primitive atrium (forming the auricle only) plus the absorbed pulmonary veins — a single pulmonary vein that divides into right and left branches, each further dividing, before the stem and its branches are absorbed into the left atrial wall so that four pulmonary veins come to open separately.

### Blood supply, innervation and lymphatics
Not separately described in this chapter; the vessels these chambers connect to — the SVC, IVC, coronary sinus and pulmonary veins — are covered in the adult-anatomy heart-chambers article.

### Development
Covered throughout this article; see Structure and Relations above.

### Surface and imaging anatomy
The fossa ovalis and annulus ovalis, visible on the adult right atrial septal wall, are the anatomical remnants of septum primum and septum secundum respectively — the one developmental landmark from this article that persists into gross anatomy.

### Clinical correlations
Premature closure of the foramen ovale in utero causes massive right atrial and right ventricular hypertrophy with left-heart underdevelopment, usually fatal shortly after birth. Atrial septal defects take several forms depending on which step of septation fails: probe-patent foramen ovale (functionally closed but anatomically probe-passable, present in 20–25% of people); ostium secundum defect (septum secundum fails to overlap the ostium secundum, from excessive septum primum resorption or a short septum secundum, producing mixing, cyanosis and breathlessness); ostium primum defect (incomplete endocardial-cushion fusion, so septum primum fails to join the septum intermedium); and cor triloculare biventriculare, or common atrium, the most serious form, from complete failure of interatrial septum formation. Tricuspid atresia (complete agenesis of the tricuspid valve, with patent foramen ovale, VSD and right ventricular changes) and persistent atrioventricular canal (failure of ventral and dorsal cushion fusion, usually with both ASD and VSD) are anomalies of the septum intermedium. Most ventricular septal defects involve the membranous part of the septum, being assembled from two converging sources rather than growing as one continuous ridge like the muscular part; a muscular VSD is less common, and the whole septum may occasionally be absent.

## summary
A spiralling septum splits one arterial trunk into the aorta and the pulmonary trunk and, by spiralling rather than running straight, explains why the adult pulmonary trunk winds anterior-then-left-then-posterior around the aorta; six paired pharyngeal arch arteries reduce, asymmetrically, to the adult carotids, subclavians and the arch of the aorta itself, with only the left side keeping a sixth-arch remnant as the ductus arteriosus. At birth, the same physiology that made the fetal circulation work — right-to-left shunting through two separate channels — reverses in one breath, and both channels close for exactly the reason they were open: a pressure gradient.

## sections
### Overview and position
The bulbus cordis is the most cranial chamber of the primitive heart tube, giving rise to the outflow tracts of both ventricles and the roots of the aorta and pulmonary trunk; the aortic arches are six paired vessels that connect the aortic sac to the two dorsal aortae and whose asymmetric fates produce the adult great vessels of the neck and thorax.

### Structure
The bulbus cordis has three parts. Its proximal part becomes the main part of the right ventricle. Its midportion, the conus cordis, is absorbed into both ventricles as their smooth parts — the right ventricle's infundibulum and the left ventricle's aortic vestibule — its own conus ridges fusing into a conus septum that divides it accordingly and joins cranially with the aorticopulmonary septum as one conotruncal septum. Its distal part, the truncus arteriosus, is divided by two spiralling truncal ridges (left and right above, anterior and posterior in the middle, right and left below) that fuse into the spiral aorticopulmonary septum, splitting the truncus into the pulmonary trunk and ascending aorta and explaining why, in the adult, the pulmonary trunk lies anterior, then left, then posterior to the ascending aorta.

The right and left primitive aortae, continuous with the two endocardial heart tubes, each split after folding into a ventral aorta, a dorsal aorta and a first aortic arch. The two ventral aortae fuse into the aortic sac, composed of a stem and two horns, connected caudally to the truncus arteriosus and cranially to the six paired aortic arch arteries as the pharyngeal arches develop in turn — not all six present simultaneously. The two dorsal aortae fuse caudally into a single dorsal aorta.

### Relations
The aortic arches' fates diverge by pair. The first and second largely disappear, leaving only the maxillary and the hyoid/stapedial arteries. The third forms the common carotid artery and the proximal internal carotid on each side. The fourth forms part of the right subclavian artery on the right and the middle part of the aortic arch on the left. The fifth disappears completely. The sixth's ventral segment forms the pulmonary arteries on each side, while its dorsal segment disappears on the right but persists on the left as the ductus arteriosus, connecting the left pulmonary artery to the aortic arch.

The aortic sac's right horn becomes the brachiocephalic artery, continuous with the right common carotid (third arch) and right subclavian (fourth arch); its stem and left horn become the arch of the aorta's proximal part, with the middle part from the left fourth arch and the distal part from the left dorsal aorta down to the seventh cervical intersegmental artery. This asymmetric fate of the sixth arch and the dorsal aortae is also why the two recurrent laryngeal nerves, which hook around the sixth pharyngeal arch on their way to the larynx, end up with different adult courses: the right nerve moves up to hook around the right subclavian artery once the right sixth arch's distal part disappears, while the left nerve keeps its hook around the ductus arteriosus (the ligamentum arteriosum after birth) and the aortic arch itself.

Three further named anomalies follow the same fate table going wrong on one side: an abnormal right subclavian artery, when the right fourth arch and dorsal aorta disappear cranial to the seventh intersegmental artery, so the vessel instead develops from that intersegmental artery and the distal right dorsal aorta; a right-sided aortic arch, from persistence of the distal right dorsal aorta with degeneration of the distal left; and a double aortic arch, from persistence of both.

### Clinical correlations
Anterior displacement of the conotruncal septum, unequally dividing the conus, produces tetralogy of Fallot — pulmonary stenosis, right ventricular hypertrophy, ventricular septal defect and an overriding aorta receiving blood from both ventricles. Failure of the conotruncal ridges to fuse and descend produces persistent truncus arteriosus, a single arterial trunk with a VSD. Failure of the septum to follow its spiral course, running straight instead, produces transposition of the great arteries, with the aorta connected to the right ventricle and the pulmonary trunk to the left.

In the fetus, oxygenated placental blood travels by the left umbilical vein through the liver, via the ductus venosus to the IVC, into the right atrium, across to the left atrium through the still-patent foramen ovale, then to the left ventricle and out the aortic arch to the head, neck and upper limbs. Deoxygenated blood from the upper body reaches the right atrium by the SVC, passes to the right ventricle and the pulmonary trunk, and — since the lungs are not yet functioning — mostly bypasses them through the ductus arteriosus to the descending aorta and, via the umbilical arteries, back to the placenta. At birth, respiration expands the lungs and establishes the pulmonary circulation; left atrial pressure rises as pulmonary venous return increases while right atrial pressure falls as placental flow stops, and that reversed gradient presses septum primum against septum secundum, functionally closing the foramen ovale, while the ductus arteriosus closes separately by contraction of its own muscular wall. The left umbilical vein later fibroses into the ligamentum teres of the liver, the ductus venosus into the ligamentum venosum, the ductus arteriosus into the ligamentum arteriosum, and the distal umbilical arteries into the medial umbilical ligaments.

Coarctation of the aorta, a narrowing below the left subclavian artery's origin, follows the same process that normally obliterates the ductus arteriosus extending into the aortic wall itself; a preductal type, with the narrowing proximal to the ductus, lets the ductus persist, while a postductal type, with the narrowing distal to it, usually allows the ductus to obliterate as normal. Failure of the ductus arteriosus to close after birth is patent ductus arteriosus, producing a shunt between the aortic arch and the left pulmonary artery.

## hold_these
Bulbus cordis, 3 parts: proximal → right ventricle proper; conus cordis (middle) → infundibulum (right) + aortic vestibule (left); truncus arteriosus (distal) → split by the SPIRAL aorticopulmonary septum into pulmonary trunk + ascending aorta.
The spiral (not straight) course of the aorticopulmonary septum is why the adult pulmonary trunk winds anterior → left → posterior around the ascending aorta — get the septum straight instead of spiral and you get transposition of the great arteries.
Sixth aortic arch: ventral segment → pulmonary arteries (both sides); dorsal segment → disappears on the right, PERSISTS ON THE LEFT as the ductus arteriosus. This asymmetry is why PDA and the left recurrent laryngeal nerve's hook are both left-sided facts.
Fetal circulation shunts: ductus venosus (bypasses the liver) + foramen ovale and ductus arteriosus (both bypass the non-functioning lungs). At birth: rising left atrial pressure + falling right atrial pressure closes the foramen ovale; the ductus arteriosus closes separately, by its own muscular contraction.
Three conotruncal anomalies from three specific septal failures: anterior displacement → tetralogy of Fallot; failure to fuse/descend → persistent truncus arteriosus; failure of the spiral course → transposition of the great arteries.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming both sides keep a sixth-arch dorsal-segment remnant. Only the left does, as the ductus arteriosus; the right dorsal segment disappears completely — which is exactly why PDA and the left recurrent laryngeal nerve's course are both left-sided, not bilateral, facts.
Explaining foramen ovale closure as a purely mechanical event at birth. It is driven by a pressure reversal — rising left atrial pressure, falling right — that precedes and causes the septa's apposition; the ductus arteriosus closes by a separate mechanism, its own muscular contraction, not the same pressure change.
Treating right-sided aortic arch and double aortic arch as the same anomaly at different severities. One dorsal aorta persisting while the other degenerates gives a right-sided arch; both persisting together gives a double arch — two different failures of the same fate table.
