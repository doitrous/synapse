General microbiology: bacterial structure, genetics and virulence

## summary
This article groups the general-microbiology facts tested by the paper: bacterial growth requirements (obligate aerobes require oxygen), cell structure (pili mediate adhesion to host surfaces; the mesosome is the prokaryotic equivalent of mitochondria), bacterial genetics (generalized transduction occurs in the phage lytic cycle; plasmids are non-essential extrachromosomal DNA that may carry antimicrobial-resistance genes; phenotypic variation is reversible and non-heritable) and virulence (the capsule is the classic anti-phagocytic factor).
## sections
### Definition
The cluster covers the vocabulary of general bacteriology: how bacteria are classified by oxygen requirement, the surface and internal structures of the prokaryotic cell, the mechanisms of bacterial genetic exchange and variation, and the factors that make a bacterium virulent.
### Mechanism
Obligate aerobes use oxygen as the terminal electron acceptor of aerobic respiration, carried out at the mesosome (the membrane invagination that substitutes for the absent mitochondrion). Adhesion to host cells is mediated by pili whose adhesin tips bind host receptors. Genetic change arises by transduction (phage packages host DNA during the lytic cycle), by acquisition of plasmids (extrachromosomal DNA that can carry resistance genes), or reversibly by phenotypic adaptation to the environment. The capsule blocks opsonisation and phagocytosis.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: obligate aerobe versus facultative anaerobe/microaerophile/aerotolerant anaerobe; pili (adhesion) versus flagella (motility); mesosome versus ribosome/endospore/inclusion granule; transduction (lytic-cycle phage) versus transformation (naked DNA uptake) and conjugation (sex pilus); phenotypic (reversible, genome unchanged) versus genotypic (mutation/transfer) variation; capsule (anti-phagocytic) versus spreading enzymes and exotoxins.
### Clinical significance
These facts underlie clinical microbiology: capsules explain why encapsulated organisms cause invasive disease and why anti-capsular vaccines work; plasmid-borne resistance drives the spread of antimicrobial resistance; adhesion by pili is the first step of colonisation and a target for prevention; oxygen requirements dictate culture conditions.

## title
Systemic bacteriology: spirochaetes, Brucella, enteric salmonellae and staphylococci
## subject
inf
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Microbiology
## summary
This article groups the systemic-bacteriology facts tested by the paper: the spirochaetes (relapsing-fever Borrelia seen on a Giemsa blood film; Borrelia transmitted by tick and louse bites; Lyme disease caused by Borrelia burgdorferi), Brucella (Malta fever caused by Brucella melitensis, transmitted by unpasteurized milk, diagnosed by the standard tube agglutination test), the enteric salmonellae (TAB vaccine prevents enteric fever; early antibiotics cause a false-negative Widal test; Salmonella Choleraesuis causes septicaemia) and staphylococcal virulence (coagulase is the anti-phagocytic enzyme).
## sections
### Definition
The cluster covers the medically important systemic bacteria examined in 309 INF: the spirochaetes Borrelia, the intracellular zoonosis Brucella, the enteric salmonellae, and staphylococcal virulence enzymes - each defined by how it presents, how it is transmitted and how it is diagnosed.
### Mechanism
Borrelia circulate in blood during febrile episodes (visible on Giemsa films) and are delivered by tick/louse bites, with B. burgdorferi causing Lyme disease. Brucella, a facultative intracellular organism from farm animals, is acquired through unpasteurized milk and detected serologically by the tube agglutination test. The Widal test measures antibody to Salmonella Typhi, so early antibiotics that abort the antibody response cause false-negatives; Salmonella Choleraesuis is invasive and septicaemic; and the TAB vaccine primes immunity to the enteric-fever salmonellae. Coagulase clots fibrinogen into a protective fibrin coat that resists phagocytosis.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: relapsing fever (Giemsa film, tetracycline) versus Lyme disease (B. burgdorferi, Ixodes ticks, special media); tick/louse bite versus other routes; TAB versus other vaccines; false-negative Widal (early antibiotics) versus false-positive causes (endemic residence, vaccination); Salmonella Choleraesuis (septicaemia) versus food-poisoning and typhoid salmonellae; Brucella facts (goat/sheep reservoir, unpasteurized milk, facultative intracellular, tube agglutination); and coagulase (anti-phagocytic) versus the spreading enzymes.
### Clinical significance
These facts underpin the diagnosis and prevention of common febrile and zoonotic infections: recognising when to examine a blood film, which serological test to order, why a negative Widal does not exclude typhoid in a treated patient, which vaccine protects travellers, and why coagulase-positive staphylococci form walled-off abscesses.
### Common misconceptions
See each concept's pitfalls field for the exact reversal each distractor exploits (relapsing fever confused with Lyme; wrong transmission route for Borrelia; wrong serological test for brucellosis; false-negative confused with false-positive Widal causes; Salmonella species confusion; spreading enzymes mistaken for the anti-phagocytic one).
