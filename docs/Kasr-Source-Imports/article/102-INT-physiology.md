## summary
Vitamin B12 and folic acid are the two vitamins erythropoiesis fails without, and they fail it the same way: no DNA synthesis, so the nucleus cannot mature and divide while the cytoplasm goes on growing. What separates them is the road each takes to the marrow. Folate is mostly a dietary problem. Vitamin B12 is an absorption problem — it needs intrinsic factor from the stomach, trypsin from the pancreas and a receptor in the lower ileum — and only vitamin B12 is also needed to myelinate nerves, which is why only its deficiency adds neurological signs to the anaemia.

## sections
### Definition
All vitamins are needed for erythropoiesis, and the department book names three as especially so: vitamin C, vitamin B12 and folic acid. It then gives the last two a chapter to themselves, because they are the two whose lack produces one named anaemia.

Vitamin B12 is of animal origin — liver, meat and chicken. The daily requirement is 5 µg, and the vitamin is stored in the liver in big amounts, 5 mg.

Folic acid comes from green vegetables, some fruits, liver and meat. It is easily destroyed by cooking.

Read those two source lines against each other before anything else. An animal-source vitamin with a large hepatic store behaves nothing like a heat-labile vegetable one, and that difference is most of what the chapter goes on to say.

### Mechanism
Two questions are answered here: what the vitamin is for, and how it gets in.

Why the marrow needs it. The cells of the bone marrow are among the most rapidly growing and dividing cells of the body, so they need nuclear maturation and division, and that is achieved by DNA synthesis. Vitamin B12 is essential for the synthesis of DNA, and therefore for the division and final maturation of red cells. Vitamin B12 is also important for the myelination of nerves.

Folic acid is essential for DNA synthesis and therefore for the division and final maturation of red cells. The book gives folic acid that role and no other; myelination is listed for vitamin B12 alone.

How vitamin B12 gets in. The parietal cells of the stomach secrete a glycoprotein called intrinsic factor, which combines with vitamin B12 and protects it from being digested.

The intrinsic factor also helps to bind vitamin B12 to a specific receptor site in the brush border membrane of the mucosal cells in the lower ileum. Pancreatic trypsin is required for vitamin B12 absorption, so pancreatic deficiency disease may impair it.

What follows is three steps, and the book gives them in order. Vitamin B12 together with the intrinsic factor enters the intestinal cell in the lower ileum by pinocytosis. Inside the cell the vitamin is set free, transferred across the intestinal epithelium and absorbed into the blood, where it is bound to a plasma protein, transcobalamin II. Bound vitamin B12 is then efficiently distributed to the tissues, and the excess is stored in the liver.

Count the places that chain can break: the stomach that makes the intrinsic factor, the pancreas that makes the trypsin, the lower ileum that carries the receptor, and — last and least often — the diet. That count is the whole of the next section.

### Key determinants
The examined answer has three parts, because the question that produced this article asked for three: absorption, importance, and the manifestations of deficiency.

Absorption, in the book's own five moves. Parietal cells secrete intrinsic factor. Intrinsic factor binds vitamin B12 and protects it from digestion. Intrinsic factor binds the vitamin to a receptor on the brush border of the mucosal cells of the lower ileum. Pancreatic trypsin must be present. The complex enters the cell by pinocytosis, the vitamin is set free, crosses the epithelium, travels in blood on transcobalamin II, and is stored in the liver.

Importance, in two lines and no more. Vitamin B12 is essential for DNA synthesis, and therefore for the division and final maturation of red cells. Vitamin B12 is needed for the myelination of nerves.

Manifestations of deficiency, in two families. Macrocytic anaemia, and neurological manifestations.

The three parts are separately marked, and the commonest way to lose marks on this question is to answer the first part beautifully and stop.

### Normal values
Daily requirement of vitamin B12: 5 µg.

Hepatic store of vitamin B12: 5 mg, which the book calls a big amount.

Macrocytic anaemia is defined by an MCV above 95 μ3, with decreased red cell mass and haemoglobin content. That figure comes from the Anaemia chapter that follows this one, printed page 20, not from the vitamins chapter itself.

### Clinical significance
Deficiency of vitamin B12 is usually due to failure of absorption and not due to deficiency in the diet, except in vegetarians. That single sentence is the reason the absorption pathway is worth learning step by step: every step is a disease.

Vitamin B12 deficiency results in macrocytic anaemia. Diminished DNA leads to failure of nuclear maturation and division of the red cells in the bone marrow, so the red cells are bigger, irregular and very fragile. Vitamin B12 deficiency may also cause neurological manifestations, because vitamin B12 is needed for the myelination of the nerves.

Folic acid deficiency also leads to macrocytic anaemia, and the book gives three causes: deficiency in the diet; the presence of gastrointestinal disease interfering with its absorption; and intake of antifolate cytotoxic drugs such as methotrexate.

The Anaemia chapter immediately after this one sets both vitamins inside its third class, macrocytic or megaloblastic anaemia, and expands the causes. Folic acid deficiency there may be due to low intake in the diet, increased need as in pregnancy, failure of absorption, and antifolate cytotoxic drugs such as methotrexate. Vitamin B12 deficiency there may be due to defective absorption — for example after gastrectomy, absence of intrinsic factor, which is pernicious anaemia, and distal small intestine diseases.

Pernicious anaemia is the worked example of the whole chain failing at its first link. The book calls it a familial disease caused by an immune reaction against the parietal cells of the stomach; destruction of the parietal cells removes both hydrochloric acid and intrinsic factor, so vitamin B12 absorption fails, and the result is a macrocytic anaemia with nervous manifestations.

### Common misconceptions
A big red cell is not a hard-working red cell. The cell is big because the nucleus cannot mature and divide while the cytoplasm goes on growing. It is a failure of division, not a compensation, and the same failure makes the cell irregular in shape and very fragile.

A dietary history that looks adequate does not exclude vitamin B12 deficiency. Except in vegetarians, the deficiency is a failure of absorption. Asking what a patient eats answers the folate question far more often than the B12 one.

Intrinsic factor is not an enzyme and does not digest anything. It is a glycoprotein that does two jobs: it shields the vitamin from being digested, and it delivers it to a receptor in the lower ileum. Both jobs are marked.

The two vitamins do not produce identical pictures. Both give a macrocytic anaemia, because both are needed for DNA synthesis. Only vitamin B12 is listed as needed for myelination, so only vitamin B12 deficiency carries neurological manifestations in this book's account.

## summary
Blood carries everything it needs to clot and does not, and the department book asks why in one question worth 9 marks. Its answer is a numbered list of six mechanisms: healthy endothelium, heparin, the liver, continuous normal flow, the balance between thromboxane A2 and prostacyclin, and the fibrinolytic system. Learn them as six, in that order, each with the thing it disables. The book prints no split of the marks between them, so an answer that gives four of the six has left two out and nothing tells you which two were cheap.

## sections
### Definition
Normally blood circulates in a fluid state and only clots in pathological conditions, such as injury to the vessel wall or atherosclerosis. This is due to the presence of many physiological limiting mechanisms that prevent excess clotting.

That is the chapter's own framing, and it matters that the question is put as "what prevents intravascular clotting?" rather than "how is a clot dissolved?". The clotting cascade is always ready. What is being asked for is the set of brakes that keep it from running in an intact vessel, and the set that stop a real clot at the edge of a real injury.

The department book numbers six of them.

### Mechanism
**1. Healthy endothelium.** The endothelium is an antithrombotic and anticoagulant tissue that plays a major role in preventing blood from clotting under normal conditions, and the book gives it five separate actions. It acts as a barrier between the blood and subendothelial collagen, preventing activation of platelets and coagulation factors. Its surface is smooth and negatively charged, so it repels the negatively charged platelets and coagulation proteins. Healthy endothelial cells produce nitric oxide and prostacyclin, which are antiplatelet agents. Endothelial cells express thrombomodulin on their surface. And endothelial cells produce tissue plasminogen activator.

Two of those five are the seeds of mechanism six, which is why endothelium is item one and not an afterthought.

**2. Heparin.** Heparin is present in small amounts in the blood. It combines with antithrombin III in the blood to inactivate factors IX, X, XI and XII.

Note the shape of that sentence. Heparin does not inactivate anything by itself; antithrombin III is the inhibitor and heparin is what makes it work. Four factors, and they are the four the book names.

**3. The liver.** The liver inactivates any activated coagulation factors. One line, one mark, and it is the line most often left out — the same organ that manufactures the factors also clears them once they have been switched on.

**4. Continuous normal flow rate of blood.** Flow itself is a limiting mechanism, because stasis allows intravascular clotting to occur. The book's own example is that prolonged bed rest after operations can lead to thrombosis.

This is the mechanism that has nothing to do with chemistry, and it is the one students most often omit because it does not look like physiology. It is also the one with the most direct bedside consequence in the chapter.

**5. The balance between thromboxane A2 and prostacyclin.** It limits the clot to the site of injury, allowing blood to flow freely in the vessels.

The two halves of that balance are described earlier in the haemostasis chapter and are worth carrying here. Thromboxane A2 is produced from arachidonic acid in the membrane of active platelets by cyclooxygenase; it is a vasoconstrictor, a stimulator of the platelet release reaction and a powerful stimulant of platelet aggregation. Prostacyclin is produced from arachidonic acid in the endothelium of blood vessels by the same enzyme; it is a powerful vasodilator, an inhibitor of platelet release and an inhibitor of platelet aggregation. Because its actions are the opposite of those of thromboxane A2, prostacyclin keeps the platelet plug localised to the site of injury.

So this is not a brake on clotting in general. It is a boundary. Inside the injury, thromboxane A2 from activated platelets wins; on the intact endothelium a millimetre away, prostacyclin does.

**6. The fibrinolytic system.** All endothelial cells except those in the cerebral microcirculation express thrombomodulin, a thrombin-binding protein. Thrombin binds with thrombomodulin to form a thrombomodulin–thrombin complex, and that complex activates protein C.

Activated protein C, with its cofactor protein S, does two things. It inactivates factors V and VIII. And it inactivates an inhibitor of tissue plasminogen activator, which leads to that activator being released to act.

Activated tissue plasminogen activator then produces plasmin, also called fibrinolysin, from plasminogen, with the help of thrombin. Plasmin lyses fibrin and fibrinogen into fibrinogen degradation products, and those products themselves inhibit thrombin.

Follow what thrombin has just done. Thrombin is the enzyme the whole cascade exists to make, and here it binds thrombomodulin, switches on protein C, helps make plasmin, and ends up inhibited by the breakdown products of the fibrin it created. The most powerful pro-coagulant in the blood is also the trigger of its own limitation, and that is the single most quotable idea in the chapter.

### Key determinants
The six, in the book's order, each with what it disables. This is the 9-mark answer and it should be written as a numbered list, because that is how the book prints it.

One. Healthy endothelium: a barrier to subendothelial collagen; smooth and negatively charged; produces nitric oxide and prostacyclin; expresses thrombomodulin; produces tissue plasminogen activator.

Two. Heparin: present in small amounts, combines with antithrombin III to inactivate factors IX, X, XI and XII.

Three. The liver: inactivates any activated coagulation factors.

Four. Continuous normal flow rate: stasis allows intravascular clotting, and prolonged bed rest after operations can lead to thrombosis.

Five. The thromboxane A2–prostacyclin balance: limits the clot to the site of injury.

Six. The fibrinolytic system: thrombin plus thrombomodulin activates protein C; activated protein C with protein S inactivates factors V and VIII and frees tissue plasminogen activator; plasmin lyses fibrin and fibrinogen to fibrinogen degradation products, which inhibit thrombin.

Two lists of factor numbers sit inside that answer and they are not the same list. Antithrombin III, facilitated by heparin, takes out IX, X, XI and XII. Activated protein C, with protein S, takes out V and VIII. Getting those the wrong way round is a specific, checkable error.

### Applied physiology
The fibrinolytic system is the one of the six that has been turned into a treatment, and the book says so in the same chapter.

Human tissue plasminogen activator is produced by recombinant DNA techniques for clinical use. If it is given within 6 hours after the onset of a myocardial infarction, it lyses clots in the coronary arteries. Other fibrinolytic substances are used in the treatment of early myocardial infarction, such as the bacterial enzymes streptokinase and urokinase.

The thromboxane–prostacyclin balance has a treatment attached to it too, from the haemostasis chapter. Aspirin inhibits cyclooxygenase, so it reduces production of both thromboxane A2 and prostacyclin. The endothelial cells produce new cyclooxygenase within hours, whereas platelets cannot manufacture the enzyme at all. Small amounts of aspirin given over prolonged periods therefore reduce clot formation, and that is why aspirin has been of value in preventing myocardial infarction.

### Clinical significance
Read the six mechanisms as six ways to thrombose, because that is how the chapter's own examples work.

Damaged endothelium, in the book's opening sentence, is what atherosclerosis does, and it is named alongside injury to the vessel wall as a pathological condition in which blood clots. Stasis is the fourth mechanism failing, and the book's example is prolonged bed rest after an operation. Failure of the liver removes the clearance of activated factors — the chapter after this one adds that the liver needs vitamin K to make factors II, VII, IX and X together with proteins C and S, which is the same organ appearing on both sides of the balance.

The last point is worth stating plainly. Coagulation and its limitation are not two systems. They are one system with a set point, and the same liver, the same endothelium and the same thrombin appear in both halves of it.

### Common misconceptions
These are not anticoagulant drugs. The chapter that follows this one is called Anticoagulants and lists oxalate, citrate, silicone-coated tubes and heparin in vitro, and heparin and the coumarin derivatives in vivo. Warfarin and administered heparin answer a different question, and writing them here answers the wrong one.

The heparin in mechanism two is the body's own. It is present in small amounts in blood, and the book names mast cells and basophils as its origin in the anticoagulants table. It works the same way the injected drug does — by facilitating antithrombin III — but it is listed here as a physiological limitation, not as a treatment.

Fibrinolysis is not the same as anticoagulation. Mechanisms one to five stop a clot forming or spreading. Mechanism six dissolves fibrin that has already formed and, through protein C, also switches off factors V and VIII. The book puts both jobs inside item six because one complex does them both.

The endothelium is not passive. Calling it "just a smooth lining" gets one of its five actions and loses four, including the two that the fibrinolytic system depends on.

## hold_these
Blood circulates in a fluid state and clots only in pathological conditions such as injury to the vessel wall or atherosclerosis, because many physiological limiting mechanisms prevent excess clotting.
The department book numbers six limiting mechanisms: healthy endothelium, heparin, the liver, continuous normal flow rate, the thromboxane A2–prostacyclin balance, and the fibrinolytic system.
Healthy endothelium acts as a barrier to subendothelial collagen, is smooth and negatively charged, produces nitric oxide and prostacyclin, expresses thrombomodulin, and produces tissue plasminogen activator.
Heparin is present in small amounts in blood and combines with antithrombin III to inactivate factors IX, X, XI and XII.
The liver inactivates any activated coagulation factors.
Stasis allows intravascular clotting, which is why prolonged bed rest after operations can lead to thrombosis.
The balance between thromboxane A2 and prostacyclin limits the clot to the site of injury and lets blood flow freely elsewhere.
All endothelial cells except those in the cerebral microcirculation express thrombomodulin, and thrombin bound to thrombomodulin activates protein C.
Activated protein C with its cofactor protein S inactivates factors V and VIII and inactivates an inhibitor of tissue plasminogen activator.
Activated tissue plasminogen activator produces plasmin from plasminogen with the help of thrombin, and plasmin lyses fibrin and fibrinogen into fibrinogen degradation products, which inhibit thrombin.
Recombinant human tissue plasminogen activator lyses coronary clots if given within 6 hours of the onset of a myocardial infarction; streptokinase and urokinase are the other fibrinolytic substances the book names.

## summary
Every autonomic pathway is two neurons long, and the ganglion is where the changeover happens. A ganglion is a collection of neurons outside the central nervous system holding the mother cells of the postganglionic fibres, and because each preganglionic fibre meets 8 or 9 of them, the ganglion is also a distribution centre that spreads the output. Four types are taught and four are marked: the paravertebral sympathetic chain, the collateral ganglia, the terminal ganglia, and the adrenal medulla as a modified sympathetic ganglion. Three of those four are not exclusively sympathetic, and that is where marks are lost.

## sections
### Definition
A ganglion is a collection of neurons outside the central nervous system. It contains the mother neurons of the postganglionic nerve fibres.

That definition only means something against the structure of an autonomic pathway. The efferent pathways to the viscera differ from the somatic motor nerves that go to skeletal muscle in one decisive way: each somatic motor pathway from the cord to a skeletal muscle is a single fibre, a one-neuron system, while each autonomic involuntary pathway is composed of two fibres, the preganglionic and the postganglionic neurons — a two-neuron system.

The ganglion is the joint between those two neurons. No ganglion, no autonomic pathway.

### Mechanism
The cell bodies of the preganglionic neurons lie in the lateral horn of the spinal cord or in the motor nuclei of the cranial nerves. Their axons are mostly myelinated B fibres.

Each preganglionic axon synapses on 8 to 9 cell bodies of postganglionic neurons located outside the central nervous system. The postganglionic axons are mostly unmyelinated C fibres, and they end on the visceral effectors.

From that arrangement the book draws the function: autonomic ganglia act as distribution centres. Because each preganglionic fibre synapses with 8 or 9 ganglionic neurons, the autonomic output is diffused, and the ratio between preganglionic and postganglionic fibres is 1:8 or 1:9.

That number is the physiological point of the whole topic. A somatic motor neuron speaks to one motor unit; an autonomic preganglionic neuron speaks to nine cells at once, which is why autonomic responses are diffuse and slow rather than pinpointed and fast. The myelination follows the same logic — a myelinated B fibre carries the signal quickly out of the cord, and unmyelinated C fibres deliver it slowly over a wide field.

### Key determinants
Four types, in the book's order, each with its site and the division it relays.

**One, the paravertebral sympathetic chain.** It is present on both sides of the vertebral column. It has a ganglion for each segment of the spinal cord, except that there are only 3 ganglia for the cervical region — superior, middle and inferior cervical. These ganglia are for the relay of sympathetic fibres only.

**Two, the collateral ganglia.** They are present midway between the spinal cord and the viscera, at the origin of the big vessels arising from the abdominal aorta, and they are named according to the vessel's name. The ganglia for the relay of sympathetic fibres are the coeliac, superior mesenteric, aorticorenal and inferior mesenteric ganglia. The ganglia for the relay of parasympathetic fibres are the ciliary, sphenopalatine, submaxillary and otic ganglia.

**Three, the terminal ganglia.** They are present near or in the visceral organ itself. They are for the relay of parasympathetic fibres. The postganglionic parasympathetic fibres are very short, from 1 mm to several centimetres in length.

**Four, the adrenal medulla.** It is considered a modified sympathetic ganglion, in which the postganglionic cells have lost their axons and secrete catecholamines directly into the bloodstream. It is supplied by preganglionic cholinergic sympathetic fibres. Stimulation of the sympathetic nerves to the adrenal medulla releases large amounts of adrenaline, 80%, and noradrenaline, 20%, into the circulating blood. These hormones have almost the same effects on the different organs as direct sympathetic stimulation, except that their effects last 5 to 10 times longer, because the hormones are removed from the blood slowly.

Read the four as a sequence about distance from the cord. The chain is beside the vertebral column, the collateral ganglia are midway out, the terminal ganglia are at the organ, and the adrenal medulla has given up on axons altogether and posts its message through the blood. Only the first type is sympathetic and nothing else.

### Normal values
Preganglionic to postganglionic ratio: 1:8 or 1:9 — each preganglionic axon synapses on 8 to 9 postganglionic cell bodies.

Preganglionic axons: mostly myelinated B fibres. Postganglionic axons: mostly unmyelinated C fibres.

Cervical ganglia of the paravertebral chain: 3, where every other region has one per spinal segment.

Postganglionic parasympathetic fibre length: 1 mm to several centimetres.

Adrenal medullary output on sympathetic stimulation: adrenaline 80%, noradrenaline 20%, with effects lasting 5 to 10 times longer than direct sympathetic stimulation.

### Clinical significance
The adrenal medulla is the reason a sympathetic discharge outlives the nerve impulse that caused it. A ganglion that has lost its axons and secretes into blood turns a neural signal into a hormonal one, and the 5-to-10-fold longer duration is the measurable consequence.

The cervical part of the paravertebral chain is the part with a named lesion. The sympathetic chapter that follows gives Horner's syndrome as a lesion on one side of the cervical sympathetic chain, with four manifestations on the same side as the lesion: miosis, a constricted pupil relative to the opposite eye; ptosis, drop of the upper eyelid; anhidrosis, absence of sweating on the affected side of the head and neck; and warm, red skin from dilatation of the blood vessels of the head and neck. Every one of those is a function that had to relay in the superior cervical ganglion to reach its target.

The terminal ganglia explain a difference students meet again in pharmacology. Parasympathetic postganglionic fibres are 1 mm to a few centimetres long, so parasympathetic effects are local; sympathetic postganglionic fibres leave the chain and re-enter the spinal nerves to supply all parts of the body, and about 8% of the fibres in spinal nerves are sympathetic. Short last leg, focal effect; long last leg plus a hormonal ganglion, body-wide effect.

### Common misconceptions
Not every collateral ganglion is sympathetic. The abdominal ones are — coeliac, superior mesenteric, aorticorenal and inferior mesenteric — but the same numbered type in the same list also contains the ciliary, sphenopalatine, submaxillary and otic ganglia, which relay parasympathetic fibres. This is the single commonest error on this question.

A ganglion is not a relay station that simply passes a signal along. It multiplies it. One fibre in, 8 or 9 out, and the book calls the ganglia distribution centres for exactly that reason.

The adrenal medulla is not an endocrine gland that happens to be near the sympathetic system. It is a sympathetic ganglion whose postganglionic cells lost their axons, and it is supplied by preganglionic cholinergic sympathetic fibres like any other sympathetic ganglion. Its cells are the postganglionic neurons; their axons are missing, not their identity.

Only one of the four types is exclusively sympathetic. The paravertebral chain relays sympathetic fibres only; the collateral group relays both; the terminal ganglia relay parasympathetic fibres; and the adrenal medulla is sympathetic but is not a relay at all in the ordinary sense.

## summary
The parasympathetic system is cranio-sacral, anabolic and energy-preserving — rest, digest, reading. It leaves the brain in four cranial nerves and the cord in the second, third and fourth sacral segments, and one of those nerves does most of the work: the vagus carries about 75% of all parasympathetic fibres and supplies the entire thoracic and abdominal viscera. Its effects run organ by organ, and one of them is a negative: the vagus does not supply the ventricles at all.

## sections
### Definition
The parasympathetic nervous system is the cranio-sacral division of the autonomic outflow. The department book labels it in one line — anabolic and energy-preserving system, rest, digest, reading — and sets it directly against the sympathetic system, which it labels catabolic and energy-consuming, stress, fight, fear and flight.

Like every autonomic pathway it is a two-neuron system. What is distinctive is where the second neuron sits. Parasympathetic preganglionic fibres are long and relay late, in terminal ganglia inside or close to the organ, so the postganglionic fibres are only 1 mm to a few centimetres long. That is why parasympathetic effects are discrete and organ-by-organ, where sympathetic discharge tends to go off as one unit.

The chapter is organised by region, and so is the answer to any question on it: head and neck, thoracic and abdominal viscera, pelvic viscera.

### Mechanism
**Head and neck: four cranial nerves, four ganglia.**

The oculomotor nerve, cranial nerve III. Preganglionic fibres arise from the Edinger–Westphal nucleus in the midbrain and relay in the ciliary ganglion. The postganglionic fibres enter the eye as the short ciliary nerves. They contract the constrictor pupillae muscle, constricting the pupil, which is miosis; and they contract the ciliary muscle, increasing the power of the lens and preparing the eye for near vision.

The facial nerve, cranial nerve VII. Preganglionic fibres arise from the superior salivary nucleus in the lower pons. Some relay in the sphenopalatine ganglion; others run in the chorda tympani, a branch of the facial nerve, to relay in the submandibular ganglion. Postganglionic fibres from the sphenopalatine ganglion supply the lacrimal and nasal glands; postganglionic fibres from the submandibular ganglion supply the submandibular and sublingual salivary glands. The function is secretomotor and vasodilator.

The glossopharyngeal nerve, cranial nerve IX. Preganglionic fibres arise from the inferior salivary nucleus at the junction of the pons and medulla and relay in the otic ganglion. Postganglionic fibres supply the parotid gland, and the function is again secretomotor and vasodilator.

**Thoracic and abdominal viscera: the vagus, cranial nerve X.**

Preganglionic fibres arise from the vagal nucleus in the medulla oblongata. They run in the vagus nerve to supply the entire thoracic and abdominal viscera, and they constitute about 75% of all parasympathetic fibres. They relay in terminal ganglia, inside or close to the organ.

On the heart, three statements, and the first is a negative. The vagus nerve does not supply the ventricles. It produces inhibition of all properties of atrial cardiac muscle only. And it decreases coronary blood flow and oxygen consumption.

On the lungs: bronchoconstriction, dilatation of the pulmonary blood vessels, and stimulation of the bronchial glands.

On the gastrointestinal tract: contraction of the plain muscles of the wall of the oesophagus, stomach, small intestine and proximal part of the large intestine; relaxation of the sphincters; and a secretory action on the gastrointestinal glands, the liver and the pancreas.

On the gall bladder: evacuation, by contraction of the wall and relaxation of the sphincter of Oddi.

Notice the pattern that runs through the last two. Wherever the vagus meets a tube with a valve on it, it squeezes the tube and opens the valve. That is one rule covering gut motility, gut sphincters and the gall bladder together, and it is the exact mirror of what the sympathetic system does to the same structures — relaxation of the wall, contraction of the sphincters.

**Pelvic viscera: the sacral outflow.**

Preganglionic fibres arise from the second, third and fourth sacral segments and run as the pelvic splanchnic nerves, the nervi erigentes. They relay in terminal ganglia inside or close to the organ.

They produce defecation, by contraction of the wall of the rectum and relaxation of the internal anal sphincter; micturition, by contraction of the wall of the bladder and relaxation of the internal urethral sphincter; erection, by vasodilatation of the blood vessels of the penis; and vasodilatation in the female genital organs.

The same rule holds here. Wall contracts, sphincter relaxes.

### Key determinants
For any region, four things are marked and the book supplies all four in the same order: origin, course, relay, functions.

The vagal supply of the thoracic and abdominal viscera, which is what the 2025 paper asked for, sets out as follows. Origin: the vagal nucleus in the medulla oblongata. Course: preganglionic fibres run in the vagus to supply the entire thoracic and abdominal viscera, about 75% of all parasympathetic fibres. Relay: terminal ganglia, inside or close to the organ. Functions: on the heart, no ventricular supply, inhibition of all properties of atrial muscle only, and decreased coronary blood flow and oxygen consumption; on the lungs, bronchoconstriction, pulmonary vasodilatation and stimulation of the bronchial glands; on the gastrointestinal tract, contraction of the wall from oesophagus to the proximal large intestine, relaxation of the sphincters, and secretion from the gut glands, liver and pancreas; on the gall bladder, contraction of the wall and relaxation of the sphincter of Oddi.

Four organs, and each carries its own marks. An answer that gives the heart in detail and forgets the gall bladder has answered three-quarters of the question.

For the head and neck the same four headings apply three times over, once per cranial nerve, and the ganglion is the item most often dropped: ciliary for the third nerve, sphenopalatine and submandibular for the seventh, otic for the ninth.

### Applied physiology
The chapter closes with the centres of the autonomic reflexes, and it belongs here because it says where all of the above is integrated.

The levels of autonomic integration within the central nervous system are variable. Simple reflexes are integrated in the spinal cord; more complex reflexes are integrated at higher levels in the central nervous system.

The book gives three examples. Contraction of the full bladder, a simple reflex, is integrated in the sacral segments of the spinal cord. The more complex reflexes that regulate respiration and blood pressure are integrated in the medulla oblongata. The reflexes that control pupillary responses are integrated in the midbrain.

Line those three up against the outflow described above and they match it: bladder at S2 to S4, cardiovascular and respiratory control in the medulla where the vagal nucleus lies, and pupillary control in the midbrain where the Edinger–Westphal nucleus lies. The centre sits where the outflow starts.

### Clinical significance
Every function in this chapter is something a clinician looks for, and the book's own statements carry the significance without needing anything added.

The pupil and the lens are the third nerve's parasympathetic output, so miosis and accommodation for near vision are its testable signs — and they are the mirror of the sympathetic pupil, which dilates and widens the palpebral fissure.

The salivary and lacrimal glands are the seventh and ninth nerves' output, and the function is secretomotor and vasodilator. The sympathetic supply to the salivary glands, in the chapter before, produces a trophic secretion that is concentrated, small in amount and viscid. Wet mouth against dry mouth is the difference between the two supplies.

On the heart the marked fact is a limit rather than an effect. The vagus does not supply the ventricles; it inhibits the properties of atrial muscle only, and it decreases coronary blood flow and oxygen consumption. That is why vagal activity is a statement about the atria and the pacemaker, not about ventricular force.

Bronchoconstriction and stimulation of the bronchial glands are vagal, and they sit opposite the sympathetic bronchodilatation described in the chapter before.

The pelvic outflow is the one whose functions are named as events rather than as tissue responses: defecation, micturition, erection. The book's own name for the nerve, nervi erigentes, records the third of them.

### Common misconceptions
The vagus does not slow the whole heart. It does not supply the ventricles at all. It inhibits all the properties of atrial cardiac muscle only, and that is the sentence the mark is on.

Seventy-five per cent is a share of fibres, not of function. The book says the vagal preganglionic fibres constitute about 75% of all parasympathetic fibres, which is a statement about how much of the outflow runs in one nerve, not about how much of the body it covers.

Parasympathetic relay is not always in a terminal ganglion. The thoracic, abdominal and pelvic outflows relay in terminal ganglia, but the head and neck outflow relays in four named ganglia that the autonomic ganglia chapter classes as collateral: ciliary, sphenopalatine, submaxillary and otic.

Relaxation of the sphincters is a parasympathetic action, not a sympathetic one. The sympathetic system contracts the sphincters and relaxes the walls; the parasympathetic system does the opposite in the gut, the bladder, the rectum and the biliary tract alike.

Bronchoconstriction is easy to attribute to the wrong division because it feels like a stress response. It is vagal, and it comes with pulmonary vasodilatation and bronchial gland secretion.

## summary
Plasma proteins are 7.2 to 7.4 g/dl of albumin, globulins, fibrinogen and the other clotting factors, and they are made in two places: the liver makes everything except the gamma globulins, which plasma cells in lymphoid tissue make. The department book then gives them eight functions, and the paper asks for six of them. Learn each function with the protein that carries it — albumin holds the water, gamma globulins do the defending, fibrinogen clots the blood and also thickens it — because a function without its protein is half a mark.

## sections
### Definition
Plasma is a yellow clear fluid containing many ions and inorganic and organic molecules, transported to various parts of the body, with a normal volume of about 3000 to 3500 ml. When blood is left in a glass tube a clot forms, and the fluid that remains is serum: plasma minus fibrinogen and the other clotting factors.

Ninety per cent of plasma volume is water and 10% is inorganic and organic substances. The inorganic substances are sodium, the chief cation; chloride and bicarbonate, the chief anions; and small amounts of phosphates and sulfates. The organic substances are the plasma proteins, the plasma lipids — cholesterol, triglycerides, fatty acids and phospholipids, carried in the blood combined with plasma proteins as lipoproteins — and other organic substances such as glucose, amino acids, vitamins, enzymes and the waste products uric acid and urea. Oxygen, carbon dioxide and nitrogen are dissolved in plasma as the blood gases.

The concentration of plasma proteins is 7.2 to 7.4 g/dl. They consist of albumin, globulins, fibrinogen and other coagulation factors, and the globulins subdivide into alpha, with subtypes 1 and 2, beta, with subtypes 1 and 2, and gamma.

Serum is the definition to hold onto, because it is the difference between the two halves of this chapter. Take fibrinogen and the clotting factors out of plasma and what is left still carries albumin, still carries the globulins, and still does most of the functions below.

### Mechanism
Formation has two sites and the answer is marked on both.

The liver is the main site for synthesis of plasma proteins, and albumin, fibrinogen, the coagulation factors and 50% of globulins are formed in the liver.

Gamma globulins are formed by plasma cells in the lymphoid tissue.

That split is what makes the albumin-globulin ratio readable. The ratio between albumin and globulin concentration in the plasma is normally between 1.2 and 1.6, and the book gives three conditions in which it falls. In liver disease the liver is unable to produce sufficient albumin, as in cirrhosis and hepatitis. In renal disease albumin is lost in urine, because of its relatively small molecular size. In infection the ratio falls the other way round, because immunoglobulin formation increases.

Read those three against the two sites of formation. The numerator fails when the liver fails or when the kidney leaks; the denominator rises when the plasma cells are working. One ratio, three different lesions, and you can tell which one is acting by asking which half of it moved.

The osmotic function is the one with a mechanism to follow, and it turns on a permeability rather than on a concentration.

The total osmotic pressure of plasma is 290 mmol/L, which is 5000 mmHg, and most of it is due to the crystalloids — sodium, chloride and bicarbonate. Plasma proteins are responsible for only 25 to 28 mmHg of it, and that little contribution is very effective regarding fluid movement across the capillaries.

The reason is stated in the next line of the book. The capillary membrane is impermeable to proteins, because of their big molecular weight, so plasma proteins are kept inside the plasma and exert an osmotic effect that draws tissue fluid into the blood — the effective colloidal osmotic pressure. Albumin is the main plasma protein responsible for it, because it has the greatest concentration, and osmotic pressure depends on the number of particles in the solution.

So the marked point is not that 25 to 28 mmHg is a large pressure. It is that it is the only part of the 5000 that acts across the capillary wall, and that the protein doing most of the acting is the one present in the greatest number of particles rather than the largest one.

### Key determinants
The book lists eight functions and the 2024 paper asked for six, so learn all eight and choose. Each is given here with the protein the book attaches to it.

One, the osmotic function. Plasma proteins supply the 25 to 28 mmHg of effective colloidal osmotic pressure that draws tissue fluid into the blood, and albumin is mainly responsible.

Two, the buffer function. Plasma proteins are responsible for 15% of the buffering power of the blood. At normal plasma pH of 7.4 they are negatively charged and act as anions, weak acids, which combine with bases, mainly sodium bicarbonate, forming the buffer system proteinic acid-sodium proteinate.

Three, the defensive function. Gamma globulins are responsible for defending the body against microorganisms and their toxins, which is humoral immunity.

Four, blood clotting. Fibrinogen and all the clotting factors present in plasma are responsible for blood coagulation.

Five, blood viscosity. Blood is 3 times and plasma 1.5 times as viscous as water. Viscosity is important in maintaining normal arterial blood pressure. Red cells and plasma proteins are responsible for it, and fibrinogen contributes more than the other proteins because of its elongated shape. Viscosity increases in polycythaemia and decreases in anaemia.

Six, the capillary function. Plasma proteins are essential for maintaining normal capillary permeability, because the pores in the capillary walls are closed by plasma proteins. Deficiency of plasma proteins increases capillary permeability.

Seven, transport and conservation of important elements. Albumin and the alpha and beta globulins act as carriers for hormones, vitamins, lipids and minerals, and binding to a plasma protein prevents their rapid loss in urine and provides a reservoir to be used when needed.

Eight, a source of amino acids for tissues. Plasma proteins allow rapid replacement of tissue proteins when tissue protein is depleted.

Two of the eight are worth pairing deliberately, because they use the same protein for opposite-sounding jobs. Fibrinogen clots the blood, function four, and fibrinogen makes the blood viscous, function five — not because it is abundant, it is the least abundant of the three, but because it is long and thin.

### Normal values
Plasma protein concentration: 7.2 to 7.4 g/dl.

Albumin 3.5 to 5.0 g/dl, molecular weight 69,000. Globulin 2.5 g/dl, molecular weight 90,000 to 156,000. Fibrinogen 0.4 g/dl, molecular weight 340,000.

Albumin-globulin ratio: 1.2 to 1.6.

Plasma volume: about 3000 to 3500 ml. Water is 90% of plasma volume.

Total plasma osmotic pressure: 290 mmol/L, equal to 5000 mmHg. The plasma proteins' share is 25 to 28 mmHg.

Share of blood buffering power carried by plasma proteins: 15%.

Viscosity: blood 3 times, plasma 1.5 times that of water.

### Clinical significance
The albumin-globulin ratio is the chapter's own clinical instrument, and its three causes map onto three organs. Liver disease reduces albumin synthesis. Renal disease loses albumin in urine, and the book gives the reason — albumin's relatively small molecular size, 69,000 against a globulin's 90,000 to 156,000. Infection raises immunoglobulins. Two of those lower the top of the ratio and one raises the bottom.

The molecular weight table earns its place here rather than as trivia. Albumin is the smallest of the three and the most concentrated, which is why it is both the protein that leaks first through a damaged glomerulus and the protein that carries most of the colloidal osmotic pressure.

Deficiency of plasma proteins increases capillary permeability. That is the book's own statement of what happens when the pores are no longer closed, and it is the sentence a question about protein deficiency is marked against.

Viscosity connects this chapter to two later ones. It rises in polycythaemia and falls in anaemia, and it is what keeps arterial blood pressure normal, so a change in the red cell count is also a change in the load the heart works against.

### Common misconceptions
Twenty-five to twenty-eight millimetres of mercury is not a small effect. It is one two-hundredth of the total plasma osmotic pressure and it is the part that matters, because the capillary membrane is impermeable to proteins and permeable to the crystalloids that supply the rest. The book calls the little contribution very effective for exactly that reason.

Albumin does not dominate the colloidal osmotic pressure because it is the biggest protein. It is the smallest of the three. It dominates because it is present in the greatest concentration, and osmotic pressure depends on the number of particles.

Serum is not plasma with the cells removed. That is what plasma already is. Serum is plasma minus fibrinogen and the other clotting factors, which is why a serum sample cannot be used to study coagulation.

Gamma globulins are not made in the liver. The liver makes albumin, fibrinogen, the coagulation factors and 50% of globulins; the gamma globulins come from plasma cells in lymphoid tissue, and that is half of a two-mark answer.

Fibrinogen is not a minor protein because it is the least concentrated. At 0.4 g/dl it still contributes more to viscosity than any other protein, because of its elongated shape, and it carries the whole of function four.

## summary
Haemostasis is prevention of blood loss after injury, and the department book runs it in four steps: the vessel constricts, the platelets build a temporary plug, coagulation turns that plug into a definitive clot with fibrin, and plasmin later dissolves it while growth factors repair the wall. The examined heart of the chapter is one pair of molecules. Thromboxane A2 from the platelet and prostacyclin from the endothelium come from the same substrate through the same enzyme and do the opposite things, and that opposition is what keeps the plug at the edge of the injury. Aspirin works because it blocks that enzyme in both cells and only one of them can build it again.

## sections
### Definition
Platelets are small, granulated, non-nucleated round or oval bodies. The normal count is 200,000 to 400,000 per mm3 and the lifespan is about 8 days. They are produced in bone marrow from megakaryocytes: myeloid stem cells, under the effect of thrombopoietin produced by the liver and kidneys, differentiate into megakaryocytes. Seventy per cent of the platelets extruded from the bone marrow pass into the blood and 30% are stored in the spleen, which is why splenectomy increases the platelet count — thrombocytosis.

Haemostasis means prevention of blood loss after injury. Whenever a blood vessel is cut, a series of changes occurs to stop blood loss and allow repair of the injured vessel.

The book numbers four steps and every later section belongs to one of them. Constriction of the blood vessel. Formation of a temporary haemostatic plug, which is primary haemostasis. Conversion of that temporary platelet plug into a definitive clot by fibrin threads produced by blood coagulation, which is secondary haemostasis. And dissolution of the clot by plasmin, with repair of the vascular endothelium and the smooth muscle of the vessel wall by platelet derived growth factor and vascular endothelial growth factor released from the platelets.

Count what the platelet does across those four steps: it releases the vasoconstrictors in step one, it is step two, it supplies the surface and several factors for step three, and it supplies the growth factors for step four. Nothing else in the chapter appears in all four.

### Mechanism
Constriction comes first. The constriction of an injured vessel occurs immediately after injury and may be so marked that the lumen is completely closed. The book gives four causes: local myogenic contraction from direct trauma to the smooth muscle of the vessel wall; nervous reflexes initiated by stimulation of pain receptors in the subendothelial layer of the traumatised vessel; local humoral factors released from platelets, which are ADP, serotonin and thromboxane A2; and endothelin, a vasoconstrictor secreted from injured endothelial cells.

Then the plug. Injury stimulates the platelets to form a mechanical plug to seal the vascular injury. If the cut is small the platelet plug alone can stop blood loss completely; if it is large, an additional blood clot is required. A blood clot is a platelet plug entangled within fibrin threads.

Six platelet reactions build that plug, in order.

Adhesion. Normally platelets circulate freely and do not adhere to the endothelial surface. When vascular surfaces are damaged, platelets adhere to the exposed subendothelial collagen and von Willebrand factor through the glycoprotein receptors on their membrane. Von Willebrand factor is a glycoprotein formed by endothelial cells, found in subendothelial tissue and also present in plasma bound to factor VIII.

Activation. Adhesion initiates an intracellular signalling pathway that activates the platelets. They swell, change shape, put out pseudopodia and discharge the contents of their granules, and activation is enhanced by ADP and thrombin.

The release reaction, which is calcium dependent. The dense granules release calcium, which leads to more release; ADP, which leads to activation, aggregation and fusion of platelets; and serotonin, which reinforces and maintains vasoconstriction of the injured vessel. The alpha granules release coagulation factors — fibrinogen, factor V, von Willebrand factor and factor XIII; platelet derived growth factor, which stimulates vessel wall repair by stimulating growth and multiplication of vascular endothelium, smooth muscle and fibroblasts; platelet activating factor, which stimulates platelet aggregation; and thromboxane A2.

Thromboxane A2 is produced from arachidonic acid in the membrane of active platelets by cyclooxygenase enzyme. It is a vasoconstrictor, a stimulator of the platelet release reaction, and a powerful stimulant of platelet aggregation.

Prostacyclin is produced from arachidonic acid in the endothelium of blood vessels by the action of the same cyclooxygenase enzyme. It is a powerful vasodilator, an inhibitor of the release of platelets, and an inhibitor of the aggregation of platelets. Since its actions are opposite to those of thromboxane A2, prostacyclin keeps the platelet plug localised to the site of injury.

Aggregation. Released ADP and thromboxane A2 cause platelets to bind to each other at the site of injury through fibrinogen receptors, with receptor-bound fibrinogen acting as a bridge between two receptor molecules on adjacent platelets. Aggregated platelets release more granule contents, liberating more ADP and thromboxane A2 and causing more aggregation, and aggregation is also stimulated by platelet activating factor. This self-propagating process forms the platelet plug.

Pro-coagulant activity. During these events the platelets act as catalysts for coagulation: the membrane surface phospholipid, PF3, is exposed and provides an ideal surface for concentration and activation of the coagulation factors.

Fusion. Irreversible fusion of the aggregated platelets at the site of injury, caused by high concentrations of ADP and thrombin and by enzymes liberated during the release reaction.

Then coagulation, which is the production of fibrin. The platelet plug is weak and can be washed away by the blood; fibrin stabilises the plug and closes the injured vessel.

The coagulation factors are plasma proteins, mainly beta globulins, formed by the liver. They are enzymes circulating in an inactive form, and when activated they run a chain of proteolytic reactions activating each other in a cascade. Most are known by capital Roman numerals except the first four, usually called by name. They fall into three groups. The fibrinogen group is factors I, V, VIII and XIII, activated by thrombin. The prothrombin group is factors II, VII, IX and X, all of which need vitamin K for their synthesis in the liver. The contact group is factors XI and XII, activated by contact with an electronegatively charged surface.

The intrinsic pathway occurs both in vivo and in vitro. It is initiated by conversion of inactive factor XII to active XIIa when blood is exposed to collagen fibres beneath the endothelium, catalysed by two circulating proteins, high-molecular-weight kininogen and plasma kallikrein. Active XII activates XI, which activates IX. Factor VIII is activated when thrombin proteolytically separates it from von Willebrand factor, and activated IX forms a complex with VIIIa. The complex of IXa, VIIIa, platelet phospholipids and calcium activates factor X. Active factor X with phospholipid, calcium and Va together are the prothrombin activators, which convert prothrombin to thrombin. In vitro, factor XII can be activated by exposing blood to an electronegatively charged wettable surface such as glass.

The extrinsic system occurs only in vivo. Tissue trauma, vascular or extravascular, releases thromboplastin — tissue phospholipid — which activates factor VII. Thromboplastin and VIIa activate factors IX and X, and active X with phospholipid, calcium and active V converts prothrombin to thrombin.

The common pathway starts where both meet. Thrombin catalyses the conversion of soluble fibrinogen polymer to insoluble fibrin monomer threads, which arrange spontaneously into a loose mesh. Fibrin stabilising factor, factor XIII of platelets, converts the loose threads into tight strands, and it is activated by thrombin in the presence of calcium. The result is a clot that closes and adheres to the injured vessel wall. Calcium is required for all the reactions of blood clotting except the first two steps of the intrinsic system.

When a vessel is injured both systems are initiated simultaneously. The extrinsic system is very rapid, 15 seconds, and extensive; the intrinsic system is slow, 1 to 6 minutes. The extrinsic system also switches the intrinsic one on: activated factor VII activates factor IX, an intrinsic factor, and factor X, a common one, and the thrombin it produces early activates factors VIII and V. When a critical amount of thrombin is formed, a vicious circle develops causing more blood clotting.

### Key determinants
The 2024 paper asked this chapter as a comparison and a mechanism: compare the function of thromboxane A2 and prostacyclin for 6 marks, and give the function of aspirin in preventing myocardial infarction for 3 marks. Both halves are answered from the same two paragraphs.

The comparison has three axes and each carries marks. Cell of origin: thromboxane A2 comes from the membrane of active platelets, prostacyclin from the endothelium of blood vessels. Substrate and enzyme: both come from arachidonic acid, and both are made by cyclooxygenase — the same substrate and the same enzyme, which is the fact the aspirin answer depends on. Actions, three each and opposite in pairs: thromboxane A2 is a vasoconstrictor where prostacyclin is a powerful vasodilator; thromboxane A2 stimulates the platelet release reaction where prostacyclin inhibits release; thromboxane A2 is a powerful stimulant of platelet aggregation where prostacyclin inhibits aggregation.

And then the consequence, which is a mark of its own. Since prostacyclin's actions are opposite to those of thromboxane A2, prostacyclin keeps the platelet plug localised to the site of injury.

The aspirin answer is three sentences and it turns on an asymmetry, not on a preference.

Aspirin inhibits the cyclooxygenase enzyme and thus reduces production of both thromboxane A2 and prostacyclin.

However, the endothelial cells produce new cyclooxygenase in a matter of hours, whereas platelets cannot manufacture the enzyme.

Thus administration of small amounts of aspirin for prolonged periods reduces clot formation and has been of value in preventing myocardial infarction.

Read those three in order and the dose makes sense. The drug is not selective for the platelet; the platelet is simply the cell that cannot recover. That is why the useful dose is small and prolonged rather than large, and why the answer is incomplete without the sentence about the endothelium making new enzyme.

### Normal values
Platelet count: 200,000 to 400,000 per mm3. Lifespan about 8 days.

Distribution: 70% of platelets extruded from bone marrow enter the blood, 30% are stored in the spleen.

Speed of the two pathways: the extrinsic system takes 15 seconds, the intrinsic system 1 to 6 minutes.

Calcium: required for all clotting reactions except the first two steps of the intrinsic system.

Coagulation factor groups: fibrinogen group I, V, VIII, XIII; prothrombin group II, VII, IX, X; contact group XI, XII.

### Clinical significance
Aspirin is the chapter's own worked drug example, and the book states the reasoning rather than the prescription: small amounts for prolonged periods reduce clot formation and have been of value in preventing myocardial infarction. No dose is printed and none is given here.

Splenectomy raises the platelet count, because 30% of the platelets are stored in the spleen. That is a one-line consequence of a distribution figure and it is the kind of fact a multiple-choice question is built on.

Vitamin K is the link between the liver and four factors. The prothrombin group — II, VII, IX and X — all need vitamin K for their synthesis in the liver, so a failure of either the vitamin or the organ removes the same four factors.

Von Willebrand factor does two separate jobs in this chapter. It is the subendothelial protein platelets adhere to at the start of the plug, and it is the plasma carrier of factor VIII, from which thrombin must separate factor VIII before factor VIII can act. One protein, two steps, in two different halves of haemostasis.

Glass is a coagulation fact with a laboratory consequence. Factor XII can be activated in vitro by an electronegatively charged wettable surface such as glass, which is why the intrinsic pathway is the one that runs in a test tube and why the anticoagulants chapter that follows lists silicone-coated tubes.

### Common misconceptions
Thromboxane A2 and prostacyclin are not products of different pathways. Both come from arachidonic acid through the same cyclooxygenase step, and only the cell differs. That is precisely why one drug acting at that step can shift the balance.

Aspirin does not spare prostacyclin. It reduces production of both. The protection comes from the endothelial cell replacing its enzyme within hours while the platelet cannot manufacture the enzyme at all, so a bigger dose is not a better one.

Prostacyclin does not stop clotting. It confines it. The marked statement is that its opposition to thromboxane A2 keeps the platelet plug localised to the site of injury.

The platelet plug is not the clot. The plug is primary haemostasis and is weak enough to be washed away; the clot is the plug entangled within fibrin threads, and fibrin is the product of secondary haemostasis.

The intrinsic pathway is not the fast one. The extrinsic system takes 15 seconds and the intrinsic 1 to 6 minutes, and the extrinsic one switches the intrinsic one on rather than running beside it.

Serotonin from the dense granules is not an aggregating agent in this account. Its job is to reinforce and maintain vasoconstriction; ADP and thromboxane A2 are what drive aggregation.

## hold_these
Haemostasis is prevention of blood loss after injury, and it runs in four steps: vasoconstriction, the temporary platelet plug, coagulation to a definitive fibrin clot, and lysis with repair.

## lose_the_mark
Treating thromboxane A2 and prostacyclin as products of different enzymes because their effects are opposite. Both come from arachidonic acid through cyclooxygenase; only the cell of origin differs.
Saying aspirin selectively blocks thromboxane A2. It blocks both; the endothelial cell can replace the enzyme within hours and the platelet cannot, and that asymmetry is the whole answer.
Recommending a larger dose of aspirin for better protection. The book says small amounts for prolonged periods, because a large dose suppresses the endothelial prostacyclin too.
Giving the actions of the two agents without giving the consequence. Prostacyclin keeps the platelet plug localised to the site of injury, and that sentence carries marks of its own.
Answering a comparison as two separate descriptions. The 6 marks are for cell of origin, substrate and enzyme, and three paired actions — set them against each other.
Calling the platelet plug the clot. A blood clot is a platelet plug entangled within fibrin threads.
Making the intrinsic pathway the fast one. The extrinsic system is 15 seconds, the intrinsic 1 to 6 minutes.
Putting the vitamin K factors in the wrong group. They are the prothrombin group, II, VII, IX and X.
Saying calcium is needed for every step of clotting. It is needed for all of them except the first two steps of the intrinsic system.
Listing serotonin among the aggregating agents. It reinforces and maintains vasoconstriction; ADP and thromboxane A2 drive aggregation.

## summary
The sympathetic system is thoraco-lumbar, catabolic and energy-consuming — stress, fight, fear and flight. The chapter sets it out region by region, and then closes on the one heading that ties the regions together: in an emergency the whole system discharges as one unit, and every effect it produces is a preparation for flight, fear or fight. That closing section is cancelled from the current written exam and was still tested by a multiple-choice question on the 2024 paper, which is worth knowing before you decide how much of it to hold.

## sections
### Definition
The sympathetic nerves originate from the lateral horn cells of all thoracic and the upper 2 lumbar segments of the spinal cord, which is why the system is labelled thoraco-lumbar.

Preganglionic sympathetic fibres leave the spinal cord with the ventral roots of the corresponding spinal nerves and pass into the ganglia of the paravertebral sympathetic chain. From there the book gives three possible courses. The fibres can synapse with postganglionic neurons in the ganglion they enter. They can pass up or down the chain and synapse in one of the other ganglia, and postganglionic fibres from the chain then re-enter the spinal nerves to supply all parts of the body — approximately 8% of the fibres in spinal nerves are sympathetic fibres. Or they can pass through the chain without relay and synapse with the neurons of a collateral ganglion.

The function of the whole system is given in one line: catabolic and energy-consuming system, stress, fight, fear and flight.

Notice what the three courses buy. Two of them keep the relay near the cord and send a long postganglionic fibre out, which is how a discharge reaches skin, vessels and hair over the whole body at once. That anatomy is the reason the last section of this chapter can talk about the system discharging as one unit.

### Mechanism
The chapter is organised by region, and each region is set out under the same four headings: origin, relay, course, functions.

Head and neck. The preganglionic neurons arise from the lateral horn cells of the first and second thoracic segments, ascend to synapse in the cervical ganglia, and the postganglionic fibres run in the outer coat of the blood vessels to the various structures.

On the eye: contraction of the dilator pupillae, dilating the pupil, which is mydriasis; contraction of the smooth muscle of the upper eyelid, widening the palpebral fissure and increasing the field of vision; contraction of Muller's muscles in some animals, protruding the eyeball forward as exophthalmos, again increasing the field of vision; and vasoconstriction of the conjunctival vessels.

On the skin: secretory to the sweat glands, vasoconstriction of cutaneous blood vessels, and contraction of the piloerector muscle, erecting the hairs.

On the glands: to the salivary glands, mainly the submaxillary, a trophic secretion of saliva that is concentrated, small in amount and viscid.

On the cerebral circulation: cerebral blood flow increases because systemic arterial blood pressure rises, while the cerebral vessels themselves are mildly constricted.

On the brain: increased mental alertness.

Thoracic viscera. The preganglionic fibres arise from the lateral horn cells of the upper 4 thoracic segments, relay in the cervical ganglia and the upper 4 thoracic ganglia of the paravertebral chain, and the postganglionic fibres travel to the organs.

On the heart: increase of all cardiac properties — rate and force of contraction, excitability and rate of conduction — and vasodilatation of the coronary blood vessels, which the book marks as an indirect effect. On the lungs: bronchodilatation, and mild vasoconstriction of the pulmonary vessels.

Abdominal viscera. The preganglionic fibres arise from the lateral horn cells of T5 to T12, pass through the paravertebral chain without relay, and leave it as the splanchnic nerves to relay in collateral ganglia. The greater splanchnic nerve, from T5 to T9, terminates on the coeliac and superior mesenteric ganglia. The lesser splanchnic nerve, from T10 to T11, terminates on the aorticorenal and superior mesenteric ganglia. The least splanchnic nerve, from T12, relays in the aorticorenal ganglion. The postganglionic fibres then run with the blood vessels.

On the gastrointestinal tract: relaxation of the plain muscle of the wall of the stomach, small intestine and proximal part of the large intestine, and contraction of the sphincters, for example the pyloric sphincter. On the blood vessels: vasoconstriction of the arterioles of the stomach, small intestine, proximal large intestine, kidney, pancreas and liver, and vasodilatation of some arterioles of the abdominal viscera. On the liver: stimulation of glycogenolysis, raising blood glucose. On the spleen: contraction of the splenic capsule, releasing stored blood rich in red cells into the circulation. On the adrenal medulla: secretion of adrenaline and noradrenaline directly into the blood.

Pelvic viscera. The preganglionic fibres arise from the lateral horn cells of the 12th thoracic and upper 2 lumbar segments. Those to the gastrointestinal tract pass through the chain without relay as the lumbar splanchnic nerve to relay in the inferior mesenteric ganglion; those to the urinary bladder and external genitalia leave the chain through the sacral ganglia as the sacral splanchnic nerves.

On the gastrointestinal tract: relaxation of the plain muscle of the wall of the distal large intestine and rectum, and contraction of the internal anal sphincter, retaining faeces. On the urinary bladder: relaxation of the wall and contraction of the internal urethral sphincter, retaining urine. On the male genital organs: contraction of vas deferens, seminal vesicles and prostate, producing ejaculation of semen, and vasoconstriction of the vessels of the pelvic viscera and external genitalia, shrinking the penis during the sexual act. On the female genital organs: variable effects on uterine muscle depending on the stage of the menstrual cycle.

Limbs: contraction of the piloerector muscles, and in skeletal muscle increased glycogenolysis, increased strength and delayed fatigue.

Metabolism and blood: basal metabolism increased up to 100%; increased blood glucose and lipids; stimulation of lipolysis in fat cells, brown fat; and enhanced clotting of the blood.

The book closes the regional survey with a warning worth keeping: the distribution of sympathetic innervation is only approximate and overlaps greatly.

One rule runs through the gut, the bladder and the rectum alike. The sympathetic system relaxes the wall and contracts the sphincter. Learn it once and it covers the pyloric sphincter, the internal anal sphincter and the internal urethral sphincter, and it is the exact reverse of what the parasympathetic system does at the same three places.

### Key determinants
The last heading of the chapter is the one that turns a regional list into a single idea, and it is where the concept this article carries lives.

The sympathetic nervous system discharges one unit in emergency situations to prepare the individual to cope with emergency, that is preparation for flight, fear or fight.

The book gives five examples, each with the advantage it buys.

It dilates the pupils, letting more light into the eyes.

It accelerates the heartbeats and raises the blood pressure, providing better perfusion of vital organs and muscles.

It constricts the blood vessels of the skin, which limits bleeding from wounds.

It lowers the threshold in the reticular formation in the brain, reinforcing the alert and aroused state.

It stimulates lipolysis and glycogenolysis, supplying more energy as glucose and free fatty acids.

Five items, and every one of them is an increase. That is what makes the sixth fact worth carrying into the same answer even though the book prints it two pages earlier, among the thoracic functions: the same sympathetic discharge produces bronchodilatation. Airway resistance therefore falls while heart rate, blood pressure, pupil size and blood glucose all rise. A multiple-choice question that asks which parameter decreases during a fright is asking about that one exception, and it can only be answered by a student who has read the regional functions and the alarm response together.

The other decrease sits in the same place. The gut wall relaxes while its sphincters contract, so motility falls too. The discharge is uniform in purpose, not in direction.

### Normal values
Origin of the outflow: lateral horn cells of all thoracic segments and the upper 2 lumbar segments.

Proportion of spinal nerve fibres that are sympathetic: approximately 8%.

Segmental origins by region: head and neck T1 to T2; thoracic viscera the upper 4 thoracic segments; abdominal viscera T5 to T12; pelvic viscera the 12th thoracic and upper 2 lumbar segments.

Splanchnic nerves: greater T5 to T9 to the coeliac and superior mesenteric ganglia; lesser T10 to T11 to the aorticorenal and superior mesenteric ganglia; least T12 to the aorticorenal ganglion.

Basal metabolism during sympathetic activity: increased up to 100%.

### Clinical significance
Horner's syndrome is the chapter's named lesion. Its cause is a lesion on one side of the cervical sympathetic chain, and its manifestations are on the same side as the lesion: miosis, a constricted pupil relative to the opposite eye; ptosis, drop of the superior eyelid; anhidrosis, absence of sweating on the affected side of the head and neck; and warm, red skin from dilatation of the blood vessels of the head and neck.

Every one of those four is a head and neck function of this chapter, lost. The dilator pupillae is unopposed no longer, so the pupil is small. The smooth muscle of the upper lid is not contracting, so the lid droops. The secretory fibres to the sweat glands are cut, so that side does not sweat. The vasoconstrictor fibres are cut, so the skin is warm and red. A student who has the four functions has the four signs without memorising a second list.

The splenic capsule is a fact with a haematological consequence: sympathetic contraction of the capsule releases stored blood rich in red cells into the circulation, which is the physiological reason a frightened or exercising patient's haematocrit can be higher than their resting one.

Enhanced clotting sits at the end of the metabolic list and connects this chapter to the haemostasis one. So does the rise in blood glucose and lipids, which is the same glycogenolysis and lipolysis the alarm response names.

The book's own caution belongs in any clinical reading of the chapter: the distribution of sympathetic innervation is only approximate and overlaps greatly, so a lesion rarely produces exactly one column of the table.

### Common misconceptions
Sympathetic discharge does not raise everything. Bronchodilatation lowers airway resistance and the gut wall relaxes, so at least two measurable parameters fall during a fright. The system is uniform in purpose, not in direction.

The sympathetic system is not simply the opposite of the parasympathetic at every organ. It contracts the sphincters and relaxes the walls, where the parasympathetic does the reverse, but on the salivary glands both are secretory — the sympathetic secretion is trophic, concentrated, small in amount and viscid, not absent.

Cerebral blood flow rising is not cerebral vasodilatation. The book is explicit: flow increases because systemic arterial pressure rises, while the cerebral vessels are mildly constricted.

Coronary vasodilatation is marked as an indirect effect. The book flags it that way in the same line, and copying it into an answer as a direct sympathetic action loses the qualification the book put there.

The three splanchnic nerves do not go to one ganglion each. The lesser splanchnic nerve terminates on two, the aorticorenal and the superior mesenteric, and the superior mesenteric ganglion receives from both the greater and the lesser.

## hold_these
The sympathetic system is thoraco-lumbar: it arises from the lateral horn cells of all thoracic and the upper 2 lumbar segments.
It is the catabolic, energy-consuming system — stress, fight, fear and flight.
A preganglionic fibre entering the chain can relay there, run up or down and relay elsewhere, or pass through without relay to a collateral ganglion.
About 8% of the fibres in spinal nerves are sympathetic.
On the eye the sympathetic system dilates the pupil, widens the palpebral fissure and constricts conjunctival vessels; on the skin it is secretory to sweat glands, vasoconstrictor and piloerector.
On the heart it increases all cardiac properties and dilates the coronaries indirectly; on the lungs it produces bronchodilatation with mild pulmonary vasoconstriction.
The greater splanchnic nerve is T5 to T9, the lesser T10 to T11 and the least T12, ending on the coeliac, superior mesenteric and aorticorenal ganglia.
Everywhere in the gut, bladder and rectum the sympathetic system relaxes the wall and contracts the sphincter.
It stimulates hepatic glycogenolysis, contracts the splenic capsule to release stored red cells, and makes the adrenal medulla secrete adrenaline and noradrenaline into the blood.

In an emergency the system discharges as one unit to prepare the individual for flight, fear or fight.

Horner's syndrome follows a lesion of one cervical sympathetic chain and gives miosis, ptosis, anhidrosis and warm red skin on the side of the lesion.

## summary
Two transmitters run the autonomic nervous system, so autonomic fibres are named after the one they release: cholinergic fibres release acetylcholine, adrenergic fibres release noradrenaline. Learn the cholinergic list first, because it is a closed list with two surprises in it, and everything left over is adrenergic. Then learn the receptors, which is where the marks are: two cholinergic types, and five adrenergic subtypes told apart by the second messenger they move and the plain muscle they act on. Note before you start that this chapter is only partly on the current written exam, and the part that is examinable is the receptor part.

## sections
### Definition
Acetylcholine and noradrenaline are the main chemical transmitters at the synaptic junctions in the autonomic nervous system. Therefore autonomic nerve fibres are divided into cholinergic and adrenergic fibres. Cholinergic receptors are activated by acetylcholine, and adrenergic receptors are activated by noradrenaline.

Cholinergic fibres are a closed list of three groups. All preganglionic autonomic fibres, sympathetic and parasympathetic alike, including those to the adrenal medulla. All postganglionic parasympathetic fibres. And some postganglionic sympathetic fibres, which are the secretory fibres to the sweat glands and the vasodilator fibres to the blood vessels of skeletal muscle.

Adrenergic fibres are what is left. All postganglionic sympathetic fibres secrete noradrenaline, except the secretory fibres to the sweat glands and the vasodilator cholinergic fibres to the blood vessels of skeletal muscle. Noradrenaline is also secreted from the adrenal medulla together with adrenaline.

Read the two lists as one rule and one exception. Every preganglionic fibre in the body is cholinergic, which is why a ganglion is the same kind of synapse in both divisions. The only place the rule breaks is on the sympathetic postganglionic side, and it breaks twice — sweat glands and skeletal muscle vessels.

### Mechanism
Acetylcholine has a synthesis, a release and a removal, and each is one short paragraph in the book.

It is synthesised in the axoplasm of the terminal endings of cholinergic nerve fibres by the enzyme choline acetyltransferase, and stored in small clear vesicles.

It is released when an action potential spreads over the terminal fibres: depolarisation increases the influx of calcium into the nerve terminals, and calcium causes exocytosis of the vesicles.

It is removed fast. Once secreted, most of it within a fraction of a second is split into acetate ion and choline by acetylcholinesterase. True cholinesterase, the specific one, has a great affinity for acetylcholine and is present in high concentration in the membranes of cholinergic nerve endings, in the synaptic area bound to collagen, and in the cell membrane at the cholinergic nerve terminal. Pseudocholinesterase, the non-specific one, is present in plasma and acts on acetylcholine that diffuses into the surrounding fluids or into the plasma. The choline produced is transported back into the nerve endings and used again to synthesise new acetylcholine.

Noradrenaline follows the same three headings and differs at every one.

It is synthesised in the terminal endings of adrenergic nerve fibres by hydroxylation of the amino acid tyrosine to form DOPA, followed by decarboxylation of DOPA to form dopamine. In the granulated dark vesicles, dopamine is converted to noradrenaline, which is stored bound to ATP and to a protein called chromogranin.

It is released by exocytosis when the nerve impulse reaches the terminal knobs, in the same way as acetylcholine.

It is removed within a few seconds in three different ways, and the proportions matter. Active re-uptake into the adrenergic nerve endings themselves accounts for the removal of 50 to 80% of the secreted noradrenaline. Diffusion away from the nerve endings into surrounding body fluids and blood accounts for some. And destruction by enzymes accounts for the rest: monoamine oxidase, located on the outer surface of mitochondria and widely distributed, particularly in adrenergic nerve fibres, brain, liver and kidneys, which deaminates noradrenaline; and catechol-O-methyl-transferase, present in all tissues but not found in adrenergic nerve endings, which methylates it.

Put the two removals side by side, because that is where the difference between the two transmitters actually lies. Acetylcholine is destroyed at the synapse in a fraction of a second by an enzyme sitting in the membrane. Noradrenaline is mostly taken back up intact and reused, over a few seconds. One transmitter is switched off; the other is recovered.

Acetylcholine receptors are of two main types. Nicotinic receptors, activated only by nicotine among the two, are found in the autonomic ganglia, on the membranes of the postganglionic neurons, and in the adrenal medulla. Muscarinic receptors, activated only by muscarine, are found on all effector cells stimulated by postganglionic neurons of the parasympathetic nervous system, and on those stimulated by the postganglionic cholinergic neurons of the sympathetic system.

That second line of the muscarinic entry is the one to notice. The sweat glands are sympathetic and their receptor is muscarinic, which is why an antimuscarinic drug stops sweating.

### Key determinants
The adrenergic receptors are what the 2024 paper asked for, at 9 marks, and the answer has two halves: where they are, and what they do.

There are 2 major types of adrenergic receptor, alpha and beta. Alpha has 2 subtypes, 1 and 2. Beta has 3 subtypes, 1, 2 and 3.

Distribution first, and it is two locations rather than one. Adrenergic receptors are present on the surface of effector organs, which are the postsynaptic receptors, and on the membrane of postganglionic nerve endings, which are the presynaptic receptors.

The presynaptic ones are auto-receptors present in the membranes of postganglionic adrenergic nerve endings. They are autoregulators that regulate the release of chemical transmitters from nerve endings. Stimulation of alpha presynaptic receptors decreases the release of noradrenaline. Stimulation of beta presynaptic receptors increases the release of noradrenaline.

The postsynaptic ones are told apart by their mechanism of action. Alpha-1 receptors produce their effects through increasing intracellular calcium. Alpha-2 receptors produce their effects through inhibiting adenyl cyclase and thus decreasing cAMP. Beta-1 and beta-2 receptors produce their effects by stimulating adenyl cyclase and thus increasing cAMP.

Then the actions, subtype by subtype.

Alpha-1 receptors are mainly excitatory and contract plain muscle: vasoconstriction of blood vessels in skin, viscera and male genitalia; contraction of the dilator pupillae, giving mydriasis; the splenic capsule; the sphincters of the gastrointestinal tract, retaining faeces; the internal urethral sphincter, retaining urine; and the seminal vesicles and vas deferens, in ejaculation.

Alpha-2 receptors are mainly inhibitory: relaxation of the plain muscle of the intestine.

Beta-1 receptors are mainly excitatory: cardiac acceleration and increased force of contraction of the heart.

Beta-2 receptors are mainly inhibitory and relax plain muscle: vasodilatation of the coronary and skeletal blood vessels; bronchodilatation; and relaxation of the gastrointestinal tract, the urinary bladder and the uterus.

Beta-3 receptors: lipolysis.

Now look at what that classification does to the letters. Alpha does not mean excitatory — alpha-2 relaxes intestine. Beta does not mean inhibitory — beta-1 drives the heart. Two of the five subtypes break the pattern a student would guess, and both are heavily examined.

The book closes the receptor section by separating the two catecholamines. Noradrenaline and adrenaline are both secreted by the adrenal medulla and have somewhat different effects. Noradrenaline excites mainly alpha receptors, and beta receptors to a slight extent as well. Adrenaline excites both types approximately equally. The relative effect of the two hormones on any organ is therefore decided by which receptor that organ carries: adrenaline, with the greater effect on beta receptors, has a greater effect on cardiac activity than noradrenaline and several times as great a metabolic effect, while noradrenaline elevates arterial blood pressure more than adrenaline, because alpha receptors have a greater affinity for noradrenaline, producing strong vasoconstriction and an increase in total peripheral resistance.

### Normal values
Receptor types: two cholinergic, nicotinic and muscarinic. Two adrenergic types, alpha with subtypes 1 and 2 and beta with subtypes 1, 2 and 3 — five adrenergic subtypes in all.

Removal of noradrenaline: active re-uptake accounts for 50 to 80% of the secreted transmitter; removal is complete within a few seconds.

Removal of acetylcholine: most of it is split within a fraction of a second.

Second messengers: alpha-1 raises intracellular calcium; alpha-2 inhibits adenyl cyclase and lowers cAMP; beta-1 and beta-2 stimulate adenyl cyclase and raise cAMP.

Presynaptic autoregulation: alpha decreases noradrenaline release, beta increases it.

### Clinical significance
The last page of the chapter is the pharmacological one, and the book keeps it entirely at the level of class and mechanism, with no drug named and no dose given.

Agents that affect the parasympathetic nervous system. Those that augment its activity are the ganglion stimulants, which stimulate nicotinic receptors for both sympathetic and parasympathetic ganglia, and the parasympathomimetic drugs, which stimulate muscarinic receptors. Those that depress it are the ganglion blockers, which block nicotinic receptors for both sets of ganglia, and the parasympatholytic drugs, which block muscarinic receptors.

Agents that affect the sympathetic nervous system. Those that augment its activity are the ganglion stimulants again, and the sympathomimetic drugs, which stimulate adrenergic receptors. Those that depress it are the ganglion blockers again, and the sympatholytic drugs, which block adrenergic receptors.

The shape of that table is the teaching point. A ganglion drug is non-selective by construction, because every preganglionic fibre in both divisions is cholinergic and acts on the same nicotinic receptor — so a ganglion blocker appears in both halves of the list. Selectivity only becomes possible at the postganglionic receptor, where the two divisions finally use different molecules.

Three receptor facts from this chapter carry directly into clinical medicine and the book states each of them plainly. Beta-2 receptors produce bronchodilatation, which is why a beta agonist opens an airway. Beta-1 receptors accelerate the heart and increase its force. Alpha-1 receptors constrict the vessels of skin and viscera and raise peripheral resistance, which is how noradrenaline raises arterial blood pressure more than adrenaline does.

And the sweat gland remains the chapter's best single example of why the fibre and the receptor must be learned separately. The fibre is sympathetic. The transmitter is acetylcholine. The receptor is muscarinic. Every one of those three is a separate fact and a question can attack any of them.

### Common misconceptions
Not every postganglionic sympathetic fibre is adrenergic. The secretory fibres to the sweat glands and the vasodilator fibres to the blood vessels of skeletal muscle are sympathetic and cholinergic, and they are the exception the book states twice.

Alpha is not always constriction and beta is not always dilatation. Alpha-2 relaxes intestinal plain muscle and beta-1 excites the heart. The subtype decides, not the letter.

Presynaptic receptors do not act on the effector organ. They are auto-receptors on the nerve ending that regulate how much transmitter is released — alpha down, beta up — and an answer that gives only the effector-organ distribution has answered half the question.

Alpha-1 and alpha-2 do not share a second messenger. Alpha-1 raises intracellular calcium; alpha-2 lowers cAMP by inhibiting adenyl cyclase. Only beta-1 and beta-2 share a mechanism.

Acetylcholine and noradrenaline are not removed the same way. Acetylcholine is destroyed at the synapse by acetylcholinesterase within a fraction of a second; 50 to 80% of noradrenaline is taken back up into the nerve ending intact and reused.

Nicotinic receptors are not the parasympathetic ones. They are in the autonomic ganglia of both divisions and in the adrenal medulla; the muscarinic receptors are the ones on the effector cells.

Catechol-O-methyl-transferase is not inside the adrenergic nerve ending. The book says it is present in all tissues but not found in adrenergic nerve endings, which is the opposite of where monoamine oxidase is concentrated.

## hold_these
Acetylcholine and noradrenaline are the main autonomic transmitters, so fibres are named cholinergic or adrenergic after the transmitter they release.
Cholinergic fibres are all preganglionic autonomic fibres including those to the adrenal medulla, all postganglionic parasympathetic fibres, and two sympathetic groups — the secretory fibres to sweat glands and the vasodilator fibres to skeletal-muscle vessels.
Every other postganglionic sympathetic fibre is adrenergic and secretes noradrenaline, which the adrenal medulla also secretes with adrenaline.
Acetylcholine is made by choline acetyltransferase in the axoplasm, stored in small clear vesicles, and released by calcium-triggered exocytosis.
Acetylcholine is destroyed within a fraction of a second by acetylcholinesterase, true cholinesterase at the nerve ending and pseudocholinesterase in plasma, and the choline is taken back and reused.
Noradrenaline is made from tyrosine through DOPA and dopamine, and stored bound to ATP and chromogranin in granulated dark vesicles.
Noradrenaline is removed within a few seconds, 50 to 80% by active re-uptake, the rest by diffusion and by monoamine oxidase and catechol-O-methyl-transferase.
Nicotinic receptors are in the autonomic ganglia and the adrenal medulla; muscarinic receptors are on the effector cells of the parasympathetic system and of the postganglionic cholinergic sympathetic fibres.
Adrenergic receptors sit both on the effector organ, postsynaptically, and on the postganglionic nerve ending, presynaptically.
Presynaptic alpha stimulation decreases noradrenaline release; presynaptic beta stimulation increases it.
Alpha-1 raises intracellular calcium; alpha-2 inhibits adenyl cyclase and lowers cAMP; beta-1 and beta-2 stimulate adenyl cyclase and raise cAMP.
Alpha-1 is mainly excitatory: vasoconstriction of skin, visceral and male genital vessels, mydriasis, splenic capsule, gastrointestinal sphincters, internal urethral sphincter, seminal vesicles and vas deferens.
Alpha-2 is mainly inhibitory: relaxation of intestinal plain muscle.
Beta-1 is mainly excitatory: cardiac acceleration and increased force of contraction.
Beta-2 is mainly inhibitory: coronary and skeletal vasodilatation, bronchodilatation, and relaxation of gut, bladder and uterus. Beta-3 drives lipolysis.
Noradrenaline excites mainly alpha receptors and raises blood pressure more; adrenaline excites alpha and beta about equally and has the greater cardiac and metabolic effect.

## lose_the_mark
Calling every postganglionic sympathetic fibre adrenergic. The sudomotor fibres to sweat glands and the vasodilator fibres to skeletal-muscle vessels are sympathetic and cholinergic.
Assuming alpha always constricts and beta always dilates. Alpha-2 relaxes intestinal plain muscle and beta-1 excites the heart; the subtype decides, not the letter.
Giving the effector-organ distribution and stopping. The question asks for distribution, and half of it is the presynaptic auto-receptors that regulate transmitter release.
Reversing the presynaptic rule. Alpha presynaptic stimulation decreases noradrenaline release; beta presynaptic stimulation increases it.
Giving alpha-1 and alpha-2 the same second messenger. Alpha-1 raises intracellular calcium; alpha-2 lowers cAMP.
Listing four adrenergic subtypes. There are five — alpha-1, alpha-2, beta-1, beta-2 and beta-3 — and beta-3, lipolysis, is the one most often dropped.
Saying noradrenaline is destroyed at the synapse like acetylcholine. Most of it, 50 to 80%, is taken back up intact by the nerve ending.
Putting catechol-O-methyl-transferase inside the adrenergic nerve ending. It is present in all tissues but not there; monoamine oxidase is the one concentrated in adrenergic fibres.
Calling nicotinic receptors parasympathetic. They are the ganglionic receptor of both divisions, and of the adrenal medulla.
Saying adrenaline raises blood pressure more than noradrenaline. Noradrenaline does, because alpha receptors have the greater affinity for it and vasoconstriction raises total peripheral resistance.
