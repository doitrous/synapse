## summary
An action potential is a series of rapid changes in membrane potential that follow an adequate stimulus. Two voltage-gated channels produce all of it: a sodium channel with an activation gate outside and an inactivation gate inside, and a potassium channel with one gate and no inactivation. Depolarisation is a regenerative sodium influx that carries the membrane from −90 mV through the −65 mV firing level to an overshoot of +35 mV. What the sodium channels do next sets the two refractory periods, which is why the ionic basis and the refractory periods are one story rather than two.

## sections
### Definition
An action potential is a series of rapid changes in the membrane potential that follow stimulation of a nerve fibre by an adequate stimulus. It is recorded with two microelectrodes, and it is preceded by a latent period — the interval between applying the stimulus and the start of the response, which is the time the impulse takes to travel from the stimulating electrode to the recording one.

The wave has three phases. Depolarisation carries the potential from the resting level to an overshoot. Repolarisation returns it, rapidly for the first 70 per cent and slowly for the remaining 30 per cent. Hyperpolarisation then takes it slightly past the resting level before it settles. The sharp rise and rapid fall together are the spike, which lasts about 2 msec; the hyperpolarisation lasts 35 to 40 msec.

### Mechanism
The main features of the action potential are determined by the voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels.

The voltage-gated sodium channel has two gates: an activation gate near the outer surface and an inactivation gate on the inner surface. At the resting membrane potential the activation gate is closed while the inactivation gate is open. Opening the outer gate makes the channel activated; closing the inner gate makes it inactivated. Those are two different ways of being shut, and confusing them is the commonest error in this topic. The voltage-gated potassium channel is simpler: one gate, on the inner surface, closed at rest, and no inactivation gate at all.

Depolarisation happens in two steps and is a positive-feedback, regenerative process. In the slow phase, a stimulus decreases the membrane potential from −90 mV toward the firing level; some sodium activation gates open, sodium enters down its electro-concentration gradient, and that inflow depolarises the membrane further, which opens more channels, until −65 mV is reached. This is the first 25 mV of depolarisation. In the rapid phase, at the firing level all the voltage-gated sodium channels open, the potential difference falls to zero and then reverses, so the inside becomes positive relative to the outside, to an overshoot of +35 mV. The amplitude of the action potential is therefore 125 mV. Sodium channels then inactivate rapidly and stay inactivated for a few milliseconds before returning to the resting state.

Repolarisation follows from two things happening together: sodium inactivation stops the influx and terminates depolarisation, while the potassium gates — which open later than the sodium gates, more slowly, and for longer — carry potassium out and complete the return. Slow closure of those potassium channels then overshoots into hyperpolarisation, and the leak potassium channels bring the membrane back to the resting level. The Na⁺–K⁺ pump restores the concentration gradients afterwards, pumping three sodium out for every two potassium in; it does not produce the spike.

### Key determinants
During the initial depolarisation up to the firing level excitability is increased. Through the rest of the action potential the neurone is refractory, and there are two refractory periods.

The absolute refractory period is the period during which another action potential cannot be produced, whatever the strength of the stimulus. It runs from the firing level to the early part of repolarisation. Its cause is that all the voltage-gated sodium channels have opened and then been rapidly inactivated by the inner gate — an inactivated channel must return to the resting state before it can open again, and no stimulus strength substitutes for that.

The relative refractory period is the period during which another action potential can be produced by a stronger stimulus above the threshold. It begins at the end of the absolute refractory period and ends when the membrane potential returns to its resting level. It has two causes rather than one: some sodium channels have returned to the resting state and are available for activation, and the potassium channels opened during repolarisation are still carrying potassium outwards, which opposes sodium moving in.

Refractoriness is not a defect. It protects the nerve from extremely rapid repetitive stimulation, and it ensures one-way forward propagation of the impulse — the segment an action potential has just left cannot be re-excited by it.

Excitability itself turns on sodium permeability. Anything that increases it, such as veratridine or a low extracellular calcium, makes the fibre depolarise more readily; anything that decreases it stabilises the membrane, which is what a high extracellular calcium and a local anaesthetic such as cocaine do. A fall in extracellular sodium reduces the size of the action potential but has little effect on the resting potential, and blocking the sodium channels with tetrodotoxin abolishes the action potential altogether. Potassium works on the resting potential instead: a rise in extracellular potassium depolarises the membrane and raises excitability, a fall hyperpolarises it and lowers excitability.

### Clinical significance
Local anaesthetics are membrane stabilisers. They reduce sodium permeability so the membrane depolarises slowly and cannot reach the firing level, and the book gives cocaine as its example. Hypercalcaemia acts the same way and hypocalcaemia the opposite way, which is why calcium disorders present with changes in nerve and muscle excitability.

Potassium disorders act on the resting potential. In familial periodic paralysis the extracellular potassium concentration is decreased, nerve excitability is greatly reduced, no nerve impulses are produced and the person becomes paralysed; the condition is treated by intravenous administration of potassium.

Tetrodotoxin blocks the sodium channel outright, and no action potential can be elicited at all. It is worth holding as the cleanest demonstration that the upstroke is a sodium current and nothing else.

### Common misconceptions
Three keep costing marks. The first is describing depolarisation as sodium being pumped in — nothing is pumped during the spike, and the pump only restores the gradients afterwards. The second is treating "closed" and "inactivated" as one channel state, which makes the absolute refractory period impossible to explain. The third is giving only the sodium half of the reason for the relative refractory period and leaving out the outward potassium current that is still flowing.

## summary
Tension is the force a muscle develops when it contracts, and it is produced by cross-bridges cycling through four steps: bind, bend, detach, reset. Calcium starts the cycle by uncovering the active site on actin; ATP is what ends each turn of it, because a cross-bridge cannot let go of actin until a fresh ATP replaces the ADP and phosphate on the myosin head. That is why a muscle without ATP goes stiff rather than limp.

## sections
### Definition
Excitation–contraction coupling is the process by which an action potential of a muscle fibre initiates the contractile process. Tension is the developed force when a muscle contracts.

The action potential comes first. A skeletal muscle fibre has a resting membrane potential of about −90 mV, its action potential lasts 2 to 4 msec, it is conducted along the fibre at about 5 m/sec, and it precedes the contraction by about 2 msec. Because the electrical event finishes before the mechanical one begins, the fibre has already recovered its excitability by the time it starts to shorten, which is why skeletal muscle can be tetanised and cardiac muscle cannot.

### Mechanism
Coupling runs in four stages, and the third is the one this article is named for.

Calcium is released first. The action potential propagates into the T-tubule, where a voltage-sensitive dihydropyridine receptor opens the ryanodine calcium channel on the terminal cisternae of the sarcoplasmic reticulum, and calcium flows out into the cytoplasm.

The muscle proteins are then activated. Calcium binds troponin C on actin; troponin changes conformation; tropomyosin moves away from the position in which it covers the myosin-binding site; and the uncovered site on actin combines with the myosin cross-bridges.

Tension is then generated by the cycling of the cross-bridges, in four steps. Binding of actin and myosin occurs spontaneously once calcium has bound troponin C and tropomyosin has moved off the active site. The cross-bridge then bends and the actin filament slides across the myosin filament; the energy used to phosphorylate the cross-bridge and generate tension comes from hydrolysis of ATP by ATPase into ADP and inorganic phosphate. The cross-bridge then detaches from actin, and for that to happen the ADP and phosphate must be removed and a new molecule of ATP put in their place — it is the new ATP that reduces the cross-bridge's affinity for the active site. Finally the cross-bridge returns to its original upright position to take part in another cycle. Cycling continues as long as calcium is attached to troponin C and ATP is available, and the force developed by the bending of the cross-bridge is transmitted through the actin filament to the Z disc, then through the sarcolemma and the tendinous insertions of the muscle to the bones.

Relaxation is the fourth stage. Calcium is removed from the cytoplasm by a calcium pump on the sarcoplasmic reticulum membrane; troponin returns to its original conformation, tropomyosin moves back to cover the binding site on actin, and cross-bridge cycling stops.

### Key determinants
Two things keep the cycle turning, and either one failing stops it. Calcium must remain bound to troponin C, which is a matter of how fast the sarcoplasmic reticulum pump clears the cytoplasm. ATP must be available, which is a matter of supply rather than of signalling.

A third determinant is geometric. The number of cross-bridges able to act at once depends on the overlap between the thick and thin filaments, so the tension a fibre can develop varies with its initial length — the length–tension relationship, which the book plots as the maximal active tension developed by isometric contraction against increasing initial fibre length.

Note what is not a determinant: the filaments themselves never change length. Contraction shortens the sarcomere because the filaments slide past one another.

### Clinical significance
If no ATP is available, the thick and thin filaments cannot be separated, and the muscle is in contracture. The same mechanism explains rigor mortis: several hours after death all the muscles of the body go into a state of contracture and become rigid even without action potentials, caused by the loss of ATP needed to separate the actin and myosin filaments.

This is worth holding because it inverts the intuition students bring. ATP is spent to break the actin–myosin bond, not to make it, so a muscle that runs out of energy locks rather than relaxes.

### Common misconceptions
Two recur. The first is that the actin and myosin filaments shorten during contraction — they do not, and the live library record on band changes is explicit that the sarcomere shortens without filament shortening. The second is that ATP powers contraction alone, so a muscle without ATP should go floppy; the book puts ATP at the detachment step, and contracture is the consequence.

## summary
Smooth muscle contracts on its own. It does so rhythmically, or as a maintained partial contraction called tone, and it keeps doing so when isolated with no nerve supply at all. Everything that acts on it therefore modifies an activity that is already running: stretch, local chemical conditions, cold, circulating and locally released humoral agents, and the dual autonomic supply, which changes the pattern and the sensitivity rather than switching the muscle on.

## sections
### Definition
Smooth muscle is characterised by its tendency to give rise to spontaneous contractions, which may occur in a rhythmic form or in a tetanic form — muscle tone, a maintained partial contraction. These occur even in isolated smooth muscle when there is no nerve supply.

Its membrane potential is unstable, averaging about −50 to −60 mV at relative rest, with waves superimposed on it. Those waves are not action potentials and cannot themselves cause contraction, but when a wave reaches about −35 mV an action potential is elicited and spreads over the muscle.

### Mechanism
The contractile machinery is regulated on the myosin side rather than the actin side, which is the structural reason smooth muscle behaves so differently from skeletal muscle.

Depolarisation opens voltage-gated calcium channels and calcium flows in down its electrochemical gradient; the calcium that enters may release further calcium from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Hormones and neurotransmitters can also open ligand-gated calcium channels in the membrane or release calcium from the sarcoplasmic reticulum through IP₃-gated channels, so a contraction can begin without any action potential at all.

Calcium then binds calmodulin. The calcium–calmodulin complex activates myosin light-chain kinase, which phosphorylates the regulatory light chain on the myosin head, and that produces ATP hydrolysis and cross-bridge cycling. The phosphorylated cross-bridges keep cycling until myosin light-chain phosphatase dephosphorylates them, so the amount of active myosin at any moment is set by the relative activity of the kinase and the phosphatase.

Relaxation follows a fall in intracellular calcium, produced by a pump that moves calcium into the extracellular fluid and a pump that moves it into the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Both are slow compared with the fast sarcoplasmic reticulum pump of skeletal muscle, which is why a smooth muscle contraction lasts longer.

Dephosphorylated cross-bridges do not all let go. Those that remain attached to actin are latch bridges, and because they do not cycle, or cycle very slowly, they hold tone for a long time on very little ATP. Smooth muscle uses less ATP than skeletal muscle and is fatigue resistant.

### Key determinants
Five groups of factors modify contraction, and the exam asks for them as a list.

Stretch is the first. Visceral smooth muscle responds to stretch by contraction, which lets a hollow organ contract automatically and evacuate its contents when it is distended.

Local factors are the second, and they act in both directions. Acids, excess carbon dioxide and lack of oxygen relax smooth muscle. Alkalis and excess potassium contract it.

Cold is the third: the book states that cold increases the contraction of smooth muscles.

Humoral factors are the fourth. A ligand binds its membrane receptor, and excitatory receptors increase cytoplasmic calcium and cause contraction while inhibitory receptors decrease cytoplasmic calcium and cause relaxation — mediated by opening potassium channels, which inhibits calcium influx, or by increasing the active transport of calcium out of the cytoplasm.

The nerve supply is the fifth. Smooth muscle has a dual supply from the two divisions of the autonomic nervous system, and the nerve supply does not initiate activity in the muscle; it modifies it, by affecting the spontaneous activity and the sensitivity to chemical agents.

### Clinical significance
Two everyday consequences follow directly.

The stretch response is why a distended hollow organ empties itself. A full bladder, a loaded rectum and a distended segment of gut all contract in response to their own filling, without waiting for a signal from outside.

Plasticity is the counterweight. Smooth muscle that is stretched first exerts increased tension, but if it is held stretched the tension gradually decreases, because the myosin cross-bridges readjust their position on the thin filaments. That is why urine can accumulate in the urinary bladder without much rise of intravesical pressure — the same organ both contracts when stretched quickly and accommodates when stretched slowly.

### Common misconceptions
The commonest is answering the exam question about factors with the coupling mechanism. Calcium, calmodulin and myosin light-chain kinase explain how a contraction is produced; they do not answer what modifies it, and a script that gives them earns none of the marks for the factors.

The second is reasoning from skeletal muscle and concluding that denervated smooth muscle is silent. It is not: cutting the nerve changes the pattern and the sensitivity, and the spontaneous activity continues.
