## summary
Gastrointestinal physiology couples motility to secretion and regional absorption. Gastric emptying is paced by inhibitory feedback from the duodenum: fat, hyperosmolar chyme and acid each slow it, so a carbohydrate meal empties fastest and a fatty meal slowest, and liquids leave before solids. Along the small intestine, contractions are triggered by wall distension and timed by the basal electrical rhythm of the interstitial cells of Cajal, while migrating motor complexes sweep residue during fasting and stop with feeding. Absorption is regionally specialised, and the terminal ileum is distinctive: it reabsorbs bile salts (and vitamin B12), so its resection causes bile-salt malabsorption and steatorrhoea while proximally absorbed nutrients are spared.

### Mechanism
Duodenal osmoreceptors and chemoreceptors drive the enterogastric reflex that slows gastric emptying; the vagus mediates receptive relaxation of the fundus. Small-intestinal smooth muscle contracts on the depolarising phase of the basal electrical rhythm generated by the interstitial cells of Cajal, with the rhythm falling from about 12/min in the duodenum to 8/min in the ileum; wall distension initiates peristalsis and segmentation, and migrating motor complexes sweep the fasting gut. Conjugated bile salts are actively reabsorbed by transporters of the terminal ileum and recycled through the enterohepatic circulation.
### Key determinants
Each item turns on one discriminator: hyperosmolar (not acidified-antral) duodenal contents slow emptying; wall distension (not a constant BER or vagal drive) initiates intestinal contraction and MMCs are a fasting pattern; and the terminal ileum (not the proximal bowel) reabsorbs bile salts, so its loss selectively causes bile-salt malabsorption.
### Clinical significance
Diabetic gastroparesis reflects exaggerated inhibitory feedback and delayed emptying with reflux; terminal-ileal disease or resection (Crohn's disease, surgery) causes bile-salt and fat malabsorption with steatorrhoea and, from colonic bile acids, a secretory diarrhoea; and disordered motility underlies the alternating constipation and diarrhoea of irritable bowel syndrome.
