## title
Endocrine physiology: pituitary, thyroid, calcium, adrenal, pancreas and renal hormones
## subject
endo
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Physiology
## summary
This article groups the endocrine and metabolic physiology facts tested by the 2026 EOM paper. It covers growth-hormone excess in the adult (acromegaly), interpretation of thyroid function tests (high TSH with low T4 = primary hypothyroidism) and thyroid-hormone action (T3 potency, tachypnoea in thyrotoxicosis), calcium and adrenal disorders (post-thyroidectomy hypoparathyroid tetany, Addisonian hypoglycaemia), pancreatic hormone biology (insulin/IGF-1 receptor cross-binding, ketoacidosis), and the renal endocrine role of erythropoietin.
## sections
### Definition
A grouping of the endocrine and metabolic physiology items in the Physiology section; each linked concept carries one atomic claim and this overview ties them together.
### Mechanism
Growth hormone acting after epiphyseal fusion enlarges acral bone and soft tissue (acromegaly). Gland failure lowers T4 and, by lost feedback, raises TSH; T3 outbinds T4 at the nuclear receptor. Loss of parathyroid tissue at thyroidectomy drops calcium and causes tetany, while cortisol lack impairs gluconeogenesis (hypoglycaemia). Insulin and IGF-1 cross-bind homologous receptors; insulin lack drives ketogenesis and Kussmaul breathing; renal cortical cells secrete erythropoietin in response to hypoxia.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: acral (not linear) growth in adult GH excess; high-TSH/low-T4 = primary hypothyroidism; receptor affinity explains T3 potency; parathyroid loss explains post-surgical tetany; cortisol deficiency explains hypoglycaemia; erythropoietin deficiency explains renal anaemia.
### Clinical significance
These principles underlie the clinical recognition of acromegaly, thyroid-function-test interpretation, post-thyroidectomy hypocalcaemia, adrenal crisis and Addison’s disease, diabetic ketoacidosis, and the anaemia of chronic kidney disease and its treatment with erythropoietin.
### Common misconceptions
See each concept’s pitfalls; distractors are built by swapping gland-level vs pituitary-level defects, by confusing hyper- and hypo-metabolic signs, and by assuming abundance rather than receptor affinity for T3.

## title
Reproductive anatomy and embryology: indifferent gonad, uterine tube and ovarian steroidogenesis
## subject
gyn
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Anatomy
## summary
This article groups the reproductive anatomy and embryology facts tested by the 2026 EOM paper: the timing of gonadal sex differentiation (the indifferent gonad persists to about the 7th week), the regional anatomy of the uterine tube (the infundibulum is its funnel-shaped lateral end), and the endocrine role of the ovarian theca interna (androstenedione secretion).
## sections
### Definition
A grouping of the reproductive anatomy and embryology items; each linked concept carries one atomic claim and this overview ties them together.
### Mechanism
The genital-ridge gonad is sexually indifferent until SRY-driven differentiation at about week 7. The uterine tube runs from an intramural part through isthmus and ampulla to the funnel-shaped infundibulum with its fimbriae. In the follicle, LH-stimulated theca interna cells make androstenedione that granulosa cells aromatise to estradiol.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: indifferent gonad until week 7; infundibulum = funnel-shaped lateral tube end; theca interna secretes androstenedione (not estrogen directly).
### Clinical significance
These principles underlie disorders of sex development, tubal (ectopic) pregnancy and tubal transport, and the two-cell model of ovarian estrogen synthesis relevant to ovulation induction and PCOS.
### Common misconceptions
See each concept’s pitfalls; distractors are built by shifting the differentiation timing, confusing tube segments, and attributing estrogen synthesis directly to the theca.

## title
Genital system histology: mammary gland, bulbourethral gland, oviduct peg cells and vas deferens
## subject
gyn
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Histology
## summary
This article groups the genital-system histology facts tested by the 2026 EOM paper: the classification of the mammary gland (compound tubuloalveolar apocrine), the function of the bulbourethral (Cowper’s) glands (lubrication and neutralising acidic urine), the secretory peg cells of the uterine tube (sperm capacitation), and the three-layered muscular coat of the vas deferens.
## sections
### Definition
A grouping of the genital-system histology items across the male and female tracts; each linked concept carries one atomic claim and this overview ties them together.
### Mechanism
The mammary gland is a compound tubuloalveolar gland with apocrine lipid secretion. Cowper’s glands add alkaline mucus that lubricates and neutralises the urethra. Non-ciliated peg cells secrete tubal fluid aiding capacitation, while ciliated cells move the ovum. The vas deferens has inner-longitudinal, middle-circular and outer-longitudinal muscle layers driving sperm emission.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: mammary = compound tubuloalveolar apocrine; Cowper’s neutralises acidic urine; peg cells aid capacitation; vas deferens has a three-layer musculosa.
### Clinical significance
These principles support the histological identification of accessory genital glands and ducts and the physiology of ejaculate formation and tubal transport.
### Common misconceptions
See each concept’s pitfalls; distractors are built by swapping secretory modes (holocrine/merocrine), confusing accessory-gland products, and assigning the three-layer muscle coat to other ducts.
