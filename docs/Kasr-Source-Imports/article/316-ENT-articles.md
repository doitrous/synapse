Otorhinolaryngology: ear trauma, discharge and tinnitus
## subject

## summary
This article groups the ear-trauma and symptom-interpretation facts tested by the first part of the bank's Ear section (Q1-8). It covers the traumatic tympanic membrane perforation (central, irregular-edged, may bleed, and managed by keeping the ear dry for spontaneous healing rather than by syringing or immediate myringoplasty), the auricular haematoma (drained by incision and evacuation with a pressure dressing to prevent cauliflower ear), the interpretation of a clear watery ear discharge that increases on straining as CSF otorrhoea, pulse-synchronous pulsatile tinnitus from a vascular glomus jugulare tumour, and the presentation of an acoustic neuroma as unilateral tinnitus with progressive sensorineural hearing loss.
## sections
### Definition
The ear is considered here as the pinna, external canal and tympanic membrane, plus a few retrotympanic and internal-auditory-canal lesions that present with ear symptoms. Each linked concept states one atomic clinical fact; this overview ties them into trauma and the interpretation of discharge and tinnitus.
### Mechanism
Trauma tears the drum centrally with irregular edges (which heal if kept dry) or collects blood under the pinna perichondrium (which fibroses to cauliflower ear if not drained). A dural/temporal-bone breach lets CSF leak and increase on straining; a vascular paraganglioma transmits the arterial pulse as pulsatile tinnitus; a schwannoma of the vestibulocochlear nerve produces slowly progressive unilateral cochlear symptoms.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: traumatic perforations are central with irregular edges and normal middle-ear mucosa (marginal/attic perforation and purulent discharge are not traumatic); watery discharge increasing on straining is CSF, not otitis media; pulsatile tinnitus means a glomus tumour; and the commonest acoustic-neuroma symptom is unilateral tinnitus with progressive sensorineural hearing loss, not vertigo.
### Clinical significance
These facts guide everyday practice: keeping a traumatic perforation dry avoids needless surgery, draining an auricular haematoma prevents deformity, recognising CSF otorrhoea prevents meningitis, and recognising unilateral cochlear symptoms prompts MRI for an acoustic neuroma.
### Common misconceptions
See each linked concept's pitfalls field for the exact reversal each distractor exploits (marginal versus central perforation, CSF versus purulent discharge, vertigo as the commonest acoustic-neuroma symptom).

Otorhinolaryngology: otitis externa, otomycosis and malignant otitis externa
## subject
mul
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Otorhinolaryngology
## summary
This article groups the external-canal infection facts tested by the second part of the bank's Ear section (Q9-18). It contrasts otomycosis (fungal otitis externa — itching with white debris studded with blackish Aspergillus spots, treated by aural toilet and topical antifungals) with the bacterial infections furunculosis, diffuse otitis externa and malignant necrotising otitis externa. It sets out malignant otitis externa as a Pseudomonas skull-base osteomyelitis of the elderly diabetic or immunocompromised, its investigations (CT/MRI petrous, blood sugar and ESR, technetium/gallium isotope bone scans, discharge culture) and its management (control of diabetes plus large-dose anti-pseudomonal antibiotics, not radiotherapy).
## sections
### Definition
Infections of the external auditory canal are grouped here by aetiology: fungal (otomycosis) versus bacterial (furunculosis, diffuse otitis externa and the aggressive malignant necrotising otitis externa). Each linked concept states one atomic clinical fact.
### Mechanism
Fungi (usually Aspergillus) colonise the canal surface to produce itchy otomycosis with visible black-spotted debris. In an elderly diabetic, Pseudomonas exploits small-vessel disease and impaired immunity to spread from the canal through the temporal bone to the skull base as osteomyelitis, which is why malignant otitis externa is aggressive and needs prolonged therapy.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: otomycosis itches (rather than causing severe pain), is fungal, and is treated topically; malignant otitis externa is bacterial (Pseudomonas), affects elderly diabetics, is investigated on several fronts at once, and is never treated by radiotherapy because it is an infection, not a tumour. In a list of bacterial canal infections, otomycosis is the odd one out.
### Clinical significance
Separating fungal from bacterial and benign from malignant external otitis directs the right treatment and the right urgency: topical antifungals for otomycosis, but admission, imaging and long anti-pseudomonal antibiotics with diabetic control for malignant otitis externa.
### Common misconceptions
See each linked concept's pitfalls field for the exact reversal each distractor exploits (otomycosis as bacterial or as painful, malignant otitis externa as a radiotherapy-treated tumour, systemic antifungals for uncomplicated otomycosis).
