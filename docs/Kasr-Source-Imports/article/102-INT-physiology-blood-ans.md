## summary
This opening chapter of the module book's Physiology half is not about blood at all — it is the vocabulary the rest of the Physiology half assumes. It defines physiology itself, breaks total body water into the compartments every later chapter's normal values are measured against (plasma volume, ECF, the numbers behind Fick's principle), and defines homeostasis and the feedback loop that keeps the internal environment constant. None of it is examinable on the written paper — the department's own exclusion list names INTRODUCTION, BODY FLUIDS and HOMEOSTASIS by name — but it is still taught and still examined practically, and a student who skips it will not understand why plasma volume, not total body water, is what a haematology test actually measures.

## sections
### Definition
The book opens by defining physiology itself: the study of the function of the organs and systems of the body and how they work together for optimum well-being and health. It then gives the organisational hierarchy the rest of the book assumes without restating it — cells with similar properties form tissues, tissues combine into organs, and organs of complementary function form systems (cardiovascular, respiratory, nervous, urogenital, reproductive, gastrointestinal). The human body is made of 75-100 trillion cells.

### Mechanism
Body composition. In a normal adult male, body weight is 60% water and 40% solids (18% protein, 15% fat, 7% mineral). Water content is higher in infants (about 75%) and lower in adult females and obese people, because fat holds relatively little water.

Body fluid compartments. Total body water (TBW) divides into the intracellular fluid compartment (ICF), 2/3 of TBW and 40% of body weight, and the extracellular fluid compartment (ECF), 1/3 of TBW and 20% of body weight. The ECF itself is two compartments: the intravascular fluid — plasma, 5% of body weight — and the interstitial fluid (ISF), 15% of body weight, the fluid outside the vessels around the cells. Total blood volume, plasma plus the cellular elements, is about 8% of body weight. The two compartments differ in ionic composition: the ECF's chief cation is sodium (with calcium), its chief anion chloride (with bicarbonate); the ICF's chief cation is potassium (with magnesium), its chief anion phosphate (with protein). Osmolarity, unlike ionic composition, is the same everywhere: about 300 mOsmol/L.

Measuring a compartment. Fick's principle: volume of compartment = amount of indicator injected ÷ concentration of indicator in plasma. An inert, non-toxic indicator the tissues do not use is injected intravenously, given time to distribute evenly, and its plasma concentration measured. Total body water uses heavy water (D2O) or antipyrine, which reach every compartment; ECF volume uses inulin or sodium thiocyanate, which stay extracellular; plasma volume uses Evans blue dye or radio-iodine-labelled plasma proteins, which stay intravascular. ICF volume is never measured directly — it is calculated as TBW − ECF — and ISF volume the same way, as ECF − plasma.

Homeostasis. Keeping the internal environment (the interstitial fluid around the cells) constant; most body systems serve it, and its failure often leads to disease and death. A control system has a stimulus, a receptor, a control centre, an effector organ, and feedback. Almost every homeostatic mechanism is negative feedback, correcting a variable back toward its set value; a handful are positive feedback, deliberately accelerating a process to completion — the book's two examples are blood clotting and uterine contraction during childbirth. Homeostasis rests on two exchange steps: across the cell membrane (cells take up O2 and food from the ISF, give up CO2 and waste) and across the capillary wall (O2 and food diffuse blood-to-ISF, waste diffuses back), with blood then carrying waste to the kidneys and CO2 to the lungs. ECF temperature is held constant by balancing heat production against heat loss; ECF pH by the body's buffers.

### Key determinants
TBW's 2:1 ICF:ECF split, and the ECF's own plasma:ISF split, are the two ratios every later chapter's normal-value section assumes. Fick's principle needs an indicator matched to the compartment being measured, not a universal one. Homeostasis is negative feedback by default; positive feedback (clotting, childbirth) is the named exception, not the rule.

### Clinical significance
This chapter is examined practically even though the written paper skips it — the exclusion list cancels INTRODUCTION, BODY FLUIDS and HOMEOSTASIS by name (src_701b6db49a7c01d79428 p1), and cancellation there is explicit written-exam-only. Every numeric normal value the rest of the Physiology half quotes — 5 L blood volume, 3000-3500 mL plasma volume, 8% of body weight as blood — is a body-fluid-compartment number defined here first. A student who has not read this chapter has no framework for why a haemorrhage is described in litres of blood volume rather than litres of total body water.

### Common misconceptions
Calling the ECF one measured compartment. It is plasma plus interstitial fluid, and only plasma is measured directly; ISF is calculated as ECF minus plasma, never observed on its own.

Assuming positive feedback is pathological by definition. The book's two named examples — clotting and childbirth — are both normal, necessary uses of it.

Picking an indicator for the wrong compartment. Using inulin (extracellular only) to estimate total body water would understate TBW, because inulin never crosses into cells.

## summary
Two pages of the department book carry the whole of this chapter, and they do exactly one job: state what blood is made of and what it is for, before the next nine chapters spend themselves on the RBC half of that composition. Every later Blood chapter assumes the 55:45 plasma-to-cells split and the 8%-of-body-weight volume figure this chapter gives once.

## sections
### Definition
Blood is the transport medium of the body. It is slightly alkaline, pH 7.4. Average volume is 5 L in a normal adult male and about 4.5 L in females, and blood constitutes about 8% of body weight in a normal healthy young adult.

### Mechanism
Composition. Blood has two main components. Plasma is 55% of total blood volume, the fluid part. The cellular elements are the remaining 45%: red blood corpuscles (RBCs), white blood cells (WBCs) and platelets (thrombocytes). Every later Blood chapter is an expansion of one line inside this split — plasma proteins and plasma composition expand the plasma half; RBCs/haemoglobin, erythropoiesis, iron, anaemia and blood groups expand the RBC line of the cellular half; platelets and haemostasis expand the platelet line.

Functions. The book lists five: delivering oxygen to body cells and removing carbon dioxide and other waste products, named explicitly as blood's main function; defence against invading microorganisms and tumours; distribution of heat; prevention of blood loss; and maintenance of homeostasis — body temperature, pH, ionic composition, and the volume of the internal environment.

### Normal values
pH: 7.4 (slightly alkaline). Blood volume: about 5 L in the normal adult male, about 4.5 L in the normal adult female. Blood as a fraction of body weight: about 8% in a normal healthy young adult. Composition: 55% plasma, 45% cellular elements (RBCs, WBCs, platelets).

### Key determinants
The 55:45 plasma:cellular-element split. Blood's five functions, with gas transport and waste removal named as the main one. The 5 L (male) / 4.5 L (female) volume figures and the 8%-of-body-weight rule.

### Clinical significance
The 55:45 ratio is what a packed cell volume (haematocrit) measures directly, and every anaemia and polycythemia figure later in the module is a statement about how that ratio has shifted, not a new principle. Losing sight of blood's main function (gas transport) is why a student can correctly recite five functions and still fail to explain, on the very next chapter, why a fall in RBC count matters more clinically than a fall in any other single blood component. It also frames why anaemia and polycythemia, two whole later chapters, are both defined purely as departures from this chapter's cellular-elements line — anaemia as too few RBCs within it, polycythemia as too many — rather than as departures from blood volume or plasma composition.

### Common misconceptions
Treating the five functions as an unordered list to memorise rather than a ranked one — the book itself marks gas transport and waste removal as blood's main function, and a question that asks for 'the main function of blood' wants that answer specifically, not the full list.
Quoting a single blood-volume figure for both sexes — the book gives 5 L for males and about 4.5 L for females, not one shared number.
Confusing '55% plasma' with 'plasma is half of blood' — 55% is closer to five-ninths, and the distinction matters once a student is asked to compute an absolute plasma or cell volume from a given blood volume.
Giving the plasma:cells ratio as a fixed number for every patient — the 55:45 figure is a population average for a normal healthy young adult; the same underlying measurement (packed cell volume) is exactly what shifts in anaemia and polycythemia, so neither disease changes the book's definition of blood, only an individual's position relative to it.
Assuming this chapter's five functions and the next chapter's plasma-protein functions are one list — this chapter's functions belong to whole blood; the plasma-protein functions the next chapter covers (osmotic, buffer, defensive, clotting, viscosity, capillary, transport, amino-acid-source) are a longer, separate list specific to one component of the 55%.

## summary
This chapter carries two ideas that examiners like to test against each other: why the red cell is shaped the way it is, and what happens chemically to the pigment packed inside it. A large legacy set of concepts already live in this catalogue states the RBC-structure half of this chapter almost verbatim (biconcavity, membrane function, free-haemoglobin hazards); this article is written to cover both that half and the haemoglobin-chemistry half the legacy set does not yet carry — haemoglobin's three types and its four named chemical reactions.

## sections
### Definition
The red blood corpuscles are the main cellular constituent of blood. They are not true cells — they have no nuclei — so the book calls them corpuscles. Average count is 5-5.5 million/mm3 in males and 4-4.5 million/mm3 in females, higher in newborns, high altitude and athletes, lower in growing children and old age. Erythrocytes are circular, biconcave, non-nucleated discs, containing haemoglobin, K+, and the carbonic anhydrase enzyme needed for CO2 transport; they lack mitochondria and ribosomes because of their specialised O2-transport function.

### Mechanism
Why biconcave. The shape gives three advantages: a larger surface area than a spherical cell of the same volume; enhanced flexibility, letting the cell squeeze through small capillaries without rupturing; and minimal membrane tension when the cell's volume increases slightly in venous blood from CO2 carriage.

Haemoglobin basics. Hb is the red O2-carrying pigment, about 34% of RBC weight. Three types: adult HbA, 96-98% of adult Hb; HbA2, 2-4%; and fetal HbF, which has a higher O2 affinity than HbA, letting the fetus extract oxygen from the mother's blood, and which is fully converted to HbA by 4 months after birth. Hb content — Hb per 100 mL blood — is 15-16 g/dL in adult males, 13-14 g/dL in adult females, up to 19 g/dL in normal newborns from relative intrauterine hypoxia.

Haemoglobin's four reactions. Hb unites loosely with O2 to form oxyhaemoglobin — the iron stays Fe2+, so this is oxygenation, not oxidation. Hb combines with CO2 to form carbaminohaemoglobin, CO2 attaching to the polypeptide chains rather than the iron. Hb reacts with carbon monoxide to form carboxyhaemoglobin. Strong oxidising agents oxidise the Fe2+ iron to ferric (Fe3+), forming dark methaemoglobin, which cannot carry oxygen; small normal amounts are reduced back by NADH-methaemoglobin reductase.

Why the membrane matters. The RBC membrane's job is to keep haemoglobin inside the cell. Free plasma haemoglobin is hazardous: it filters through the renal glomeruli and blocks the renal tubules; it increases blood viscosity, increasing cardiac workload; and it can raise the plasma colloidal osmotic pressure from 25-28 mmHg toward 70 mmHg, again increasing blood volume and cardiac workload.

### Key determinants
Three biconcavity advantages: surface area, flexibility, low membrane tension. Three haemoglobin types: HbA 96-98%, HbA2 2-4%, HbF converting fully to HbA by 4 months. Four Hb reactions — oxy-, carbamino-, carboxy- and met-haemoglobin — and which one is a true oxidation (only met-Hb). Three hazards of free plasma haemoglobin: renal tubule blockage, raised viscosity, raised oncotic pressure.

### Clinical significance
Methaemoglobin's inability to carry oxygen is the clinical hook of the chemical-reactions section — a patient exposed to a strong oxidising agent (certain drugs, nitrites) can become functionally hypoxic despite a normal Hb concentration and normal PaO2, because the Hb present has been converted to a form that cannot bind O2. The free-haemoglobin hazards explain, ahead of the haemolysis discussed in the Anaemia and Blood transfusion chapters, why intravascular haemolysis is dangerous independent of the anaemia it causes: the released haemoglobin itself damages the kidney and burdens the heart.

### Common misconceptions
Calling oxyhaemoglobin formation 'oxidation' — the iron stays Fe2+ throughout; true oxidation (to Fe3+) is what makes methaemoglobin, a different reaction with an opposite clinical meaning (loss of O2-carrying capacity, not its normal form).
Assuming HbF lingers for years after birth — the book states full conversion to HbA by 4 months.
Treating the RBC's lack of a nucleus as incidental — the book frames it as freeing space for haemoglobin, part of the same specialisation the biconcave shape and the missing mitochondria/ribosomes all serve.
Defending the RBC's missing organelles as pure loss rather than trade-off — the space freed by absent mitochondria and ribosomes is exactly what is filled with additional haemoglobin, so the 'missing' structures are the mechanism, not a limitation, behind the cell's O2-carrying capacity.

## summary
Erythropoiesis is the chapter that turns the previous chapter's static red cell into a manufactured, regulated product, and the book organises it as a single question with six answers: what controls how many red cells get made. Oxygen supply and erythropoietin lead the list, but the book is explicit that a healthy marrow, liver, kidneys, the right hormones and an adequate diet are equally necessary conditions, not background detail.

## sections
### Definition
Erythropoiesis is the process of formation of new erythrocytes. Its site differs by age: liver and spleen in the fetus; after birth, active red marrow in the cavities of all bones; by age 20, the marrow in the cavities of long bones (except the upper humerus and femur) becomes inactive yellow marrow infiltrated by fat, so after 20 only the membranous bones — vertebrae, skull, ribs, pelvis — produce erythrocytes. The bone marrow is one of the largest and most active organs in the body, approaching the liver in size and weight.

### Mechanism
Lifespan and fate. Erythrocytes circulate for an average of 120 days, becoming fragile and rupturing in tight circulation sites, especially the spleen. Released haemoglobin is picked up by macrophages and broken into globin and heme; heme is broken into iron, which is reused to make new RBCs, and protoporphyrin, which becomes bilirubin, secreted by the liver in bile.

Role of the spleen. Beyond fetal erythropoiesis, the spleen is a blood filter removing old or abnormal RBCs, a blood-storage site that can add volume in acute haemorrhage, a store for 30% of the body's platelets, and an immune organ with lymphocytes and macrophages.

Six factors the book names as controlling erythropoiesis. (1) Oxygen supply and erythropoietin: hypoxia — from high altitude, exercise, haemorrhage, lung disease or heart failure — stimulates erythropoietin release, which stimulates every step of erythropoiesis by binding erythropoietin-sensitive-cell receptors, promoting mitosis and inhibiting apoptosis. Erythropoietin is a glycoprotein present at low plasma concentration; fetally it is almost entirely hepatic, but in the adult 85% is renal and only 15% hepatic — so renal failure causes severe anaemia because the liver's 15% cannot compensate. Besides hypoxia, high-altitude alkalosis, cobalt salts, androgens, β-adrenergic stimulants and adenosine also stimulate secretion; recombinant erythropoietin treats the anaemia of renal failure and malignancy. (2) Healthy bone marrow — its destruction (X-ray, atomic radiation, drugs such as chloramphenicol, malignant tumours) causes aplastic anaemia. (3) Healthy liver — forms 15% of erythropoietin, stores iron and vitamin B12, and forms globin. (4) Healthy kidneys — form 85% of erythropoietin. (5) Hormones — erythropoietin, androgens, thyroid hormones, glucocorticoids. (6) Diet — iron (haemoglobin synthesis), vitamin B12 and folic acid (RBC division and maturation), high-biological-value protein (globin), and the trace elements copper and cobalt (Hb-formation cofactors; cobalt is also part of vitamin B12 and stimulates erythropoietin).

### Key determinants
Site by age: fetal liver/spleen, then all bone marrow, then (after 20) membranous bones only. The 85%/15% renal/hepatic erythropoietin split, and why the liver's 15% cannot compensate for kidney failure. Six named control factors: oxygen/EPO, marrow, liver, kidneys, hormones, diet. 120-day RBC lifespan, and the iron-reuse/bilirubin fate of a dead cell.

### Clinical significance
The renal/hepatic 85:15 split of erythropoietin production is the single most clinically loaded number in the chapter — it is the direct physiological explanation for the anaemia of chronic kidney disease, and for why recombinant erythropoietin is a standard renal-failure treatment rather than a curiosity. The marrow-site-by-age material explains why a bone-marrow biopsy is taken from the sternum, iliac crest or vertebra in an adult and not from a long-bone shaft — the shaft's marrow has been fatty and inactive since around age 20.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming the liver can substitute for failed renal erythropoietin production — the book is explicit that it cannot, and hepatic production alone is insufficient.
Treating 'bone marrow' as one uniform tissue throughout life — after 20, only the membranous bones (vertebrae, skull, ribs, pelvis) still make blood; long-bone shafts do not.
Listing only erythropoietin as a control factor and forgetting the other five the book names as equally necessary — a healthy marrow, liver and kidneys, the right hormones, and diet.

## summary
Iron is the longest of the module's dietary-factor chapters, and the book builds it as a single pipeline: how much iron the body holds and where, how dietary iron gets from the gut lumen into an enterocyte, what the enterocyte does with it once it is inside, and what one hormone does to control the whole system. Hepcidin is the newest and most mechanistic idea in the chapter, and the one most likely to separate a student who has memorised iron's numbers from one who understands why they hold.

## sections
### Definition
The body contains about 3-5 g of iron: 70% in RBC haemoglobin, 25% stored as ferritin, 3% in muscle myoglobin, 2% distributed in tissue enzymes (cytochromes, peroxidase, catalase), and a small remainder bound to plasma transferrin. Free iron is extremely toxic — it generates reactive oxygen species — so the body keeps it protein-bound at every step. Ferritin, the main storage form, sits mainly in liver cells, spleen, intestinal enterocytes and bone marrow; ferritin molecules coalesce into haemosiderin, a second storage form.

### Mechanism
Daily balance. Iron for new haemoglobin (about 30 mg/day) comes mostly from recycled iron released when macrophages break down old RBCs in the liver and spleen. A normal adult male still loses about 0.5-1 mg/day (sloughed enterocytes, hair, sweat, any blood loss), more in females (about 2 mg/day) from menstruation; keeping iron balance means absorbing dietary iron equal to that loss.

Dietary forms. Haem iron (10% of dietary iron, meat and meat products) is more absorbable; non-haem iron (90%, cereals, vegetables, beans, fruit) is less so. Most dietary iron is ferric (Fe3+), non-absorbable until reduced to ferrous (Fe2+) — ascorbic acid and other reducing substances do this with the help of gastric HCl, so achlorhydria commonly causes iron deficiency, and dietary oxalates, phosphates and phytic acid reduce absorption by forming insoluble complexes with iron.

Absorption mechanism. Enterocytes, mainly duodenal, absorb iron at the apical membrane: DMT1 (divalent metal transporter 1) carries in Fe2+; unreduced Fe3+ is reduced by a duodenal reductase first; haem iron enters separately via HCP1 (haem carrier protein 1). Inside the enterocyte, fate depends on body demand — with decreased demand, iron is stored as ferritin/haemosiderin and lost with the sloughed enterocyte; with increased demand, iron exits at the basolateral border via ferroportin, is oxidised to Fe3+ by ferroxidase, and binds plasma transferrin (two Fe3+ sites, normally 35% saturated), which delivers it to receptor-bearing cells in bone marrow, liver and muscle.

Hepcidin. The body has no mechanism to regulate iron excretion — iron leaves only via shed enterocytes or blood loss — so plasma iron is regulated almost entirely through absorption, and hepcidin (a 25-amino-acid, liver-secreted hormone) is the main regulator. It binds ferroportin on enterocytes (internalising and degrading it, blocking new dietary iron) and on macrophages/hepatocytes (blocking release of recycled iron) — so hepcidin promotes cellular iron storage and lowers plasma iron. The liver senses plasma iron and sets hepcidin accordingly. Hepcidin falls with iron deficiency, hypoxia and erythropoietin; it rises with iron excess and inflammation, which is the mechanistic reason anaemia accompanies many inflammatory conditions.

### Key determinants
The 70/25/3/2% iron distribution across haemoglobin, ferritin/haemosiderin, myoglobin and tissue enzymes. Three transporters with three separate jobs: DMT1 (ferrous iron in), HCP1 (haem iron in), ferroportin (iron out). Hepcidin lowers plasma iron by degrading ferroportin on both enterocytes and macrophages; it rises with inflammation and iron excess, falls with hypoxia, erythropoietin and iron deficiency.

### Clinical significance
Achlorhydria causing iron deficiency is a direct test of whether a student understands that dietary iron absorption is a chemical reduction step, not just an intake question — a patient can eat enough iron and still become deficient if gastric acid cannot reduce it to the absorbable ferrous form. Hepcidin's dual action on enterocytes and macrophages is the physiological basis of anaemia of chronic disease: inflammation raises hepcidin, which locks iron inside macrophages even when total body iron is normal or high, producing a functional (not absolute) iron deficiency that iron supplementation alone does not fully correct.

### Common misconceptions
Conflating DMT1 and ferroportin — DMT1 imports iron at the enterocyte's gut-facing (apical) surface; ferroportin exports it at the opposite (basolateral) surface, and hepcidin regulates ferroportin, not DMT1.
Assuming more dietary iron intake always raises plasma iron — with decreased demand, absorbed iron is simply stored and shed with the enterocyte, never reaching plasma at all.
Treating hepcidin as only an enterocyte hormone — it acts equally on macrophages, which is exactly why inflammation can cause anaemia despite normal or high iron stores.

## summary
Anaemia is where every earlier Blood chapter's failure mode lands — a bone marrow that cannot make cells, an iron pipeline that cannot deliver its raw material, a vitamin that never arrived, a red cell destroyed too early. The book's organising trick is the three-way MCV/MCH split (normocytic, microcytic, macrocytic), which sorts every cause from the preceding chapters into exactly one of three boxes, plus polycythemia as anaemia's numeric opposite.

## sections
### Definition
Anaemia is a decrease in the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood, from decreased RBC number or RBC dysfunction. The book's thresholds: RBC count under 4.5 million/µL in adult males or 3.9 million/µL in females; Hb under 13.5 g/dL in males or 11.5 g/dL in females. Symptoms: shortness of breath, fatigue, tachycardia, palpitation, exercise intolerance, dizziness.

### Mechanism
Three classes by red cell size and haemoglobin content. Normocytic normochromic anaemia — decreased RBC mass and Hb content, MCH and MCV both normal — from bone marrow depression, acute blood loss (the liver replaces lost plasma within two days while the marrow cannot replace lost RBCs as fast, so the remaining cells are diluted), or haemolytic anaemia (excessive RBC destruction, usually with jaundice; intrinsic causes such as hereditary spherocytosis and G6PD deficiency, or extrinsic causes such as incompatible transfusion). Microcytic hypochromic anaemia — MCV under 80 μ3, MCH under 25 pg — is iron-deficiency anaemia, from dietary deficiency (commoner in growing children and pregnancy), deficient absorption (partial gastrectomy, vitamin C deficiency, high phytate/oxalate/phosphate intake, small-intestinal disease), or chronic blood loss (hookworm, peptic ulcer or piles, excessive menstruation). Macrocytic (megaloblastic) anaemia — MCV over 95 μ3 — comes from folic acid deficiency (low intake, increased need as in pregnancy, malabsorption, antifolate cytotoxic drugs such as methotrexate) or vitamin B12 deficiency (defective absorption — post-gastrectomy, absent intrinsic factor as in pernicious anaemia, distal small-intestinal disease).

Polycythemia. The numeric opposite: an increased RBC count, up to 6-8 million/mm3. Primary polycythemia (polycythemia vera) arises from the RBC-forming organs themselves and usually raises WBC and platelet counts too. Secondary polycythemia is tissue hypoxia's compensatory response — obstructive lung disease, high altitude — and does not raise the other cell lines.

### Key determinants
The three-way MCV/MCH split: normocytic (normal MCV/MCH — marrow depression, acute loss, haemolysis), microcytic (MCV under 80, MCH under 25 — iron deficiency), macrocytic (MCV over 95 — B12/folate deficiency). Sex-specific RBC-count and Hb thresholds for anaemia. Primary polycythemia raises WBC and platelets too; secondary (hypoxia-driven) polycythemia does not.

### Clinical significance
The MCV/MCH classification is the single most efficient diagnostic tool the chapter gives: a clinician who knows only a patient's MCV and MCH can already narrow the cause list to one of the three boxes above before ordering a single further test — normocytic points to marrow failure, acute loss or haemolysis; microcytic points to iron; macrocytic points to B12/folate. Distinguishing primary from secondary polycythemia by whether WBCs and platelets are also raised is exactly the kind of one-line discriminator this module rewards on a short-answer question.

### Common misconceptions
Quoting one universal RBC-count or Hb threshold for 'anaemia' regardless of sex — the book gives separate male/female cut-offs for both measures.
Assuming acute haemorrhage anaemia is a red-cell problem — in the first two days it is a dilutional problem: the liver replaces lost plasma faster than the marrow replaces lost cells.
Treating secondary polycythemia as a marrow disease — it is a normal, appropriate hypoxic response, and (unlike primary polycythemia) leaves WBC and platelet counts unaffected.
That single discriminator — whether WBC and platelet counts move together with the RBC count, or move alone — is often the fastest way to separate the two forms of polycythemia before a bone marrow examination is even considered, in exactly the same way MCV and MCH separate anaemia's three classes before any further work-up begins.
Collapsing 'macrocytic' and 'megaloblastic' into two separate ideas — the book uses them as one name for the same class, defined by the shared MCV-over-95 criterion regardless of which of the two vitamin deficiencies produced it.

## sections
### Definition
Anticoagulants are substances used, in vitro or in vivo, to prevent blood clotting.

### Mechanism
In vitro anticoagulants prevent clotting outside the body: precipitation of Ca++ by oxalate; deionisation of Ca++ by citrate (used to prevent clotting of blood collected for transfusion); collecting blood in unwettable silicone-coated tubes, which prevents activation of factor XII and platelets; and heparin. Each of the first three works by a different chemistry on the same target — coagulation's near-universal requirement for free calcium: oxalate removes it by forming an insoluble calcium-oxalate precipitate; citrate removes it by chelation, binding calcium into a soluble but unreactive complex, which is why citrate rather than oxalate is the anticoagulant used for blood collected for transfusion (a precipitate is not something you want to reinfuse); and an unwettable silicone coating denies factor XII and platelets the electronegative, wettable surface that would otherwise activate them purely by contact, the same contact-activation route the book describes in the coagulation chapter that precedes this one.

In vivo anticoagulants prevent clotting inside the body: heparin and the coumarin derivatives (warfarin, dicumarol).

Heparin. A naturally occurring anticoagulant, produced by mast cells and basophils, present in small amounts in normal blood. It facilitates the action of antithrombin III, which then blocks activated factors IXa, Xa, XIa and XIIa. It acts both in vivo and in vitro, has a rapid onset and a short duration, is given by injection, and its antidote is protamine sulfate 1%.

Dicumarol (coumarin/warfarin). Acts by competitive inhibition of vitamin K in the liver, which blocks formation of prothrombin and factors VII, IX and X, plus proteins C and S. Unlike heparin it acts only in vivo, has a slow onset and a long duration, is given orally, and its antidote is vitamin K (plus, if urgent, blood transfusion). Because warfarin acts by blocking synthesis of new clotting factors rather than by neutralising factors already circulating, its effect only appears once the existing factors have naturally decayed — which is also why its reversal by vitamin K is slow compared with protamine's near-immediate reversal of heparin.

### Applied physiology
The onset/duration/route profile in this chapter's own table is not incidental detail — it is the reason clinical practice pairs the two drug classes the way it does. Heparin's rapid onset and short duration make it the anticoagulant for situations needing immediate, easily reversible effect: starting anticoagulation in hospital, or maintaining an uncoagulated blood sample outside the body. Warfarin's slow onset and long duration make it unsuitable for an emergency but appropriate for long-term outpatient management, where a once-daily oral tablet with a durable effect is the practical requirement — at the cost of needing time to establish and time to reverse.

### Key determinants
Four in vitro mechanisms (oxalate, citrate, silicone-coated tubes, heparin) against two in vivo drug classes (heparin, coumarins). The heparin-versus-dicumarol table — origin, mechanism, site of action, onset, duration, route, antidote — is the chapter's entire examinable content in one comparison.

### Clinical significance
The heparin-versus-dicumarol table is not academic trivia — it is the physiological basis for why heparin is the anticoagulant used for immediate, in-hospital anticoagulation (rapid onset, reversible with protamine, works even in vitro for a blood sample) while warfarin is the anticoagulant used for long-term outpatient management (oral, slow onset, requires monitoring because its effect persists once started). A patient bridged from heparin to warfarin at the start of anticoagulation therapy is being managed around exactly this onset-time difference.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming heparin and warfarin share a mechanism because both are 'anticoagulants' — heparin potentiates an existing inhibitor (antithrombin III); warfarin blocks the liver's ability to manufacture the clotting factors in the first place, by competing with vitamin K.
Forgetting that citrate and oxalate both work by the same principle (removing free Ca++, which coagulation requires at almost every step) via two different chemistries (chelation versus precipitation).
Calling this chapter 'not examinable' outright — it is excluded from the written paper only; it remains taught and practically examinable.

## summary
Excluded from the written paper but the module's most clinically named chapter, this one sorts every haemostasis failure the book covers into three buckets — too much bleeding, too much clotting, or both at once — and closes with the three lab tests that tell them apart. It is written to be read as the applied companion to the Platelets/haemostasis and Physiological-limitations-of-coagulation chapters that precede it.

## sections
### Definition
Three categories of haemostatic failure. Conditions causing excessive bleeding: thrombocytopenic purpura, vitamin K deficiency, haemophilia. Conditions causing excessive intravascular clotting (thromboembolic conditions): from slow blood flow (e.g. leg veins after prolonged bed rest) or atherosclerosis (roughened vascular endothelium). Conditions causing both bleeding and clotting together: disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC).

### Mechanism
Thrombocytopenic purpura. From a platelet count below 50,000/mm3, or from defective platelet function (thrombasthenia). Characterised by petechiae (many subcutaneous haemorrhages) and a prolonged bleeding time — the time for bleeding to stop without clotting, normally 1-3 minutes, depending on platelet count and function.

Vitamin K deficiency. Vitamin K, a fat-soluble vitamin made by intestinal bacterial flora, is needed for hepatic formation of factors II, VII, IX, X and proteins C and S. Deficiency lowers formation of these factors and prolongs coagulation time. Causes: absent intestinal flora (newborns), prolonged antibiotic treatment, or bile-duct obstruction (bile is needed to absorb the fat-soluble vitamin).

Haemophilia. Sex-linked recessive, carried by females, manifested almost always in males; causes haemorrhage into joints or internal organs after minor trauma, and prolongs whole blood coagulation time. Three types: haemophilia A (factor VIII deficiency, 85% of cases, 'classic' haemophilia), haemophilia B (factor IX absent), haemophilia C (factor XI absent).

DIC. Widespread clotting over-utilises platelets and coagulation factors, dropping their plasma level and causing bleeding from that consumption — clotting and bleeding at once, from the same event. Elicited by massive thromboplastin release from necrotic tissue: shock, tissue necrosis, septicaemia, or a dead fetus retained in the uterus for weeks.

The three screening tests. Coagulation time — time for blood to clot, normally 3-10 minutes, prolonged in both extrinsic and intrinsic disorders (vitamin K deficiency, haemophilia, liver disease). Prothrombin time — tests the extrinsic system (factor VII), normal 15 seconds, prolonged in vitamin K deficiency. APTT — tests the intrinsic system (factors XII, XI, IX, VIII), normal 30-40 seconds, prolonged in haemophilia.

### Key determinants
Three bleeding disorders (thrombocytopenic purpura, vitamin K deficiency, haemophilia) against thromboembolic conditions (pure clotting) against DIC (both at once, from consumption). Three screening tests mapped to three targets: bleeding time (platelets), prothrombin time (extrinsic pathway/factor VII), APTT (intrinsic pathway/factors VIII, IX, XI, XII). Haemophilia A (factor VIII) is 85% of haemophilia cases.

### Clinical significance
The three-test panel (coagulation time, PT, APTT) is the applied point of the whole chapter: given a prolonged APTT with a normal PT, the book's own logic identifies an intrinsic-pathway defect such as haemophilia before any factor assay is run; given both PT and APTT prolonged with a low platelet count, DIC becomes the leading diagnosis rather than a single-factor deficiency. This is the closest the module comes to teaching a diagnostic algorithm rather than a fact list.

### Common misconceptions
Confusing bleeding time with coagulation time — bleeding time (1-3 min) tests the platelet plug and is what is prolonged in thrombocytopenic purpura; coagulation time (3-10 min) tests the full cascade and is prolonged in vitamin K deficiency and haemophilia instead.
Expecting PT to be prolonged in haemophilia — PT tests the extrinsic pathway (factor VII) and stays normal; APTT (intrinsic pathway) is the test that is prolonged, since haemophilia's factors (VIII, IX, XI) are all intrinsic.
Calling DIC simply 'a clotting disorder' — its bleeding half, from consumption of platelets and factors, is the more dangerous clinical problem and is what most often brings the patient to attention.
Assuming all three haemophilia types are equally common — type A (factor VIII) alone is 85% of cases.
A clinician who reasons through DIC only as 'excessive clotting' will keep looking for thrombosis on examination while the patient is actually bruising and oozing from every venepuncture site — the bleeding is not a separate, coincidental problem but the direct arithmetic consequence of the clotting that came before it, exactly as the Mechanism section above states.

Blood groups (ABO and Rhesus systems) are genetically determined antigens on RBC membranes, detected by reaction with corresponding plasma antibodies.

### Mechanism
ABO system. RBC membranes carry A antigen, B antigen, both, or neither; plasma correspondingly carries anti-B, anti-A, neither, or both antibodies — never the antibody against one's own antigen. The antigen-antibody reaction causes agglutination, so the antigens are agglutinogens, the antibodies agglutinins. Four groups: A (genotype AA/AO, anti-B antibody, 40%), B (BB/BO, anti-A, 10%), AB (both antigens, no antibody, 5%), O (neither antigen, both antibodies, 45%). Group O is the universal donor, AB the universal recipient. Before transfusion, a cross-matching test (donor blood added to recipient serum) is required — donor-RBCs-meet-recipient-antibodies is the reaction that matters, since the reverse is diluted in the recipient's larger plasma volume. ABO typing can exclude, never prove, paternity.

Rh system. The D antigen (Rh factor) sits on 85% of the population's RBCs (Rh-positive); 15% lack it (Rh-negative). Neither group normally carries anti-D antibodies. An Rh-negative person transfused with Rh-positive blood becomes sensitised, forming anti-D; a second Rh-positive exposure then causes agglutination and haemolysis. An Rh-positive person never forms anti-D, regardless of what they receive.

Erythroblastosis fetalis. When an Rh-positive father and Rh-negative mother conceive an Rh-positive fetus, fetal RBCs cross into the maternal circulation at delivery and sensitise the mother (IgG anti-D). In a later Rh-positive pregnancy, that IgG crosses the placenta and hemolyses fetal RBCs; the first baby usually escapes, later ones are affected (unless the mother was already sensitised by a prior transfusion). The affected baby is severely anaemic and jaundiced; because the fetal blood-brain barrier is immature, bilirubin can reach the brain (kernicterus), and severe cases are stillborn. ABO causes no equivalent fetal disease — its antibodies are IgM and cannot cross the placenta. Prophylaxis: Rh-negative women should never receive Rh-positive blood; anti-D immunoglobulin is given to non-sensitised Rh-negative women with Rh-positive partners after delivery of an Rh-positive baby (within 72 hours), at abortion, antepartum haemorrhage or amniocentesis, and now routinely at 28 weeks; contraindicated once already sensitised (positive indirect Coombs test, or a previously affected baby).

Blood transfusion. Indications: restoring whole blood in haemorrhage, restoring one deficient element (RBCs, WBCs, platelets, plasma proteins, clotting factors), treating erythroblastosis fetalis. Precautions: compatibility with cross-matching; Rh-negative patients get Rh-negative blood; blood free of disease/contamination; blood fresh, stored under 21 days at 4°C, high Hb%. Incompatible transfusion: donor RBCs agglutinate under recipient antibodies, then haemolyse — clumped RBCs block capillaries (backache, joint pain, anginal pain if coronary), and the haemolysis itself causes shock (histamine/vasodilators dropping BP), hyperkalaemia (K+ from RBCs, arrhythmia), haemolytic jaundice (bilirubin), and renal tubule blockage (filtered Hb forms acid haematin).

### Key determinants
Four ABO groups by antigen/antibody pairing, with O the universal donor and AB the universal recipient. Rh sensitisation always needs a first exposure before a second one can react. Erythroblastosis fetalis needs two pregnancies — sensitise, then attack — and anti-D prophylaxis only works before sensitisation, never after. Four hazards of incompatible-transfusion haemolysis: shock, hyperkalaemia, jaundice, renal tubule blockage.

### Clinical significance
The reason cross-matching, not just ABO/Rh typing, precedes every transfusion is directly this chapter's own logic: agglutination in vivo happens when donor RBCs meet recipient antibodies, and cross-matching tests exactly that direction. Anti-D prophylaxis timing (within 72 hours of delivery, or at 28 weeks) is not an arbitrary protocol number — it follows directly from the sensitisation mechanism, destroying fetal RBCs before the mother's first immune response can form.

### Common misconceptions
Reversing which direction of agglutination matters — donor RBCs into recipient plasma is dangerous; the reverse is diluted and rarely matters.
Assuming Rh incompatibility causes a reaction on first exposure like ABO can — the first Rh-positive exposure of an Rh-negative person only sensitises; the dangerous reaction is on re-exposure.
Giving anti-D prophylaxis to an already-sensitised mother — explicitly contraindicated, since prophylaxis prevents a first immune response that, in a sensitised woman, has already happened.
Attributing intravascular-haemolysis hazards to the agglutination step itself — shock, hyperkalaemia, jaundice and renal blockage are all downstream of the haemolysis that follows agglutination, not of agglutination directly.

## summary
Before the book can teach the autonomic nervous system it teaches the nervous system in general — its two anatomical divisions, its two physiological divisions, the neuron and the synapse as its building blocks, and the reflex arc as its functional unit. This short opening chapter is the vocabulary every later ANS chapter (ganglia, sympathetic, parasympathetic, chemical transmission) assumes without re-explaining.

## sections
### Definition
The nervous system, with the endocrine system, is one of the body's two major regulatory systems. It receives millions of pieces of information every minute from sensory organs about the internal and external environment, and integrates them to determine a rapid, precise bodily response.

### Mechanism
Anatomical division. Central nervous system (CNS): the brain and the spinal cord. Peripheral nervous system (PNS): peripheral ganglia and peripheral nerves.

Physiological division of the PNS. A sensory division, carrying information from the periphery to the CNS via sensory fibres — somatic sensory (skin, muscles, joints, special senses) and visceral sensory (from the viscera). A motor division, carrying information from the CNS outward, via two systems operating in parallel: the somatic nervous system (controls skeletal muscle throughout the body) and the autonomic nervous system (controls smooth muscle, glands, and cardiac muscle). Sensory information is processed — the nervous system's integrative function — at three levels: the spinal cord, the lower brain/subcortical level, and the higher brain/cortical level.

The neuron. The nervous system's structural unit: a cell body (nucleus, organelles), dendrites (receive incoming impulses), and an axon/nerve fibre (carries impulses away from the cell body — myelinated or non-myelinated). A nerve is made of many nerve fibres. Neurons interconnect at synapses — the site where one neuron's axon terminates on another's dendrites, soma or axon — transmitting signals; the synaptic cleft is the gap chemical transmitters cross, since there is no direct neuron-to-neuron communication. This single fact — that every synapse is a chemical, not electrical, handoff — is the premise the Chemical transmission chapter later in this module is built on.

Afferent and efferent fibres. Afferent (sensory) fibres carry impulses from peripheral receptors to the CNS — about the external world (light, sound, temperature, pressure) and the internal state (muscle tension, visceral distension) — entering the spinal cord through the posterior root. Efferent (motor) fibres carry impulses from the CNS to muscles, glands and organs, leaving through the anterior root.

The reflex arc. The nervous system's functional unit: a sensory organ, an afferent neuron, one or more CNS synapses, an efferent neuron, an effector organ. A monosynaptic reflex has a single afferent-efferent synapse (the simplest reflex arc); a polysynaptic reflex has one or more interneurons between them. Even the simplest reflex the book describes still passes through the CNS once; nothing in this module's account of the nervous system routes a reflex around the CNS entirely.

### Key determinants
Two anatomical divisions (CNS/PNS), two physiological divisions (sensory/motor), and the motor division's own two parallel systems (somatic/autonomic). The reflex arc's five components, and monosynaptic versus polysynaptic by synapse count alone. Afferent fibres enter posteriorly; efferent fibres leave anteriorly.

### Clinical significance
The posterior-root/anterior-root rule (afferent in, efferent out) is the anatomical basis of a spinal nerve root lesion's sensory-versus-motor sign pattern, and the somatic/autonomic split in the motor division is the reason a single spinal cord injury can produce both a voluntary-movement deficit and a separate, distinct autonomic deficit (bladder, bowel, vasomotor control) — two findings from one lesion, explained by two different fibre systems running in parallel from the start.

### Common misconceptions
Equating 'motor' with only the somatic system — the book names two parallel motor systems, somatic and autonomic, not one with an autonomic exception.
Swapping afferent and efferent by root — afferent enters posteriorly, efferent leaves anteriorly, not the reverse.
Assuming a monosynaptic reflex has no CNS involvement — it still has exactly one CNS synapse; 'monosynaptic' describes the count, not the absence, of a central relay. Even the simplest reflex the book describes still passes through the CNS once; nothing in this module's account of the nervous system routes a reflex around the CNS entirely.

## summary
One architectural fact organises everything the book teaches about the ANS from here on: the autonomic efferent pathway is always two neurons, never one. This short chapter states that fact and the book's own five-row comparison against the somatic system, and every later ANS chapter (ganglia, sympathetic, parasympathetic, chemical transmission) is built as a further specification of the same two-neuron chain.

## sections
### Definition
The autonomic nervous system controls visceral functions: arterial blood pressure, gastrointestinal motility and secretion, urinary bladder emptying, sweating, body temperature, and more.

### Mechanism
General organisation of autonomic outflow. It operates through visceral reflexes: impulses from visceral receptors are relayed by afferent fibres to CNS centres in the spinal cord, brainstem, hypothalamus and parts of the cerebral cortex, then transmitted by efferent pathways to visceral effectors — plain (smooth) muscle, heart, glands. The efferent pathways divide into two major subdivisions: the sympathetic nervous system and the parasympathetic nervous system.

The one-neuron-versus-two-neuron rule. Somatic motor pathways, from cord to skeletal muscle, are single fibres — a one-neuron system. Autonomic, involuntary pathways are always two fibres — the preganglionic neuron and the postganglionic neuron — a two-neuron system. Every autonomic pathway, without exception, relays through a ganglion outside the CNS; there is no direct CNS-to-effector autonomic fibre. This is the single structural fact that makes 'autonomic' and 'somatic' different categories in the first place, not just different destinations for the same kind of wiring.

The book's five-row somatic-versus-autonomic comparison. Afferent relay: dorsal horn cells or cranial nuclei (somatic) versus lateral horn cells or cranial nuclei (autonomic). Efferent origin: anterior horn cells of the spinal cord (somatic) versus cranial nuclei or lateral horn cells (autonomic). Efferent fibre count: one-neuron system (somatic) versus two-neuron system (autonomic). Myelination: mostly myelinated (somatic) versus preganglionic myelinated B fibre, postganglionic unmyelinated C fibre (autonomic). Chemical transmitter: one type only, acetylcholine (somatic) versus two types, acetylcholine and noradrenaline (autonomic). Reading the five rows as one pattern rather than five isolated facts, each autonomic row is a direct structural consequence of the same two-neuron architecture: two neurons need two release sites, hence potentially two transmitters; a peripheral relay is a synapse outside the CNS, hence a distinct efferent origin from the CNS's own anterior horn; and a peripheral relay through the sympathetic chain or a parasympathetic terminal ganglion is what makes a short, unmyelinated postganglionic fibre worthwhile in a way a long myelinated one would not be.

### Key determinants
The defining rule: one-neuron somatic pathway versus two-neuron (preganglionic + postganglionic) autonomic pathway, with no exception. The book's five-row somatic-versus-autonomic comparison: afferent relay site, efferent origin, efferent fibre count, myelination, and transmitter type.

### Clinical significance
The two-neuron rule is the anatomical reason autonomic ganglion-blocking drugs exist as a distinct pharmacological class — a drug acting at the ganglionic synapse can interrupt both sympathetic and parasympathetic outflow at once, something no analogous single-synapse target exists for in the one-neuron somatic system, since a somatic pathway has no intervening synapse outside the CNS for a drug to act on at all. The myelination row of the comparison table (preganglionic B fibre myelinated, postganglionic C fibre unmyelinated) is the physiological basis for autonomic conduction being measurably slower than somatic conduction, which matters directly when interpreting a nerve-conduction study that includes autonomic fibres.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming the autonomic pathway can run directly from CNS to effector the way a very short somatic reflex might feel like it does — it cannot; the ganglion relay is what defines the system as autonomic in the first place.
Assuming both autonomic neurons share the same myelination as the somatic efferent fibre — only the preganglionic B fibre is myelinated; the postganglionic C fibre is not.
Treating 'chemical transmitter' as a single fact for the whole nervous system — the book is explicit that autonomic pathways use two transmitter types (acetylcholine and noradrenaline) against the somatic system's one (acetylcholine only).
Reducing 'visceral reflex' to a synonym for 'autonomic reflex' without noting where each half of the arc runs — the afferent half of a visceral reflex still travels through ordinary sensory pathways to the CNS; it is only the efferent half, once the CNS has responded, that is committed to the two-neuron autonomic architecture this chapter describes.
