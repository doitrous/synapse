## title
Histology of the pituitary gland: cell types and the two lobes
## subject
endo
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Histology
## summary
The pituitary has two histologically distinct parts. The adenohypophysis (pars distalis) is built of chromophils and chromophobes: acidophils, which are smaller and take up the acid dye orange G, secrete growth hormone and prolactin, while basophils, which are larger and PAS-positive, secrete the glycoprotein hormones TSH, FSH, LH and the ACTH-related peptides. The thin pars intermedia processes pro-opiomelanocortin to melanocyte-stimulating hormone. The neurohypophysis (pars nervosa) is neural tissue holding only unmyelinated hypothalamic axons, their Herring-body storage terminals, glial pituicytes and fenestrated capillaries; it has no neuron cell bodies, because oxytocin and vasopressin are made by the magnocellular supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei and merely stored here.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the pituitary histology facts tested by the Dr Zahra endocrine set: the staining and size of acidophils versus basophils, orange-G selectivity for acidophils, the hormones of the chromophils (excluding oxytocin), MSH from the pars intermedia, the glial nature of pituicytes, and the cellular composition of the pars nervosa.
### Mechanism
Cell classification follows staining: acidophils bind orange G, basophils are PAS-positive because their glycoprotein hormones carry carbohydrate. The posterior lobe stores rather than makes its hormones: magnocellular neurons in the hypothalamus synthesise oxytocin and vasopressin and transport them down the hypothalamo-hypophyseal tract to Herring bodies, with pituicytes providing glial support.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: acidophil (orange G, smaller) versus basophil (PAS, larger); anterior-pituitary chromophil hormones versus the neurohypophyseal oxytocin; presence of axons/Herring bodies/pituicytes but absence of neuron cell bodies in the pars nervosa; and MSH as the pars intermedia product.
### Clinical significance
These distinctions underlie the histological reading of pituitary adenomas by cell type (acidophil versus basophil tumours), the source of ectopic MSH/ACTH activity, and the understanding that posterior-pituitary lesions reflect hypothalamic neurons rather than local secretory cells.

See each concept's pitfalls field for the specific reversal a hurried reader makes; the distractors in these questions are built from exactly those reversals (pituicytes secreting hormone, oxytocin as an anterior-lobe product, acidophils being the larger PAS-positive cells).

## title
Histology of the thyroid and parathyroid glands
## subject
endo
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Histology
## summary
The thyroid follicular cell both synthesises and releases thyroid hormone: thyroglobulin is made on the rough endoplasmic reticulum, iodinated extracellularly in the follicular lumen, then re-endocytosed as colloid droplets that fuse with lysosomes whose enzymes hydrolyse thyroglobulin to liberate T3 and T4. Lysosomes are therefore essential for hormone release. The parathyroid gland has two parenchymal cells: the numerous chief cells, which secrete parathyroid hormone, and the larger but far fewer oxyphil cells, which are acidophilic, mitochondria-rich, appear at puberty, increase with age and are regarded as degenerated chief cells with no established function.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the thyroid and parathyroid histology facts tested by the Dr Zahra endocrine set: the role of follicular-cell lysosomes in releasing T3 and T4, and the identity and staining of parathyroid oxyphil cells.
### Mechanism
Thyroid-hormone release is a lysosomal event: endocytosed colloid fuses with lysosomes, and proteolysis of thyroglobulin frees T3 and T4. Oxyphil identity follows from their packed mitochondria, which make the cytoplasm strongly acidophilic and account for their appearance and increase with age.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: hydrolysis (lysosomal) versus synthesis (rER) versus iodination (apical/lumen) in the follicular cell; and oxyphil cells being acidophilic, few and large rather than basophilic.
### Clinical significance
The lysosomal release step underlies how antithyroid drugs and thyroglobulin handling affect hormone output, while oxyphil cells matter in the histological reading of parathyroid adenomas and their age-related expansion.
### Common misconceptions
See each concept's pitfalls field for the specific reversal a hurried reader makes; the distractors here are built from those reversals (oxyphils as basophilic, or lysosomes synthesising rather than hydrolysing thyroglobulin).

## title
Histology of the adrenal medulla, mineralocorticoids and paraganglia
## subject
endo
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Histology
## summary
The adrenal medulla is a modified sympathetic ganglion built of catecholamine-storing chromaffin cells together with scattered true sympathetic ganglion cells. Chromaffin cells also occur outside the gland as paraganglia, small neural-crest-derived aggregates along the sympathetic chain such as the para-aortic bodies of Zuckerkandl. In the overlying cortex the zona glomerulosa secretes mineralocorticoids (chiefly aldosterone) as steroid hormones, and their release is regulated mainly by angiotensin II of the renin-angiotensin system rather than by ACTH.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the adrenal facts tested by the Dr Zahra endocrine and department sets: the cell composition of the medulla, the extra-adrenal chromaffin paraganglia, and the angiotensin-II control of mineralocorticoid secretion.
### Mechanism
Chromaffin cells are modified postganglionic sympathetic neurons that give the chromaffin reaction; the same cell type forms paraganglia elsewhere. Mineralocorticoid output is set by angiotensin II acting on the zona glomerulosa, with plasma potassium as a second stimulus.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: chromaffin plus ganglion cells (not glial) in the medulla; paraganglia as chromaffin aggregates (not cortical zona cells); and angiotensin II (not ACTH) as the main aldosterone regulator, aldosterone being steroid not protein.
### Clinical significance
These points underlie phaeochromocytoma (chromaffin-cell tumour) and extra-adrenal paraganglioma, and the renin-angiotensin control that is central to hyperaldosteronism and blood-pressure regulation.
### Common misconceptions
See each concept's pitfalls field for the specific reversal a hurried reader makes; the distractors here are built from those reversals (medulla as glial, aldosterone as ACTH-driven or protein, paraganglia as cortical).

## title
Histology of the pineal gland: pinealocytes and brain sand
## subject
endo
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Histology
## summary
The pineal gland is dominated by pinealocytes, secretory cells whose output follows the light-dark cycle: they release melatonin during darkness and its precursor serotonin during daylight, linking the gland to circadian timing. With age the gland accumulates corpora arenacea (brain sand), extracellular calcified concretions that are a normal, age-increasing finding; because they are radio-opaque and midline they serve as a landmark on skull radiographs and CT.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the pineal histology facts tested by the Dr Zahra endocrine set: the diurnal secretion of pinealocytes and the nature and use of corpora arenacea (brain sand).
### Mechanism
Pinealocyte secretion is driven by the light-dark cycle through the sympathetic pathway, giving nocturnal melatonin and daytime serotonin. Brain sand forms by progressive calcification within the gland over the lifespan.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: melatonin at night versus serotonin by day; and brain sand as normal, age-increasing and radio-opaque rather than pathological or shrinking.
### Clinical significance
Melatonin timing underlies circadian and jet-lag physiology, while midline pineal calcification is a practical radiological landmark for detecting midline shift.
### Common misconceptions
See each concept's pitfalls field for the specific reversal a hurried reader makes; the distractors here are built from those reversals (reversed melatonin timing, or brain sand treated as disease).
