## summary
The head and neck's named nerves each have a signature exit point and territory that exam questions test directly: the facial nerve leaves the skull at the stylomastoid foramen before its five parotid branches, the abducent nerve supplies lateral rectus alone, the hypoglossal nerve crosses the exposed floor of the submandibular triangle and its injury deviates the protruded tongue toward the weak side, and the glossopharyngeal nerve carries both sensation and taste from the posterior third of the tongue, putting it at risk during tonsillectomy. The neck's fascial planes and triangles are equally landmark-driven: omohyoid subdivides the posterior triangle, the digastric bellies bound the submental and submandibular triangles, the deep cervical nodes and internal jugular vein travel together inside the carotid sheath, and the stylomandibular ligament — a thickening of the investing fascia layer — separates the parotid and submandibular gland beds. A short list of muscles breaks their group's usual innervation pattern and is worth memorising as exceptions: tensor veli palatini alone among palate muscles is trigeminal (not vagal); palatoglossus alone among tongue muscles is vagal (not hypoglossal); and lateral pterygoid alone among the muscles of mastication opens rather than closes the jaw. Regional anatomy rounds out the picture — the maxillary sinus drains via the middle meatus, Little's area on the anterior nasal septum is the commonest bleeding site, the cavernous sinus sits beside the pituitary and is the classic site of septic sinus thrombosis, the tympanic membrane forms the lateral wall of the middle ear, and the internal laryngeal nerve is purely sensory above the vocal folds while the recurrent laryngeal nerve does the rest.

## sections

### Definition
The facial nerve (CN VII) exits the skull through the stylomastoid foramen, just posterior to the styloid process, before entering the parotid gland and dividing into five terminal branches (temporal, zygomatic, buccal, marginal mandibular, cervical); the marginal mandibular branch supplies only the lower lip muscles, and the facial nerve supplies the posterior (not anterior) belly of digastric. The abducent nerve (CN VI) supplies a single muscle, lateral rectus, so an isolated abducent palsy produces a pure horizontal deviation without affecting lid position or other eye movements. The hypoglossal nerve (CN XII) loops through the floor of the submandibular triangle, the most exposed named structure there to a penetrating wound; its injury paralyses ipsilateral genioglossus, and because the healthy contralateral genioglossus goes unopposed on protrusion, the tongue deviates toward the WEAK (injured) side — a common localising vignette. The glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX) runs against the tonsillar bed and carries both general sensation and taste from the posterior third of the tongue, making it the nerve classically injured during tonsillectomy and explaining isolated posterior-tongue taste loss afterward (the anterior two-thirds is lingual nerve/chorda tympani territory instead). The great auricular nerve, a cervical plexus branch (C2-C3) rather than a cranial nerve proper, supplies skin over the angle of the mandible and parotid region, and is at particular risk during parotidectomy because of its superficial course.

The superior laryngeal nerve splits into an internal branch (purely sensory, piercing the thyrohyoid membrane to supply mucosa ABOVE the vocal folds) and an external branch (motor, supplying cricothyroid only). Every other intrinsic laryngeal muscle, and all mucosal sensation AT and below the vocal folds, is recurrent laryngeal nerve territory. This split is why superior laryngeal nerve injury classically reduces sensation above the folds together with vocal pitch, rather than causing airway obstruction (which instead follows recurrent laryngeal nerve injury).

### Mechanism
Omohyoid's inferior belly crosses the posterior triangle and subdivides it into an occipital triangle above and a subclavian (supraclavicular) triangle below — the subclavian triangle being where the subclavian artery and brachial plexus trunks are surgically accessed. In the anterior triangle, the two anterior bellies of digastric bound the submental triangle between them, while a penetrating wound of the submandibular triangle (bounded by both digastric bellies and the mandible) most often injures the hypoglossal nerve running through its floor. The ansa cervicalis, embedded in the anterior wall of the carotid sheath, has a superior root that travels with the hypoglossal nerve (ultimately C1 fibres) and a separate inferior root (descendens cervicalis) that arises directly from C2 and C3; together the loop supplies the infrahyoid strap muscles. The deep cervical lymph node chain runs the length of the neck alongside the internal jugular vein inside the carotid sheath, distinct from the superficial cervical nodes that run with the external jugular vein instead. The stylomandibular ligament, a thickened band of the investing layer of deep cervical fascia running from the styloid process to the mandibular angle, keeps the parotid and submandibular gland beds anatomically separated. Each sternocleidomastoid rotates the face toward the OPPOSITE side while tilting the head to its own side — a right sternocleidomastoid contraction turns the face left, which is also why testing SCM strength against resistance evaluates the accessory nerve on the side opposite the direction the patient turns.

Three small, frequently-tested exceptions run through this module. Among the muscles of mastication (masseter, temporalis, medial pterygoid, lateral pterygoid), only lateral pterygoid opens (depresses) the jaw; the other three close it, so a lateral pterygoid palsy deviates the jaw toward the weak side on opening. Among the palate muscles (levator veli palatini, tensor veli palatini, palatoglossus, palatopharyngeus, musculus uvulae), only tensor veli palatini is supplied by the trigeminal nerve (CN V3); every other palate muscle is pharyngeal plexus (vagal). Among the tongue muscles, only palatoglossus is supplied by the pharyngeal plexus (vagal) rather than the hypoglossal nerve, because it is developmentally a palatal rather than a true tongue muscle. Mylohyoid, by contrast, is the intact "diaphragm of the oral cavity" — its paired sheet, meeting in a median raphe and attached along the mandible's mylohyoid line, forms the floor of the mouth supporting the tongue above it. The superior pharyngeal constrictor arises in part from the pterygomandibular raphe, a fibrous (not bony) band running from the pterygoid hamulus to the posterior mylohyoid line.

### Key determinants
The maxillary sinus drains through its ostium into the hiatus semilunaris, located in the MIDDLE meatus of the lateral nasal wall (alongside the frontal and anterior ethmoidal sinus openings), so a middle-meatus obstruction can block several sinuses' outflow at once. Little's area (Kiesselbach's plexus), an anastomotic network of small vessels on the ANTERIOR nasal septum, is the commonest site of epistaxis because these vessels sit superficially with little protective tissue. The tympanic membrane forms almost the entire LATERAL wall of the middle ear cavity, allowing direct otoscopic visualisation and explaining why a perforation connects the middle ear to the external environment. The cavernous sinus lies directly lateral to the pituitary gland and sphenoid sinus — the dural venous sinus closest to the pituitary — and is the classic site of septic dural sinus thrombosis, because it receives the ophthalmic veins and drains the facial venous plexus, giving facial/orbital infection a direct, valveless route in.

### Clinical significance
Localising a lesion from a clinical sign is this module's recurring exam pattern: the direction a protruded tongue deviates on protrusion identifies the side of a hypoglossal nerve injury, isolated posterior-tongue taste loss after tonsillectomy identifies glossopharyngeal nerve injury, and a mild (rather than complete) ptosis points to sympathetic loss of levator palpebrae superioris's smooth part specifically (Horner's syndrome) rather than a full oculomotor palsy. The three cutaneous branches of the ophthalmic nerve (V1) split the upper eyelid and forehead by position: the lacrimal nerve (most lateral) supplies the lateral upper eyelid, while the supraorbital and supratrochlear nerves cover the medial/central territory — levator palpebrae superioris's smooth-muscle part (superior tarsal/Müller's muscle) is supplied by sympathetic fibres, distinct from the skeletal part's oculomotor supply. Vascular anatomy also carries direct clinical weight: the middle meningeal artery's bony course through foramen spinosum is exactly why a pterion fracture can lacerate it and produce a rapidly expanding extradural haematoma, while the ascending pharyngeal artery (an external carotid branch, distinct from the subclavian-system inferior thyroid, internal thoracic and superficial cervical arteries) and the cavernous sinus's septic-thrombosis risk from facial/orbital infection round out the vessel-based exam and clinical picture for this module.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming the tongue deviates AWAY from a hypoglossal nerve injury, rather than toward the weak (injured) side, because the healthy contralateral genioglossus is what wins unopposed. Assuming sternocleidomastoid turns the face toward its OWN side — the action is opposite-side rotation. Placing the maxillary sinus ostium in the inferior or superior meatus rather than the middle meatus. Attributing all palate or tongue muscles to one nerve — tensor veli palatini (trigeminal) and palatoglossus (vagal) are each the sole exception in their group. Confusing the internal laryngeal nerve's purely sensory, above-the-folds territory with the recurrent laryngeal nerve's motor and below-the-folds territory. Crediting the superficial cervical chain (external jugular vein) for a fact that belongs to the deep cervical chain (internal jugular vein), or vice versa.
