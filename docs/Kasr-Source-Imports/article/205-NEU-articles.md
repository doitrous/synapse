## title
Head and neck anatomy: cranial nerves, neck triangles and fascia, nose, ear, pharynx and orbit

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## topic
Anatomy

## summary
The head and neck's named nerves each have a signature exit point and territory that exam questions test directly: the facial nerve leaves the skull at the stylomastoid foramen before its five parotid branches, the abducent nerve supplies lateral rectus alone, the hypoglossal nerve crosses the exposed floor of the submandibular triangle and its injury deviates the protruded tongue toward the weak side, and the glossopharyngeal nerve carries both sensation and taste from the posterior third of the tongue, putting it at risk during tonsillectomy. The neck's fascial planes and triangles are equally landmark-driven: omohyoid subdivides the posterior triangle, the digastric bellies bound the submental and submandibular triangles, the deep cervical nodes and internal jugular vein travel together inside the carotid sheath, and the stylomandibular ligament — a thickening of the investing fascia layer — separates the parotid and submandibular gland beds. A short list of muscles breaks their group's usual innervation pattern and is worth memorising as exceptions: tensor veli palatini alone among palate muscles is trigeminal (not vagal); palatoglossus alone among tongue muscles is vagal (not hypoglossal); and lateral pterygoid alone among the muscles of mastication opens rather than closes the jaw. Regional anatomy rounds out the picture — the maxillary sinus drains via the middle meatus, Little's area on the anterior nasal septum is the commonest bleeding site, the cavernous sinus sits beside the pituitary and is the classic site of septic sinus thrombosis, the tympanic membrane forms the lateral wall of the middle ear, and the internal laryngeal nerve is purely sensory above the vocal folds while the recurrent laryngeal nerve does the rest.

## sections

### Definition
The facial nerve (CN VII) exits the skull through the stylomastoid foramen, just posterior to the styloid process, before entering the parotid gland and dividing into five terminal branches (temporal, zygomatic, buccal, marginal mandibular, cervical); the marginal mandibular branch supplies only the lower lip muscles, and the facial nerve supplies the posterior (not anterior) belly of digastric. The abducent nerve (CN VI) supplies a single muscle, lateral rectus, so an isolated abducent palsy produces a pure horizontal deviation without affecting lid position or other eye movements. The hypoglossal nerve (CN XII) loops through the floor of the submandibular triangle, the most exposed named structure there to a penetrating wound; its injury paralyses ipsilateral genioglossus, and because the healthy contralateral genioglossus goes unopposed on protrusion, the tongue deviates toward the WEAK (injured) side — a common localising vignette. The glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX) runs against the tonsillar bed and carries both general sensation and taste from the posterior third of the tongue, making it the nerve classically injured during tonsillectomy and explaining isolated posterior-tongue taste loss afterward (the anterior two-thirds is lingual nerve/chorda tympani territory instead). The great auricular nerve, a cervical plexus branch (C2-C3) rather than a cranial nerve proper, supplies skin over the angle of the mandible and parotid region, and is at particular risk during parotidectomy because of its superficial course.

The superior laryngeal nerve splits into an internal branch (purely sensory, piercing the thyrohyoid membrane to supply mucosa ABOVE the vocal folds) and an external branch (motor, supplying cricothyroid only). Every other intrinsic laryngeal muscle, and all mucosal sensation AT and below the vocal folds, is recurrent laryngeal nerve territory. This split is why superior laryngeal nerve injury classically reduces sensation above the folds together with vocal pitch, rather than causing airway obstruction (which instead follows recurrent laryngeal nerve injury).

### Mechanism
Omohyoid's inferior belly crosses the posterior triangle and subdivides it into an occipital triangle above and a subclavian (supraclavicular) triangle below — the subclavian triangle being where the subclavian artery and brachial plexus trunks are surgically accessed. In the anterior triangle, the two anterior bellies of digastric bound the submental triangle between them, while a penetrating wound of the submandibular triangle (bounded by both digastric bellies and the mandible) most often injures the hypoglossal nerve running through its floor. The ansa cervicalis, embedded in the anterior wall of the carotid sheath, has a superior root that travels with the hypoglossal nerve (ultimately C1 fibres) and a separate inferior root (descendens cervicalis) that arises directly from C2 and C3; together the loop supplies the infrahyoid strap muscles. The deep cervical lymph node chain runs the length of the neck alongside the internal jugular vein inside the carotid sheath, distinct from the superficial cervical nodes that run with the external jugular vein instead. The stylomandibular ligament, a thickened band of the investing layer of deep cervical fascia running from the styloid process to the mandibular angle, keeps the parotid and submandibular gland beds anatomically separated. Each sternocleidomastoid rotates the face toward the OPPOSITE side while tilting the head to its own side — a right sternocleidomastoid contraction turns the face left, which is also why testing SCM strength against resistance evaluates the accessory nerve on the side opposite the direction the patient turns.

Three small, frequently-tested exceptions run through this module. Among the muscles of mastication (masseter, temporalis, medial pterygoid, lateral pterygoid), only lateral pterygoid opens (depresses) the jaw; the other three close it, so a lateral pterygoid palsy deviates the jaw toward the weak side on opening. Among the palate muscles (levator veli palatini, tensor veli palatini, palatoglossus, palatopharyngeus, musculus uvulae), only tensor veli palatini is supplied by the trigeminal nerve (CN V3); every other palate muscle is pharyngeal plexus (vagal). Among the tongue muscles, only palatoglossus is supplied by the pharyngeal plexus (vagal) rather than the hypoglossal nerve, because it is developmentally a palatal rather than a true tongue muscle. Mylohyoid, by contrast, is the intact "diaphragm of the oral cavity" — its paired sheet, meeting in a median raphe and attached along the mandible's mylohyoid line, forms the floor of the mouth supporting the tongue above it. The superior pharyngeal constrictor arises in part from the pterygomandibular raphe, a fibrous (not bony) band running from the pterygoid hamulus to the posterior mylohyoid line.

### Key determinants
The maxillary sinus drains through its ostium into the hiatus semilunaris, located in the MIDDLE meatus of the lateral nasal wall (alongside the frontal and anterior ethmoidal sinus openings), so a middle-meatus obstruction can block several sinuses' outflow at once. Little's area (Kiesselbach's plexus), an anastomotic network of small vessels on the ANTERIOR nasal septum, is the commonest site of epistaxis because these vessels sit superficially with little protective tissue. The tympanic membrane forms almost the entire LATERAL wall of the middle ear cavity, allowing direct otoscopic visualisation and explaining why a perforation connects the middle ear to the external environment. The cavernous sinus lies directly lateral to the pituitary gland and sphenoid sinus — the dural venous sinus closest to the pituitary — and is the classic site of septic dural sinus thrombosis, because it receives the ophthalmic veins and drains the facial venous plexus, giving facial/orbital infection a direct, valveless route in.

### Clinical significance
Localising a lesion from a clinical sign is this module's recurring exam pattern: the direction a protruded tongue deviates on protrusion identifies the side of a hypoglossal nerve injury, isolated posterior-tongue taste loss after tonsillectomy identifies glossopharyngeal nerve injury, and a mild (rather than complete) ptosis points to sympathetic loss of levator palpebrae superioris's smooth part specifically (Horner's syndrome) rather than a full oculomotor palsy. The three cutaneous branches of the ophthalmic nerve (V1) split the upper eyelid and forehead by position: the lacrimal nerve (most lateral) supplies the lateral upper eyelid, while the supraorbital and supratrochlear nerves cover the medial/central territory — levator palpebrae superioris's smooth-muscle part (superior tarsal/Müller's muscle) is supplied by sympathetic fibres, distinct from the skeletal part's oculomotor supply. Vascular anatomy also carries direct clinical weight: the middle meningeal artery's bony course through foramen spinosum is exactly why a pterion fracture can lacerate it and produce a rapidly expanding extradural haematoma, while the ascending pharyngeal artery (an external carotid branch, distinct from the subclavian-system inferior thyroid, internal thoracic and superficial cervical arteries) and the cavernous sinus's septic-thrombosis risk from facial/orbital infection round out the vessel-based exam and clinical picture for this module.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming the tongue deviates AWAY from a hypoglossal nerve injury, rather than toward the weak (injured) side, because the healthy contralateral genioglossus is what wins unopposed. Assuming sternocleidomastoid turns the face toward its OWN side — the action is opposite-side rotation. Placing the maxillary sinus ostium in the inferior or superior meatus rather than the middle meatus. Attributing all palate or tongue muscles to one nerve — tensor veli palatini (trigeminal) and palatoglossus (vagal) are each the sole exception in their group. Confusing the internal laryngeal nerve's purely sensory, above-the-folds territory with the recurrent laryngeal nerve's motor and below-the-folds territory. Crediting the superficial cervical chain (external jugular vein) for a fact that belongs to the deep cervical chain (internal jugular vein), or vice versa.

## title
Special senses and nerve histology: blood-brain barrier, cerebellum, eye, ear and peripheral nerve

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## topic
Histology

## summary
The blood-brain barrier's structural basis is the tight-junctioned, continuous capillary endothelium (few pinocytotic vesicles, continuous basal lamina) completely ensheathed by protoplasmic astrocyte end-feet, which induce and maintain the barrier phenotype. In the cerebellar cortex, Golgi cell dendrites uniquely span all three grey-matter layers, unlike granule cells' short intra-granular dendrites. The eye contributes several paired distinctions worth memorising exactly: the retina's inner nuclear layer holds horizontal and amacrine cell bodies (not the nerve fibre layer's unmyelinated ganglion-cell axons, nor the inner/outer plexiform layers' different synapse types); the lacrimal gland is a serous, tubuloalveolar gland with myoepithelial-cell-wrapped acini (not mucous, not simple tubular); and the palpebral conjunctiva is stratified columnar with goblet cells while the bulbar conjunctiva is stratified squamous. The ear supplies a parallel set: the middle ear communicates posteriorly with the mastoid air cells via the aditus ad antrum; the cochlear duct's outer wall is the ion-transporting stria vascularis (roof = vestibular membrane, floor = basilar membrane bearing the organ of Corti, contents = endolymph); the crista ampullaris's cupula is a proteoglycan gel lacking CaCO3 crystals (unlike the otoconia-bearing otolithic membrane of the maculae), so it is a pure angular-acceleration detector; and cochlear implants specifically treat deafness caused by loss of the cochlear sensory (hair) cells. Peripheral nerve and receptor histology round out the module: the sheath of Henle is reticular fibres formed around individual fibres by Schwann cells (distinct from the epineurium/perineurium/endoneurium proper, the perineurium being the true tight-junctioned blood-nerve barrier); Golgi tendon organs sense tension via sensory endings wound around tendon collagen bundles; and Wallerian degeneration's distal-stump Schwann cells form digestion chambers to clear debris, distinct from the separate cell-body chromatolysis response.

## sections

### Definition
The blood-brain barrier (BBB) is built from continuous, non-fenestrated capillary endothelial cells joined by tight junctions on a continuous basal lamina, with very few pinocytotic vesicles limiting transcellular transport; protoplasmic astrocytes (grey-matter astrocytes with many short, thick processes, as opposed to the longer, fewer processes of white-matter fibrous astrocytes) send end-feet that completely surround these capillaries and help induce/maintain the barrier phenotype, though the tight-junctioned endothelium itself is the actual diffusion barrier. The cupula of each crista ampullaris is a dome-shaped gelatinous mass of proteoglycans embedding hair-cell stereocilia and kinocilium, and — critically — it lacks the calcium carbonate (CaCO3) otoconia that characterise the otolithic membrane of the maculae; this composition difference is exactly why the cupula responds to angular acceleration (semicircular canals) while the otolithic membrane responds to linear acceleration and gravity (utricle/saccule).

### Mechanism
A lesion at the nucleus gracilis itself produces ipsilateral, not contralateral, lower-limb (leg) proprioceptive loss: fasciculus gracilis fibres ascend uncrossed to synapse in the nucleus in the closed medulla, and only the second-order internal arcuate fibres decussate afterward to form the medial lemniscus — a lesion this proximal is pre-decussation. In the cochlear duct, the stria vascularis (outer wall) is a vascularised, ion-transporting epithelium that actively generates the endolymph's distinctive ionic composition and the endocochlear potential, distinct from the vestibular (Reissner's) membrane forming the roof and the basilar membrane (bearing the organ of Corti) forming the floor; the duct itself contains endolymph, while the flanking scala vestibuli and scala tympani contain perilymph. A Golgi tendon organ's group Ib sensory ending winds around and interdigitates with the tendon's collagen fibre bundles, making it most responsive to active muscle contraction (tension), unlike a muscle spindle's intrafusal fibres, whose central region is relatively non-striated (not striated) to remain stretch-sensitive. Wallerian degeneration in the distal stump proceeds through Schwann cell (and recruited macrophage) formation of digestion chambers (myelin ovoids) that clear axon/myelin debris and leave a Schwann-cell guiding column (band of Bungner) for eventual regrowth — a distinct, distal-stump process from the separate chromatolysis/peripheral-nucleus changes that occur in the injured neuron's cell body.

### Key determinants
The peripheral nerve's three connective-tissue coats each have a distinct composition and role: the epineurium (dense connective tissue) encases the whole nerve; the perineurium, formed of flattened epithelium-like cells joined by TIGHT junctions (not gap junctions), surrounds each fascicle and forms the blood-nerve barrier; and the endoneurium surrounds individual fibres without itself forming a barrier. Within the endoneurium, the sheath of Henle is specifically reticular (collagen III) fibres associated with the investing Schwann cells. The middle ear cavity communicates posteriorly with the mastoid air cell system via the aditus ad antrum (the route for infection to cause mastoiditis); the oval window is closed by the stapes footplate (the round window, not the oval window, is sometimes termed the "secondary tympanic membrane"); the auditory tube is lined by respiratory-type ciliated epithelium; and tensor tympani attaches to the malleus (stapedius to the stapes). In the retina, the inner nuclear layer specifically houses horizontal and amacrine cell bodies (plus bipolar and Muller cells), distinct from the nerve fibre layer's unmyelinated ganglion-cell axons, the inner limiting membrane's Muller-cell footplate junctions, and the outer (not inner) plexiform layer's photoreceptor-to-bipolar synapses.

### Clinical significance
Cochlear implants are indicated specifically for sensorineural deafness caused by loss of the cochlear sensory (hair) cells, since the device bypasses hair-cell transduction and directly stimulates spiral ganglion afferents electrically — an isolated stereocilia or kinocilium defect without hair-cell loss is a different lesion. A nucleus gracilis lesion is a rare but classic localiser for ipsilateral, isolated lower-limb proprioceptive/vibration loss, testing whether the decussation point of the dorsal column pathway is correctly placed after (not at) the gracile/cuneate nuclei. Understanding that Wallerian degeneration's digestion chambers are a distal-stump, Schwann-cell-driven process — separate from the cell body's chromatolysis — underlies how peripheral nerve injury and regeneration are staged and monitored.

### Common misconceptions
Believing BBB endothelium has numerous pinocytotic vesicles or a discontinuous basal lamina (both features of fenestrated, non-BBB capillaries instead); confusing protoplasmic (grey matter, BBB-forming) with fibrous (white matter) astrocytes, or crediting microglia with centrioles and active division, or placing interfascicular oligodendrocytes in grey matter, or ependymal cells around neurons in ganglia (that is satellite cells' location). Assuming any V1-adjacent dorsal-column lesion is automatically contralateral without checking whether it sits before or after the nucleus/decussation point. Applying the "dorsal column signs are contralateral" rule learned for lesions above the decussation to a lesion at the nucleus itself. Swapping the cupula (semicircular canals, no otoconia, angular acceleration) with the otolithic membrane (maculae, otoconia present, linear acceleration/gravity). Calling the epineurium the perineurium or vice versa, or believing the perineurium's barrier cells are joined by gap junctions rather than tight junctions. Placing Ruffini corpuscles in the dermal papillae of the palm (that is Meissner's corpuscle territory) or free nerve endings as vibration/pressure mechanoreceptors (that is Pacinian corpuscle territory).

## title
CNS gross anatomy: cerebral cortex localisation, ventricles, meninges, diencephalon, internal capsule, cerebellum, brainstem and cerebral vasculature

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## topic
Anatomy

## summary
Cortical localisation is the highest-yield theme of this cluster: general somatic sensation maps to the primary somatosensory cortex (areas 3, 1, 2) and skilled voluntary movement to the primary motor cortex (area 4), while expressive speech is Broca's area (44, 45), colour and higher visual interpretation belong to the visual association cortex (18, 19), and the paracentral lobule on the medial surface carries the lower-limb representation together with cortical control of the bladder and bowel — so a parasagittal lesion there causes incontinence. The ventricular system runs from the C-shaped lateral ventricles inside the cerebral hemispheres, through the interventricular foramina to the third ventricle of the diencephalon (choroid plexus in its roof), down the cerebral aqueduct to the fourth ventricle between the pons/medulla and the cerebellum. The meninges divide by behaviour — the pia follows the brain into every sulcus, the arachnoid bridges over them leaving cisterns such as the cisterna magna (cerebellomedullary cistern) beneath — and the diencephalon's metathalamus (medial and lateral geniculate bodies) hangs from the posterior thalamus. White-matter fibres split into commissural (corpus callosum, joining corresponding areas of the two hemispheres), association (cingulum, superior longitudinal fasciculus, within one hemisphere) and projection (internal capsule) groups, with the optic radiation running in the retrolenticular part of the internal capsule. Brainstem landmarks include the trochlear nerve as the only cranial nerve leaving the dorsal surface and the corpora quadrigemina forming the posterior surface of the midbrain, while the cerebellum's posterolateral fissure separates the flocculonodular lobe from the posterior lobe. Vascular facts round out the cluster: the middle cerebral artery supplies the lateral surface (including the auditory area), the superficial middle cerebral vein runs in the lateral sulcus toward the cavernous sinus, and the arteria radicularis magna (of Adamkiewicz) is the dominant supply to the lumbar enlargement of the spinal cord. A few head-and-neck items sit alongside: the pterygopalatine ganglion as the lacrimal secretomotor relay, the transverse cervical nerve crossing sternocleidomastoid, and two embryology vignettes (second-pouch/cervical-sinus branchial fistula and maxillary-to-intermaxillary cleft lip).

## sections

### Definition
The cerebral cortex is mapped by function into primary and association areas that exam vignettes test by lesion. The primary somatosensory cortex (Brodmann areas 3, 1, 2) in the postcentral gyrus receives general somatic sensation from the contralateral body, mirrored across the central sulcus by the primary motor cortex (area 4) in the precentral gyrus, whose large hand representation is why fine manual work fails after a lesion there. Broca's area (areas 44, 45) in the dominant inferior frontal gyrus programmes speech output, so its lesion gives a non-fluent expressive aphasia; the visual association cortex (areas 18, 19) interprets colour, form and movement, distinct from the primary visual cortex (area 17) that merely detects; and the paracentral lobule on the medial surface holds both the lower-limb representation and the higher control of the bladder and rectal sphincters, so a parasagittal lesion produces incontinence with leg weakness. The ventricular system links these regions internally: each lateral ventricle is a C-shaped cavity within a cerebral hemisphere, draining through the interventricular foramina into the third ventricle of the diencephalon (whose choroid plexus lies in its roof), then through the cerebral aqueduct into the fourth ventricle, the tent-shaped hindbrain cavity between the pons/medulla in front and the cerebellum behind.

### Mechanism
The meninges are best learned by how each layer behaves at the brain surface: the pia mater is a delicate vascular film that dips into every sulcus and fissure and cannot be stripped from the cortex, whereas the arachnoid bridges over the sulci, and where the arachnoid spans a wide gap it leaves a subarachnoid cistern beneath — the cisterna magna (cerebellomedullary cistern) between the cerebellum and medulla being the largest, receiving fourth-ventricle CSF through the median aperture. The diencephalon's parts are positioned around the thalamus: the metathalamus (medial and lateral geniculate bodies, the auditory and visual relays) is attached to the posterior thalamus (pulvinar), while the epithalamus sits on the roof, the hypothalamus below and anterior, and the subthalamus below. Cerebral white matter divides into three fibre types: commissural fibres cross the midline to join corresponding areas of the two hemispheres, and the corpus callosum is the great commissure carrying most of them (so callosal lesions disconnect the hemispheres); association fibres such as the cingulum and superior longitudinal fasciculus link regions within one hemisphere; and projection fibres run vertically through the internal capsule between cortex and lower centres. Within the internal capsule, the retrolenticular part (behind the lentiform nucleus) carries the optic radiation from the lateral geniculate body to the visual cortex, which is why a retrolenticular lesion causes a contralateral field defect.

### Key determinants
Brainstem and cerebellar surface anatomy supply several single-fact answers. The trochlear nerve (CN IV) is the only cranial nerve to emerge from the dorsal (posterior) surface of the brainstem, just below the inferior colliculi, and the only one to decussate before exiting; the corpora quadrigemina (paired superior and inferior colliculi) form the posterior surface of the midbrain (the tectum), while the cerebral peduncles form its anterior surface. In the cerebellum, the posterolateral fissure — the first cerebellar fissure to appear in development — separates the phylogenetically oldest flocculonodular lobe from the posterior lobe, distinct from the primary fissure that divides the anterior and posterior lobes. Cerebral vasculature adds three determinants: the middle cerebral artery supplies most of the lateral surface of the hemisphere, including the auditory area (areas 41, 42) and the face-and-arm motor/sensory strip and language areas, while the lower-limb strip on the medial surface is anterior cerebral artery territory; the superficial middle cerebral vein runs along the posterior ramus of the lateral sulcus and drains chiefly into the cavernous sinus (reaching the transverse and superior sagittal sinuses only indirectly via the anastomotic veins of Labbe and Trolard); and the arteria radicularis magna (great anterior radicular artery of Adamkiewicz) is the dominant reinforcement of the anterior spinal artery in the lower cord and the principal supply to the lumbar enlargement.

### Clinical significance
Localising a lesion from a sign is the cluster's recurring exam pattern and its clinical payoff. Incontinence of urine and stool with leg weakness points to a medial-surface paracentral lobule lesion (classically a parasagittal meningioma or anterior cerebral artery infarct), an expressive aphasia points to Broca's area, and an isolated colour-discrimination deficit with preserved basic sight points to visual association cortex rather than area 17. The internal capsule's retrolenticular optic radiation explains post-capsular visual field loss, and the artery of Adamkiewicz's dominance over the lumbar cord explains why its interruption during aortic surgery risks anterior spinal artery syndrome and paraplegia. The head-and-neck items carry their own clinical weight: loss of the pterygopalatine ganglion's parasympathetic relay produces a dry eye from reduced lacrimal secretion (distinct from sympathetic vasomotor loss); a complete branchial fistula opening at the anterior border of sternocleidomastoid and ending at the tonsillar fossa reflects a persistent second pharyngeal pouch plus cervical sinus; and a cleft lip reflects failure of the maxillary prominence to fuse with the intermaxillary segment.

### Common misconceptions
Placing sphincter (bladder/bowel) control on the lateral convexity rather than the medial paracentral lobule, so that parasagittal incontinence is missed. Assuming the middle cerebral artery supplies the whole-body motor/sensory strip, when the lower-limb representation on the medial surface is anterior cerebral artery territory. Swapping the third ventricle's communications — the interventricular foramina lead to the lateral ventricles and the cerebral aqueduct to the fourth ventricle, not the reverse. Confusing the pia (enters all sulci) with the arachnoid (bridges over sulci). Expecting all cranial nerves to emerge ventrally and so missing the trochlear nerve's unique dorsal exit. Attributing the optic radiation to the posterior limb of the internal capsule instead of the retrolenticular part. Crediting the anterior spinal artery in general for the lumbar enlargement rather than the specific arteria radicularis magna that reinforces it there. Mixing up commissural fibres (corpus callosum, across hemispheres) with association fibres (cingulum, superior longitudinal fasciculus, within one hemisphere).
