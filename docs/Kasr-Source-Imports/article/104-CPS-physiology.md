## summary
The cardiac cycle repeats in a fixed order of seven phases, triggered by the P wave, and a rising heart rate shortens it by taking the time almost entirely out of diastole. Two pressure traces make the cycle's mechanical events visible without opening the chest: the aortic pressure curve, whose dicrotic notch and dicrotic wave mark the instant the aortic valve shuts, and the jugular venous pulse, whose six named waves each correspond to a specific right atrial or tricuspid-valve event. This article covers the phase order, the two curves, and how they line up against the ECG and the carotid pulse — it does not describe the heart sounds, which the source chapter references but does not itself explain (see evidence_gaps).
## sections
### Definition
The cardiac cycle is the period during which the heart completes one contraction (systole) and one relaxation (diastole). It is initiated by the P wave of the ECG, so one full cycle is the period between two successive P waves. At the average resting heart rate of 75/min, one cycle lasts 0.8 seconds and proceeds through seven phases in a fixed order: atrial systole, isovolumetric contraction, rapid ejection, reduced ejection, isovolumetric relaxation, rapid filling, and reduced filling.

Atrial systole occupies only the first 0.1 second of the cycle; the atria then stay relaxed for the remaining 0.7 seconds. The ventricles go through their own systole across the next 0.3 seconds (phases 2 to 4) and their own diastole across the following 0.5 seconds (phases 5 to 7, plus the first 0.1 second of the next cycle). Because atrial systole falls inside the last 0.1 second of ventricular diastole, it is sometimes called "late diastole", and atria and ventricles relax together for 0.4 seconds of every cycle even though they never normally contract together.

### Mechanism
As heart rate rises, the cycle shortens overall, but the two halves do not shrink by the same proportion. Moving from 75/min to 200/min takes the whole cycle from 0.8 to 0.3 seconds. Ventricular systole only roughly halves, from 0.3 to 0.15 seconds, while ventricular diastole falls to well under a third of its resting value, from 0.5 to 0.15 seconds. A fast heart buys its extra beats almost entirely out of diastole, which is also why very high heart rates compromise ventricular filling time before they compromise ejection time.

The aortic pressure curve is the record of aortic pressure across one cycle, and it has two limbs. The ascending, "anacrotic", limb coincides with the phase of maximal ejection and carries pressure up to its peak, about 120 mmHg — the systolic arterial blood pressure. The descending, "catacrotic", limb runs through the rest of the cycle down to a trough of about 80 mmHg — the diastolic arterial blood pressure. On the catacrotic limb, the aortic valve's closure produces the dicrotic notch, a sharp drop in pressure, and this is followed by the dicrotic wave, a small rise caused by blood that is still moving backward bouncing against the now-shut, elastic aortic valve during isovolumetric relaxation.

### Key determinants
Because no valves lie between the right atrium and the venae cavae, pressure changes inside the right atrium are transmitted directly to the jugular veins as the jugular venous pulse, and the book names six waves in it. The "a" wave is a positive deflection due to atrial contraction, and follows the ECG's P wave. The "c" wave is a positive deflection due to the tricuspid valve leaflets bulging into the atrium during isovolumetric contraction, and follows the QRS complex, appearing at the start of the carotid pulse. The plain "x" wave is a negative deflection due to blood escaping from the atrium into the ventricle. A second, separate negative deflection, the "x prime" wave, is due to the atrioventricular ring being pulled downward by ventricular contraction during rapid ejection, and it coincides with the carotid pulse — which is why the jugular veins normally collapse with each carotid pulsation. The "v" wave is a positive deflection due to venous return accumulating in the atrium while the tricuspid valve is still closed, and it occurs with the descending, collapsing limb of the carotid pulse. Finally, the "y" wave is a negative deflection due to blood flowing out of the right atrium once the tricuspid valve reopens for ventricular filling.

### Clinical significance
Because both curves are direct, external read-outs of chamber pressure, they let the timing of valve and chamber events be inferred without instrumenting the heart itself. A jugular venous pulse read against the carotid pulse and the ECG lets a clinician time the "v" wave to right atrial filling behind a shut tricuspid valve, or the "a" wave to atrial contraction — useful whenever atrial or tricuspid-valve timing is in question. On the arterial side, the position and sharpness of the dicrotic notch reflect the moment and completeness of aortic valve closure, so a notch that has flattened or shifted is a marker worth noting even before any other sign of a change in the aortic valve or in arterial compliance appears.

### Common misconceptions
It is tempting to assume a faster heart "does everything faster" in the same proportion, but the book's own numbers show otherwise: systole is comparatively protected while diastole is what shortens. It is also easy to conflate the dicrotic notch with the dicrotic wave that follows it — the notch is the sharp pressure drop at the instant the aortic valve shuts, the wave is the smaller rebound afterward — and to mix up the jugular venous pulse's two negative deflections, since the book gives "x" (atrial emptying into the ventricle) and "x prime" (the AV ring pulled down during rapid ejection) separate mechanical causes even though both are labelled negative waves.

## summary
Cardiac output is the volume each ventricle pumps per minute, defined simply as heart rate times stroke volume, and it swings widely with everyday states such as exercise, anxiety, meals, posture and arrhythmia. During real dynamic exercise the heart's own sympathetic drive — faster rate, stronger contraction, lower peripheral resistance — only raises output a little by itself; the large rise depends on the systemic circulation bringing back more blood for the heart to pump. This article covers the definition and formula, the everyday conditions that raise or lower cardiac output, and the cardiac side of the exercise response. It deliberately stops short of naming the cardioinhibitory, cardioacceleratory or vasomotor centres individually, because the given chapter text does not identify them by those names (see evidence_gaps).
## sections
### Definition
Cardiac output (CO) is the volume of blood pumped by each ventricle per minute, normally about 5 litres/minute in an adult. The value of CO depends on body size — it is higher for people with a larger body size — so CO on its own is not a size-independent measure; the book corrects for this with the cardiac index, calculated by dividing CO in L/min by body surface area in m2, normally about 3.2 L/min/m2. CO is determined by two variables: heart rate, the number of beats per minute, and stroke volume, the volume of blood ejected by each ventricle per beat — so that CO = stroke volume x heart rate. Because CO is a product of two variables, the same output can in principle be reached by a lower heart rate with a larger stroke volume, or a higher heart rate with a smaller one, and the rest of this article is largely about which of those two the body actually uses under different conditions.

### Key determinants
Cardiac output is not a fixed number; the book lists the everyday conditions that move it away from its resting value. It rises with physical exercise (up to 700%), with anxiety and excitement (up to 100%), after meals (30%), in a high environmental temperature, and in pregnancy. It falls on standing up from a supine position (30%), and with rapid cardiac arrhythmias and many other heart diseases — a faster heart rate does not by itself guarantee a higher output, and arrhythmia is the book's own example of the opposite. These are stated as observed conditions rather than derived from a single shared mechanism, which is why exercise and arrhythmia can both change heart rate yet move cardiac output in opposite directions.

### Mechanism
During dynamic muscular exercise, strong sympathetic stimulation together with reduced vagal (parasympathetic) tone acts on the heart directly. Heart rate rises, driven by impulses reaching the medullary cardiovascular centres both from the cerebral cortex and from peripheral receptors in the exercising muscles, and inotropy (the force of contraction) increases alongside it. At the same time, total peripheral resistance falls, mainly through the effect of local metabolites produced by the contracting muscles, with sympathetic vasodilator cholinergic fibres also contributing, chiefly at the start of exercise. So the cardiac-side signature of dynamic exercise is: heart rate up, inotropy up, total peripheral resistance down. This is a departure from what simply doubling the heart rate alone does — the book notes elsewhere that doubling heart rate by itself, for example by an artificial pacemaker, drives stroke volume down as diastole and ventricular filling time shorten, leaving cardiac output roughly unchanged; during real exercise, by contrast, stroke volume rises together with heart rate, so cardiac output increases by more than heart rate alone would predict, through mechanisms the book does not detail further at this point.

### Clinical significance
Acting alone, these cardiac-side changes raise cardiac output only a little. The book states plainly that cardiac output is limited by the return of blood to the heart and by ventricular filling, so the much larger rise seen during real exercise depends on the systemic circulation changing too: decreased venous capacity and decreased systemic vascular resistance together let far more blood return to the heart to be pumped. This is the same limitation seen at the bedside whenever venous return is restricted — a heart that is well stimulated but starved of venous return cannot raise its output very far, regardless of how hard the heart itself is driven. The practical lesson generalises beyond exercise: any state in which the heart is stimulated to work harder still needs an adequate venous return to translate that stimulation into a higher output.

### Common misconceptions
It is tempting to assume any rise in heart rate raises cardiac output; the book instead places rapid cardiac arrhythmias among its causes of decreased cardiac output. It is just as tempting to credit the large rise in cardiac output during exercise to the heart's own sympathetic stimulation alone. The book's own conclusion is the opposite: cardiac stimulation in a normal heart has only a little effect on cardiac output if acting alone, and it is the change in the systemic circulation — not a harder-working heart by itself — that lets output climb to the much higher levels seen in real exercise. It is also worth not confusing "heart rate doubling alone" (which leaves CO roughly unchanged, because stroke volume falls to compensate) with "heart rate doubling during exercise" (where stroke volume rises instead of falling) — the same numerical change in heart rate has opposite effects on stroke volume depending on why the heart rate changed.

## summary
Blood flow anywhere in the circulation follows the same simple relationship as current in a wire: flow equals the pressure gradient divided by resistance, and applying this one equation to measured pressures and cardiac output is how the book arrives at a systemic resistance of about 18 mmHg/L/min against a pulmonary resistance of only about 1.4 mmHg/L/min. Arterial pressure itself splits into systolic, diastolic and mean values that are not interchangeable, and both the size of the pulse pressure and the pressure profile across the whole vascular tree — aorta to capillary to vein — are shaped by how compliant each part of the circulation is.
## sections
### Definition
Flow is the volume of fluid that crosses a point per unit time. The overall blood flow of the entire circulation is the amount of blood pumped into the aorta each minute — the cardiac output. Flow (F), pressure (P) and resistance (R) in blood vessels are related by F = ΔP/R, the same form as Ohm's law for an electrical circuit.

Systolic blood pressure is the peak pressure reached during systole in the aorta and other large arteries, about 120 mmHg (normal range 90-140 mmHg). Diastolic blood pressure is the lowest pressure reached during diastole, about 80 mmHg (normal range 60-90 mmHg). Mean arterial pressure (MAP) is the average pressure throughout the whole cardiac cycle, approximately equal to diastolic pressure plus one-third of the pulse pressure, about 90 mmHg normally. Pulse pressure is simply the difference between systolic and diastolic pressure, normally about 30-50 mmHg.

### Mechanism
Applying F = ΔP/R to the whole systemic circulation gives its total peripheral resistance: TPR = (MAP - CVP) / CO = (90 - 0) / 5 = 18, so TPR is about 18 mmHg/L/min. Applying the same relationship to the pulmonary circulation gives PulR = (MPP - LAP) / COP = (15 - 8) / 5 = 1.4, about 1.4 mmHg/L/min — roughly a thirteenth of the systemic value, even though the pulmonary circulation carries the same cardiac output. That low pulmonary resistance is why pulmonary artery pressure runs so much lower than aortic pressure (about 25/15 mmHg against 120/80 mmHg) for the same flow.

MAP is not the simple arithmetic mean of systolic and diastolic pressure, and the book explains why: systole, during which pressure rises, is the shorter part of the cycle, while diastole, during which pressure falls, is the longer part, so the true time-averaged pressure sits closer to the diastolic value than to the midpoint. When heart rate is rapid, the cycle shortens mainly at the expense of diastole, and under those conditions MAP moves closer to the true arithmetic mean.

Pulse pressure is not fixed either. When arterial compliance falls — the book's example is atherosclerosis — the same stroke volume has to be accommodated by a stiffer artery: systolic pressure rises because the artery cannot distend enough to take up the stroke volume, and diastolic pressure falls because the artery has less elastic recoil left to sustain pressure through diastole. The combined effect is a wider pulse pressure from a lower-compliance artery, for the same stroke volume.

### Key determinants
Pressure and compliance are distributed very differently across the vascular tree. Mean pressure runs about 90 mmHg in the aorta and only about 10 mmHg in the pulmonary artery; within the systemic capillary bed it falls from about 35 mmHg at the arteriolar end to 15 mmHg at the venous end, averaging a "functional" capillary pressure of about 25 mmHg — a low enough pressure, and low enough in the pulmonary bed particularly, that fluid does not filter out of the vessels faster than it can be handled.

Vascular compliance is the ratio of change in volume to change in pressure in a vessel, and it is not constant along that vessel's own pressure-volume curve: compliance is higher at low volumes and falls, so the vessel stiffens, at higher volumes. Arterial compliance serves three functions the book lists explicitly: it converts the intermittent, pulsatile flow leaving the aorta during systole into continuous flow in the peripheral vessels, by recoiling during diastole to keep pushing blood forward while the ventricles are relaxed; it minimises both the rise of systolic pressure during ejection and the fall of pressure during diastole; and, because cardiac work is the product of pressure and volume, it reduces the work the heart must do to eject a given cardiac output — for the same output, low compliance means high pressure means more work.

Venous compliance is about 24 times arterial compliance, so veins can accommodate far more blood than arteries for the same change in pressure, which is why veins are described as reservoir, or capacitance, vessels. Venous compliance is itself set mainly by venous tone: higher tone, for example from sympathetic stimulation, lowers venous compliance, and lower tone raises it.

### Clinical significance
Because falling arterial compliance simultaneously raises systolic pressure and lowers diastolic pressure, a widened pulse pressure is a useful, easily measured sign of reduced arterial compliance — the pattern the book associates with atherosclerosis. On the venous side, because veins hold so much more blood for the same pressure change than arteries do, a shift of venous tone (and so venous compliance) moves a large amount of blood into or out of circulation without a comparable arterial pressure change, which is part of why the venous system rather than the arterial system is where the circulation's reserve of blood is held.

## summary
Capillaries exchange material with interstitial fluid by three separate mechanisms — diffusion, trans-capillary filtration governed by the Starling forces, and vesicular transport — and the balance of those forces normally tips slightly toward net filtration, which is why lymphatics exist at all. This article covers all three exchange mechanisms, the worked Starling-force example the book gives for a muscle capillary, what sets interstitial fluid volume, the mechanisms and functions of lymphatic drainage, and the book's own four-way classification of what causes oedema.
## sections
### Definition
The main function of capillaries is to exchange materials with the interstitial fluid, and three mechanisms are involved. Diffusion is quantitatively the most important. Trans-capillary filtration (bulk flow), also called the Starling forces, moves fluid in bulk according to the balance of hydrostatic and osmotic pressure gradients. Vesicular transport carries large lipid-insoluble molecules across the endothelial cells themselves.

### Mechanism
Diffusion's rate depends on two kinds of factor. Capillary permeability differs by capillary type: continuous capillaries have the lowest permeability, fenestrated capillaries have higher permeability, and discontinuous capillaries have the highest permeability, and permeability itself can change under conditions such as inflammation, when it increases. The substance being exchanged matters too: the rate of diffusion is directly proportional to the concentration gradient, and molecular size determines how readily a substance passes through the capillary pores. Vesicular transport is the separate route for large lipid-insoluble molecules such as proteins, and it is how tissues receive high-molecular-weight molecules — antibodies, cytokines, protein-bound hormones — that diffusion alone could not deliver.

### Key determinants
Trans-capillary filtration is set by four opposing pressures, the Starling forces. Two favour movement out of the capillary into the interstitium: capillary hydrostatic pressure (Pc) and interstitial colloid osmotic pressure (πi). Two favour movement back into the capillary: interstitial hydrostatic pressure (Pi) and capillary colloid osmotic pressure (πc). Net fluid movement = k [(Pc + πi) − (Pi + πc)], where the capillary filtration coefficient k is proportionate to the permeability of the capillary wall and the area available for filtration; the book notes that πi is usually very small and can be ignored, leaving the balance largely between Pc and πc in practice. Along a representative muscle capillary the book works this through: at the arteriolar end, (37 + 0) − (1 + 25) = 11 mmHg, so fluid moves out of the capillary into the interstitial space; at the venular end, (17 + 0) − (1 + 25) = −9 mmHg, so fluid moves back into the capillary from the interstitial space. Because the outward force at the arteriolar end slightly exceeds the inward force at the venular end across the whole capillary bed, fluid efflux normally exceeds fluid influx, and this small surplus is exactly what becomes lymph.

Interstitial fluid (ISF) volume is set by five factors: capillary hydrostatic pressure, capillary osmotic pressure, the capillary filtration coefficient, the number of active capillaries, and the lymph flow. The extra fluid that filtration produces enters the lymphatics through loose junctions between endothelial cells in the walls of the initial lymphatics — a feature of regions such as the intestines and skeletal muscles — and drains into collecting lymphatics, which carry it onward into the large thoracic veins, the subclavian or internal jugular. Normal lymph flow is 2-4 L/day. Three separate mechanisms drive this flow centrally: the collecting lymphatics have smooth muscle in their walls whose peristaltic contraction is the main force pushing lymph forward, together with one-way valves that stop it flowing backward; contraction of the skeletal muscles surrounding the lymphatics squeezes lymph centrally; and negative intra-thoracic pressure sucks lymph upward.

### Clinical significance
Lymphatics do more than drain excess volume. The book lists four functions: draining the excess filtered fluid that is not reabsorbed at the venous end of the capillary; carrying proteins and large particles out of the tissue spaces; transporting absorbed long-chain fatty acids and cholesterol from the intestine; and removing bacteria, delivering them to lymph nodes.

Oedema — an abnormally large accumulation of ISF, which tends to pool in dependent parts (the lower limbs when standing, the back when recumbent, because of gravity) — follows whenever any of the five determinants of ISF volume shifts toward net filtration, and the book groups the causes into four mechanisms. Increased filtration pressure can come from arteriolar dilation, venular constriction, or increased venous pressure itself (from gravity, a raised total extracellular fluid volume, incompetent venous valves, venous obstruction, or heart failure). A decreased osmotic pressure gradient across the capillary can follow a fall in plasma protein — nutritional oedema, liver cirrhosis reducing plasma protein synthesis, or kidney disease such as nephrosis losing plasma proteins in the urine — or an accumulation of osmotically active substances in the interstitial space. Increased capillary permeability can be driven by mediators such as substance P, histamine, or kinins. And inadequate lymph flow, from lymphatic obstruction, produces a distinctive picture: because the protein that would normally have been carried away by the lymphatics stays behind, the oedema fluid has a high protein content, and in longstanding disease such as elephantiasis this is associated with inflammation and fibrosis of the ISF, giving non-pitting rather than ordinary pitting oedema.

### Common misconceptions
It is easy to treat all four Starling forces as equally weighty; the book specifically flags interstitial colloid osmotic pressure as usually negligible, so the working balance in practice is dominated by capillary hydrostatic pressure against capillary colloid osmotic pressure. It is also easy to picture oedema as one uniform condition; the book's own account distinguishes ordinary, low-protein, pitting oedema from filtration-pressure or osmotic causes from the high-protein, non-pitting, inflamed and fibrosed oedema that follows lymphatic obstruction — the mechanism, not just the volume of fluid, changes what the swelling looks and feels like.

## summary
The baroreceptor reflex (covered elsewhere) is only one of five afferent streams feeding the medulla's two blood-pressure centres. This article covers the other rapid mechanisms: the peripheral chemoreceptors' oxygen-driven reflex, the central chemoreceptors' CO2-driven one, and two extreme, medulla-ischaemia-driven reflexes — the CNS ischaemic response and the Cushing reflex — plus the two medullary centres themselves and what each does when stimulated.
## sections
### Definition
The vasomotor area and the cardiac inhibitory area, both in the medulla, are the two centres that set arterial blood pressure from moment to moment, driven by five afferent streams: arterial baroreceptors (covered elsewhere), peripheral chemoreceptors, central chemoreceptors, and two extreme reflexes triggered only once the medulla itself is starved of blood — the CNS ischaemic response and the Cushing reflex.

### Mechanism
The vasomotor area, in the rostral ventrolateral medulla, mediates sympathetic outflow to the heart and vessels; stimulating it raises arterial pressure by arteriolar constriction (raising total peripheral resistance), venoconstriction (raising venous return), a rise in heart rate and stroke volume, and a fall in vagal tone. The cardiac inhibitory area — the nucleus ambiguus and the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus — mediates vagal outflow to the heart; stimulating it lowers heart rate and cardiac output.

Peripheral chemoreceptors sit in the carotid and aortic bodies (carried by the carotid sinus/glossopharyngeal nerve and the vagus respectively) and are stimulated primarily by a fall in arterial PO2; only when arterial pressure itself falls to 40-60 mmHg, low enough to make the bodies ischaemic, do they respond to pressure as such. Their stimulation raises sympathetic discharge, producing tachycardia and vasoconstriction that push a low pressure back up. Central chemoreceptors, near the medulla, are driven chiefly by a rise in blood/CSF PCO2 — CO2 crosses the blood-brain barrier freely while ionised H+ and O2 changes in plasma do not — and hypercapnia (and, to a lesser degree, hypoxia) can also stimulate the vasomotor area directly.

### Key determinants
The CNS ischaemic response triggers once arterial pressure falls below about 50 mmHg: the medullary vasomotor area itself becomes ischaemic, local PCO2 rises, and the area is driven to marked vasoconstriction — the single most powerful stimulator of the sympathetic nervous system the book describes. The Cushing reflex is the response to raised intracranial pressure: compression of the cerebral vessels causes brain ischaemia, and the resulting local hypercapnia and hypoxia drive a pressor response — marked hypertension — with a secondary, baroreceptor-mediated bradycardia.

### Clinical significance
The CNS ischaemic response is a last-resort mechanism: it only engages once pressure has already fallen far enough to threaten the brainstem itself, so its presence is a sign of a severe circulatory emergency, not a first-line defence. The Cushing reflex's triad of hypertension and bradycardia is used clinically as a late warning sign of dangerously raised intracranial pressure — by the time it appears, herniation risk is already high, so it is a signal to act rather than a stable finding to observe.

### Common misconceptions
Treating the peripheral chemoreceptor reflex as a direct pressure sensor. It is an O2 sensor first; it only engages with pressure once pressure has fallen low enough to make the receptors themselves hypoxic.
Assuming the Cushing reflex's bradycardia is a direct effect of raised intracranial pressure. It is secondary — a baroreceptor response to the hypertension the ischaemic vasomotor area itself produces.

## summary
Not every adjustment of blood flow is nervous. This article covers the local mechanisms that match a single tissue's flow to its own metabolism, the two catecholamines that pull the vasculature in opposite directions, what actually happens — and what can go wrong — when a person simply stands up, the endothelium's own vasoactive secretions (nitric oxide, endothelin, prostacyclin), the classification of circulating vasoconstrictor and vasodilator hormones, the renin-angiotensin system's formation cascade and AT1/AT2 receptor actions, carotid sinus syndrome as a clinical extension of the baroreceptor reflex, and the distinct causes of circulatory shock, including why some patients progress to refractory shock.
## sections
### Definition
Blood flow is matched to local demand without any nervous or hormonal input by two local mechanisms, redirected regionally by two catecholamines that pull the vasculature in opposite directions, and — on simply standing up — challenged and then restored by gravity and the baroreflex. Beyond these, the vascular endothelium itself secretes vasoactive substances, a further set of circulating hormones acts on the vasculature systemically, and the renin-angiotensin system provides both an acute vasoconstrictor and the module's own account of long-term arterial pressure regulation.

### Mechanism
Active hyperaemia is the rise in flow that accompanies a rise in tissue metabolism, driven by local hypoxia, vasodilator metabolites (CO2, H+, adenosine) and local heat dilating the arterioles and precapillary sphincters. Autoregulation is the separate tendency of flow to drift back toward normal within under a minute after a change in perfusion pressure, by two mechanisms: myogenic (a pressure rise stretches the arteriolar wall, driving calcium entry and constriction; a fall does the opposite) and metabolic (a fall in flow itself causes local hypoxia and metabolite build-up that dilates the vessels; a rise does the opposite). Reactive hyperaemia — the marked overshoot in flow seen once a period of vascular occlusion is released — is an extreme case of the metabolic mechanism.

Epinephrine dilates vessels in skeletal muscle and liver through β2 receptors, and this normally outweighs its α1-mediated constriction elsewhere, so total peripheral resistance falls overall — except at very high plasma levels, where the α1 effect takes over. Norepinephrine, by contrast, constricts vessels in almost all organs through α1 receptors and has no comparable vasodilator action; both are released by the adrenal medulla, and norepinephrine is also the sympathetic postganglionic transmitter, with secretion of both raised by sympathetic stimulation, emotional stress, hypoglycaemia and cold.

### Key determinants
On standing, gravity pools blood in the leg veins, lowering venous return, cardiac output and arterial pressure; prolonged standing compounds this as raised capillary hydrostatic pressure filters fluid out of the blood. A fall of more than 20 mmHg is called postural (orthostatic) hypotension. Normally the baroreflex compensates within seconds: falling baroreceptor discharge raises sympathetic outflow, producing tachycardia, arterial and venous constriction (which restores venous return) and increased renin-angiotensin-aldosterone secretion.

### Clinical significance
The compensatory reflex to standing can fail — a slow or weak sympathetic response after bed rest, in old age, in autonomic neuropathy (e.g. diabetes) or with sympatholytic drugs — or be overwhelmed by hypovolaemia (haemorrhage, dehydration), and either failure lets postural hypotension appear, with dizziness or syncope as the presenting complaint. Reactive hyperaemia is exploited at the bedside as a rough test of arterial supply (e.g. capillary refill after releasing a cuff or compression), and epinephrine's dose-dependent switch from net vasodilation to vasoconstriction is why its clinical effect on blood pressure differs at physiological versus pharmacological doses.

Carotid sinus syndrome is a clinical extension of the same baroreceptor reflex driving the standing response above: in some people the carotid sinus baroreceptors become abnormally sensitive, so trivial external pressure (a tight collar, or the pressure of shaving) activates the reflex strongly enough to produce marked bradycardia and a fall in arterial pressure, which can cause cerebral ischaemia and fainting — an acquired hypersensitivity, not a normal finding especially in young adults. Severe or recurrent cases may need denervation of the hypersensitive carotid sinus or implantation of a permanent artificial cardiac pacemaker.

Circulatory shock — inadequate tissue perfusion — has several distinct causes beyond haemorrhage. Anaphylactic shock follows an excessive allergic reaction releasing histamine, a strong vasodilator, producing marked vasodilation; septic shock follows severe infection releasing bacterial toxins that are themselves strong vasodilators; neurogenic shock follows loss of vasomotor tone (brain damage affecting the vasomotor area, deep general anaesthesia, or high spinal anaesthesia); cardiogenic shock follows pump failure itself. Vasopressor drugs are particularly useful in neurogenic and anaphylactic shock specifically because the underlying defect in both is inappropriate vasodilation rather than a primary pump or volume problem. Some patients with severe haemorrhage progress to refractory (irreversible) shock: it persists for hours, becomes resistant to treatment, and death follows even if blood volume is restored to normal, because cardiac output stays low regardless. Three mechanisms drive this: rising capillary hydrostatic pressure drives excessive fluid filtration, further lowering blood volume, while granulocytes adhering to injured capillary walls release free oxygen radicals that cause more tissue damage, and bacteria entering the blood through injured vessels release toxins that are themselves strong vasodilators; severe cerebral ischaemia, from the marked fall in arterial pressure, causes vasomotor centre failure, which lowers pressure further in a deadly positive-feedback loop; and severe cardiac ischaemia, from the same fall in pressure, further lowers cardiac output despite coronary vasodilation, again in a self-worsening loop.

### Endothelium-derived vasoactive substances
The vascular endothelium is not a passive lining: it continuously secretes nitric oxide (NO), formed by endothelial NO synthase (eNOS) acting on L-arginine, with a short half-life (about 6 seconds) that keeps its action mainly local. Shear stress, acetylcholine, and the inflammatory mediators bradykinin and histamine all raise NO secretion; NO itself acts on vascular smooth muscle via guanylate cyclase, raising cGMP and relaxing the myocyte — the target of PDE-5 inhibitors such as sildenafil, which prolong this effect. A primary deficiency of NO synthesis contributes to chronic hypertension, since it removes a vasodilator tone the vasculature depends on. Endothelin-1, by contrast, is the most potent vasoconstrictor known, released in response to endothelial injury, thrombin and hypoxia, and reduced by NO, prostacyclin and atrial natriuretic peptide. Prostacyclin (PGI2), formed from arachidonic acid, is a vasodilator that also inhibits platelet aggregation and facilitates NO release, working alongside it rather than independently.

### The renin-angiotensin system
Renin, secreted by the kidney's juxtaglomerular apparatus in response to hypovolaemia/hypotension, renal ischaemia, reduced distal tubular Na+ delivery, or sympathetic (β1) stimulation — not in response to hypertension — cleaves hepatic angiotensinogen to angiotensin I, which angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE, concentrated in pulmonary vascular endothelium) converts to angiotensin II. Angiotensin II acts through two receptor types with opposing profiles: AT1 receptors, widespread in adult tissue, mediate vasoconstriction, aldosterone secretion, direct renal Na+/water reabsorption, renin-secretion inhibition, increased sympathetic and vasopressin drive, thirst, and cardiac/vascular hypertrophy; AT2 receptors, much more limited in adults, counterbalance AT1 with vasodilatation, diuresis and natriuresis. Because angiotensin II's downstream renal Na+/water-retaining effect builds and persists over minutes to days, the renin-angiotensin system — unlike the second-to-second baroreceptor and chemoreceptor reflexes — is the module's own account of long-term arterial pressure regulation.

### Common misconceptions
Treating active hyperaemia and autoregulation as one mechanism. Active hyperaemia responds to a rise in tissue metabolism; autoregulation responds to a change in perfusion pressure — they share the same metabolic logic but different triggers.
Assuming epinephrine and norepinephrine both raise total peripheral resistance because both are catecholamines. Epinephrine's β2 vasodilation in muscle and liver normally wins out and lowers TPR; norepinephrine has no such vasodilator arm.
Confusing NO's second messenger (cGMP) with cAMP, or assuming adrenaline directly stimulates NO synthase — the book's own listed stimuli are shear stress, acetylcholine, bradykinin and histamine.
Assuming AT1 and AT2 share the same direction of effect because both are angiotensin II receptors — AT2 specifically counterbalances AT1, and diuresis/natriuresis are AT2, not AT1, actions.
Assuming hypertension itself raises renin secretion — the reverse is true: hypovolaemia/hypotension, not hypertension, is what raises it.
Treating carotid sinus syndrome as a normal finding in healthy young adults rather than an acquired abnormal hypersensitivity.
Assuming refractory shock is simply severe hypoxaemia that restoring blood volume fixes — cardiac output stays low even after volume restoration, because self-worsening cerebral- and cardiac-ischaemia feedback loops, capillary damage and bacterial toxin release have taken over by that point.

## summary
Two circulations break the rules the rest of the cardiovascular system runs by: the coronary bed, whose flow is highest not when the heart works hardest but the instant afterward, and the pulmonary bed, whose vessels constrict — not dilate — when starved of oxygen. This article covers both, exactly as far as the department book's own "Special Circulation" chapter takes them.
## sections
### Definition
Two circulations run by different rules from the rest of the cardiovascular system: coronary flow, timed to the cardiac cycle rather than to systemic pressure, and pulmonary flow, whose vessels respond to hypoxia in the opposite direction from every other vascular bed.

### Mechanism
Resting coronary flow (about 84 ml/100 g tissue/min, 250 ml/min for the whole heart) can rise to about 400 ml/100 g/min. Because the myocardium already extracts 70-80% of the O2 delivered to it at rest, a rise in demand cannot mostly be met by extracting more — coronary flow itself must rise. Flow is not constant through the cycle: ventricular contraction compresses the intramural vessels, driving flow to a minimum during systole (hardest on the subendocardium, the layer most vulnerable to ischaemia) before it peaks in early diastole and falls passively with aortic pressure — so diastolic aortic pressure is what mainly perfuses the coronaries.

Pulmonary artery pressure (about 25/10 mmHg, mean 15) sits far below systemic arterial pressure (120/80, mean 90), which is why pulmonary capillaries normally filter no fluid. Uniquely, pulmonary arterioles constrict — rather than dilate, as systemic vessels do — in response to local alveolar hypoxia and to high alveolar PCO2, usefully diverting blood away from poorly ventilated lung regions.

### Key determinants
Coronary flow is matched to demand primarily by metabolic autoregulation (adenosine, CO2, H+ and prostaglandins released as cardiac metabolism rises), assisted by myogenic autoregulation across a 40-130 mmHg perfusion-pressure range, a net vasodilator effect of sympathetic stimulation (because raised myocardial metabolism outweighs direct α1 constriction), and endothelial NO/prostacyclin (dilators) against endothelin-1 (constrictor). Pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) is lowest at functional residual capacity and rises toward both total lung capacity (expanding alveoli compress the alveolar vessels, outweighing the falling resistance of the extra-alveolar ones) and residual volume (rising intrapleural pressure narrows the extra-alveolar vessels, outweighing the falling resistance from alveolar collapse); PVR also falls as pulmonary blood flow itself rises, through distension and recruitment of previously closed capillaries.

### Clinical significance
Because diastolic aortic pressure is what perfuses the coronaries, a fast heart rate — which shortens diastole disproportionately — can worsen myocardial ischaemia independent of any change in coronary resistance, which is part of why tachycardia is avoided in coronary disease. Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, useful locally to match perfusion to ventilation, becomes harmful when it is global: widespread alveolar hypoxia (e.g. at high altitude or in chronic lung disease) raises pulmonary vascular resistance throughout the lung and can drive pulmonary hypertension and right heart strain.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming coronary flow peaks during systole, when the heart is working hardest. It is lowest then, because contraction compresses the vessels running through the wall; flow peaks in early diastole.
Assuming pulmonary vessels dilate in hypoxia, as systemic vessels do. They constrict — the opposite response — which is what protects gas exchange by redirecting blood to better-ventilated alveoli.

## summary
Not every millilitre of a breath reaches an alveolus, and not every cmH2O of pressure swing in the chest does the same job. This article covers the anatomy behind those two facts: the conducting and respiratory zones, the dead-space arithmetic that separates minute ventilation from the alveolar ventilation that actually matters for gas exchange, and the pressure that does the work of expanding the lung, at its normal and its extreme values.
## sections
### Definition
Not every millilitre of a breath reaches an alveolus, and not every cmH2O of pressure swing in the chest does the same job: the conducting zone moves and conditions air without exchanging gas, dead space is the volume that never reaches an alveolus at all, and transpulmonary pressure is specifically the force that expands the lung.

### Mechanism
The lower respiratory passages divide functionally into the conducting zone (trachea to terminal bronchioles: ciliated, transports, humidifies and warms air, and filters particulates) and the respiratory zone (from the respiratory bronchioles onward, where alveoli appear and gas exchange occurs across a thin, 0.5-micrometre membrane totalling 70-80 m2). The alveolar wall carries type I pneumocytes for exchange, type II pneumocytes that secrete surfactant, and alveolar macrophages that engulf foreign particles.

Anatomic dead space — the conducting-zone air where no gas exchange occurs — is about 150 ml; physiologic dead space adds any non-functioning alveoli and normally equals the anatomic figure, rising above it only in disease. Because the 150 ml of old air sitting in the dead space moves into the alveoli before fresh air does on the next breath, only tidal volume minus dead space is functionally fresh: alveolar ventilation = (tidal volume - dead space) x respiratory rate, which at rest gives (500 - 150) x 12 = 4200 ml/min against a minute (pulmonary) ventilation of 500 x 12 = 6000 ml/min.

Transpulmonary pressure — intra-alveolar pressure minus intrapleural pressure — is the force that expands the lung against its own elastic recoil: about 3 cmH2O at the end of normal expiration, about 6 cmH2O at the end of normal inspiration.

### Key determinants
Because dead space is roughly fixed regardless of tidal volume, increasing the depth of breathing raises alveolar ventilation far more effectively than increasing its rate: shallow rapid breathing (e.g. 200 ml x 30/min) can hold minute ventilation at a normal 6 L/min while alveolar ventilation falls well below normal, producing hypoxia and hypercapnia, while slow deep breathing (1000 ml x 6/min) raises alveolar ventilation well above normal for the same minute ventilation. Intrapleural pressure itself runs about -3 cmH2O at the end of normal expiration and -6 to -8 cmH2O at the end of normal inspiration, but these are only the resting range: a maximal forced inspiratory effort against a closed glottis (Muller's experiment) can drive it to -30 to -40 cmH2O, and a maximal forced expiratory effort against a closed glottis (Valsalva's experiment) can drive it positive, to about +50 cmH2O.

### Clinical significance
A patient breathing shallow and fast (e.g. from chest-wall pain splinting the breath) can look adequately ventilated by minute-ventilation arithmetic while their alveolar ventilation, and so their gas exchange, is genuinely failing — this is why respiratory rate and depth are assessed together rather than rate alone. In diseases that destroy elastic fibres, such as emphysema, reduced lung recoil makes intrapleural pressure less negative than normal at any given lung volume, which is part of why such patients hyperinflate.

### Common misconceptions
Treating minute ventilation and alveolar ventilation as interchangeable. Two breathing patterns can share an identical minute ventilation while alveolar ventilation — the part that actually reaches the alveoli — differs several-fold, because a fixed dead space is subtracted once per breath no matter how large the tidal volume is.
Assuming intrapleural pressure is always negative. It is negative throughout normal breathing, but a Valsalva-type forced expiratory effort against a closed glottis can drive it strongly positive.

## summary
Getting a gas from alveolus to blood needs three things lined up: a membrane thin and wide enough for the gas to cross, ventilation and perfusion matched region by region, and a circulation that is not quietly diluting its own output before it leaves the lung. This article works through the physics of diffusion across the respiratory membrane, why gravity leaves the apex over-ventilated and the base over-perfused, and the small physiologic shunt every healthy lung carries.
## sections
### Definition
Getting a gas from alveolus to blood needs three things lined up: a membrane thin and wide enough for the gas to cross, ventilation and perfusion matched region by region, and a circulation that is not quietly diluting its own output before it leaves the lung.

### Mechanism
The rate of gas diffusion through the respiratory membrane rises with the pressure gradient across it, the membrane's surface area (70-80 m2), temperature and the gas's solubility, and falls with the membrane's thickness (about 0.5 micrometre) and the square root of the gas's molecular weight. O2's pressure gradient across the membrane (alveolar 105 minus capillary 40, about 60 mmHg) is roughly ten times CO2's (about 6 mmHg) — yet CO2 still diffuses faster overall: it is about 24 times more soluble in water than O2 despite a 1.4-times larger molecule, giving it a relative diffusion coefficient about 20.3 times that of O2.

Neither ventilation nor perfusion is uniform in the upright lung. Gravity makes the intrapleural space more negative at the apex (about -10 cmH2O) than the base (about -2.5 cmH2O), leaving apical alveoli already more expanded at rest and so changing volume less on inspiration — ventilation is highest at the base. Gravity acts even more strongly on the low-pressure pulmonary circulation: apical capillaries, under low arterial pressure, nearly collapse (zone 1, poor perfusion); mid-lung pressures balance (zone 2); basal capillaries, under the highest hydrostatic pressure, stay fully open (zone 3, highest perfusion).

### Key determinants
The ventilation-perfusion ratio (VA/Q) — alveolar ventilation (about 4 L/min) over pulmonary perfusion (about 5 L/min) — averages 0.8-1.2 for the lung as a whole, but perfusion falls faster than ventilation moving from base to apex, giving a high VA/Q of about 3.0 at the apex and a low VA/Q of about 0.6 at the base. Pulmonary capillaries normally keep the lung dry: colloidal osmotic pressure (about 28 mmHg, absorptive) exceeds mean capillary hydrostatic pressure (about 7 mmHg, filtering), so fluid is normally absorbed rather than filtered — pulmonary oedema develops only once hydrostatic pressure rises above the osmotic pressure. Separately, the bronchial circulation, which supplies the bronchial smooth muscle and pleura and arises from the aorta, drains its deoxygenated venous blood into the pulmonary vein, mixing it with oxygenated blood before it reaches the systemic circulation — this venous admixture, present even in a perfectly healthy lung, is the physiologic shunt.

### Clinical significance
Because O2 exchange is far more diffusion-limited than CO2 elimination, a diffusion-impairing disease (fibrosis, oedema, emphysema) typically produces hypoxaemia well before it produces hypercapnia — a patient can be "blowing off" CO2 normally while still failing to oxygenate. A pathological V/Q mismatch (rather than the lung's own normal apex-to-base gradient) is one of the commonest mechanisms of hypoxaemia in lung disease, and pulmonary oedema — once hydrostatic pressure exceeds the 28 mmHg osmotic pressure, as in left heart failure — directly worsens gas exchange by thickening the diffusion path.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming a larger pressure gradient always wins the diffusion race. O2 has the bigger pressure gradient across the membrane, but CO2 diffuses faster overall because its far higher solubility outweighs it.
Treating VA/Q as one fixed number for the whole lung. It averages 0.8-1.2, but ranges from about 3.0 at the apex to about 0.6 at the base of the upright lung.
Assuming any shunt means disease. A small physiologic shunt, from bronchial venous drainage into the pulmonary vein, exists in every healthy lung.

## summary
Every heartbeat starts without a nerve telling it to, because pacemaker cells carry a phase of their own action potential that ordinary cardiac muscle does not: a phase 4 that depolarizes itself. This article follows the ionic housekeeping that keeps the resting cardiac myocyte stable, then the currents that make pacemaker cells spontaneously unstable instead, and the hierarchy of pacemaker tissues that results — SA node fastest, AV node next, Purkinje fibres last, each ready to take over if the one above it fails.
## sections
### Definition
Every cardiac action potential leaves the myocyte with slightly more intracellular Na+ and Ca++, and slightly less K+, than it started with. Left uncorrected over many beats, this would erode the ionic gradients the resting membrane potential itself depends on. Three transporters in the sarcolemma correct it. The Na+-K+ ATPase pumps three Na+ out of the cell for every two K+ it brings in — an unequal, and therefore electrogenic, exchange that contributes its own small outward current rather than being electrically silent. A separate Ca++-ATPase pumps Ca++ out. And the Na+-Ca++ exchanger normally trades three incoming Na+ for one outgoing Ca++, but it is not locked into that direction: whenever intracellular Na+ rises — for instance because a drug such as digitalis has inhibited the Na+-K+ ATPase — the exchanger reverses, moving Na+ out and Ca++ in instead.

Against this housekeeping, pacemaker cells behave differently from ordinary atrial and ventricular myocytes. Electrical activity of the heart is spontaneously initiated by specialised cells called pacemaker cells, found in the cardiac conduction system: the sino-atrial (SA) node, the atrio-ventricular (AV) node, and the Purkinje fibres. These cells generate a "pacemaker potential" on their own, without needing to be triggered by a neighbouring cell, and this ability is what gives the heart automaticity (initiating its own contraction independent of external stimuli) and rhythmicity (beating regularly).

### Mechanism
The pacemaker action potential has three phases: 4, 0 and 3. Phase 4, the pre-potential, is a spontaneous, gradual depolarization beginning at about -60 mV. From -60 mV to -40 mV it is carried first by Na+ funny channels, which open to conduct an inward Na+ current, the funny current (If), and second by the Na+-Ca++ exchanger, whose activity increases early in phase 4 because of a spontaneous release of Ca++ from the sarcoplasmic reticulum, adding a further inward Na+ current. Then, from about -50 mV to -40 mV, transient (T-type) Ca++ channels open and add an inward Ca++ current; they are called transient because they inactivate rapidly once open.

This is the essential contrast with a working atrial or ventricular myocyte. In those cells, phase 4 is simply the resting membrane potential: a slow outward leak of K+ through inward rectifying channels holds it flat, and it stays that way until current arrives from an adjacent active myocyte. A working myocyte's phase 4 does not depolarize itself; a pacemaker cell's does.

Once phase 4 reaches the firing level, about -40 mV, phase 0 begins. It is produced by activation of long-lasting (L-type) Ca++ channels — called long-lasting because they inactivate slowly after opening — together with inactivation of the funny channels and T-type channels that drove phase 4. Because Ca++ movement through channels is not rapid, phase 0's upstroke is slower in pacemaker cells than the fast Na+-channel-driven upstroke of a working myocyte, which is exactly why the pacemaker action potential is called the "slow response action potential." Phase 3 then repolarizes the cell back toward -60 mV, driven by an outward K+ current through delayed rectifying K+ channels together with inactivation of the L-type Ca++ channels; at -60 mV the K+ current itself inactivates, and a new phase 4 begins, so the whole cycle repeats spontaneously.

### Key determinants
Not every pacemaker tissue discharges at the same intrinsic rate. The SA node fires at about 90-105/min, the AV node at about 60/min, and Purkinje cells at about 20-40/min. Because the SA node is faster than the natural rhythm of every other pacemaker tissue, it suppresses them and acts as the normal pacemaker of the human heart — it, and not the others, controls heart rate under ordinary conditions. This hierarchy also functions as a built-in safety net: if the SA node fails, the AV node's own intrinsic rhythm takes over as the pacemaker; if the AV node also fails, the Purkinje cells become the pacemaker instead, each tier slower than the one above it.

### Clinical significance
The reversibility of the Na+-Ca++ exchanger is not an abstract detail: it is the mechanism by which digitalis works. By inhibiting the Na+-K+ ATPase, digitalis raises intracellular Na+; the exchanger then runs in reverse, exporting Na+ and importing Ca++, which is how the drug raises intracellular Ca++ and contractility (a link this article's sibling on inotropic mechanisms develops further). The pacemaker hierarchy is equally consequential at the bedside: an escape rhythm from the AV node or the Purkinje fibres, though slower, is what keeps a patient's circulation running when the SA node itself is diseased or suppressed, which is why the existence of these lower pacemakers — not merely the SA node — is worth knowing cold.

### Common misconceptions
The commonest error is assuming every cardiac cell has a spontaneously depolarizing phase 4. Only pacemaker tissue does; a working atrial or ventricular myocyte's phase 4 is a stable resting potential that depolarizes only when it is driven to by an adjacent cell. A second error is assuming the pacemaker upstroke uses the same fast Na+ channels a working myocyte's phase 0 does — it does not, which is precisely why it is called the slow response action potential. A third is thinking the AV node or the Purkinje fibres cannot pace the heart on their own; they can, and normally would, were the SA node not consistently faster and therefore suppressing them.

## summary
Two properties of the heart's electrical system are steered by the same autonomic nerves in the same direction — how fast it beats and how fast the impulse travels through it — while a third property, how long each cell stays refractory, is what stops the whole system from ever being driven into a sustained contraction. This article ties the autonomic rate/conduction story to the fixed geography of conduction velocities across the heart, and then to the refractory periods that make a tetanised heart impossible.
## sections
### Definition
Two named properties of cardiac electrical activity are shaped by the autonomic nervous system: chronotropy, the effect on the SA node's discharge rate (heart rate), and dromotropy, the effect on conduction velocity through the conducting system. Both properties move in the same direction under the same nerve: sympathetic activity raises both, parasympathetic activity lowers both.

### Mechanism
On rate: sympathetic nerve terminals at the SA node release norepinephrine, which binds beta1-adrenoreceptors, generates cAMP, and increases the funny current; the diastolic depolarization of phase 4 starts earlier (from a potential more negative than -60 mV) and its slope becomes steeper, so the firing threshold is reached sooner — positive chronotropy, producing tachycardia. Parasympathetic (vagal) activity has the mirror-image effect on SA discharge, producing negative chronotropy and bradycardia.

On conduction: the same two divisions act on the conducting system's own ionic conductance rather than on the SA node. Sympathetic stimulation, again through beta1 receptors, increases ionic conductance and so the upstroke velocity of action potentials, speeding conduction. Parasympathetic stimulation, through muscarinic receptors, decreases ionic conductance and slows conduction; digitalis works on this route too, decreasing conduction velocity by stimulating parasympathetic activity.

Crucially, at rest the two divisions are not in balance: the parasympathetic effect on the SA node is stronger than the sympathetic effect, a state called the "vagal tone." This is why the normal resting heart rate is only about 72 beats/minute, distinctly slower than the SA node's own intrinsic discharge rate of 90-105 beats/minute — the SA node is constantly being held back by the vagus.

### Key determinants
Autonomic tone is layered on top of a fixed anatomical geography of conduction velocities that does not itself depend on nerve activity. Cell-to-cell conduction happens through gap junctions at the intercalated discs, whose low electrical resistance lets charge pass rapidly between neighbouring myocytes. From the SA node, specialised internodal bundles carry the impulse to the AV node at about 1 m/sec — faster than the roughly 0.5 m/sec at which the impulse spreads through ordinary atrial myocardium. Within the AV node itself, conduction slows sharply, to about 0.05 m/sec. From the AV node the impulse enters the bundle of His and the bundle branches at a rapid 2 m/sec, and the Purkinje fibre network then carries it throughout the ventricles at a high 4 m/sec, spreading from the subendocardial surface outward to the epicardium so that essentially all ventricular myocytes are activated at nearly the same instant.

The AV node's slowness is doing real work, not merely marking time. It gives the atria enough time to finish contracting and empty their blood into the ventricles before ventricular contraction begins. And in disease, when the atrial rate becomes abnormally rapid, the AV node's low conduction velocity limits how many of those rapid atrial impulses can reach the ventricles per minute — a protective ceiling on ventricular rate that the anatomy of the node itself provides.

A separate limit on how often the heart can be driven is set at the level of the single myocyte, by its refractory periods. During the absolute refractory period (ARP), the myocyte cannot be re-excited by any stimulus, because the inactivation gates of its fast Na+ channels are still closed; the ARP spans phases 0, 1 and 2 and part of phase 3, down to about -50 mV. The relative refractory period (RRP) follows, lasting until about -75 mV, during which only a stronger-than-normal (supra-threshold) stimulus can trigger a new action potential. Late in phase 3, before the membrane is fully repolarized, there is a brief supernormal period in which a weaker-than-normal stimulus suffices — also called the vulnerable period, because many cardiac arrhythmias can be triggered during it.

### Clinical significance
Because of the plateau in the cardiac action potential, this refractory period is much longer than a skeletal myocyte's, and it occupies almost the whole period of contraction and the early part of relaxation. That is functionally deliberate: it is what prevents the heart from developing the sustained, tetanic contractions ordinary skeletal muscle can be driven into — a heart that could be tetanised could not relax and fill between beats, and would fail as a pump. The AV nodal delay and the myocyte's own refractory period are therefore two separate safeguards working toward the same end: protecting the ventricles from being driven faster than they can usefully fill and eject.

### Common misconceptions
Students often assume the resting heart rate reflects the SA node's own unforced pace. It does not — a dominant resting vagal tone keeps the measured rate (about 72/min) well below the node's intrinsic rate (90-105/min). A second error is treating the AV nodal delay as an unwanted transmission lag rather than a functional necessity: it is exactly what buys the atria time to empty and what limits ventricular rate when the atria misbehave. A third is describing the cardiac refractory period as simply "long" without connecting it to the plateau that causes it, or to the specific consequence — no tetanus — that makes it matter.

## summary
A cardiac action potential only matters because it triggers a mechanical event, and that link runs through one specific relay: a small trigger calcium current that unlocks a much larger calcium store. This article follows that relay from depolarization to contraction and back to relaxation, then the two dials — preload and stimulation frequency — that a book-level account of isolated cardiac muscle uses to explain how much and how fast the muscle shortens.
## sections
### Definition
Contraction of the cardiac myocyte is preceded by its action potential, and the process that links the electrical event to the mechanical one is excitation-contraction coupling. Membrane depolarization opens L-type Ca++ channels in the sarcolemma and the T-tubules, letting a small amount of Ca++ enter and raise the Ca++ concentration in the thin region just inside the sarcolemma.

### Mechanism
That local rise in Ca++ is sensed by a calcium release channel in the terminal cisterns of the sarcoplasmic reticulum — the ryanodine-sensitive calcium release channel, or ryanodine receptor (RyR) — and it triggers the release of a much larger amount of Ca++ from the sarcoplasmic reticulum itself. This relay, a small trigger current unlocking a large store, is what the book calls calcium-induced calcium release. The released Ca++ binds troponin-C and starts the same actin-myosin interaction described for skeletal muscle, producing contraction. As the action potential ends, Ca++ release falls, and relaxation begins once Ca++ is removed from the cytoplasm and dissociates from troponin-C, which lifts the inhibition on actin-myosin interaction. Three mechanisms remove that Ca++: the sarco-endoplasmic reticulum reuptakes it through the ATP-dependent SERCA pump, whose activity is held in check by the inhibitory protein phospholamban (phosphorylating phospholamban relieves the inhibition and speeds reuptake); the sarcolemmal Na+-Ca++ exchanger carries Ca++ out of the myocyte; and a separate ATP-dependent Ca++ pump in the sarcolemma removes still more.

Because Ca++ release under normal resting conditions is not maximal, the amount of Ca++ available in the cytoplasm sets how forcefully the myocyte contracts — its contractility, or inotropic state. More available Ca++ means greater force (a positive inotropic state); less available Ca++ means less force (a negative inotropic state). Beta-adrenergic stimulation, whether from sympathetic nerves or circulating catecholamines, raises cAMP and activates Protein Kinase A (PKA), which increases cytoplasmic Ca++ by two separate routes — phosphorylating the L-type Ca++ channel so it stays open longer, and phosphorylating the ryanodine receptor so more Ca++ is released. The same PKA activation also speeds relaxation (a positive lusitropic effect), by phosphorylating phospholamban to accelerate SERCA-driven Ca++ reuptake and by decreasing troponin's affinity for Ca++. Hypoxia and ischaemia work against both processes at once: hypoxia inhibits the ATP production contraction depends on, and ischaemia's ATP deficiency slows the pumps that remove cytoplasmic Ca++, so Ca++ accumulates, cannot detach from troponin, and the myocyte contracts weakly and relaxes poorly at the same time.

### Key determinants
Beyond the amount of Ca++ available, the mechanical performance of isolated cardiac muscle — how much and how fast it shortens — depends on how far it was stretched before contracting, its preload. Passive stretching of cardiac muscle raises the tension within it even before contraction begins (the passive length-tension relationship), and on top of that, the maximum tension developed during an isometric contraction rises in direct proportion to that stretch (the active length-tension relationship). Frank-Starling's law formalises this: within limits, tension developed during isometric contraction is directly proportional to the degree of stretching of the muscle, i.e. to its preload. In an isolated cardiac muscle preparation this produces two distinct effects: the degree of shortening rises directly with preload, because a higher preload stretches the muscle to a greater length before it contracts, and separately, a higher preload also increases the velocity of shortening at any given afterload — so a more stretched cardiac muscle contracts both further and faster.

A second dial is the frequency of stimulation itself. Raising it produces a gradual, stepwise rise in contractile force over the first few beats, until a new, higher steady-state force is reached and sustained for as long as the higher frequency continues — the staircase phenomenon, or treppe (lowering frequency reverses it). The cause is kinetic rather than receptor-driven: faster beats leave less time between contractions for complete removal of the Ca++ just released, so Ca++ accumulates in the cytoplasm across successive beats, and that accumulated Ca++ is what raises the force.

### Clinical significance
Because calcium-induced calcium release is the hinge between the electrical and mechanical events, anything that changes the trigger current (through the L-type channel) or the store's own release channel (the ryanodine receptor) changes contractile force — which is exactly the route beta-adrenergic drugs and digitalis-class agents use. Ischaemia's dual hit on contraction and relaxation is a direct, testable consequence of the same calcium-handling machinery running out of ATP, not two separate problems.

### Common misconceptions
The commonest error is to credit the L-type Ca++ current itself with activating troponin-C. It mostly does not: it is the trigger that opens the ryanodine receptor, and the Ca++ that actually saturates troponin-C comes overwhelmingly from the sarcoplasmic reticulum's own store. A second error is treating inotropy and lusitropy as unrelated properties; the book's own beta-adrenergic-PKA pathway drives both from the same signalling cascade. A third is conflating preload's two separate effects on isolated muscle — increasing the degree of shortening and increasing the velocity of shortening are given as two distinct statements, not one restated twice — and a fourth is assuming treppe reflects some change in the channels themselves rather than simple incomplete calcium removal between closely spaced beats.

## summary
Almost all the oxygen blood carries rides on haemoglobin, and almost all the carbon dioxide it carries back rides as bicarbonate born inside the red cell itself — and the two gases are not carried independently: haemoglobin's grip on one changes its grip on the other. This article covers the arithmetic of O2 content and capacity, the three forms CO2 travels in, the chloride shift that keeps the red cell electrically neutral while it manufactures that bicarbonate, the Haldane effect that ties O2 and CO2 transport together, and — at minimal, exam-matched scope only — the classification of hypoxia, the absolute-quantity rule behind cyanosis, and why carbon monoxide poisoning produces neither a fall in arterial PO2 nor cyanosis.
## sections
### Definition
Oxygen is carried dissolved and haemoglobin-bound; carbon dioxide is carried dissolved, as carbamino compounds, and as bicarbonate. P50 anchors where the oxygen curve sits, and the Haldane effect is the rule that binding one gas to haemoglobin changes its grip on the other.
### Mechanism
About 98% of blood O2 is bound to haemoglobin (about 19.5 ml/100 ml arterial blood) rather than dissolved (about 0.3 ml/100 ml); O2 capacity — the maximum haemoglobin could carry fully saturated — works out to about 20.1 ml O2/100 ml blood from 1.34 ml O2 per gram of a normal 15 g Hb/100 ml, and P50, the PO2 at half saturation, is normally 27 mmHg. CO2 is carried dissolved (about 3 ml/100 ml, setting blood PCO2), as carbamino compounds on terminal amine groups mostly on haemoglobin (about 3 ml/100 ml), and as bicarbonate (about 42 ml/100 ml, the majority): carbonic anhydrase inside the red cell rapidly hydrates CO2 to carbonic acid, which dissociates to H+ (buffered by deoxyhaemoglobin) and HCO3-, which then diffuses out to plasma down its own gradient — and because the red cell membrane favours anions over cations, chloride moves in to replace the charge bicarbonate takes with it, the chloride shift, raising red cell chloride and osmotic pressure (and so venous haematocrit) while lowering plasma chloride.
### Key determinants
O2 saturation is a ratio of content to capacity, so it does not fall in anaemia even though O2 content does — content and capacity fall together. The Haldane effect determines how much CO2 a given blood sample carries at a fixed PCO2: reduced (deoxygenated) haemoglobin carries more CO2 than oxygenated haemoglobin, because O2 binding lowers haemoglobin's CO2 affinity — the mirror of the Bohr effect, in which CO2/H+ lowers haemoglobin's O2 affinity. This is what makes tissue O2 unloading and CO2 loading self-reinforcing, and lung O2 loading and CO2 unloading likewise self-reinforcing.
### Clinical significance
Because % saturation (read by pulse oximetry) does not fall in anaemia, a pulse oximeter can read a comfortably normal value in a severely anaemic patient whose actual O2 content — and so O2 delivery to tissue — is dangerously low; content, not saturation, is what tissues receive. The chloride shift is also why venous blood has a measurably higher chloride content in red cells and slightly higher haematocrit than arterial blood of the same person.
### Common misconceptions
Treating O2 saturation as a proxy for how much oxygen the blood is actually delivering. It only measures what fraction of available haemoglobin is loaded; a low-haemoglobin (anaemic) patient can have normal saturation and still dangerously low O2 content.
Assuming bicarbonate forms mainly in plasma. The hydration of CO2 is slow in plasma and fast inside the red cell because of carbonic anhydrase, which is exactly why bicarbonate then has to diffuse back out and chloride has to shift in.
### Types of hypoxia, cyanosis and carbon monoxide poisoning
The department book's own Gas Transport chapter does not carry this classification — it is added here at minimal, exam-matched scope, cited to a standard physiology textbook (Guyton & Hall, Textbook of Medical Physiology) rather than the department book, because the module's question banks test it directly. Hypoxia is classified by where the defect sits. Hypoxic hypoxia is a fall in arterial PO2 itself (from hypoventilation, a diffusion defect, ventilation-perfusion mismatch, a right-to-left shunt, or high altitude) — arterial O2 saturation falls with it, and because a normal total haemoglobin is present to sit unsaturated, this is the type classically accompanied by cyanosis. Anaemic hypoxia (too little haemoglobin) and histotoxic hypoxia (tissue cells unable to use the oxygen delivered to them) both leave arterial PO2 and % saturation normal, and in each case there is too little absolute reduced haemoglobin for cyanosis to appear, even though tissue O2 delivery or use is impaired. Stagnant (circulatory) hypoxia, from a reduced rate of blood flow, lets tissues extract more O2 from the blood that does pass, raising local reduced haemoglobin enough that cyanosis is often seen despite normal arterial PO2 and content.

Cyanosis itself is a bluish skin and mucous membrane discolouration, classically appearing once capillary blood contains more than 5 g of reduced (deoxygenated) haemoglobin per 100 ml — an absolute quantity of reduced haemoglobin, not a percentage or a saturation figure, which is why a severely anaemic patient (too little total haemoglobin to reach that 5 g/dL of the reduced fraction) can be profoundly hypoxic without ever turning cyanosed.

Carbon monoxide poisoning does not produce cyanosis, and this is the clearest illustration of the same absolute-quantity rule: CO binds the same haemoglobin site as O2 with roughly 200 times O2's own affinity, so carboxyhaemoglobin — not reduced (deoxygenated) haemoglobin — accumulates, giving the classic cherry-red skin colour rather than a blue one. Because the defect is in what haemoglobin is carrying (CO instead of O2) rather than in the lung's own gas exchange, arterial and alveolar PO2 both stay normal, which is also why carbon monoxide poisoning is not itself an instance of hypoxic hypoxia. Oxygen therapy is correspondingly of limited value in the two hypoxia types where the defect lies beyond how much oxygen reaches or is dissolved in the blood: stagnant hypoxia (the problem is flow, not content) and histotoxic hypoxia such as cyanide poisoning (the problem is cellular use, not delivery) — supplemental O2 raises dissolved and Hb-bound O2 further, which cannot compensate for a pump or a poisoned enzyme.

Transecting the brainstem at successively lower levels — and combining each cut with vagotomy — is the classic experimental evidence for how these pieces fit together.
### Mechanism
The dorsal respiratory group (DRG, around the nucleus of the tractus solitarius) is the primary inspiratory centre, firing continuous rhythmic signals to the inspiratory muscles during normal quiet breathing on its own slow, irregular inherent rhythm, generated at root by pacemaker neurons in the medullary pre-Botzinger complex. The ventral respiratory group (VRG) is totally inactive at rest and switches on only for forced breathing. Two pontine centres tune the DRG: the apneustic centre sends it tonic excitatory drive (and, isolated from its own inhibitory input, produces apneusis — prolonged inspiratory gasps); the pneumotaxic centre inhibits the DRG and apneustic centre, switching off inspiration and so setting the rate and depth of breathing.

Central chemoreceptors, just beneath the ventral medullary surface and protected by the blood-brain barrier, supply about 75-80% of resting drive; their direct stimulus is H+ in the CSF, which plasma H+ cannot reach, so CO2 — which crosses the barrier freely and acidifies the CSF — is the potent indirect stimulus, made highly sensitive by the CSF's weak protein buffering. Peripheral chemoreceptors (carotid body glomus cells via the glossopharyngeal/Hering's nerve, aortic bodies via the vagus) supply the remaining 20-25%; their very high blood flow lets them sense dissolved PO2 directly, so they are unaffected by anaemia or CO poisoning, and they stay almost silent as PO2 falls from 100 to 60 mmHg before responding steeply below it.

The Hering-Breuer reflex: lung inflation stretches receptors in the bronchial walls, which send inhibitory signals through the vagus to the DRG and apneustic centre, stopping inspiration.

Transection at four successively lower levels reveals what each structure contributes. Level 1 (upper border of the pons, above both pontine centres): respiration is unaffected, since every respiratory centre below the cut is intact; adding bilateral vagotomy at this level makes breathing slower and deeper, showing that vagal (Hering-Breuer) afferents normally help set tidal volume and rate. Level 2 (mid-pons, between the pneumotaxic and apneustic centres, removing pneumotaxic input): breathing becomes slightly slower and deeper, confirming the pneumotaxic centre's normal role in adjusting rate and depth; adding bilateral vagotomy at this level removes the apneustic centre's only remaining source of inhibition, and breathing stops in full inspiration — apneusis, continuous inspiratory stimulation. Level 3 (between pons and medulla, removing both pontine centres): respiration continues, but irregularly, on the DRG's own crude intrinsic rhythm alone. Level 4 (between medulla and the upper border of the spinal cord, disconnecting the DRG/VRG entirely from the phrenic and intercostal motor neurons that actually drive breathing): respiration stops completely, leading to death.
### Key determinants
For CO2 specifically, about 70-80% of the ventilatory response runs through the central chemoreceptor route and 20-30% through the peripheral one — CO2 is the most potent everyday respiratory stimulus, and a 3% rise in arterial PCO2 can double ventilation, while a marked rise above about 70 mmHg instead depresses the respiratory centre (CO2 narcosis). O2 lack, by contrast, is sensed only peripherally: ventilation barely rises as PO2 falls from 100 to 60 mmHg, rises sharply from 60 down to 30 mmHg, and below about 20 mmHg the effect reverses to direct central depression from brain hypoxia. The Hering-Breuer reflex shapes every breath in the newborn but, in the human adult, only engages once tidal volume rises to more than three times normal, as in exercise. Level 4 transection is functionally equivalent to a clinical high cervical cord or brainstem injury above the phrenic outflow (C3-C5): with no surviving connection between the medullary respiratory centres and the muscles of breathing, immediate stoppage of respiration and death follow — a plane below the phrenic origin (e.g. below C7) instead leaves diaphragmatic breathing intact.
### Clinical significance
Because central chemoreceptors respond to CO2 and not directly to O2, a patient who relies on hypoxic drive (peripheral chemoreceptors) — as in chronic CO2 retention — can have their only remaining respiratory stimulus blunted by giving high-flow O2, which is part of why controlled oxygen therapy is used in such patients. CO2 narcosis (respiratory centre depression above about 70 mmHg PCO2) is a real clinical danger of uncontrolled hypercapnia, and the transition from peripheral-chemoreceptor-driven hyperventilation to central depression below about 20 mmHg PO2 marks the point at which severe hypoxia stops stimulating breathing and starts suppressing it. COPD is the clearest clinical example of hypoventilation with a mechanism traceable to this same chemoreceptor system: longstanding CO2 retention lets the central chemoreceptors tolerate higher CO2 levels than normal, shifting these patients onto hypoxic (peripheral chemoreceptor) drive as their main stimulus to breathe — the same drive-shift that makes uncontrolled O2 therapy dangerous in them. Level 4 transection's clinical parallel — a high spinal cord or brainstem injury above the phrenic outflow — is why such injuries are immediately fatal without ventilatory support, while injuries below the phrenic segments (C3-C5) spare diaphragmatic breathing.
### Common misconceptions
Assuming O2 lack is the everyday driver of breathing the way CO2 is. Under normal conditions CO2 (via central chemoreceptors) dominates; O2 lack only becomes the dominant peripheral drive once PO2 falls below about 60 mmHg.
Assuming the ventral respiratory group contributes to every breath. It is silent at rest; only the DRG drives quiet breathing.
Assuming the Hering-Breuer reflex meaningfully limits every adult breath. It is essentially dormant at normal tidal volumes in adults and only engages at markedly increased tidal volume.
Assuming any brainstem transection stops breathing. Only a level 4 cut (between medulla and spinal cord) does; levels 1-3 leave breathing going, just increasingly irregular as more of the pontine tuning is removed.
Assuming vagotomy alone, or a mid-pontine cut alone, stops breathing. It takes both together (removing pneumotaxic input AND vagal/Hering-Breuer input) to unmask the apneustic centre's unopposed excitatory drive and produce apneusis.
