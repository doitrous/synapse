## summary
The dermis is the connective tissue under the epidermis, and it is built in two layers that differ on six axes at once. The department book prints them only as a paired table, and the 2025 end-of-year paper asked for exactly that comparison. The single fact that makes the table make sense rather than something to memorise is that the epidermis has no blood vessels of its own.

## sections
### Definition
The dermis is the deep connective tissue layer lying under the epidermis. It is derived from mesoderm and it is thicker than the epidermis. Its upper surface is not flat: it throws up projections called dermal papillae, which fit into matching concavities in the epidermis called epidermal ridges.

The dermis is formed of two layers, the papillary layer and the reticular layer.

The papillary layer is the thinner superficial layer and it forms the dermal papillae.
The reticular layer is the thicker deep layer.

Two things that sit beside the dermis are not part of it. The hypodermis — the deep layer of adipose connective tissue that corresponds to the superficial fascia — is not part of the skin at all, and the department book is explicit about that. The epidermis above is epithelium, not connective tissue.

### Mechanism
One fact drives most of the table: the epidermis is avascular and receives its nutrition by diffusion. Everything that has to be delivered to the epidermis has to arrive through the dermis immediately underneath it, and diffusion over distance is slow. So the layer directly under the epidermis is the vascular one, and it is loose rather than dense, because loose connective tissue leaves room for the vessels.

The interdigitation between dermal papillae and epidermal ridges does two jobs at once. It provides the attachment of epidermis to dermis, and it increases the surface area available for nutrition of the epidermis. Both jobs need the vessels to be in the papillae, and both are helped by the zigzag rather than a straight boundary.

The reticular layer does not need to feed anything. It carries load. Its type I collagen is arranged in bundles rather than as fine fibres, and bundled collagen resists tension. That is why the deep layer is dense connective tissue and the superficial layer is loose, and why the reticular layer is the thicker of the two.

The receptors follow the same logic. Meissner's corpuscles respond to light touch, and light touch has to be sampled close to the surface, so they lie in the papillary layer. Pacinian corpuscles respond to deep pressure and vibration, which reach further, so they lie deep with Ruffini's end organs and Krause's end bulbs.

### Key determinants
The book compares the two layers on six axes, and the paper marks on them.

**Thickness and position.** The papillary layer is the thinner, superficial layer, and it is the one that forms the dermal papillae. The reticular layer is the thicker, deep layer.

**Type of connective tissue.** The papillary layer is loose connective tissue. The reticular layer is dense connective tissue.

**Cellularity.** The papillary layer is more cellular; the reticular layer is less cellular. Both hold the same cast — fibrocytes, lymphocytes, macrophages, mast cells and adipocytes — so the difference is one of number, not of cell type.

**Fibres.** The papillary layer has fine connective tissue fibres: type III collagen with elastic fibres. The reticular layer has type I collagen arranged in bundles, again with elastic fibres.

**Vascularity.** The papillary layer is more vascular, and the book gives the reason in the same line: to nourish the epidermis. The reticular layer is less vascular.

**Receptors.** The papillary layer carries Meissner's corpuscles. The reticular layer carries Pacinian corpuscles, Ruffini's end organ and Krause's end bulb.

### Clinical significance
The zigzag interdigitation between dermal papillae and epidermal ridges is what forms the finger prints, and the book records these as being of medico-legal importance.

Rapid stretch of the skin ruptures the fibre bundles in the dermis. Scar tissue forms in the area and appears as white lines — the pregnancy lines are the example the book gives.

Ageing acts on both layers. Atrophy of the connective tissue elastic fibres reduces the elasticity of the skin and produces wrinkling. The blood vessels of the dermis become more fragile, which leads to bruising. Sebaceous glands produce less oil, and the subcutaneous fat layer thins so it insulates less.

Three things fix the epidermis to the dermis, and a student asked why skin does not simply shear off should be able to name them: the basement membrane of the epidermis, hemidesmosomes between the basal epidermal cells and that basement membrane, and the dermal papillae interdigitating with the epidermal ridges.

### Common misconceptions
Counting the hypodermis as the third layer of the skin. The book names it, describes it, and then says it is not a part of the skin — it is subcutaneous tissue, corresponding to the superficial fascia.

Expecting a visible line between the two dermal layers. The book separates them on what the tissue is made of and how much blood it carries, not on a boundary you can point at, and a stained section shows a gradient.

## summary
Bone holds four cell types, and two of them do opposite jobs. The osteoblast lays matrix down; the osteoclast takes it away. They differ in where they come from, where they sit, what they look like down a light microscope and what their ultrastructure is — and every one of those differences follows from the job. The 2025 end-of-year paper asked for all four axes and gave the question 6 marks, the largest in the histology section.

## sections
### Definition
Bone contains four cells: the osteogenic (osteoprogenitor) cell, the osteoblast, the osteocyte and the osteoclast. Three of them are one lineage. The osteogenic cell divides and gives rise to the osteoblast; the osteoblast, once surrounded by the calcified matrix it has made, becomes an imprisoned cell called an osteocyte. The osteoclast is not part of that sequence at all.

The osteoblast is the bone-forming cell.
The osteoclast is the bone-destroying cell.

### Mechanism
The osteoblast is a secretory cell, and its whole appearance is the appearance of a cell making protein for export. It secretes the organic part of the matrix; the newly formed organic matrix is called osteoid. It then calcifies that matrix by means of alkaline phosphatase, which helps the deposition of calcium. A cell doing this needs ribosomes on membranes to make the protein, mitochondria to power it and a Golgi apparatus to package it — which is exactly the ultrastructure it has, and exactly why its cytoplasm is darkly basophilic at the light-microscopic level, since rough endoplasmic reticulum takes up basic dye.

The osteoclast does the reverse, and needs different tools. Bone matrix is 35% organic and 65% inorganic, so it has to be attacked twice. The osteoclast contains carbonic anhydrase, which drives the reaction of water and carbon dioxide to bicarbonate and hydrogen ion; releasing that hydrogen ion through the ruffled border acidifies the medium under the cell and dissolves the calcium salts. It then secretes lysosomal proteolytic (osteolytic) enzymes, which lyse the organic part of the matrix. Acid first for the mineral, enzymes second for the collagen.

Both halves of that need surface area, so the membrane facing the bone is thrown into folds — the brush border seen by light microscopy, which electron microscopy resolves as a ruffled surface bearing microvilli. Both halves need lysosomes, which is why the cytoplasm is rich in them. And the cell has to be big enough to excavate, which is what the fusion of many progenitors buys: the osteoclast is multinucleated because it was assembled from several cells, not because one nucleus divided.

### Key determinants
The paper names four axes. Take them in the order it prints them.

**Origin.** The osteoblast arises from osteogenic cells. The osteoclast is formed by fusion of mononuclear haemopoietic progenitor cells. That single line explains the multinucleation and separates the two lineages for good.

**Site.** Osteoblasts are present immediately under the periosteum, in the form of a continuous single layer covering the bone surface, and are also present under the endosteum. Osteoclasts are present on the bone surface near the bone marrow, within a cavity called Howship's lacuna — a hollow the cell has itself dissolved out.

**Light microscopy.** The osteoblast is an oval cell with minute few processes and an eccentric nucleus, and the size of the cell depends on its activity. Its cytoplasm is darkly basophilic with a negative Golgi image near the nucleus, and it is rich in alkaline phosphatase. The osteoclast is a large irregular cell with a brush border facing the near bony surface, multinucleated with 6 to 12 nuclei, and with foamy acidophilic cytoplasm.

**Electron microscopy.** The osteoblast shows the characters of protein-forming cells: it is rich in rough endoplasmic reticulum, mitochondria and a well-developed Golgi apparatus. The osteoclast has a ruffled surface which shows microvilli, and its cytoplasm is rich in lysosomes, mitochondria and Golgi apparatus and shows multiple vesicles.

Two details are worth carrying. The negative Golgi image is not an artefact: the Golgi apparatus excludes the basic dye that stains the rest of the cytoplasm, so it appears as a pale area beside the nucleus and is positive evidence of active secretion. And the "foamy" acidophilic cytoplasm of the osteoclast is the light-microscopic appearance of the multiple vesicles that electron microscopy resolves.

### Clinical significance
Osteoporosis is a common bone disease, characterised by progressive loss of normal bone density that increases the risk of bone fracture. It is caused by increased bone resorption over bone formation — that is, by the osteoclast outrunning the osteoblast rather than by either cell being abnormal in itself.

The same balance runs normally: the book lists bone remodelling in ossification as one of the osteoclast's two functions, alongside bone resorption.

The osteoclast is also a phagocytic cell. It contains exocytotic vesicles that carry lysosomal enzymes out to Howship's lacuna, and endocytotic vesicles that carry degraded bone products into the cell; the contents of the endocytotic vesicles are then discharged by exocytosis from the basolateral aspect of the cell.

### Common misconceptions
Deriving the osteoclast from the osteogenic cell. It comes from the haemopoietic line, by fusion, and this is the single most common error on this comparison.

Assuming every osteoblast becomes an osteocyte. The book is specific: 10% to 20% of osteoblasts differentiate into osteocytes in lacunae, some flatten and cover the matrix as bone lining cells, and the majority undergo apoptosis.

Confusing the osteoclast with the megakaryocyte because both are large and multinucleated in appearance. The department book prints a comparison between the two precisely because students mix them up; the osteoclast sits in Howship's lacuna and the megakaryocyte does not.

## summary
A cardiac muscle fibre is not one long cell. It is many separate cells joined end to end, and the junction between them is the intercalated disc. Light microscopy shows it as a line; electron microscopy shows it is folded, with two components in two orientations doing two different jobs — mechanical anchorage across the fibre, electrical coupling along it. The 2025 end-of-year paper asked for both pictures in one 3-mark question.

## sections
### Definition
Cardiac muscle forms the middle layer of the heart, the myocardium, which is the main bulk of the heart wall and is thicker in the ventricles than in the atria. It is made of branched, interconnected muscle fibres, each surrounded by a delicate sheath of connective tissue endomysium rich in capillaries.

Each cardiac muscle fibre is made up of many separate cells, called cardiac myocytes, linked to each other. Intercalated discs represent the sites of junctions between the sarcolemma of adjacent cardiac muscle cells in the fibre.

By LM, intercalated discs are clear lines that appear transversely at intervals along the length of cardiac muscle fibre.

### Mechanism
The disc has to solve two problems at once, and it solves them in two different places.

The first problem is that the fibre pulls on itself. Cells joined end to end and contracting many times a minute, for a lifetime, will separate unless they are anchored. So the transverse component of the disc — the part that crosses transversely the cardiac muscle fibre, at right angles to the pull — carries the mechanical junctions. Desmosomes and adherent junctions (fascia adherens) are located in this region of the disc; they bind cardiac muscle cells firmly together to prevent their separation during repetitive contractions. The fascia adherens is where the thin filaments of the terminal sarcomere anchor, so the pull of contraction is handed on to the next cell rather than tearing the membrane.

The second problem is that the myocardium has to contract as one. That needs electrical continuity, and electrical continuity means gap junctions. But a gap junction is a delicate structure and the transverse face is where the force is. So the gap junctions are placed where the force is not: the lateral component of the disc lies parallel to muscle fibres, and gap junctions are located in this segment of the disc, allowing the contraction signals to pass from cell to other. Their position in the lateral parts of the disc protects them from the contraction forces.

That is the design in one sentence: strength across the pull, communication along it.

### Key determinants
**The light-microscopic picture.** Intercalated discs are clear lines that appear transversely, at intervals, along the length of the cardiac muscle fibre. They are one of the three features that identify cardiac muscle on a stained section, alongside branching fibres and a single central nucleus in each cell. The sarcoplasm by light microscopy is acidophilic and exhibits non-clear transverse striations — less distinct than in skeletal muscle — as well as the discs.

**The electron-microscopic picture.** The disc is formed of transverse and lateral regions. The transverse component crosses the fibre and carries desmosomes and adherent junctions, the fascia adherens. The lateral component lies parallel to the fibre and carries gap junctions.

**What the disc is not.** It is not a straight partition. The step between the transverse and lateral parts is why a disc drawn as a single flat line is wrong, and why the light-microscopic "line" is a simplification of something folded.

**The comparison the book draws.** Skeletal muscle has no cell junctions at all, because each fibre is a single cell. Smooth muscle has gap junctions but no discs. Only cardiac muscle has the intercalated disc, with fascia adherens, desmosome and gap junction together.

### Clinical significance
Cardiac muscle does not contain progenitor cells, so it cannot regenerate. When cardiac myocytes are injured, as in myocardial infarction, they heal by fibrous tissue. Fibrous tissue neither contracts nor conducts, so an infarcted area is lost twice over — and the gap junctions that would have carried the signal through it are gone with the cells.

The department book records myocardial infarction as complete obstruction of a coronary artery leading to cardiac muscle cell death, which releases proteins such as troponin-T and creatine kinase into the bloodstream; measuring these cardiac enzymes early in a patient presenting with chest pain is what allows early diagnosis and rapid treatment.

Purkinje muscle fibres are the exception that proves the design. They are highly specialised cardiac muscle fibres that transmit contractile impulses rapidly via gap junctions, faster than the ordinary myocardium — and they have no intercalated discs. A conducting fibre needs the coupling but not the end-to-end anchorage.

### Common misconceptions
Drawing the intercalated disc as one flat plate across the fibre. It has two components in two orientations, and putting the gap junctions in the transverse part removes the reason they survive contraction.

Treating a cardiac muscle fibre as a single multinucleated cell like a skeletal myofibre. It is several cells; that is exactly why it needs discs and why the nucleus is single and central rather than multiple and peripheral.

Expecting the discs to look like Z lines. Z lines are inside a sarcomere and are found in every striated muscle; intercalated discs are cell boundaries and are found only in cardiac muscle.

## summary
Smooth muscle is the easiest of the three muscle types to describe and the hardest to remember, because almost every feature is defined by what it does *not* have. The way to hold it is as a set of substitutions: caveolae instead of T-tubules, dense bodies instead of Z lines, irregularly arranged filaments instead of sarcomeres, calmodulin instead of troponin. The 2025 end-of-year paper asked for the electron-microscopic picture for 3 marks.

## sections
### Definition
Smooth muscle fibres are non-branched, spindle-shaped fibres; each fibre is a single cell with a wider central area and tapering ends. Cells are arranged so that their tapered parts lie adjacent to the broad parts of the neighbouring cells, so they form sheets — which is why, in transverse section, smooth muscle cells appear with variable diameter. They are the smallest of the three muscle cells, 8 µm in diameter, with a length that varies from 20 µm in small blood vessels to 500 µm in the pregnant uterus.

They lie in the walls of hollow viscera — digestive, urinary, genital and respiratory tracts — in the walls of blood vessels, and in the skin and the eye. Smooth muscle fibres are connected together by gap junctions; each fibre is surrounded by thin reticular fibres, the endomysium, and bundles are surrounded by a thin collagenous perimysium carrying vessels and nerves.

### Mechanism
Read the ultrastructure as four substitutions, each one the consequence of a cell that contracts slowly and does not need to contract in step with a nerve impulse arriving at one point.

**Caveolae in place of T-tubules.** The sarcolemma is thin and surrounded by a basal lamina. It shows no T-tubules; instead there are invaginations along the cell surface, called caveolae. There is no tubular system at all. The caveolae can control calcium release and muscle contraction. A skeletal fibre is large and needs T-tubules to carry depolarisation into its depth quickly; a smooth muscle cell is 8 µm across and slow, so surface invaginations are enough.

**Dense bodies in place of Z lines.** Actin filaments insert into sarcoplasmic and sarcolemma-associated dense bodies, which correspond to the Z line in striated muscle, and extend into the sarcoplasm to interact with myosin filaments. Because the dense bodies are scattered through the cytoplasm and anchored to the membrane rather than lined up in register, the filaments they hold run in many directions and the cell shortens as a whole rather than along one axis.

**Irregular filaments in place of sarcomeres.** Myofibrils, of thick myosin and thin actin filaments, are irregularly arranged. Therefore striations do not appear in smooth muscle fibres. The filaments are there; the register is not.

**Calmodulin in place of troponin.** There is no troponin; instead there is calmodulin protein.

One more feature ties the sheet together. Abundant intermediate desmin filaments also insert into the dense bodies, and the attachments of actin and intermediate filaments to the dense bodies transmit contractile force to adjacent smooth muscle cells. The cells pull on each other through the cytoskeleton, which is what lets a sheet of them narrow a lumen.

### Key determinants
This is the list the paper marks.

**Sarcolemma.** Thin, surrounded by basal lamina. No T-tubules. Caveolae along the cell surface instead. No tubular system. The caveolae control calcium release and contraction.

**Nucleus.** Single, oval and central. Its shape is like a corkscrew during contraction — a useful sign, because it means the section was taken from contracted muscle.

**Sarcoplasm.** Acidophilic. Contains numerous mitochondria, sarcoplasmic reticulum, free ribosomes, a small Golgi apparatus and glycogen granules, present mainly at the perinuclear region.

**Myofilaments.** Thick myosin and thin actin, irregularly arranged, so no striations.

**Dense bodies.** Sarcoplasmic and sarcolemma-associated; correspond to the Z line of striated muscle. Actin inserts into them and extends into the sarcoplasm to interact with myosin.

**Intermediate filaments.** Abundant desmin filaments, also inserting into the dense bodies, transmitting contractile force to adjacent cells.

**Regulatory protein.** No troponin; calmodulin instead.

### Clinical significance
Smooth muscle produces slow, sustained, involuntary contraction, modulated by autonomic innervation and hormones, and it can secrete connective tissue matrix and fibres as in the wall of a blood vessel — a muscle cell that is also a fibroblast when it needs to be.

Unlike cardiac muscle, it repairs. Smooth muscle can grow by hypertrophy and increase in number by hyperplasia, as in hypertensive arteries and the pregnant uterus, and regeneration after damage occurs by mitotic division; pericytes can repair the smooth muscle of blood vessels. The 25-fold length change between a small vessel and the pregnant uterus in the book's own figures is the same capacity seen from outside.

Oxytocin is a potent stimulator of smooth muscle contraction and plays an essential role in uterine contraction during parturition, which is why it is used to induce or enhance labour.

### Common misconceptions
Concluding that smooth muscle has no contractile filaments because it has no striations. It has both actin and myosin; they are arranged irregularly rather than in register, and that is the whole reason no bands appear.

Expecting a tubular system because the other two muscle types have one. Smooth muscle has none — not a triad, not a diad, nothing. Caveolae are surface invaginations, not a tubular system, and the book says so in the same sentence.

Reading a corkscrew nucleus as an abnormality. It is the normal appearance of the nucleus in a contracted cell.

Calling the dense bodies Z lines. They correspond to the Z line functionally, as the anchorage for actin, but they are scattered and membrane-associated rather than transverse plates, which is precisely why the cell is not striated.
