## title
Nerves and arteries of the pelvic wall and ureter
## subject
fnd
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Anatomy
## summary
The sacral plexus supplies the gluteal nerves (the inferior gluteal nerve, L5-S2, to gluteus maximus), while the femoral, obturator and lateral cutaneous nerve of the thigh come from the lumbar plexus. The internal pudendal artery is the main perineal artery and ends as the erectile arteries, including the deep artery of the penis. The ureter has a segmental supply, receiving branches in the abdomen from the renal and gonadal (testicular/ovarian) arteries.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the pelvic-wall neurovascular facts tested by the department [RPR] bank: the inferior gluteal nerve as a sacral-plexus branch, the deep artery of the penis as a terminal branch of the internal pudendal artery, and the gonadal contribution to the ureteric blood supply.
### Mechanism
The gluteal nerves leave the greater sciatic foramen to reach the buttock; the internal pudendal artery gives its erectile terminal branches in the perineum; and the ureter, crossing three regions, draws blood segmentally from whichever large vessel lies nearest.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: sacral- versus lumbar-plexus nerves; internal pudendal (perineal) versus aortic (median sacral) or inferior-mesenteric (superior rectal) arteries; and the multi-source segmental ureteric supply (renal, gonadal, vesical) versus vessels that give it no branch (superior mesenteric, external iliac).
### Clinical significance
These points underlie buttock-flap and perineal surgery (nerve and pudendal-artery preservation) and the vulnerability of the ureter's segmental blood supply during pelvic and abdominal operations.

ART-AND-207END-RPR-PROSTATE-MALE-URETHRA
## title
The prostate and the male urethra
## subject
androl
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Anatomy
## summary
The prostate is an inverted cone below the bladder: its base is continuous with the bladder neck and its apex rests on the sphincter urethrae. Its posterior surface, separated from the rectal ampulla by Denonvilliers' fascia, is palpable per rectum, while its fibromuscular anterior lobe (isthmus) is devoid of glands. The male urethra runs prostatic, membranous and spongy (penile) parts, the spongy part lying within the corpus spongiosum. After a vasectomy the ejaculate loses its sperm but keeps its seminal and prostatic fluids.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the prostate and male-urethra facts tested by the department [RPR] bank: the prostatic base–bladder-neck continuity, the posterior-surface rectal relation, the glandless anterior lobe, the spongy urethra within the corpus spongiosum, and the ejaculate composition after vasectomy.
### Mechanism
The gland's surgical relations follow from its position below the bladder and in front of the rectum; the urethral parts follow the course of the canal from bladder to meatus; and the post-vasectomy ejaculate follows from cutting the sperm conduit while leaving the accessory-gland ducts intact.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: base (bladder neck) versus apex (sphincter urethrae); posterior surface (rectum) versus anterior surface; fibromuscular anterior lobe versus glandular posterior/median lobes; spongy versus prostatic/membranous urethra; and sperm-free versus sperm-containing ejaculate.
### Clinical significance
These points underlie the digital rectal examination of the prostate, transurethral surgery, urethral catheterisation and rupture patterns, and the counselling of a man after vasectomy.

## title
Lymphatic drainage of the rectum and anal canal, and haemorrhoids
## subject
gi
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Anatomy
## summary
The pectinate line divides the anal canal into a visceral upper part and a somatic lower part, and this division governs both lymph drainage and haemorrhoid type. Below the line lymph goes to the superficial inguinal nodes; the rectum and the anal canal above the line drain to the inferior mesenteric and internal iliac nodes. External haemorrhoids are skin-covered varices of the inferior rectal veins below the line, whereas internal haemorrhoids are mucosa-covered varices of the superior rectal veins above it.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the rectum/anal-canal facts tested by the department [RPR] bank: the inguinal drainage of the anal canal below the pectinate line, the inferior-mesenteric/internal-iliac drainage of the rectum, and the nature of external haemorrhoids.
### Mechanism
Lymph and venous drainage both follow the pectinate-line watershed between the endodermal (visceral, portal) and ectodermal (somatic, systemic) territories of the anal canal, while rectal lymph follows the superior and middle rectal arteries.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: superficial inguinal (below the line) versus internal iliac/inferior mesenteric (rectum and above the line); and skin-covered inferior-rectal (external, painful) versus mucosa-covered superior-rectal (internal, painless) haemorrhoids.
### Clinical significance
The pectinate-line watershed explains the metastatic spread and the pain of anorectal disease and underlies the clinical distinction and management of internal versus external haemorrhoids.

ART-FND-207END-RPR-PERINEAL-POUCHES
## title
Contents of the superficial and deep perineal pouches
## subject
fnd
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Anatomy
## summary
The perineal membrane divides the urogenital perineum into a superficial and a deep pouch. The superficial pouch contains the ischiocavernosus, bulbospongiosus and superficial transverse perineal muscles, the crura and bulb of the erectile tissue and, in the female, the greater vestibular (Bartholin) glands. The deep pouch contains the deep transverse perineal muscle and sphincter urethrae with the internal pudendal vessels and dorsal nerve; in the male it also holds the bulbourethral glands.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the perineal-pouch content facts tested by the department [RPR] bank: the deep transverse perineal muscle as a deep-pouch content, the ischiocavernosus as a superficial-pouch muscle, and the female greater vestibular (Bartholin) glands as superficial-pouch structures.
### Mechanism
The perineal membrane is the shared floor of the deep pouch and roof of the superficial pouch; which layer a structure lies in follows from its relation to that membrane.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: superficial-pouch muscles (ischiocavernosus, bulbospongiosus, superficial transverse perinei) versus deep-pouch muscles (deep transverse perinei, sphincter urethrae); and the female Bartholin glands (superficial pouch) versus the male bulbourethral glands (deep pouch).
### Clinical significance
Pouch anatomy determines the spread of extravasated urine after a urethral rupture and the site of a Bartholin cyst or abscess.
