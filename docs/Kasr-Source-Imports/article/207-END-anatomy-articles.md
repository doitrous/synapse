ART-GYN-207END-FEMALE-REPRO-ANATOMY
## title
Anatomy of the female reproductive tract and its supports
## subject
gyn
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Anatomy
## summary
The female genital tract is slung across the pelvis by the broad ligament, whose mesometrium reaches the uterine body, mesosalpinx the tube and mesovarium the ovary. The uterus is normally anteverted and anteflexed, resting on the bladder, with peritoneum reflected over its fundus and body into the rectouterine pouch; downward descent after pelvic-floor injury is prolapse. The cervix pierces the anterior wall of the upper vagina and rests on the base of the bladder, while the anterior fibres of levator ani (pubovaginalis) act as a vaginal sphincter. Blood comes from the uterine artery, which crosses above and in front of the ureter, and from the ovarian artery off the abdominal aorta that also supplies the lateral tube. Developmentally the tubes, uterus, cervix and upper vagina are paramesonephric while the lower vagina is urogenital-sinus derived; failed fusion gives anomalies such as uterus bicornis unicollis.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the female pelvic anatomy facts tested by the Anatomy section (Q28-29, 32, 34, 37, 40, 44, 47, 49, 52, 54, 57); each linked concept carries the atomic claim and this overview ties them into one picture.
### Mechanism
Support and position follow the ligamentous slings of the broad ligament and pelvic floor; blood supply follows a dual uterine/ovarian anastomosis in the broad ligament; and the developmental origin (paramesonephric vs urogenital sinus) explains both the normal parts and the fusion anomalies.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: the ovarian artery supplies the lateral tube and arises from the aorta; peritoneum covers only the fundus/body of the uterus; the supravaginal cervix sits on the bladder base; the lower vagina is not paramesonephric; the mesometrium relates to the uterine body; the uterine artery crosses over the ureter; the uterus is anteverted-anteflexed; bicornis unicollis has one cervix; and the pubovaginalis sphincters the vagina.
### Clinical significance
These relations underlie hysterectomy hazards (ureter under the uterine artery), the reading of pelvic imaging, the classification of Müllerian anomalies, and the pathophysiology of uterine prolapse.

ART-REN-207END-LOWER-URINARY-ANATOMY
## title
Anatomy of the lower urinary tract and prostate
## subject
renal
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Anatomy
## summary
The bladder base carries the trigone, a smooth triangular area bounded superiorly by the interureteric ridge with its apex at the internal urethral orifice; classically the trigone is mesodermal (from the absorbed mesonephric ducts) while the rest of the bladder is endodermal. The bladder neck is the lowest, most fixed part, about 3-4 cm behind the lower border of the pubic symphysis, and behind the male bladder base lie the rectovesical pouch, ureters, ampullae of the vasa, seminal vesicles and Denonvilliers' fascia (not the posterior vaginal wall). The prostate's median lobe raises the uvula vesicae behind the internal urethral orifice, and the membranous urethra, passing through the sphincter urethrae, is the narrowest and least distensible part.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the lower urinary and prostatic anatomy facts tested by Q30, 33, 36, 46, 51 and 56; each linked concept carries the atomic claim and this overview ties them together.
### Mechanism
Structure follows the trigone-and-neck plan of the bladder base, the fixed retropubic position of the neck, and the sphincteric course of the membranous urethra; the median lobe of the prostate encroaches on the outflow at the uvula vesicae.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: the trigone is mesodermal and interureteric-ridge bounded; the bladder neck lies 3-4 cm behind the lower symphysis; the median lobe raises the uvula vesicae; the trigone is triangular, not rectangular or pale; the membranous urethra is narrowest and least distensible; and the posterior vaginal wall is not a relation of the male bladder base.
### Clinical significance
These features underlie cystoscopy landmarks, the outflow obstruction of median-lobe prostatic hyperplasia, the vulnerability of the membranous urethra to straddle injury, and the surgical planes behind the bladder base.

ART-GIT-207END-ANORECTAL-ANATOMY
## title
Anatomy of the rectum and anal canal
## subject
gi
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Anatomy
## summary
The rectum rests on the lower sacral and coccygeal vertebrae, is related posteriorly to the median sacral vessels and sympathetic chains, and has peritoneum over the front and sides of its upper third; it develops from the hindgut (dorsal cloaca), not the urogenital sinus, and drains its upper part to the inferior mesenteric (pre-aortic) nodes. The anal canal is closed by an internal sphincter (smooth muscle, autonomic, involuntary) whose lower border reaches the white line of Hilton, and an external sphincter (skeletal, voluntary). Failure of the anal membrane to rupture leaves an imperforate anus.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the anorectal anatomy facts tested by Q27, 31, 41, 50 and 55; each linked concept carries the atomic claim and this overview ties them together.
### Mechanism
Position and relations follow the sacral bed of the rectum and its peritoneal cover; sphincter function splits into an involuntary smooth internal sphincter and a voluntary striated external sphincter; and the developmental hindgut origin explains both lymph drainage and the anal-membrane anomaly.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: the median sacral artery is a posterior relation; the internal sphincter ends at the white line; the internal sphincter is the involuntary one; a persistent anal membrane gives imperforate anus; and the rectum is hindgut-derived, not urogenital-sinus derived.
### Clinical significance
These features underlie digital rectal examination landmarks, the intersphincteric plane in anal surgery, the classification of anorectal malformations, and the nodal spread of rectal cancer.

ART-END-207END-ENDOCRINE-GLAND-ANATOMY
## title
Surgical anatomy of the thyroid, parathyroid and pituitary glands
## subject
endo
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Anatomy
## summary
The thyroid lobe presents an anterolateral surface covered by the pretracheal fascia (false capsule) and the strap muscles, while its posteromedial surface holds the parathyroids, recurrent laryngeal nerve and inferior thyroid artery. The superior parathyroid gland develops from the 4th pharyngeal pouch and, migrating little, ends up above the 3rd-pouch inferior gland. The pituitary sits in the sella turcica, related laterally on each side to the cavernous sinus with its carotid artery and cranial nerves, below and in front to the sphenoidal air sinus, and above to the optic chiasma.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the endocrine-gland surgical anatomy facts tested by Q35, 38 and 43; each linked concept carries the atomic claim and this overview ties them together.
### Mechanism
Each gland's relations follow its fascial bed: the thyroid within the pretracheal fascia among the strap muscles, the parathyroids on its deep surface, and the pituitary walled by the cavernous sinuses and sphenoid.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: the anterolateral thyroid surface relates to the pretracheal fascia and strap muscles (not the prevertebral fascia or parathyroids); the superior parathyroid is 4th-pouch; and the pituitary is related laterally to the cavernous sinus (not the sphenoid sinus or orbit).
### Clinical significance
These relations underlie the risks of thyroid and parathyroid surgery (recurrent laryngeal nerve, gland identification), the trans-sphenoidal route to the pituitary, and the cranial-nerve signs of a laterally expanding pituitary tumour.

ART-FND-207END-PERINEUM-PELVIC-WALL-ANATOMY
## title
Anatomy of the perineum and pelvic wall
## subject
fnd
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Anatomy
## summary
The pelvic wall is supplied by the internal iliac artery, whose branches (iliolumbar, superior gluteal, uterine, internal pudendal) supply the pelvic viscera, walls and perineum; the inferior epigastric artery, by contrast, is an external-iliac branch. The internal pudendal artery runs with the pudendal nerve in Alcock's canal on the lateral wall of the ischiorectal fossa, giving the inferior rectal artery and the vessels of the erectile tissue. The perineal muscles of both pouches are supplied by the perineal branch of the pudendal nerve; the superficial pouch holds bulbospongiosus, ischiocavernosus and superficial transverse perinei, while the sphincter urethrae lies in the deep pouch. The perineal membrane is pierced by the urethra and the erectile-tissue vessels, but not by the pudendal nerve trunk.
## sections
### Definition
This article groups the perineum and pelvic-wall anatomy facts tested by Q39, 42, 45, 48, 53 and 58; each linked concept carries the atomic claim and this overview ties them together.
### Mechanism
Vessels and nerves follow the internal iliac / internal pudendal tree into the perineum via Alcock's canal; the perineal pouches partition the muscles into superficial and deep groups; and the perineal membrane forms the boundary that select structures pierce.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: the inferior epigastric is external-iliac, not internal-iliac; the perineal muscles take the perineal branch of the pudendal nerve; the inferior rectal artery is an internal pudendal branch; the sphincter urethrae is in the deep (not superficial) pouch; the pudendal nerve lies in the lateral wall of the ischiorectal fossa; and the pudendal nerve trunk does not pierce the perineal membrane.
### Clinical significance
These relations underlie the pudendal nerve block, the drainage of ischiorectal abscesses, the anatomy of perineal trauma and episiotomy, and the ligation of pelvic vessels.
