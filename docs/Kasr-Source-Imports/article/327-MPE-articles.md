## title
Patient safety: medical-error taxonomy and the regulatory framework
## subject
mul
## status
Draft
## owner

## topic
Medical Professionalism & Ethics
## summary
This article groups the medical-error and regulatory facts tested by the opening MCQs of the bank (the Egyptian Medical Syndicate section and the first part of the Medical errors section). It sets out the error taxonomy — an error of omission is a failure to do something indicated (unlabelled samples, a delayed diagnosis or a delayed indicated operation), while an error of commission is a wrong act actually carried out (overdose, wrong dose, wrong patient/side, a retained surgical towel). It covers the everyday error categories tested here: most laboratory errors are pre-analytical; using an outdated, prolonged operative technique that harms the patient is a surgical error carrying medical responsibility; prescribing without checking a known allergy is a medication (prescribing) error; and safe medication orders avoid error-prone abbreviations and decimals and use the standard metric system. It also frames the legal background, distinguishing general laws (the Penal Code) from the special laws of medical practice (Law 415/1954, Profession Ethics Regulations 238/2003).
## sections
### Definition
Medical errors are classified by whether the wrong thing was done (commission) or a right thing was not done (omission), and by the process step where they arise (laboratory, surgical, medication). The regulation of the profession is framed by general laws that apply to everyone and special laws written for medical practice.
### Mechanism
Omission errors are failures to act; commission errors are wrong acts performed. Laboratory errors cluster in the pre-analytical phase because it holds the most manual, human-dependent steps. Medication errors are reduced by unambiguous orders — no error-prone abbreviations or decimals, standardised metric dosing, and a check of known allergies at the prescribing step.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: a delayed diagnosis or delayed indicated operation is omission, whereas an overdose, wrong-patient/side surgery or retained swab is commission; most lab errors are pre-analytical (not analytical); harm from an outdated prolonged technique is a surgical error with responsibility (not an anaesthetic error and not "no responsibility"); and the Penal Code is a general law, whereas Law 415/1954 and Regulations 238/2003 are special laws.
### Clinical significance
Correctly classifying and preventing these errors underpins safe practice and medico-legal responsibility: unambiguous prescribing and metric dosing prevent medication errors, correct sample handling prevents laboratory errors, and applying current operative technique prevents avoidable surgical harm.
### Common misconceptions
See each linked concept's pitfalls field for the exact reversal each distractor exploits (omission mislabelled as commission, a special law called "general", or a prescribing error relabelled as professional misconduct).

## title
Patient safety: anaesthesia-related death and airway safety
## subject
mul
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Medical Professionalism & Ethics
## summary
This article groups the anaesthesia-safety facts tested by the later Medical errors MCQs. It explains vagal (parasympathetic) stimulation under anaesthesia — provoked by tracheal intubation at induction and, under light anaesthesia, by many stimuli (airway instrumentation, traction on viscera, even suturing) — which can cause reflex bradycardia or arrest. It covers the causes of death under anaesthesia (airway obstruction from bronchospasm, vomitus or a foreign body; hypoxia; anaesthetic-overdose depression; and reflex shock), noting that light anaesthesia provokes parasympathetic, not sympathetic, reflexes. It sets out death from a prolonged operation as surgical shock, and the pattern of death in dental surgery (airway events and vagal shock rather than surgical shock).
## sections
### Definition
This cluster concerns iatrogenic death and near-death events around anaesthesia and operative care: reflex (vagal) responses, airway compromise, and shock. Each linked concept states one atomic fact.
### Mechanism
Airway and surgical stimulation, exaggerated at light anaesthetic depth, drives the vagus to slow or stop the heart. The airway may be obstructed by bronchospasm, aspirated vomitus or a foreign body. A prolonged operation exhausts the patient into surgical shock, while dental procedures kill mainly through aspiration, an inhaled tooth or vagal shock.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: the reflex under light anaesthesia is parasympathetic (vagal), not sympathetic; airway obstruction has several causes (any of the above); prolongation kills through surgical shock; and surgical shock is the exception among dental-surgery causes of death, where airway and vagal mechanisms dominate.
### Clinical significance
Recognising these mechanisms guides safe anaesthesia: keep adequate depth and use vagolytic premedication to blunt reflexes, protect and clear the airway, and limit operative time to avoid surgical shock.
### Common misconceptions
See each linked concept's pitfalls field for the exact reversal each distractor exploits (sympathetic rather than parasympathetic reflex, surgical shock attributed to dental surgery, a single airway cause treated as complete).

# Item
## id
ART-MPE-327-MALPRACTICE
## title
Malpractice & medical responsibility: negligence, incompetence and consent
## subject
mul
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## topic
Medical Professionalism & Ethics
## summary
This article groups the medico-legal facts tested by the Malpractice & medical responsibility MCQs of the bank. It defines the core vocabulary: incompetence is a lack of reasonable skill or knowledge, negligence is an inadequacy of medical care (a careless failure to apply care one is capable of), and malpractice is the umbrella charge of professional wrongdoing that harms a patient. It sets out the elements every malpractice case must prove — a fault (negligence or incompetence) that actually caused patient harm — so that a fault without harm (a retained instrument causing no damage) or a bad outcome without fault (death from disease progression after proper surgery, an unsuccessful operation) is not actionable. It covers duty of care and consent: failure to obtain consent for a non-emergency surgical procedure is negligence, while failure to obtain consent in a genuine emergency, and good-faith emergency aid to a stranger with no pre-existing duty (street CPR), are not. It also gathers the applied facts of this section: a breach of duty by omission (failing to admit an at-risk head-trauma patient), the physician (not the hospital) as the target of a malpractice claim, the defence when a patient unilaterally ends care, doctors' obligations, and the clinical facts of an incompatible transfusion (negligence; jaundice; death from renal failure) and the pre-anaesthetic thorough examination.
## sections
### Definition
Medical responsibility distinguishes incompetence (inadequate skill/knowledge) from negligence (inadequate care) and from professional misconduct (an ethical/disciplinary offence); malpractice is the general charge when professional wrongdoing harms a patient.
### Mechanism
Liability turns on a duty of care that was breached and caused harm. Fault plus harm together make an actionable claim; either alone does not. A pre-existing doctor-patient relationship creates the duty, which is why emergency aid to a stranger, or care a patient unilaterally terminated, falls outside it.
### Key determinants
Discriminators tested here: incompetence = inadequate knowledge/skill, negligence = inadequate care; a bad outcome without fault (proper surgery, unsuccessful operation) is not malpractice; a fault without harm (retained instrument, no damage) is not actionable; consent is required for elective procedures but presumed in a genuine emergency; the malpractice claim is raised upon the physician.
### Clinical significance
Correctly classifying responsibility protects both patients and practitioners: it identifies which failures are actionable, frames the duty to obtain consent and to admit/observe at-risk patients, and underlies the safe-practice facts of transfusion and anaesthesia (confirm compatibility, examine before anaesthesia).
### Common misconceptions
See each linked concept's pitfalls field for the exact reversal each distractor exploits (incompetence confused with negligence, a bad outcome read as fault, an emergency treated like an elective procedure, or the hospital named instead of the physician).
