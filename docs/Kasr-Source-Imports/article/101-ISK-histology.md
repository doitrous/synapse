## summary
Three white cells carry specific granules, and the department book separates them on one variable only: what those granules stain with. Everything the exam asks — the differential count, the shape of the nucleus, and what the cytoplasm looks like on a stained film — follows from that one decision, and the granules, not the nucleus, are what finally settle a neutrophil against an eosinophil.
## sections
### Definition
Granular leucocytes are the white blood cells whose cytoplasm carries specific granules. They live only a few days, and the loss is made good continuously by new cells arriving from the blood stem cells of the bone marrow.

The three share a common plan. The nucleus is single but segmented and condensed, with heterochromatin at the periphery and euchromatin centrally. The cytoplasm holds few organelles — some Golgi, mitochondria and rough endoplasmic reticulum — and two populations of granules: non-specific granules, called azurophilic because they have an affinity for azure dye, which is oxidised methylene blue, and specific granules.

The classification of granular leucocytes is made on the staining affinity of the specific granules, and on nothing else. Granules that take neither dye strongly give the neutrophil; granules that take the acid dye eosin give the eosinophil; granules that take the basic dye give the basophil.

### Mechanism
Each cell's granules are its function, released in a defined order.

The neutrophil is the first line of non-specific defence. Toxins released by bacteria attract it, and it leaves the blood by migrating between the endothelial cells of the vessel wall — diapedesis — into the connective tissue, where it becomes a motile microphage throwing out pseudopodia. Having engulfed a bacterium, it discharges its granules in sequence. The specific, or secondary, granules go first: lactoferrin binds iron and is bacteriostatic, phagocytin is bactericidal, lysozyme destroys bacterial proteins, and collagenase destroys collagen to open a path for the cell to move through. The azurophil, or primary, granules follow, releasing myeloperoxidase and the lysing enzymes. Neutrophils that die in the process become pus cells. Pus raises the body temperature by stimulating the heat-regulating centre in the brain. The neutrophil also attracts monocytes to clean the site, stimulates the bone marrow to form more neutrophils, and secretes a trephone substance that helps wound healing.

The eosinophil does two jobs. It terminates an allergic reaction, secreting histaminase and sulphatase to destroy histamine and heparin, and phagocytosing antigen–antibody complexes. Against parasites it is cytotoxic: the basic protein of the granule core forms pores in the parasite's body, and the eosinophil-derived neurotoxin causes nervous dysfunction in it.

The basophil starts the reaction the eosinophil ends. Its heparin prevents clotting and promotes allergy. Its histamine causes vasodilatation with a sudden drop in blood pressure, which is anaphylaxis. Its eosinophil chemotactic factor brings the eosinophils in. Its leukotrienes cause bronchospasm and bronchial asthma. Its cell membrane carries receptors for IgE, and its phagocytic power is limited.

### Key determinants
The department book asks the comparison in three columns — differential count, shape of nucleus, and light-microscopic cytoplasmic granules — and adds the electron-microscopic picture.

The neutrophil is 60–70% of the white cells and 10–12 µm across. Its nucleus is dark and segmented into two to five lobes joined by thin chromatin threads, and because the number and arrangement of lobes vary the cell is called polymorphonuclear. In a proportion of female neutrophils a Barr body is visible. The cytoplasm holds numerous fine, pale specific granules and a few large purple azurophil granules. On electron microscopy it shows pseudopodia, few organelles, and more glycogen than its neighbours, because its energy comes from glycolysis.

The eosinophil is 1–4% of the white cells and 10–14 µm across. Its nucleus is bilobed and horse-shoe shaped, the two lobes connected by a thick chromatin thread. Its specific granules are large and acidophilic. On electron microscopy each specific granule is oval, with an electron-dense core of basic protein — the internum — and a less dense periphery, the externum, which contains histaminase, sulphatase and eosinophil-derived neurotoxin. Small azurophil granules are also present, and these are lysosomes.

The basophil is 0–1% of the white cells and 10–12 µm across. Its nucleus is irregular, segmented and S-shaped, and is usually obscured by the granules lying over it. The specific granules are coarse and basophilic, and stain metachromatically — purple where the dye is blue — with toluidine blue, because of the heparin they contain. On electron microscopy they are large, rounded and electron dense, and hold histamine, heparin, eosinophil chemotactic factor and leukotrienes.

The decision rule follows from this. A count settles the question first: at 60–70% against 1–4%, most granulocytes on a film are neutrophils. If the count is not available, the granules settle it — fine and pale against coarse and deeply eosinophilic — and the connecting thread helps, being thin in the neutrophil and thick in the eosinophil. The lobe count alone does not settle it.

### Normal values
Neutrophils are 60–70% of white cells, 10–12 µm in diameter, with a nucleus of two to five lobes. A Barr body is seen in 3–6% of female neutrophils. Neutrophilia is a neutrophil percentage above 75%, neutropenia below 60%.

Eosinophils are 1–4%, 10–14 µm in diameter. Eosinophilia is above 5%, eosinopenia below 1%.

Basophils are 0–1%, 10–12 µm in diameter. Basophilia is above 2%.

### Clinical significance
The three counts move in disease and the book names the causes it expects.

Neutrophilia accompanies acute pyogenic infection — acute tonsillitis, acute appendicitis, an abscess. Neutropenia is caused by typhoid fever, which the book names as the commonest cause, by tuberculosis, and by viral infections such as influenza.

Eosinophilia accompanies allergic disease — urticaria, eczema, bronchial asthma — and parasitic infection such as bilharziasis, which matters in Egypt. Eosinopenia follows cortisone treatment, which inhibits the release of eosinophils from the bone marrow.

Basophilia accompanies allergic disease, parasitic disease, viral infection such as chicken pox, and liver cirrhosis. The basophil's own secretions are what make anaphylaxis and asthma: histamine causing the vasodilatation and the sudden fall in blood pressure, leukotrienes causing the bronchospasm.

### Common misconceptions
The basophil and the mast cell are not the same cell, although both have metachromatic granules — because both contain histamine and heparin — and both carry surface receptors for IgE. The book separates them on life span, size, nuclear shape and phagocytic ability: the basophil lives a few days and is 10–12 µm, the mast cell lives weeks to months and is 20–30 µm.

The word azurophilic describes an affinity, not a colour that identifies a cell. All three granulocytes have azurophil granules; only the neutrophil's are large, few and dark enough to be a described feature, and in the eosinophil they are small and are lysosomes.
## hold_these
Neutrophils are 60–70% of the differential count and eosinophils 1–4%, so most granulocytes on a normal film are neutrophils.
The neutrophil nucleus has two to five lobes joined by thin chromatin threads; the eosinophil nucleus is bilobed and horse-shoe shaped with one thick thread.
Neutrophil specific granules are fine and pale, eosinophil specific granules coarse and deeply acidophilic.
On electron microscopy the eosinophil specific granule has an electron-dense core, the internum, of basic protein, and a less dense externum containing histaminase, sulphatase and eosinophil-derived neurotoxin.
Neutrophil azurophil granules are primary lysosomes containing myeloperoxidase; the specific granules carry collagenase, phagocytin, lysozyme and lactoferrin.
In phagocytosis the specific granules discharge before the azurophil granules.
Diapedesis is migration between the endothelial cells of a vessel, and it is what turns a circulating neutrophil into a tissue microphage.
Eosinophilia above 5% points to allergy or to a parasite such as bilharziasis.
## lose_the_mark
Counting lobes alone. A young neutrophil may be bilobed, and the granules, not the nucleus, are what settle it.
Giving the eosinophil count as 2–4%. The department book states 1–4%, and the book's figure is the one the paper marks against.
Writing that eosinophil granules are lysosomes. The specific granules are not; only the small azurophil granules of the eosinophil are.
Calling a basophil a mast cell because both stain metachromatically. They differ in life span, size, nuclear shape and phagocytic ability.
Releasing the azurophil granules first in the account of phagocytosis. The order is specific granules, then azurophil.
Attributing metachromasia to histamine. It is the heparin in the granule that produces it.
Saying eosinophils cause allergy. They terminate it, by destroying histamine and heparin and clearing antigen–antibody complexes.

## summary
A platelet is a fragment of cytoplasm with no nucleus, and the department book divides it into two zones that do two different things. The granules are all in the dark central granulomere. The machinery that holds the platelet's shape, changes that shape, and opens the granules to the outside is all in the pale peripheral hyalomere — which is why a zone that looks empty is the one the exam asks about.
## sections
### Definition
Platelets, or thrombocytes, are small oval non-nucleated fragments of cytoplasm that develop from the megakaryocyte. They are 2–4 µm in diameter and circulate at 200,000–400,000 per cubic millimetre.

On light microscopy each platelet shows two zones. The outer, pale, basophilic peripheral zone is the hyalomere. The central dark granular zone is the granulomere. The distinction is not cosmetic: the book assigns different contents and different jobs to each, and the whole of the platelet's behaviour follows from which zone a structure sits in.

The cell membrane carries a thick cell coat, and that coat is what allows platelets to aggregate with one another into the platelet plug that stops bleeding.

### Mechanism
The hyalomere holds two systems, a cytoskeleton and a set of membranous channels, and each performs one step of platelet function.

The cytoskeleton is microtubules and actin microfilaments. The microtubules maintain the cell's shape, holding the resting platelet in its flat discoid form. The actin microfilaments allow the activated platelet to change shape, and they aid clot retraction, drawing the edges of the clot together after it has formed.

The membranous channels are two. The open canalicular system is a set of invaginations of the cell membrane, continuous with the exterior, and it is the route by which serotonin is released — the serotonin causing vasoconstriction of the injured vessel. The dense tubular system does not open to the exterior and stores calcium.

The granulomere is the store. It contains a few mitochondria, ribosomes and glycogen supplying energy, and three types of granule. Alpha granules, also called specific granules, contain clotting factors and growth factors. Delta granules, also called dense granules, contain serotonin, ATP, ADP and calcium. Lambda granules are lysosomes, and they are used to remove the clot once the vessel has healed.

Read together, the sequence is: the microtubules hold the resting shape, the actin filaments change it when the platelet is activated, the open canalicular system releases the serotonin that constricts the injured vessel, the dense tubular system supplies the calcium, the thick cell coat lets the platelets stick to one another into a plug, the alpha granules deliver the clotting and growth factors, and the lambda granules clear the clot away afterwards.

### Key determinants
Which zone a structure sits in decides what it does, and this is the distinction the examiner is testing.

The hyalomere is peripheral, pale and basophilic, and contains no granules. It contains the microtubules, the actin microfilaments, the open canalicular system and the dense tubular system. It is the acting part of the platelet.

The granulomere is central, dark and granular. It contains the mitochondria, ribosomes and glycogen, and the alpha, delta and lambda granules. It is the stored part of the platelet.

An answer that puts the granules in the hyalomere, or the canalicular system in the granulomere, has lost the distinction the question is built on. The rule to hold is that the hyalomere is machinery and the granulomere is cargo.

### Normal values
Platelet count is 200,000–400,000 per cubic millimetre. Platelet diameter is 2–4 µm. There are three types of granule in the granulomere. Purpura is diagnosed when the platelet count falls below 50,000 per cubic millimetre.

### Clinical significance
Purpura, or thrombocytopenia, is a decreased number of platelets, and the book defines it as a count below 50,000 per cubic millimetre. It is characterised by a prolonged bleeding time and excessive bleeding after minor trauma.

Its causes fall into two groups. Production may be decreased, as in bone marrow depression. Breakdown may be increased, as in autoimmune disease.

The clinical picture follows directly from the mechanism above. Without enough platelets there is no adequate plug: too few thick cell coats to aggregate, too little serotonin released to constrict the vessel, and too few alpha granules delivering clotting factors to the site. The bleeding is prolonged rather than sudden, because the failure is in the first plug rather than in the vessel.
## hold_these
The hyalomere is the pale peripheral zone and the granulomere the central dark zone, and only the granulomere carries granules.
Microtubules maintain the platelet's shape; actin microfilaments change it on activation and aid clot retraction.
The open canalicular system is continuous with the cell surface and releases serotonin, which causes vasoconstriction of the injured vessel.
The dense tubular system stores calcium and does not open to the exterior.
Alpha granules carry clotting factors and growth factors, delta granules carry serotonin, ATP, ADP and calcium, and lambda granules are lysosomes that remove the clot after healing.
The thick cell coat on the platelet membrane is what allows aggregation into a platelet plug.
Platelet count is 200,000-400,000 per cubic millimetre and diameter 2-4 µm.
Platelets are non-nucleated cytoplasmic fragments derived from the megakaryocyte.
## lose_the_mark
Treating the hyalomere as inert because it looks empty on the film. The granules are in the granulomere, but the machinery that acts on them is in the hyalomere.
Putting the dense granules and the dense tubular system in the same place. The delta granules are granulomere, the dense tubular system is hyalomere.
Confusing the open canalicular system with the dense tubular system. One opens to the exterior and releases serotonin, the other is closed and stores calcium.
Writing that the platelet is a cell. It is a non-nucleated fragment of megakaryocyte cytoplasm.
Naming only two granule types. The book names three, and the lambda granule is the one most often dropped.
Giving purpura as any low count. The book's threshold is below 50,000 per cubic millimetre.

## summary
Connective tissue proper holds nine named cell types, seven resident and two transient, and the identification exam turns on telling apart the two that look most alike. The mast cell and the plasma cell are both large and both basophilic. Only one has metachromatic granules, and only one has a cart-wheel nucleus, and that pair of features is what an answer has to reach.
## sections
### Definition
Connective tissue links the epithelial, muscular and nervous tissues so that the body works as one integrated whole. It arises from the middle, mesodermal layer of the embryo, from which mesenchymal cells migrate throughout the body and give rise to every connective tissue cell.

Its plan is the opposite of an epithelium: widely separated cells with a large amount of intercellular matrix between them, and blood vessels, lymphatics and nerves running through it. It supports structurally, provides the medium across which metabolic waste, nutrients and oxygen pass between blood and body cells, stores fat, and defends the body through its phagocytic and immune cells.

Connective tissue is cells plus matrix, and the consistency of the matrix names the type: soft gives connective tissue proper, rubbery gives cartilage, solid gives bone, and fluid gives blood.

The cells of connective tissue proper divide into resident cells, which are stable and long-lived, and transient cells, which are short-lived. The resident cells are the undifferentiated mesenchymal cell, the pericyte, the fibroblast, the fat cell, the reticular cell, the mast cell and the macrophage. The transient cells are the plasma cell and the leucocytes. The book also offers a second classification, by function: undifferentiated cells, connective-tissue-forming cells, fat-containing cells, and cells responsible for immunity and defence.

### Mechanism
The mast cell is the cell this leaf is examined on, and its appearance is a direct consequence of what it holds.

It arises from the undifferentiated mesenchymal cell and lies in loose connective tissue around blood vessels and beneath the epithelium of the lung and the digestive tube — which is to say, at every surface where the outside world meets the inside. It is a large oval cell whose cytoplasm is filled with basophilic granules, and whose nucleus is central, spherical and pale. On electron microscopy it has a well developed Golgi, many mitochondria, few cisternae of rough endoplasmic reticulum, and electron-dense membrane-bound granules.

Those granules stain metachromatically with toluidine blue, appearing purple or red where the dye itself is blue. The metachromasia is produced by the heparin in the granule, so the stain is not an arbitrary trick — it is a chemical test that reports the granule's content.

The cell surface carries receptors for IgE, and binding of an antigen to that IgE is what discharges the granules. Four secretions follow. Heparin acts as an anticoagulant. Histamine causes vasodilatation and increased vascular permeability. Leukotrienes contract the smooth muscle of the bronchial tree, causing bronchial asthma. Eosinophil chemotactic factor attracts eosinophils to the allergic site, which is where this article joins the granular leucocytes.

The other resident cells are recognised on the same principle, appearance following content. The active fibroblast is branched with long thin processes, a deeply basophilic cytoplasm and a large pale oval nucleus with a prominent nucleolus, and on electron microscopy it is a protein-synthesising cell with well developed rough endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi, many mitochondria and a euchromatic nucleus; the inactive fibroblast, or fibrocyte, is a smaller spindle cell with few processes, paler cytoplasm, a smaller darker nucleus with more heterochromatin, and less of all three organelles. The unilocular fat cell stores its fat as a single large droplet that pushes cytoplasm and nucleus to the periphery, and because H&E dissolves the fat away it is left as the signet ring appearance, while Sudan III stains the fat orange. The macrophage is large and irregular, with pale basophilic cytoplasm and an eccentric dark kidney-shaped nucleus, and is demonstrated by vital stains such as trypan blue or India ink, which it phagocytoses.

### Key determinants
Three things identify a mast cell, and the exam asks for all three.

On light microscopy: a large oval cell, cytoplasm full of coarse basophilic granules, a central spherical pale nucleus, sitting in loose connective tissue around vessels or under an epithelium.

On special staining: metachromatic granules with toluidine blue, purple or red where the dye is blue, because of the heparin.

On electron microscopy: a well developed Golgi, many mitochondria, few rough endoplasmic reticulum cisternae, and electron-dense membrane-bound granules.

The discriminating comparison is with the plasma cell, which the book places on the same page. The plasma cell arises from the B lymphocyte, is numerous in lymphoid tissue, and is a large oval cell with deeply basophilic cytoplasm. But its basophilia comes from rough endoplasmic reticulum, not from granules, and beside the nucleus it shows a pale negative Golgi image. Its nucleus is eccentric, not central, and its dark heterochromatin alternates with lighter euchromatin to give the cart-wheel or clock-face appearance. On electron microscopy it is a protein-forming cell, rich in rough endoplasmic reticulum with a well developed Golgi, many mitochondria, a euchromatic nucleus and no secretory granules at all. It secretes antibodies. It is not metachromatic.

So: central pale nucleus with granules is the mast cell; eccentric cart-wheel nucleus with a negative Golgi image and no granules is the plasma cell. Toluidine blue separates them outright.

### Normal values
The unilocular adipose cell is 50–150 µm across, the largest cell described in this chapter. The book names seven resident cell types and two transient cell types in connective tissue proper, and four functional classes. Where the granular leucocyte chapter compares the mast cell with the basophil, it gives the mast cell as 20–30 µm against the basophil's 10–12 µm, and a life span of weeks to months against the basophil's few days.

### Clinical significance
The mast cell is the cell of allergy, and the book states the consequence in two grades.

In some people the immune system recognises harmless antigens — allergens — as foreign bodies. Binding of the allergen to IgE on the mast cell surface triggers the release of its secretions, and the result is an allergic, or hypersensitivity, reaction: erythema and itching of the skin, a runny nose, bronchospasm, and vomiting, diarrhoea and abdominal cramping. Each of those maps onto one secretion, and a student who has learnt the four secretions can derive the list rather than memorise it.

A massive discharge of mast cells causes anaphylactic shock, which is life-threatening, through severe vasodilatation and increased vascular permeability leading to severe hypotension.

The macrophage carries the other clinical weight in this chapter. It phagocytoses and destroys foreign particles, microorganisms and dead cells, fuses with its fellows into multinucleated foreign body giant cells to engulf particles too large for one cell, presents antigen to activate lymphocytes, and destroys old red cells in the liver and spleen.

### Common misconceptions
Basophilia of the cytoplasm is not one thing. In the mast cell it is granular and metachromatic, produced by heparin in membrane-bound granules. In the plasma cell it is diffuse and orthochromatic, produced by ribosomes on rough endoplasmic reticulum. Two cells can be described with the same word and be told apart in one stain.

The mast cell is a resident cell and the plasma cell a transient one, which is why the book lists them in different columns even though it compares them side by side.

## summary
The cytoplasm is sorted twice before anything is described: living organelles against non-living inclusions, then membranous organelles against non-membranous ones. The lysosome sits in the membranous group, and the examinable question about it is one of appearance — a primary lysosome and a secondary lysosome differ on electron microscopy by whether the enzymes inside have yet met anything to digest.
## sections
### Definition
Cytoplasm is formed of cytosol, organelles and inclusions. The cytosol is the fluid, carrying carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, minerals, ions and salts, RNA, metabolites, oxygen and carbon dioxide.

The first division is between organelles and inclusions. Organelles are living, permanent, essential and active, and have vital functions. Inclusions are non-living, temporary, usually not essential and inert, and are the result of the cell's activity.

The second division sorts the organelles by whether they have a limiting membrane. The membranous organelles are the plasma membrane, the mitochondria, the endoplasmic reticulum, the Golgi apparatus, the lysosomes and the peroxisomes. The non-membranous organelles are the ribosomes and the cytoskeleton.

A lysosome is a membranous organelle containing hydrolytic enzymes — proteases, sulfatases — responsible for intracytoplasmic digestion. It is abundant in phagocytic cells such as macrophages and neutrophils, and because it is small and unstained in routine preparations it requires histochemical stains to demonstrate.

### Mechanism
The lysosomal enzymes are made where every exported protein is made, and travel a fixed route. They are synthesised in the rough endoplasmic reticulum, carried by transfer vesicles to the Golgi apparatus, and released from the Golgi inside lysosomes.

A lysosome newly released from the Golgi is a primary lysosome. Its enzymes have not yet acted on anything, so its contents are uniform: on electron microscopy it appears as a homogeneous, moderately electron-dense vesicle.

A secondary lysosome is a primary lysosome that has fused with something. Because the something is now inside it, its content is heterogeneous, and the book names three kinds by what was fused with.

A heterolysosome is a primary lysosome plus a phagosome, and it digests solid particles taken in from outside the cell. A multivesicular body is a primary lysosome plus a pinocytic vesicle, and it digests fluid. An autolysosome is a primary lysosome plus an autophagic vesicle containing an endogenous substrate the cell has itself condemned, such as a worn-out mitochondrion.

Whatever cannot be digested is retained in a residual body. A residual body has two fates. It may be discharged from the cell by exocytosis, which the book calls cytostool. Or, in long-lived cells that do not divide — cardiac muscle and nerve cells — it accumulates over years as lipofuscin, the age pigment, which is then counted among the cell's inclusions rather than its organelles.

### Key determinants
Two features separate a primary from a secondary lysosome on electron microscopy, and both follow from the same fact: whether digestion has begun.

Density and uniformity. The primary lysosome is homogeneous and moderately electron dense, because nothing but enzyme is inside it. The secondary lysosome is heterogeneous, because it contains a substrate in some stage of breakdown.

Size and content. The primary lysosome is small. The secondary lysosome is larger, and what it contains identifies which of the three types it is — recognisable solid material for a heterolysosome, small vesicles within the larger vesicle for a multivesicular body, and a recognisable organelle such as a mitochondrion for an autolysosome. A residual body is the end state, holding material that has stopped changing.

The trap this sets is that electron density on its own does not identify a lysosome. Many granules are dense. The neutrophil's azurophil granule is dense and is a primary lysosome; the basophil's specific granule is dense and is not. Density says nothing about enzyme content, and what a body contains and what it has fused with are what the answer must name.

### Normal values
The book gives the following measurements in this chapter. The plasma membrane is 7.5–10 nm thick, and protein makes up about 50% of the total membrane mass. A liver cell may hold up to 2000 mitochondria. A Golgi stack is 3–10 saccules. A microtubule wall is 13 parallel protofilaments and the microtubule is 24 nm in diameter, a microfilament is 5–7 nm and an intermediate filament 8–10 nm. Three types of endocytosis are named, and three types of secondary lysosome.

### Clinical significance
The book states the consequences of losing each organelle's function, and the lysosome's is the most direct. A lack of lysosomal enzymes such as the sulfatases results in intracellular accumulation of sulfated compounds, which interfere with the normal function of nerve cells.

The other organelles follow the same logic. A defect in the mitochondrial enzymes means failure to produce the ATP every vital activity needs, and in muscle that presents as muscular weakness. A lack of peroxisomal enzymes affects the function of organs such as the liver. Lack of growth hormone receptors at the cell membrane of target cells produces a type of dwarfism even though the blood level of the hormone is normal. Cancer chemotherapy arrests proliferation in tumours by preventing microtubule formation. Identifying intermediate filament proteins by immunocytochemistry matters for the diagnosis and treatment of tumours, because it reveals the cell the tumour came from.

The book also names four ordinary jobs the lysosome does that are worth holding: digesting nutrients and phagocytosed bacteria and viruses, removing excess or non-functional organelles, carrying out postmortem autolysis, helping the sperm head penetrate the ovum at fertilisation, and activating thyroid hormone by breaking the hormone–protein bond.

### Common misconceptions
A residual body is not a fourth type of secondary lysosome. It is the end state of any of the three, defined by having stopped digesting rather than by what it fused with.

Peroxisomes are not small lysosomes. They bud off the rough endoplasmic reticulum, their enzymes are made on free ribosomes, and their oxidases perform beta-oxidation of long-chain fatty acids to produce heat rather than ATP, generating hydrogen peroxide that their catalase then splits into water and oxygen. Nothing about that is hydrolytic digestion.

The multivesicular body is easy to misfile because its name describes its appearance and not its origin. Its origin is fusion with a pinocytic vesicle, and its job is digesting fluid.

## summary
An epithelial cell is polarised, and the book takes its specialisations in three groups by surface: apical, lateral and basal. The apical group is where cilia sit, and the examinable point about a cilium is that it is a microtubular structure with a stated ancestry — a centriole becomes a basal body, the basal body migrates to the apex, and the shaft it grows has a countable number of microtubules that a microvillus does not have at all.
## sections
### Definition
Membranous specialisations of the epithelial cell are grouped by which surface they occupy.

Apical specialisations are the cilia, flagella, microvilli and stereocilia. The book teaches these in the cytology chapter and cross-refers to it here rather than repeating them, so the account below is drawn from that chapter of the same book.

Lateral specialisations are the cell junctions, of which the book recognises four: the tight or occluding junction, the adherens junction, the macula adherens or desmosome, and the gap junction or nexus.

Basal specialisations are the hemidesmosomes, the basement membrane and the basal infoldings.

A cilium itself is defined as a motile process with a microtubular core covered by plasmalemma. It has three parts: a basal body, a shaft — the axoneme — and rootlets.

### Mechanism
A cilium comes from a centriole. A centriole is a cylindrical structure built of microtubules, whose wall is nine bundles of three microtubules, so twenty-seven in all. In a cell that is going to become ciliated, the centrioles duplicate thousands of times, and the daughters migrate into the apical cytoplasm where each becomes the basal body of one cilium. The basal body therefore keeps the centriole's own arrangement: twenty-seven microtubules in nine triplets.

From the basal body the shaft grows. The shaft has a different arrangement: nine peripheral doublets around two central singlets, which is twenty microtubules, and this is what "9 + 2" names.

The rootlets are formed by the C microtubule of each triplet of the basal body growing down into the cytoplasm — nine microtubules — and they fix the basal body and the shaft in place, anchoring the cilium against the force of its own beat.

The cilium beats repeatedly by the bending of adjacent doublets against one another, and the beat moves secretions or particles across the surface in one direction. This is what the respiratory tract and the female genital tract use it for. A cilium can also be modified as a receptor, as in the rods and cones of the retina.

A flagellum has exactly the axonemal structure of a cilium but is far longer, and it is designed to move the cell itself rather than move material past it. In humans the one flagellum is the sperm tail.

The lateral junctions work by adhesive transmembrane proteins, and they differ in geometry and in what they anchor to. In the zonula occludens the two adjacent membranes fuse at points through transmembrane proteins on each cell, and the junction completely encircles the apex of the cell like a belt, restricting the passage of substances between adjacent cells. In the zonula adherens the two membranes are separated by a wide intercellular space, the extracellular parts of the transmembrane proteins are connected with the help of calcium ions, and on the cytoplasmic side condensed proteins bind them to actin filaments; it also encircles the cell like a belt, and it provides lateral adhesion. The macula adherens has the same wide space and the same calcium dependence, but its thickened cytoplasmic side is an attachment plaque made of several proteins that anchor intermediate filaments, and it does not encircle the cell — it is scattered as circular spots. The gap junction has a narrow gap bridged by channels, each channel formed of six symmetrical transmembrane protein molecules, and it allows ions and small molecules to pass between adjacent epithelial cells, and impulses between muscle cells.

The basal specialisations attach and filter. Hemidesmosomes take the shape of half a desmosome at the basal aspect of the basal cells, and fix the epithelium to the basement membrane and connective tissue below. Basal infoldings are invaginations of the basal cell membrane that increase surface area, with mitochondria arranged vertically between them supplying the energy for active transport, which is why they occur in ion-transporting cells such as those of the kidney tubules.

### Key determinants
For the cilium, the identification rests on the count and on the origin.

Basal body: twenty-seven microtubules in nine triplets, the same as a centriole, because that is what it was. Shaft: nine peripheral doublets plus two central singlets, twenty microtubules. Rootlets: nine microtubules, one from the C tubule of each basal-body triplet.

For the distinction that the exam sets, the comparison is with the microvillus. A microvillus is a finger-like projection shorter than a cilium, seen by light microscopy as an apical striated brush border. Its core is actin filaments inserted into a terminal web, not microtubules. It has no axoneme, no basal body and no rootlets, it does not beat, and its function is to increase surface area for absorption, as in the small intestine. A stereocilium is not a cilium at all despite the name: it is a long, non-motile microvillus with an actin core and no microtubules, and it helps absorption in the male genital system, in the epididymis.

For the junctions, three properties separate them: whether the junction encircles the cell or is a spot, what it anchors to inside the cell, and whether anything passes through it. The zonula occludens encircles and seals. The zonula adherens encircles and anchors actin. The macula adherens is a spot and anchors intermediate filaments, and it is the strongest of the junctions, which is why it is commonest in stratified squamous epithelium, where the surface is subjected to friction. The gap junction lets material through, which none of the other three does. Where the first three occur together between adjacent cells — as between the simple columnar cells lining the small intestine — the arrangement is called the junctional complex.

For the basement membrane, the two electron-microscopic layers are named by which tissue made them. The basal lamina is the epithelial component: an electron-dense sheet of type IV collagen granules and glycoproteins. The reticular lamina is the connective tissue component: type III collagen, which is the reticular fibres, and ground substance. Both are attached to the underlying connective tissue by structures including type VII collagen anchoring fibrils. On light microscopy the basement membrane is an amorphous dense layer next to the basal surface of the epithelium, seen as a red line with PAS or brown with silver, and it may be clear and thick, as in skin, or non-clear and thin, as in transitional epithelium.

### Normal values
The book states four types of lateral cell junction. A junctional complex is three of them together. Each gap junction channel is formed of six symmetrical transmembrane protein molecules. The basement membrane has two layers on electron microscopy. From the cytology chapter: the ciliary basal body is twenty-seven microtubules in nine triplets, the ciliary shaft is nine peripheral doublets plus two central singlets, giving twenty, and the rootlets are nine microtubules. A centriole wall is nine bundles of three, twenty-seven microtubules.

### Clinical significance
The book states the consequence of immotile cilia directly: when the cilia cannot move, secretions accumulate and bacterial infection develops on top of them, giving chronic respiratory infections. Because the flagellum has the same axonemal structure, immotile flagella cause male infertility — one structural defect producing two apparently unconnected clinical pictures, which is the reason the shared 9 + 2 arrangement is worth knowing.

The basement membrane's second function is clinical in the same way. It supports the epithelium and fixes it to connective tissue, and it controls the passage of molecules — in the capillaries of the kidney and in the lung alveoli, where that control is filtration and gas exchange.

The desmosome's clinical weight is in the tissues it holds together: it provides firm adhesion where surfaces are subjected to friction, which is why it is abundant between the cells of stratified squamous epithelium.

### Common misconceptions
Giving microvilli the cilium's answer is the commonest error on this material. A microvillus has an actin core and no axoneme, and it does not beat.

Stereocilia are the trap inside the trap. The name says cilium, the structure is a microvillus, and the book is explicit that they are not true cilia.

The basal body is not a different structure from the centriole. It is a centriole that has migrated apically, which is why its microtubule count is the centriole's and not the shaft's, and why the numbers 27 and 20 both belong to one cilium.
## hold_these
A cilium develops from a centriole that duplicates thousands of times, migrates to the apical cytoplasm, and becomes a basal body.
A cilium has three parts: basal body, shaft (axoneme) and rootlets.
The basal body has 27 microtubules in 9 triplets, the same arrangement as the centriole it came from.
The ciliary shaft has 9 peripheral doublets plus 2 central singlets, so 20 microtubules, which is the 9 + 2 arrangement.
The rootlets are 9 microtubules formed by the C microtubule of each basal-body triplet, and they fix the basal body and shaft.
A flagellum has exactly the axonemal structure of a cilium but is far longer, and in humans it is the sperm tail.
The four lateral junctions are zonula occludens, zonula adherens, macula adherens and gap junction, and the junctional complex is the first three together.
The desmosome anchors intermediate filaments to an attachment plaque, does not encircle the cell, and is the strongest junction.
The basement membrane has two EM layers: basal lamina of type IV collagen from the epithelium, and reticular lamina of type III collagen from the connective tissue.
Basal infoldings increase surface area with vertically arranged mitochondria between them, and occur in ion-transporting cells such as kidney tubules.
## lose_the_mark
Giving microvilli the same answer as cilia. A microvillus has an actin core and no axoneme, and does not beat.
Calling stereocilia cilia. They are long non-motile microvilli with an actin core and no microtubules.
Giving 9 + 2 for the basal body. The 9 + 2 is the shaft. The basal body is 9 triplets, 27 microtubules.
Saying the cilium arises from the Golgi or from the nucleus. It arises from a centriole that becomes a basal body.
Swapping the two adherens junctions. Zonula adherens is a belt anchoring actin, macula adherens is a spot anchoring intermediate filaments.
Putting four junctions in the junctional complex. The gap junction is not one of the three.
Naming the basal lamina as the connective tissue layer. The basal lamina is the epithelial component, the reticular lamina the connective tissue one.

## summary
Surface epithelium is classified twice: by the number of layers, and then, if there is more than one, by the shape of the cells at the surface. That second decision is the whole of the oesophagus-against-bladder comparison, because the oesophagus is named for a flattened surface cell that resists abrasion and the bladder for a dome-shaped one that flattens only when the organ fills.
## sections
### Definition
Epithelial tissue is named for what it does: it mostly covers the outer surfaces of the body or lines the surfaces of its cavities. Its cells are crowded, with minimal intercellular space. It has a free upper surface and a basal surface resting on a basement membrane, which may be clear or not clear. It is avascular, because blood and lymph vessels cannot penetrate between epithelial cells although nerves can, so nutrition reaches it by diffusion from the connective tissue underneath. And it has a high power of regeneration.

Epithelium may be ectodermal, as in skin, mesodermal, as in the serous membranes, or endodermal, as in the gastrointestinal tract.

The book divides epithelial tissue into four classes: surface epithelium, which covers surfaces or lines cavities; glandular epithelium, whose cells are modified to secrete; neuro-epithelium, whose cells are modified as receptors; and myo-epithelium, whose cells contract.

Surface epithelium is then classified by the number of layers into simple and stratified.

### Mechanism
The classification is driven by mechanical demand, and each type is the answer to a different demand.

Simple epithelium is one layer of cells all resting on the basement membrane, and it is subdivided by cell shape into simple squamous, simple cubical, simple columnar, simple columnar ciliated and pseudostratified columnar. Simple squamous epithelium is one layer of flat cells with flat nuclei, providing a smooth surface for the easy passage of fluids and the easy movement of organs, and a membrane thin enough for gas exchange. It takes a different name in each site: endothelium in blood vessels and heart, pneumocytes in the lung alveoli, mesothelium on the serous membranes — pleura, pericardium, peritoneum — and it lines Bowman's capsule in the kidney, where the demand is filtration. Simple cubical epithelium is one layer of cube-shaped cells with central rounded nuclei, secreting and reabsorbing in the secretory acini of glands such as the thyroid follicles and in the convoluted tubules of the kidney. Simple columnar epithelium is one layer of tall cells with basal oval nuclei, secreting and absorbing along the digestive tract. Adding cilia gives simple columnar ciliated epithelium, which transports fluid or particles across the surface in one direction: upwards in the bronchioles, and along the uterus and fallopian tube to move the ovum.

Pseudostratified columnar epithelium is actually simple. All its cells rest on the basement membrane, but some columnar cells do not reach the surface, and crowding places the nuclei at more than one level, giving a false appearance of stratification. It comes in three forms: non-ciliated, in the vas deferens and the membranous urethra; ciliated with motile cilia and goblet cells, which is the respiratory epithelium of nose, trachea and bronchi, the goblet cell being a mucous-secreting cell; and ciliated with non-motile cilia — stereocilia, which are long microvilli and not true cilia — in the epididymis.

Stratified epithelium is more than one layer, its main function is protection, and it is classified by the shape of its superficial cells into stratified squamous, transitional, stratified columnar and stratified cubical.

### Key determinants
The comparison the exam sets is between the oesophagus and the urinary bladder, and the book's own table compares them column by column.

The oesophagus is lined by stratified squamous non-keratinised epithelium. Its basement membrane is clear and wavy. Its basal layer is columnar cells with basal oval nuclei. Its intermediate layers are polyhedral cells with central rounded nuclei, joined by desmosomes and becoming smaller as they approach the surface. Its superficial layer is flat cells with flat nuclei. It is 5–30 layers thick. It is non-keratinised, meaning no keratin layer, which it shares with the oral cavity, cornea, anal canal, tip of the urethra and vagina; the keratinised form of the same epithelium is the epidermis of the skin. Its function is protection against the abrasion of a passing bolus.

The urinary bladder is lined by transitional epithelium, also called urothelium. Its basement membrane is non-clear and not wavy. Its basal layer is cuboidal cells with central rounded nuclei. Its intermediate cells are polyhedral, and in the full bladder they are pushed laterally, which is how the epithelium thins. Its superficial cells are dome-shaped with an upper convex surface, joined by junctional complexes, and their luminal surface is covered by rigid plaques that act as a barrier; in the full bladder these superficial cells become flat. It is 6–8 layers in the empty bladder and 3–4 layers in the full bladder. It lines the whole urinary tract — prostatic urethra, pelvis of the ureter, ureter and urinary bladder. Its function is distensibility, and protection against the cytotoxic effect of urine.

Three features, then, decide the answer: the basement membrane, clear and wavy against non-clear and flat; the shape of the superficial cell, flat against dome-shaped; and the layer count, which is fixed in the oesophagus and changes with filling in the bladder.

The two remaining stratified types are minor and the book treats them briefly. Stratified cubical epithelium is rare, usually two layers of cubical cells, as in the ducts of the sweat glands. Stratified columnar epithelium resembles stratified squamous but with few layers and columnar superficial cells: ciliated in the fetal oesophagus, which is rare, and non-ciliated at the recto-anal junction, in the large ducts of glands and in the penile urethra.

### Normal values
Stratified squamous epithelium is 5–30 layers. Transitional epithelium is 6–8 layers in the empty bladder and 3–4 layers in the full bladder. Stratified cubical epithelium is usually 2 layers. The book names five types of simple epithelium and four types of stratified epithelium, three embryonic origins of epithelium, and four classes of epithelial tissue.

### Clinical significance
Metaplasia is the transformation of one type of epithelium into another under certain abnormal conditions, and the book gives two examples that are both regionally important.

In heavy cigarette smokers the pseudostratified columnar ciliated epithelium lining the bronchi may transform into stratified squamous epithelium. The cost is exactly what the classification predicts: the new epithelium protects against abrasion but has no cilia, so the mucociliary escalator that cleared the airway stops working.

In bilharziasis the epithelium of the urinary bladder may change into stratified squamous epithelium. Here the loss is the dome-shaped superficial cell and its plaques, which were what made the bladder both distensible and impermeable to urine.

Both examples are worth carrying because they show the classification is not a taxonomy exercise. Each epithelium is fitted to a demand, and a change of epithelium is a change in what the organ can withstand.

### Common misconceptions
Calling urothelium stratified squamous because its surface cells flatten when the bladder is distended is the error the concept singles out. The flattening is the point. In the full bladder the superficial cells do become flat and the epithelium does thin to 3–4 layers, and it is still transitional epithelium, because what defines it is that the shape changes with filling.

Calling pseudostratified epithelium stratified is the mirror error. Every one of its cells reaches the basement membrane, so it is simple epithelium with a false appearance, and the book classifies it among the simple types.
