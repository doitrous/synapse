## label
Basal metabolic rate falls with age mainly because of the loss of metabolically active skeletal muscle mass
## canonical_key
bmr.decreases-with-age.loss-of-muscle-mass
## aliases
Basal metabolic rate and its determinants
bmr decreases with age loss of muscle mass
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Basal metabolic rate (BMR) is the resting energy expenditure measured awake, post-absorptive and thermally comfortable, expressed per unit body surface area (kcal/m2/hour). It declines with age chiefly because ageing reduces lean skeletal muscle mass, the main heat-producing tissue. Cold environments raise BMR (thyroid/shivering thermogenesis), so a low environmental temperature increases rather than decreases it.
## explicit_objective
State the determinants of basal metabolic rate and how age, environmental temperature and body surface area affect it.
## pitfalls
Assuming BMR is measured during sleep (it is measured awake at rest), that it falls in the cold (it rises), or that it is expressed per unit length rather than per unit surface area.

## label
Prolonged cold exposure increases thyroid hormone release, raising metabolic rate for sustained heat production
## canonical_key
thermoregulation.cold-exposure.thyroid-hormone-release
## aliases
Physiological responses to cold exposure
thermoregulation cold exposure thyroid hormone release
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Acute cold triggers cutaneous vasoconstriction and shivering (raising heat production ~4-5 fold), while sympathetic stimulation of brown fat uncouples oxidative phosphorylation to generate heat. If cold exposure is prolonged, the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis is activated and thyroid hormone secretion rises, increasing basal metabolic rate and non-shivering thermogenesis. This thyroid response is the slower, sustained adaptation to chronic cold.
## explicit_objective
Describe the acute and adaptive responses to cold, including sustained thyroid-driven thermogenesis and brown-fat uncoupling.
## pitfalls
Overstating shivering as a 10-fold increase, calling the cutaneous response vasodilation (it is vasoconstriction), or describing brown-fat metabolism as coupled (it is uncoupled).

## definition
The hypothalamus (pre-optic/anterior region) is the integrating center that defends a thermoregulatory set-point. Pyrogens (e.g. IL-1) induce hypothalamic prostaglandin E2, which raises the set-point so the body generates and conserves heat (shivering, vasoconstriction) until the new higher temperature is reached, producing fever. Naming the thalamus (rather than the hypothalamus) as the thermoregulatory center, or assuming that above the set-point heat production exceeds heat loss (heat loss dominates).

## arabic_label

## definition
Appetite is controlled by the lateral-hypothalamic feeding center and the ventromedial satiety center. Anorexigenic (satiety) signals include GLP-1 and peptide YY from intestinal L cells, and insulin from beta cells, all of which reduce food intake; the orexigenic hormone ghrelin is released from the gastric oxyntic mucosa during fasting to stimulate hunger. GLP-1 and insulin therefore decrease appetite, whereas ghrelin increases it.
## explicit_objective
Classify the main orexigenic and anorexigenic signals and locate the hypothalamic feeding and satiety centers.
## pitfalls
Swapping the hypothalamic centers (lateral = feeding, ventromedial = satiety), or calling peptide YY orexigenic or ghrelin a satiety signal.

## definition
The specific dynamic action (SDA, thermic effect of food) is the increase in metabolic rate that follows ingestion of a meal, due mainly to the metabolic processing (hepatic deamination and assimilation) of absorbed nutrients. Define the specific dynamic action of food and identify protein as producing the greatest thermic effect.
## pitfalls
Quoting the SDA of protein as 6% (it is about 30%), attributing SDA to the physical work of digestion, or thinking it lasts only about one hour.

## definition
The juxtaglomerular apparatus contains granular (juxtaglomerular) cells in the afferent arteriolar wall and macula densa cells in the distal tubule. Define tubular secretion and distinguish it from filtration, reabsorption and excretion.

Salicylate (aspirin) poisoning causes a high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis

## definition
Metabolic acidosis is a primary fall in bicarbonate; salicylate (aspirin) overdose causes a high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis by adding fixed acid and generating lactate and ketoacids through uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation. Salicylate also stimulates the respiratory center to add a respiratory alkalosis, giving a characteristic mixed picture, but the metabolic component is a genuine acidosis. In contrast, Cushing and Conn syndromes and persistent vomiting all cause metabolic alkalosis.
## explicit_objective
Distinguish causes of metabolic acidosis (salicylate poisoning) from causes of metabolic alkalosis.
## pitfalls
Selecting Cushing, Conn or vomiting; each of these causes metabolic alkalosis, whereas salicylate poisoning causes metabolic acidosis.
