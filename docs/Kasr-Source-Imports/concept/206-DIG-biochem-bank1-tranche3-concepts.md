# Item
## id
CON-GIT-22CE2817B0A695
## label
Lipoprotein lipase is insulin-induced and needs apo C-II; its deficiency causes type I hyperlipoproteinaemia
## canonical_key
lipoprotein.lipase.regulation-and-cofactors
## aliases
LPL apo C-II cofactor
lipoprotein lipase regulation
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Lipoprotein lipase is anchored to the luminal surface of capillary endothelial cells in extrahepatic tissues (adipose, muscle, lactating breast), where it hydrolyses the triacylglycerol of chylomicrons and VLDL. Its synthesis is increased by insulin (favouring fat storage after a meal), and heparin releases the enzyme from the endothelium into plasma. It requires apo C-II, supplied by circulating lipoproteins, as an activator. Deficiency of lipoprotein lipase (or of apo C-II) causes type I hyperlipoproteinaemia with fasting chylomicronaemia.
## explicit_objective
Recall the tissue location, insulin induction, apo C-II requirement and deficiency disease of lipoprotein lipase.
## pitfalls
Stating that insulin decreases lipoprotein lipase (it increases it), or forgetting that apo C-II is its obligatory activator.

## id
CON-GIT-0B7919101C9228
## label
Pancreatic lipase is a hydrolase that cleaves triacylglycerol ester bonds
## canonical_key
lipase.pancreatic.hydrolase-class
## aliases
pancreatic lipase enzyme class
lipase is a hydrolase
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Pancreatic lipase catalyses the hydrolysis of the ester bonds of dietary triacylglycerol, removing the fatty acids at positions 1 and 3 to yield 2-monoacylglycerol and two free fatty acids. Because it cleaves a bond by adding water, it belongs to the hydrolase class of enzymes (specifically an esterase). It is anchored at the lipid-water interface and activated by colipase in the presence of bile salts. It is neither a peptidase, a carboxylase nor a dehydrogenase.
## explicit_objective
Classify pancreatic lipase as a hydrolase acting on triacylglycerol ester bonds.
## pitfalls
Calling lipase a peptidase (acts on peptides), a carboxylase (adds CO2, needs biotin) or a dehydrogenase (redox).

CON-GIT-B187F5205ADFF6
## label
L-amino acids are absorbed from the intestine by sodium-coupled active transport
## canonical_key
aminoacids.intestinal-absorption.active-transport
## aliases
amino acid absorption mechanism
sodium-dependent amino acid uptake
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
The products of protein digestion, chiefly free L-amino acids, are absorbed across the enterocyte apical membrane mainly by secondary active transport coupled to the inward sodium gradient (maintained by the basolateral Na+/K+-ATPase), which allows uptake against a concentration gradient. Several sodium-dependent carrier systems exist for different amino-acid classes, and small di- and tri-peptides are also taken up by a proton-coupled peptide transporter and hydrolysed inside the cell. Passive diffusion and pinocytosis are not the principal adult mechanisms.
## explicit_objective
State that L-amino acids are absorbed by sodium-coupled active transport.
## pitfalls
Attributing amino-acid absorption to passive/facilitated diffusion, or to pinocytosis (a transient neonatal route for intact protein).

## label
Glutathione is synthesised from glutamate, cysteine and glycine
## canonical_key
glutathione.synthesis.glutamate-cysteine-glycine
## aliases
glutathione tripeptide constituents
gamma-glutamyl-cysteinyl-glycine
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Glutathione is the tripeptide gamma-glutamyl-cysteinyl-glycine, synthesised from the amino acids glutamate, cysteine and glycine by gamma-glutamylcysteine synthetase and glutathione synthetase. The glutamate is linked through its gamma-carboxyl (an unusual gamma-peptide bond), and cysteine contributes the reactive thiol that gives glutathione its antioxidant and conjugation functions. Serine is not a constituent and is not required for its synthesis.
## explicit_objective
State that glutathione is built from glutamate, cysteine and glycine (serine is not involved).
## pitfalls
Including serine among glutathione's amino acids, or forgetting that the glutamate link is a gamma-peptide bond.

## label
Phase I detoxification comprises oxidation, reduction and hydrolysis (including hydroxylation)
## canonical_key
detoxification.phase-one.reaction-types
## aliases
phase I biotransformation reactions
cytochrome P450 phase I
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Phase I of xenobiotic metabolism (biotransformation) introduces or unmasks a reactive polar group on the drug or toxin by oxidation, reduction or hydrolysis; oxidation, largely by the cytochrome P450 system, includes hydroxylation. This functionalisation prepares the molecule for phase II conjugation. Methylation belongs to phase II, not phase I.
## explicit_objective
List oxidation, reduction and hydrolysis (with hydroxylation) as phase I reactions.
## pitfalls
Placing conjugation reactions such as methylation or sulfation in phase I.

## label
Phase II detoxification comprises conjugation reactions (methylation, sulfation, glucuronidation, acetylation)
## canonical_key
detoxification.phase-two.conjugation-types
## aliases
phase II biotransformation reactions
conjugation reactions
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Phase II of xenobiotic metabolism conjugates the functional group produced in phase I with a polar endogenous molecule, greatly increasing water solubility for excretion. The conjugation reactions include glucuronidation, sulfation, methylation, acetylation, glutathione conjugation and amino-acid (e.g. glycine) conjugation. Hydroxylation is an oxidative phase I reaction, not a phase II conjugation.
## explicit_objective
List conjugation reactions (methylation, sulfation, glucuronidation, acetylation) as phase II, excluding hydroxylation.
## pitfalls
Classifying hydroxylation (a phase I oxidation) as a phase II reaction.
