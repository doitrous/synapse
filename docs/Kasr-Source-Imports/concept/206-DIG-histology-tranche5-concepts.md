## label
Masticatory oral mucosa (gingiva and hard palate) is covered by keratinized stratified squamous epithelium
## canonical_key
mucosa.oral-keratinized-masticatory.gingiva-hard-palate
## aliases
Keratinized regions of the oral mucosa
oral mucosa keratinized gingiva hard palate
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
The oral mucosa is masticatory over the gingiva and hard palate, where the friction of chewing demands a keratinized (or parakeratinized) stratified squamous epithelium firmly bound to bone. Elsewhere (cheeks, soft palate, floor of mouth, pharynx) it is lining mucosa with a non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium. A third specialised type covers the dorsum of the tongue.
## explicit_objective
Distinguish masticatory mucosa (gingiva, hard palate; keratinized) from lining mucosa (cheek, soft palate, pharynx; non-keratinized).
## pitfalls
Assuming the whole oral cavity is keratinized, or that mobile lining mucosa such as the cheek and soft palate is keratinized.

## label
Circumvallate lingual papillae are surrounded by a furrow into which the serous glands of von Ebner open
## canonical_key
papillae.circumvallate.von-ebner-serous-glands
## aliases
Lingual papillae and von Ebner's glands
circumvallate papillae von Ebner serous glands
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Circumvallate (vallate) papillae sit in a deep circular furrow whose walls carry numerous taste buds; the serous glands of von Ebner empty into the base of the furrow and wash it to dissolve tastants. Filiform papillae are keratinized, most numerous and taste-bud-free (mechanical); fungiform papillae are non-keratinized with a few apical taste buds; foliate papillae are leaf-like ridges on the sides of the tongue.
## explicit_objective
Match each lingual papilla to its epithelium, taste-bud content and (for circumvallate) the serous glands of von Ebner.
## pitfalls
Attributing taste buds to filiform papillae, keratinization to fungiform papillae, or von Ebner's glands to any papilla other than circumvallate.

Taste-bud sustentacular cells are the dark, peripheral supporting cells around the pale central gustatory cells

Cells of the taste bud

## arabic_label

## definition
A taste bud contains three cell types. Sustentacular (supporting) cells are elongated, dark, peripherally arranged cells that insulate and support the receptor cells. Gustatory (taste) cells are the pale, central receptor cells that synapse with sensory nerve fibres. Basal cells are the stem cells that regenerate both the gustatory and the sustentacular cells.
## explicit_objective
Identify the sustentacular (dark, peripheral, supporting), gustatory (pale, central, receptor) and basal (stem) cells of the taste bud.
## pitfalls
Swapping the dark/pale or peripheral/central descriptions of sustentacular versus gustatory cells, or thinking basal cells regenerate gustatory cells only.

## label
The oesophageal muscularis externa changes from striated (upper third) through mixed (middle third) to smooth muscle (lower third)
## canonical_key
oesophagus.muscularis-externa.middle-third-mixed-smooth-striated
## aliases
Histology of the oesophagus
oesophagus muscularis externa muscle gradient
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
The oesophagus is lined by non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium. Its muscularis externa is skeletal (striated) muscle in the upper third, a mixture of skeletal and smooth muscle in the middle third, and smooth muscle only in the lower third, matching the transition from voluntary to involuntary swallowing. Auerbach's (myenteric) plexus lies between the muscle layers; the outer coat is adventitia except for the short intra-abdominal segment, which has a serosa.
## explicit_objective
Describe the oesophageal wall: squamous mucosa, myenteric versus submucosal plexuses, the skeletal-to-smooth muscle gradient, and adventitia/serosa.
## pitfalls
Placing Auerbach's plexus in the submucosa, calling the mucosa columnar, or saying the serosa is acquired above (rather than below) the diaphragm.

## label
A peptic ulcer is a break (erosion) of the gastric mucosa, promoted by NSAID loss of protective prostaglandins
## canonical_key
ulcer.peptic-nsaid.gastric-mucosal-erosion
## aliases
Histology of a peptic ulcer
peptic ulcer NSAID gastric mucosal erosion
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
NSAIDs inhibit cyclo-oxygenase and lower the mucosal prostaglandins that maintain the gastric mucus-bicarbonate barrier, letting acid and pepsin injure the mucosa. The histological hallmark of a peptic ulcer is a focal break (erosion) of the mucosa that may extend through the muscularis mucosae into deeper layers, with loss of surface epithelium, inflammation and necrotic debris. It is defined by mucosal destruction, not by increased mucous secretion or neck-cell proliferation.
## explicit_objective
Relate NSAID-induced prostaglandin loss to the defining histology of a peptic ulcer: erosion of the gastric mucosa.
## pitfalls
Choosing increased goblet/mucous secretion or mucous-neck-cell proliferation instead of mucosal erosion as the defining feature.

## definition
Enteroendocrine (APUD) cells of the gut release their hormone product basally, into the underlying capillaries rather than into the lumen. Their secretory apparatus is therefore polarised downward, with an infranuclear (basal) Golgi complex and basally located dense-core secretory granules. ## explicit_objective
Explain that enteroendocrine cells secrete basally, giving an infranuclear Golgi and basal granules, unlike apically secreting mucosal cells.
## pitfalls
Attributing an infranuclear Golgi to apically secreting exocrine cells (chief, oxyntic, Paneth, goblet, enterocytes).

## label
Chief (peptic) cells are basal, basophilic, rough-ER-rich cells with apical electron-dense zymogen granules
## canonical_key
chiefcell.gastric-peptic.apical-zymogen-granules
## aliases
Peptic (chief) cells of the stomach
chief peptic cells zymogen granules pepsinogen
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Chief (peptic) cells lie at the base of the fundic/oxyntic gastric glands and secrete pepsinogen. As protein-secreting serous cells they have abundant basal rough endoplasmic reticulum (making the base basophilic) and store their product as apical, membrane-bound, electron-dense zymogen granules. They differ from parietal (oxyntic) cells, which are large, rounded and deeply acidophilic with abundant mitochondria and intracellular canaliculi.
## explicit_objective
Characterise chief (peptic) cells: basal location, basophilic rough-ER-rich cytoplasm and apical electron-dense zymogen granules.
## pitfalls
Giving chief cells acidophilic cytoplasm or abundant smooth ER (parietal-cell features), or placing them in the pyloric glands.

The pylorus shows deep gastric pits, branched coiled mucous glands, a less-folded mucosa and G cells

## definition
In the pyloric region the gastric pits (foveolae) are deep, reaching about half the mucosal thickness, and lead into short, branched, coiled mucus-secreting pyloric glands. The mucosa is comparatively less folded with a relatively narrow submucosa. The pyloric glands lack parietal (oxyntic) cells but contain gastrin-secreting G cells; the pyloric sphincter is a thickening of the circular muscle layer.
## explicit_objective
Recognise the pylorus by its deep gastric pits, branched mucous glands, less-folded mucosa and G cells.
## pitfalls
Describing pyloric pits as shallow, or claiming pyloric glands contain goblet or oxyntic cells.

## canonical_key

## definition
Goblet cells increase steadily in number from the duodenum (fewest) towards the distal large intestine (most). The large intestine, and the distal colon in particular, has the highest goblet-cell density because its role is lubricating mucus secretion to move increasingly solid faeces. ## explicit_objective
Recall that goblet-cell density rises from duodenum to distal colon, peaking in the large intestine.
## pitfalls
Choosing the duodenum or ileum, or a more proximal large-bowel segment, over the most distal colonic option.

The classic hepatic lobule is a hexagonal unit with a central vein, portal triads at its corners, outlined by thin CT septa

## definition
The classic hepatic lobule is a roughly hexagonal unit centred on a central vein, with portal tracts (triads of bile duct, portal-vein branch and hepatic-artery branch) at its corners and outlined by thin connective-tissue septa (well seen in the pig, fainter in man). Plates of hepatocytes are separated by blood sinusoids, while bile canaliculi run between adjacent hepatocytes within a plate. The portal lobule (bile-centred) and the liver acinus with its zones I-III (zone I best supplied, zone III least) are alternative functional units.
## explicit_objective
Distinguish the classic lobule (central vein, thin CT septa) from the portal lobule and the zones of the liver acinus.
## pitfalls
Saying hepatocyte plates are separated by bile canaliculi, that portal lobules drain into the central vein, or that acinar zone I is poorly supplied.

Kupffer cells are phagocytic macrophages within the hepatic sinusoids that clear the portal blood
## canonical_key

Von Kupffer cells of the liver
Kupffer cells sinusoidal macrophage phagocytic
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Kupffer cells are stellate resident macrophages of the mononuclear-phagocyte system that lie within the hepatic sinusoids, attached to or between the endothelial cells. As blood from the portal vein and hepatic artery flows through the sinusoid they phagocytose worn-out red cells, bacteria and debris, keeping the blood clear. Being active phagocytes they have an irregular surface with pseudopodia and lysosome-rich cytoplasm; they do not line the central vein and are not secretory.
## explicit_objective
Identify Kupffer cells as phagocytic sinusoidal macrophages that clear the portal blood within the liver sinusoids.
## pitfalls
Giving Kupffer cells a regular membrane, apical secretory granules, or a lining role in the central vein.
