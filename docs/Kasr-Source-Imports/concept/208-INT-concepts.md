## aliases

## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Suppurative inflammation is acute inflammation with substantial purulent exudate (pus) — neutrophils, pus cells, necrotic tissue and edema fluid — caused by pyogenic organisms. It splits into two patterns by the causative organism's own enzymes. Localized suppurative inflammation (abscess, boil/furuncle, carbuncle) is commonly caused by Staphylococcus aureus, which secretes coagulase; coagulase converts fibrinogen to fibrin and walls the pus off into a discrete cavity. Diffuse suppurative inflammation — cellulitis in loose tissues (subcutaneous tissue, orbit, scrotum), acute diffuse suppurative appendicitis, septic peritonitis — is commonly caused by Streptococcus pyogenes (haemolyticus), whose fibrinolysin breaks down fibrin (so nothing walls the pus off) and whose hyaluronidase is a spreading factor; its pus is thin and blood-stained because streptococcal toxins haemolyse red cells. Pseudomembranous inflammation is a distinct, severe pattern on mucous membranes: a necrotizing bacterial toxin (diphtheria bacilli in the throat/larynx, shigella in the colon causing bacillary dysentery) kills the surface epithelium, and the mucosa is covered by a false membrane of precipitated fibrin, necrotic epithelium and leukocytes — it is not simply a mild form of acute suppurative inflammation.
## explicit_objective
Distinguish localized from diffuse suppurative inflammation by causative organism and its virulence enzyme, and separate pseudomembranous inflammation as its own toxin-mediated mucosal pattern rather than a mild variant of suppuration.
## pitfalls
Calling pseudomembranous inflammation a 'mild form' of acute suppurative inflammation — it is a severe, toxin-mediated necrotizing pattern (diphtheria, bacillary dysentery), not suppurative at all in mechanism. Assigning coagulase to streptococci and fibrinolysin to staphylococci — it is the reverse: staphylococcal coagulase walls infection off (favouring an abscess), streptococcal fibrinolysin dissolves the wall and lets infection spread diffusely (favouring cellulitis).

Granulation tissue is proliferating capillaries and fibroblasts, moist red and easily bleeding, formed early in healing by fibrosis
## canonical_key

## arabic_label

## definition
Granulation tissue is the early stage of repair by fibrosis: it is formed of proliferating capillaries and fibroblasts (some transformed into myofibroblasts). The name derives from the granular appearance of the newly formed capillaries at the base of a healing skin ulcer. Granulation tissue is moist, red and bleeds easily — it is neither avascular nor dry. It forms through angiogenesis (new capillary sprouting from existing venules, driven mainly by VEGF and FGF) together with fibroblast migration and proliferation (driven mainly by PDGF, FGF-2 and TGF-beta, largely from macrophages) and deposition of extracellular matrix. As repair continues, granulation tissue matures: proliferating endothelial cells and fibroblasts decrease in number while collagen type I deposition increases, giving the eventual scar its tensile strength.
## explicit_objective
State what granulation tissue is composed of, what it looks like grossly, and how it differs from the mature scar it becomes.
## pitfalls
Describing granulation tissue as avascular or dry scar tissue — that description fits the late, remodelled scar, not granulation tissue itself, which is vascular, moist and red because it is still actively forming new capillaries.

Healing occurs by regeneration when injury affects only labile or minimally damaged stable parenchymal cells, and by fibrosis when it destroys the tissue framework or permanent cells; overdone or defective repair produces its own complications
## canonical_key
woundhealing.regeneration-vs-fibrosis.liver-cirrhosis-and-complications
## aliases
Regeneration vs fibrosis (healing)
Liver cirrhosis mechanism

## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Healing is replacement of injured tissue by new tissue, occurring by regeneration (same cell type, restoring normal structure) or fibrosis (scar formation). Regeneration follows damage to rapidly dividing labile cells (surface epithelium, hematopoietic cells) or minimal damage confined to the parenchymal cells of a stable organ such as the liver, which has excellent regenerative capacity despite replicating slowly. Fibrosis follows damage to permanent, non-dividing cells (cardiac muscle, nerve cells) or severe damage that destroys both the parenchymal cells and the supporting tissue framework — in the liver, injury confined to hepatocytes is followed by regeneration, but when chronic injury destroys both hepatocytes and the connective-tissue architecture, the liver cells regenerate as disorganised nodules surrounded by fibrous scarring: cirrhosis. Endometrial lining, being labile surface epithelium, also regenerates. Wound healing itself can produce its own complications distinct from the underlying disease: cosmetic deformity from extensive scarring, contracture (scar shortening limiting joint movement), keloid (genetically determined overproduction of fibroblasts and collagen after dermal injury — overdone repair), implantation epidermal cyst (dermoid cyst) from epidermal cells trapped in the dermis during regeneration, chronic ulcer/sinus/fistula from defective repair with persistent infection, and, rarely, carcinoma arising from the edge of a chronic ulcer or burn scar (Marjolin's ulcer).
## explicit_objective
State which tissue and injury pattern predicts regeneration versus fibrosis, explain why liver injury to the framework (not just hepatocytes) produces cirrhosis, and list the recognised complications of wound healing.
## pitfalls
Assuming any liver injury heals by regeneration because the liver famously regenerates — regeneration only restores normal architecture when the connective-tissue framework survives; once the framework itself is destroyed, regenerating hepatocyte nodules are walled off by fibrous scar (cirrhosis), which is fibrosis, not true regeneration. Listing cosmetic deformity as an exception to normal complications rather than recognising it, along with carcinoma, dermoid cyst and chronic sinus, as one of the genuinely recognised complications of wound healing.

## definition
Chronic inflammation is inflammation of prolonged duration (weeks to years) in which continuing inflammation, tissue injury and healing by fibrosis proceed together — arising from persistent infection (e.g. Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Treponema pallidum), immune-mediated/autoimmune disease, or prolonged exposure to toxic agents such as silica. Its pathologic hallmark is infiltration by mononuclear cells rather than neutrophils: blood monocytes migrate in and become macrophages (the key cell of chronic inflammation, activated by microbial products or T-cell cytokines to proliferate, persist, and secrete proteolytic enzymes and growth factors that drive both tissue damage and fibrosis), and B lymphocytes that transform into antibody-secreting plasma cells. T lymphocytes are also present, activated to secrete cytokines. There is minimal edema fluid compared with acute inflammation, and ongoing tissue destruction is matched by attempted healing through fibrosis.
## explicit_objective
Name the key cell types of chronic inflammation and state why macrophages, lymphocytes and plasma cells (not neutrophils) dominate the picture.
## pitfalls
Naming eosinophils or neutrophils as the defining cells of chronic inflammation — eosinophils are specific to parasitic/allergic settings and neutrophils are the hallmark of acute inflammation (though they can persist in 'acute on chronic' lesions); the key triad the department book names for chronic inflammation itself is lymphocytes, plasma cells and macrophages.

A sinus is a blind-ended tract from a deep focus to the surface; a fistula is a tract that connects two surfaces or hollow organs
## canonical_key
sinus.definition.blind-ended-tract-vs-fistula
## aliases
Sinus vs fistula
Abscess complications (sinus, fistula, ulcer)
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
A sinus is a blind-ended tract between a deep-seated focus (typically an abscess) and the body surface — for example a peritoneal abscess tracking to a sinus on the abdominal wall. A fistula is a double-ended tract communicating between two surfaces or hollow organs, formed when a deep abscess evacuates and its tract connects two epithelialised surfaces (an ano-rectal fistula complicating a perianal abscess is the worked example). Both are complications of evacuation and defective healing of a suppurative focus and, if the underlying infection persists, both become chronic. An ulcer, by contrast, is a local surface defect (skin or mucosa) from separation of inflammatory necrotic tissue — it has only one open end and does not tunnel to a deep focus, which is what distinguishes it from a sinus.
## explicit_objective
Distinguish a sinus (blind-ended, one open end) from a fistula (double-ended, connects two surfaces) and from an ulcer (surface defect, no tract).
## pitfalls
Confusing a sinus with a fistula by the number of open ends — a sinus has exactly one open end (blind internally), a fistula has two. Confusing either with an ulcer, which is a surface defect with no tract at all.

## definition
Serum and immunohistochemical tumor markers cannot make a definitive diagnosis of cancer on their own, but they help detect cancer and monitor treatment response or recurrence. By hormone: human chorionic gonadotrophin marks trophoblastic tumors, calcitonin marks medullary carcinoma of the thyroid. By oncofetal antigen: alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) marks hepatocellular carcinoma, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) marks carcinomas of colon, pancreas, lung, stomach and breast. By antigen: prostatic specific antigen (PSA) marks prostate cancer, immunoglobulins mark multiple myeloma. By mucin/glycoprotein: CA-125 marks ovarian cancer, CA-19-9 marks colon and pancreatic cancer. Separately, immunohistochemistry uses antibodies to identify the tissue of origin of poorly differentiated tumors: leucocyte common antigen (LCA) for lymphomas, cytokeratins for carcinomas, vimentin for sarcomas, and S100 protein for Schwann-cell tumors and malignant melanoma.
## explicit_objective

## pitfalls

## definition
Proto-oncogenes are normal cellular genes whose products drive cell proliferation; a gain-of-function change (point mutation, chromosomal translocation, or gene amplification) converts a proto-oncogene into a cancer-causing oncogene. The four main categories of oncogene the department book names are genes producing growth factors, genes producing growth factor receptors (e.g. erbB2/HER2), genes encoding signal transduction proteins (e.g. RAS), and genes encoding DNA transcription factors (e.g. MYC) — genes that stop cell proliferation are, by definition, not oncogenes. Tumor suppressor genes fall into two groups: 'governors', classic tumor suppressors whose products normally stop cell proliferation (retinoblastoma gene, RB — its mutation permits uncontrolled proliferation), and 'guardians', which sense genomic damage and arrest proliferation for DNA repair or trigger apoptosis if the damage cannot be repaired. TP53 is the prototypic guardian, the 'guardian of the genome' — its mutation does not directly transform cells (loss of guardian function has no direct effect on proliferation or apoptosis by itself), but it permits and accelerates the accumulation of further mutations in oncogenes and other tumor suppressor genes that do transform the cell.

## pitfalls
Calling TP53 a governor rather than the prototypic guardian — RB is the department book's worked governor example (stops proliferation directly), TP53 is the worked guardian example (senses damage, and its mutation only accelerates further mutation rather than directly driving proliferation). Listing 'genes which encode products that stop cell proliferation' as a category of oncogene — that description is the tumor suppressor gene's role, the functional opposite of what an oncogene does.

## definition

## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Leukocyte recruitment in acute inflammation follows an ordered sequence. First, slowed blood flow at the site of inflammation lets neutrophils move out of the normal central (axial) stream into the peripheral plasmatic zone near the endothelium — margination. Loosely adherent leukocytes then tumble along the activated endothelium, mediated by the selectin family of adhesion molecules — rolling. Firmer adhesion follows — pavementing — mediated by integrins on the leukocyte surface binding their endothelial ligands. Leukocytes then emigrate through the vessel wall, chiefly at venules, by squeezing between endothelial cells, driven by chemokines in the extravascular tissue; after crossing the endothelium they secrete collagenases to cross the basement membrane. Finally, having left the blood, leukocytes move toward the site of injury along a chemical gradient — chemotaxis, driven by bacterial products, chemokines, complement component C5a, and leukotriene B4. Sugammadex is a selective relaxant binding agent that forms a tight, inactive complex directly with the steroidal non-depolarizing neuromuscular blockers rocuronium and vecuronium. By encapsulating the blocker molecule itself, it lowers the free plasma concentration of the blocker, drawing more of it away from the neuromuscular junction into the circulation as the drug-complex, ultimately excreted in the urine. This is a fundamentally different, non-enzymatic mechanism from the acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (edrophonium, neostigmine, pyridostigmine), which instead work indirectly by raising acetylcholine to compete with the blocker, and it allows rapid reversal even of a deep block.
## explicit_objective
Distinguish sugammadex's direct encapsulation mechanism for reversing rocuronium/vecuronium block from the indirect, acetylcholinesterase-inhibitor mechanism of edrophonium, neostigmine and pyridostigmine.
## pitfalls
Grouping sugammadex with the anticholinesterase reversal agents -- it works by directly binding and encapsulating the blocker molecule, not by raising acetylcholine to out-compete it.
