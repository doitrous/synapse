# Item

CON-GIT-73ED6CB2341438
## label
The lower end of the oesophagus is a portosystemic anastomosis site, and its dilated collaterals (oesophageal varices) are the cause of life-threatening haematemesis in portal hypertension
## canonical_key
portosystemicanastomosis.sites.esophageal-varices-lower-oesophagus
## aliases
Oesophageal varices site
Portocaval anastomosis - oesophagus
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
At the lower end of the oesophagus, the oesophageal branches of the left gastric vein (portal system) anastomose with the oesophageal veins draining into the azygos system (systemic circulation). In portal hypertension, this submucosal plexus dilates into oesophageal varices, which are thin-walled and prone to rupture, producing the severe, often life-threatening haematemesis that is the clinically dominant portosystemic anastomosis in practice.
## explicit_objective
Name the lower oesophagus as the portosystemic anastomosis responsible for variceal haematemesis in portal hypertension, and distinguish it from the other three classic sites (anorectal, paraumbilical, retroperitoneal) by clinical consequence.
## pitfalls
Treating all four classic portosystemic anastomosis sites as clinically equivalent — the anorectal site produces haemorrhoids, the paraumbilical site produces caput medusae, and the bare-area/retroperitoneal (veins of Retzius) site produces asymptomatic collaterals, but only the oesophageal site routinely causes severe haematemesis, because its submucosal varices are thin-walled and exposed to swallowing trauma and acid reflux.

## label
The splenic artery runs a tortuous course along the superior border of the pancreas on its way to the spleen
## canonical_key
pancreas.relations.splenic-artery-superior-border
## aliases
Splenic artery course
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
The splenic artery, the largest of the three coeliac trunk branches, runs a markedly tortuous course from the coeliac trunk to the left along the superior border of the pancreas (forming part of the stomach bed posteriorly), giving off multiple small pancreatic branches, before dividing into terminal branches that enter the spleen at the hilum.
## explicit_objective
Identify the splenic artery as the vessel running along the superior border of the pancreas, distinguishing it from the splenic vein (posterior to the pancreas), the SMA (inferior to the pancreatic neck) and the portal vein (behind the pancreatic neck).
## pitfalls
Confusing the splenic artery's superior-border course with the splenic vein, which runs a separate, straighter course along the posterior surface of the pancreas before joining the SMV behind the pancreatic neck to form the portal vein — artery superior, vein posterior are two different relations to the same organ.

## label
A perforating ulcer on the posterior wall of the stomach spills its contents into the omental bursa (lesser sac)
## canonical_key
lessersac.boundaries.omental-bursa-posterior-gastric-perforation
## aliases
Lesser sac perforation
Omental bursa
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
The omental bursa (lesser sac) is the peritoneal space lying posterior to the stomach and lesser omentum, communicating with the greater sac only through the narrow epiploic (omental) foramen of Winslow. Because the posterior wall of the stomach forms the lesser sac's anterior wall, a perforation there spills gastric contents and blood directly into the omental bursa rather than into the general peritoneal cavity.
## explicit_objective
Localise a posterior gastric perforation to the omental bursa (lesser sac), distinguishing it from the greater-sac recesses (right subhepatic space, hepatorenal space/Morison's pouch, right subphrenic space) reached by an anterior perforation.
## pitfalls
Assuming any gastric perforation drains into the same greater-sac recesses regardless of site — only an anterior perforation spills directly into the greater sac (subphrenic/subhepatic spaces); a posterior perforation is contained within the lesser sac unless it later tracks through the epiploic foramen.

## label
The third (horizontal) part of the duodenum is crossed anteriorly by the superior mesenteric artery and vein
## canonical_key
duodenum.relations.third-part-crossed-by-sma
## aliases
Duodenum parts and relations
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
The duodenum has four parts. Only the first (superior) part is partly intraperitoneal (its proximal ~2.5 cm, the duodenal cap); the rest is retroperitoneal. The bile duct and main pancreatic duct open together at the major duodenal papilla on the second (descending) part, which lies anterior to the hilum of the right kidney, not the left. The third (horizontal) part is crossed anteriorly by the superior mesenteric artery and vein (and the root of the mesentery), a relation that matters clinically because SMA compression of this segment causes superior mesenteric artery syndrome.
## explicit_objective
State that the third part of the duodenum is crossed by the superior mesenteric vessels, and correct the paired distractors: only part 1 is intraperitoneal, the bile duct opens on part 2 (not part 3), and part 2 relates to the right kidney (not the left).
## pitfalls
Assuming the whole duodenum is retroperitoneal (only most of it is — the duodenal cap is intraperitoneal), or swapping the kidney side/part number for the bile duct opening and the kidney-hilum relation, both of which are classic single-word distractor swaps in this exam's own options.

## id
CON-GIT-5E64D4B77F0397
## label
The liver is a derivative of the embryonic foregut
## canonical_key
foregut.derivatives.liver
## aliases
Foregut derivatives
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
The foregut extends from the pharynx to the point midway along the second part of the duodenum (at the entry of the bile duct) and gives rise to the oesophagus, stomach, proximal duodenum, liver, gallbladder and biliary tree, and the pancreas. The liver specifically arises as the hepatic diverticulum budding from the foregut endoderm. The midgut (distal half of duodenum to the proximal two-thirds of the transverse colon — jejunum, ileum, caecum, appendix, ascending colon) and hindgut (distal third of transverse colon to the upper anal canal — descending colon, sigmoid colon, rectum) give rise to different, non-overlapping structures.
## explicit_objective
Place the liver correctly under foregut derivatives, and distinguish it from midgut derivatives (jejunum, caecum) and hindgut derivatives (descending colon, rectum).
## pitfalls
Assigning the jejunum to the foregut (it is midgut) or the descending colon/rectum to the foregut (they are hindgut) — the three primitive gut divisions are non-overlapping and each has its own dedicated arterial supply (coeliac trunk, SMA, IMA respectively), which is exactly why the derivative assignment matters clinically.

# Item
## id
CON-GIT-E67F3871315202
## label
The midgut extends from the entry of the bile duct into the duodenum to the junction of the proximal two-thirds and distal one-third of the transverse colon, and all of it drains venously into the superior mesenteric vein
## canonical_key
midgut.definition.ileocaecal-junction-and-smv-drainage
## aliases
Midgut boundaries
Midgut venous drainage
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
The midgut is the embryonic gut segment from the opening of the bile duct into the duodenum (mid-second part) to the junction of the proximal two-thirds and distal one-third of the transverse colon — not the ileocaecal junction, a common distractor since the caecum and appendix are themselves midgut structures well beyond the ileocaecal valve. Every midgut derivative drains venously into tributaries of the superior mesenteric vein, which joins the splenic vein behind the neck of the pancreas to form the portal vein. Within the midgut mesentery, the superior mesenteric artery lies to the left of the superior mesenteric vein (not the right), and the SMA itself arises from the abdominal aorta at the level of L1, not L3.
## explicit_objective
State the midgut's true distal boundary (the transverse-colon junction, not the ileocaecal valve) and its single venous outflow route (the SMV), and correct the SMA-to-SMV lateral relation and the SMA's vertebral level of origin.
## pitfalls
Stopping the midgut at the ileocaecal junction (it continues through the caecum, appendix, ascending colon and proximal two-thirds of transverse colon), placing the SMA to the right of the SMV (it is to the left), or confusing the SMA's L1 origin with the IMA's L3 origin — a paired distractor this exam uses across two consecutive questions.

## label
The inferior mesenteric artery arises from the abdominal aorta at the level of the third lumbar vertebra
## canonical_key
inferiormesentericartery.origin.l3-vertebra
## aliases
IMA origin
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
The inferior mesenteric artery, the artery of the hindgut, arises from the anterior surface of the abdominal aorta at the level of the third lumbar vertebra (L3), roughly 3-4 cm above the aortic bifurcation (which occurs at L4), and supplies the distal one-third of the transverse colon through to the upper anal canal. This is distinct from the coeliac trunk (T12) and the superior mesenteric artery (L1), giving the three unpaired ventral aortic branches a fixed, testable vertebral sequence.
## explicit_objective
State that the IMA arises at L3, and place it correctly in the coeliac trunk (T12) / SMA (L1) / IMA (L3) sequence of unpaired ventral aortic branches.
## pitfalls
Swapping the IMA's L3 origin with the SMA's L1 origin — this exam pairs the two facts across separate questions specifically to test whether the vertebral levels are memorised precisely rather than just 'the mesenteric arteries are somewhere in the lumbar region.'

## id
CON-GIT-BD707CA9C8C7B7
## label
The liver occupies a major portion of the right hypochondrium, and the fissures for the ligamentum teres and ligamentum venosum lie on different liver surfaces
## canonical_key
liver.topography.right-hypochondrium
## aliases
Liver surface anatomy
Liver topography
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
The liver occupies most of the right hypochondrium and extends across the epigastrium into the left hypochondrium; it is almost, but not completely, covered by the costal margin and diaphragm — a small epigastric area lies in direct contact with the anterior abdominal wall, so 'normally completely under the costal margin' overstates its cover. On the inferior (visceral) surface, the fissure for the ligamentum teres (round ligament) runs in the left sagittal fissure between the quadrate and left lobes; the fissure for the ligamentum venosum, by contrast, lies on the posterior surface of the liver, alongside the groove for the inferior vena cava, separating the caudate lobe from the left lobe.
## explicit_objective
State that the liver mainly occupies the right hypochondrium (with a small exposed epigastric area, not full costal cover), and correctly place the ligamentum teres fissure (inferior/visceral surface) versus the ligamentum venosum fissure (posterior surface).
## pitfalls
Swapping the ligamentum teres and ligamentum venosum fissures between the inferior and posterior surfaces — both lie in the same left sagittal plane dividing the liver's functional left lobe from the caudate/quadrate lobes, but on two different named surfaces, which is exactly the pairing this exam's distractors reverse.
