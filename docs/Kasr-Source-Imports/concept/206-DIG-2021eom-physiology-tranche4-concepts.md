## label
Thermoregulatory sweating begins at an ambient temperature of about 32-34 degrees C
## canonical_key
sweating.onset.ambient-temperature-32-34
## aliases
Ambient temperature at which sweating begins
sweating onset 32-34 C
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Sweating is an active, energy-requiring secretory process of the eccrine glands that becomes an effective avenue of heat loss once the environmental temperature rises to about 32-34 degrees C, the point beyond which radiation and convection can no longer dissipate the body's heat load. Eccrine sweat glands are driven by sympathetic cholinergic (not noradrenergic) fibres. Evaporation of the secreted sweat, which is impaired by high ambient humidity, then carries away heat.
## explicit_objective
State that thermoregulatory sweating begins at about 32-34 degrees C and is a cholinergically driven active process.
## pitfalls
Assuming sweat glands use noradrenergic sympathetic fibres (they are cholinergic), that sweating is passive, or that high humidity aids evaporation.

## label
The macula densa releases adenosine to constrict the afferent arteriole in tubuloglomerular feedback
## canonical_key
tubuloglomerular-feedback.macula-densa-adenosine.afferent-constriction
## aliases
macula densa adenosine afferent constriction
tubuloglomerular feedback mediator
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
When a rise in GFR delivers more NaCl to the macula densa, the increased NaCl uptake makes these cells release adenosine (together with ATP), which acts on A1 receptors of the afferent arteriolar smooth muscle to cause vasoconstriction. This lowers the glomerular hydrostatic pressure and returns GFR toward normal, the essence of tubuloglomerular feedback. Adenosine is the vasoconstrictor mediator here, distinct from renin (from the granular cells) and from efferent-acting angiotensin II.
## explicit_objective
Identify adenosine as the macula densa mediator constricting the afferent arteriole in tubuloglomerular feedback.
## pitfalls
Confusing the macula densa signal (adenosine, afferent constriction) with renin release or with angiotensin II’s efferent action; forgetting nitric oxide is a dilator that opposes it.

Filtration fraction is the ratio of GFR to renal plasma flow (about 0.2)
## canonical_key
filtration-fraction.definition.gfr-over-renal-plasma-flow
## aliases
filtration fraction definition

## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
The filtration fraction (FF) is defined as the glomerular filtration rate divided by the renal plasma flow (FF = GFR/RPF), normally about 0.2, so roughly one-fifth of the plasma reaching the glomeruli is filtered. It is a dimensionless ratio expressing filtration efficiency, not an absolute filtered volume. Changes in efferent arteriolar tone or plasma oncotic pressure alter the filtration fraction.
## explicit_objective
Define the filtration fraction as GFR divided by renal plasma flow (about 0.2).
## pitfalls
Equating the filtration fraction with GFR (an absolute volume), or computing it from whole blood rather than plasma.

## label
Increased afferent arteriolar tone lowers the GFR; normal net filtration pressure is about 10 mmHg
## canonical_key

## arabic_label

## definition
Glomerular filtration is driven by a net filtration pressure of about 10 mmHg, the balance of the glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure (about 60 mmHg) against the plasma colloid osmotic pressure (about 32 mmHg) and Bowman's capsule hydrostatic pressure (about 18 mmHg). Constricting the afferent arteriole reduces glomerular blood flow and capillary pressure, so it decreases GFR, whereas efferent constriction tends to raise it.
## explicit_objective
Recall the Starling forces of glomerular filtration and that afferent constriction lowers GFR.
## pitfalls
Believing afferent constriction raises GFR; mixing up the afferent (lowers GFR) and efferent (raises GFR) effects.

## label
The thick ascending limb of Henle is impermeable to water and reabsorbs NaCl (the diluting segment)
## canonical_key
loop-of-henle.thick-ascending-limb.impermeable-to-water
## aliases
thick ascending limb water impermeable
diluting segment loop of Henle
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
The thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle actively reabsorbs NaCl through the apical Na-K-2Cl cotransporter while being essentially impermeable to water. This separation of solute from water dilutes the tubular fluid and deposits solute in the medullary interstitium, making the limb the 'diluting segment' and the engine of the countercurrent multiplier. Other segments (the thin descending limb, and the ADH-sensitive collecting duct) are water-permeable.
## explicit_objective
Identify the thick ascending limb as the water-impermeable NaCl-reabsorbing diluting segment.
## pitfalls
Thinking the thick ascending limb is water-permeable, or confusing it with the water-permeable thin descending limb.

## label
ADH acts on collecting-duct principal cells to raise water permeability via aquaporin-2
## canonical_key
adh.collecting-duct-principal-cells.increases-water-permeability
## aliases
ADH principal cells aquaporin-2
vasopressin collecting duct water permeability
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Antidiuretic hormone (vasopressin), released from the posterior pituitary when plasma osmolality rises, binds V2 receptors on the basolateral membrane of collecting-duct principal cells and, through cyclic AMP, inserts aquaporin-2 channels into the apical membrane. This raises collecting-duct water permeability so water is reabsorbed down the medullary osmotic gradient and the urine is concentrated (up to about 1200 mOsmol/kg). Loss of this action produces dilute polyuria (diabetes insipidus).
## explicit_objective
Explain ADH's action on principal cells to concentrate urine via aquaporin-2.
## pitfalls
Placing ADH release in the anterior pituitary, thinking a rise (not fall) in osmolality inhibits it, or forgetting the medullary gradient rises toward the papilla.

## definition
Most hydrogen ion secretion in the proximal convoluted tubule is carried out by the apical Na+/H+ exchanger (NHE3), a secondary active antiporter. The inwardly directed sodium gradient maintained by the basolateral Na-K ATPase powers the extrusion of H+ into the lumen in exchange for Na+ entering the cell (counter-transport). Describe proximal H+ secretion as secondary active Na+/H+ counter-transport driven by the sodium gradient.

Calling it co-transport (same direction) or primary active transport; forgetting it is driven by the sodium gradient.

In the distal tubule and collecting duct, H+ secreted by the alpha-intercalated cells combines with ammonia (NH3) diffusing from the tubular cells to form ammonium (NH4+), which is trapped in the lumen and excreted. The respiratory system controls blood pH by adjusting arterial PCO2
## canonical_key

## definition
The lungs regulate acid-base balance by altering alveolar ventilation, which sets the arterial PCO2 and hence the carbonic-acid level of the blood: hyperventilation blows off CO2 and raises pH, while hypoventilation retains CO2 and lowers pH. This respiratory control is rapid (minutes) and potent, operating between the instantaneous chemical buffers and the slow but powerful renal (bicarbonate) regulation.
## explicit_objective
State that the respiratory system controls pH by adjusting arterial PCO2 through ventilation.
## pitfalls
Saying the lungs adjust bicarbonate (that is the kidney) or that respiration is the most rapid buffer (chemical buffers are instantaneous).

## label
Primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn's syndrome) causes metabolic alkalosis
## canonical_key
metabolic-alkalosis.cause.primary-hyperaldosteronism
## aliases
Conn syndrome metabolic alkalosis
hyperaldosteronism alkalosis
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
In primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn's syndrome) the excess aldosterone drives distal sodium reabsorption coupled to increased secretion of H+ and K+, so the urine loses acid while plasma bicarbonate is retained, producing a metabolic alkalosis. The accompanying hypokalaemia shifts H+ into cells and further enhances renal acid excretion, sustaining the alkalosis. This contrasts with acid-gaining states (renal failure, ketoacidosis, salicylate poisoning) that cause metabolic acidosis.
## explicit_objective
Identify hyperaldosteronism (Conn's) as a cause of metabolic alkalosis and contrast it with acid-gaining states.
## pitfalls
Grouping Conn's with the acidosis causes; forgetting the role of hypokalaemia in sustaining the alkalosis.
