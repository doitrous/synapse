# Item
## id
CON-GIT-D7513EB5719195
## label
The incretin effect: oral glucose releases GIP and produces more insulin than intravenous glucose
## canonical_key
incretin.effect.oral-glucose-more-insulin-than-iv
## aliases
incretin effect
oral versus intravenous glucose insulin
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
An oral glucose load elicits a substantially larger insulin response than the same amount of glucose given intravenously. This is the incretin effect: glucose in the gut lumen releases the incretin hormones — glucose-dependent insulinotropic peptide (GIP) from duodenal K cells and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) from ileal L cells — which potentiate glucose-stimulated insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells. Because the extra insulin promotes glucose uptake, the oral load is cleared from the blood faster than an intravenous load, which bypasses the gut and releases no incretin. The effect explains why the route of glucose administration changes the insulin and clearance response.
## explicit_objective
Explain the incretin effect and predict that oral glucose clears faster than intravenous glucose because it releases GIP.
## pitfalls
Attributing faster clearance to CCK or to glucagon; forgetting that intravenous glucose releases no incretin.

# Item
## id
CON-GIT-86E627FFC45163
## label
The cephalic phase of gastric secretion is mediated entirely by the vagus, so vagotomy abolishes it
## canonical_key
gastricsecretion.cephalicphase.vagus-mediated
## aliases
cephalic phase gastric secretion
vagal control of gastric acid
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
The cephalic phase accounts for roughly 30 percent of the acid secreted in response to a meal and is triggered by the sight, smell, taste and thought of food, and by chewing and swallowing. All of these signals reach the stomach through efferent fibres of the vagus nerve, which stimulate parietal cells directly and through gastrin and histamine release. Because the phase is entirely vagally mediated, cutting the vagus (vagotomy) completely abolishes it, whereas atropine or an H2 blocker only reduces acid output. This is why vagotomy was historically used to lower acid secretion in peptic ulcer disease.
## explicit_objective
State that the cephalic phase of gastric secretion is mediated entirely by the vagus, so vagotomy abolishes it.
## pitfalls
Assuming atropine or an H2 blocker can eliminate the cephalic phase; forgetting that sympathetic activity is inhibitory to secretion.

CON-GIT-091BB095D3C7D0
## label
Cholera toxin raises enterocyte cAMP and irreversibly opens chloride channels, causing secretory diarrhoea
## canonical_key
choleratoxin.enterocyte.camp-chloride-secretion
## aliases
cholera toxin mechanism
secretory diarrhoea cAMP chloride
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Cholera toxin irreversibly activates the stimulatory G protein (Gs) of adenylate cyclase in intestinal epithelial cells, producing a sustained rise in intracellular cyclic AMP. The elevated cAMP phosphorylates and irreversibly opens the apical chloride channel (CFTR), so chloride is secreted into the lumen; sodium follows to maintain electrical neutrality, and water follows the osmotic gradient. The result is a massive isotonic secretory diarrhoea and dehydration. This contrasts with the heat-stable E. coli enterotoxin, which acts through cGMP rather than cAMP.
## explicit_objective
Explain that cholera toxin raises enterocyte cAMP and irreversibly opens the chloride (CFTR) channel to drive secretory diarrhoea.
## pitfalls
Confusing the cAMP mechanism of cholera toxin with the cGMP mechanism of heat-stable E. coli toxin; thinking the toxin increases sodium or chloride absorption.

CON-GIT-637DA19715DC49
## label
Somatostatin is the paracrine brake on gastric acid; a fall in it after meal buffering permits acid secretion
## canonical_key
gastricsecretion.somatostatin.paracrine-acid-brake
## aliases
somatostatin gastric acid inhibition
antral D cell paracrine brake
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Antral D cells secrete somatostatin in response to a low luminal pH. Somatostatin acts in a paracrine manner to inhibit gastric acid secretion both directly, on the parietal cell, and indirectly, by suppressing gastrin release from neighbouring G cells. When the stomach is empty and acidic, somatostatin release is high and acid secretion is held down; when a meal buffers the acid and raises the pH, somatostatin release falls, removing the brake and allowing a secondary rise in acid secretion. Somatostatin therefore acts as the negative-feedback control that couples luminal pH to acid output.
## explicit_objective
Explain that somatostatin is the paracrine inhibitor of gastric acid, so a fall in somatostatin after meal buffering allows acid secretion to rise.
## pitfalls
Thinking a decrease in gastrin (a stimulant) could raise acid; forgetting that somatostatin acts both directly on parietal cells and indirectly via gastrin.

## label
Back-leak of hydrogen ions through a damaged gastric mucosal barrier is the forerunner of gastric ulcer
## canonical_key
gastricmucosalbarrier.hydrogen-backleak.ulcer-forerunner
## aliases
gastric mucosal barrier
hydrogen ion back-leak ulcer
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
The gastric mucosal barrier — surface mucus, tight junctions between epithelial cells, and rapid mucosal blood flow — normally prevents secreted hydrogen ions from leaking back into the mucosa. When agents such as aspirin, NSAIDs, bile salts, ethanol or Helicobacter pylori damage this barrier, hydrogen ions leak back into the mucosa. Intracellular buffers become saturated, cell pH falls, and injury with histamine release, capillary damage, focal ischaemia and cell death follows. This mucosal lesion is the forerunner of gastric ulcer formation, whereas mucus, tight junctions and adequate blood flow protect against it.
## explicit_objective
State that back-leak of hydrogen ions through a damaged gastric mucosal barrier is the forerunner of gastric ulcer formation.
## pitfalls
Mistaking protective features (mucus, tight junctions, proton-pump inhibition) for causes of ulceration.

CON-GIT-BB7556BB099567
## label
Gastrin has a trophic effect on the gastrointestinal mucosa, so reduced gastrin causes mucosal atrophy
## canonical_key
gastrin.trophic.mucosal-growth
## aliases
gastrin trophic effect
gastrin mucosal growth
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Besides stimulating gastric acid secretion, gastrin has an important trophic (growth-promoting) action on the mucosa of the stomach, small intestine and large intestine. Luminal nutrients — especially the products of protein digestion — release gastrin from antral G cells. When a patient is fed only intravenously, these luminal stimuli are absent, blood gastrin falls, and the loss of the trophic drive leads to atrophy of the gastrointestinal mucosa. Enteral nutrition, by restoring gastrin release, helps maintain mucosal mass.
## explicit_objective
State that gastrin is the main trophic hormone for the GI mucosa, so reduced gastrin during intravenous feeding causes mucosal atrophy.
## pitfalls
Attributing mucosal growth to CCK or secretin (which are trophic mainly to the pancreas) rather than to gastrin.
