## label
Phase 1 of the cardiac myocyte action potential is a transient potassium efflux and phase 2 a plateau that balances potassium efflux against calcium entry
## id
CON-CVS-818EC10C20A623
## canonical_key
cardiac-myocyte-action-potential.phase-1-and-2-ionic-basis
## definition
Phase 1 is the rapid small repolarization that follows the upstroke: transient outward potassium channels open and carry Ito, chloride enters, and the fast sodium channels inactivate. Phase 2 is the plateau, about 200 msec in a ventricular myocyte and held near zero millivolts by two opposing currents that cancel — a small outward potassium current through delayed rectifier channels, and an inward positive current made of calcium entering through long-lasting (L-type) channels together with the sodium-calcium exchanger, which moves one calcium ion out for three sodium ions in and so admits a net positive charge.
## explicit_objective
Name the ions and channels responsible for phase 1 and phase 2 of the non-pacemaker cardiac action potential, and say why the plateau stays flat.
## pitfalls
Reading the plateau as a period without current. It is two large opposing currents cancelling, which is why a small change in either potassium or calcium conductance moves the whole plateau.

## label
Rapid and reduced ejection differ in the pressure gradient and the rate of emptying, not in whether the semilunar valves are open
## id
CON-CVS-A70930DB23A5B4
## canonical_key
cardiac-cycle.rapid-versus-reduced-ejection-phase
## definition
Both phases of ventricular ejection run with the semilunar valves open. In rapid ejection the ventricle is still developing force: ventricular pressure rises to its peak, about 120 mmHg on the left and 25 mmHg on the right, arterial pressure rises with it, and ventricular volume falls steeply. In reduced ejection the gradient has reversed and ejection continues only on the momentum of the moving column: ventricular and arterial pressure both fall, and ventricular volume falls slowly to the end-systolic volume of about 60 ml. When the momentum is spent a small backflow produces the dicrotic notch and the valves close.
## explicit_objective
Set the two ejection phases side by side for semilunar valve state, ventricular pressure, ventricular volume and arterial pressure.
## pitfalls
Expecting ejection to stop the moment ventricular pressure falls below arterial pressure. It continues on momentum, and it is the loss of that momentum, not the crossing of the pressures, that ends it.

## label
Increased inotropy shifts the end-systolic pressure-volume relation up and to the left, so the loop starts at the same end-diastolic volume and ends at a lower end-systolic volume
## id
CON-CVS-C1D705743C07E3
## canonical_key
ventricular-pressure-volume-loop.effect-of-increased-inotropy
## definition
The end-systolic pressure-volume relation is the line on which the upper left corner of every loop lies, and its slope is the ventricle's contractility. A positive inotropic intervention shifts that line upwards and to the left. Contraction still begins at the same end-diastolic volume, so the loop is unchanged on its right-hand edge, but the ventricle now empties to a lower end-systolic volume; the loop is therefore wider and the stroke volume larger at the same preload.
## explicit_objective
Draw what increased inotropy does to the ventricular pressure-volume loop, and say which corner of the loop moves and which does not.
## pitfalls
Reading the wider loop as a preload effect. Preload moves the right-hand edge of the loop; inotropy moves the end-systolic relation and therefore the left-hand edge.

## label
Heart rate reserve runs from about 75/min to 220 minus age, and stroke volume reserve from about 70 ml to 200 ml
## id
CON-CVS-A99309543A270D
## canonical_key
cardiac-reserve.heart-rate-and-stroke-volume-reserve
## definition
Cardiac reserve is the maximum percentage by which cardiac output can rise above rest. Heart rate reserve is the span between the resting rate of about 75/min and the maximal rate, estimated as 220 minus age in years, about 200/min in a normal young adult. Stroke volume reserve is the span between about 70 ml at rest and up to 200 ml at maximal exercise, obtained by raising end-diastolic volume through Starling's mechanism and by lowering end-systolic volume through positive inotropy.
## explicit_objective
Give the range of each reserve and the mechanism by which stroke volume reserve is realised.
## pitfalls
Treating maximal heart rate as trainable. Training raises stroke volume reserve and cardiac mass; maximal rate is set by age and does not rise with training.

## label
Raising mean systemic filling pressure shifts the venous return curve up and to the right without changing its slope
## id
CON-CVS-B21C3D54DE291E
## canonical_key
venous-return-curve.effect-of-changing-mean-systemic-filling-pressure
## definition
Mean systemic filling pressure is the pressure throughout the systemic circulation when the heart has stopped and flow is zero, normally 6 to 8 mmHg, and it is the force that drives blood back to the right atrium. It reflects blood volume against the capacity of the circulation, which is mainly the capacity of the veins: it rises with increased blood volume, as in renal failure, or with reduced capacity, as in sympathetic venoconstriction, and falls with haemorrhage or with sympathetic withdrawal. On the venous return curve a rise in mean systemic filling pressure produces a parallel shift up and to the right, so at any right atrial pressure venous return is greater; a fall shifts the curve down and to the left.
## explicit_objective
Say what mean systemic filling pressure is, what changes it, and how a change in it moves the venous return curve.
## pitfalls
Confusing this with a change in resistance to venous return, which pivots the curve by changing its slope rather than shifting it in parallel.

Inspiration makes intrapleural pressure more negative, lowering right atrial pressure and widening the gradient for venous return
## id
CON-CVS-08B8764A5D501B
## canonical_key
venous-return.thoracic-pump
## definition
During inspiration intrapleural pressure falls from about -4 to -8 mmHg. That fall is transmitted to the intrathoracic great veins and the right atrium, so right atrial pressure falls, the gradient between the mean systemic filling pressure and the right atrium widens, and venous return increases. During expiration intrapleural pressure rises from about -8 back to -4 mmHg, right atrial and caval pressures rise with it, the gradient narrows and venous return decreases.
## explicit_objective
Explain how the respiratory swing in intrapleural pressure alters venous return in each phase of the breath.
## pitfalls
Describing it as the chest sucking blood in. It works by lowering the downstream pressure at the right atrium; the driving pressure is still the mean systemic filling pressure upstream.

Baroreceptor discharge rises and falls with arterial pressure and, through the nucleus of the tractus solitarius, opposes whichever change caused it
## id

## canonical_key
arterial-baroreceptor-reflex.response-to-a-rise-and-a-fall-in-arterial-pressure
## definition
Arterial baroreceptors are stretch receptors in the carotid sinus and the aortic arch, reporting to the nucleus of the tractus solitarius through the carotid sinus (Hering's) nerve and the aortic nerve. When arterial pressure rises their discharge increases: the vasomotor area is inhibited more, so sympathetic drive to heart and vessels falls, giving a lower heart rate, stroke volume and cardiac output with vasodilatation; and the cardiac inhibitory area is excited more, so vagal tone rises and the rate falls further. When arterial pressure falls the discharge decreases and every one of those changes reverses, raising the pressure back towards normal.

## pitfalls
Answering with the atrial volume receptors. Those are low-pressure receptors whose reflex is about volume, not arterial pressure — and that is the answer the solved copy of this paper actually gives.

## id

## canonical_key
respiratory-mechanics.causes-of-lung-recoil-and-chest-wall-expansion
## definition
The lung's inward recoil has two sources: its collagen and elastin fibres, which account for about one third, and the surface tension of the fluid lining the alveoli, which accounts for about two thirds. The chest wall's outward tendency comes from the elasticity of its muscles, tendons and ligaments. Each has a relaxation volume of its own, about 1 litre for the lungs and 5 litres for the thorax, and at the end of a quiet expiration their combined volume is about 2.3 litres — so the lungs are partially stretched and pulling in while the thorax is partially compressed and pushing out. These two opposing pulls are what keep intrapleural pressure negative.

## pitfalls
Giving elastic tissue alone. Surface tension is the larger of the two contributions, which is why surfactant matters so much to the work of breathing.

## label
Surfactant lowers alveolar surface tension, which eases inflation, prevents alveolar collapse and prevents pulmonary oedema
## id
CON-RES-4D4CBF3BB8AF1E
## canonical_key
pulmonary-surfactant.functions-and-causes-of-deficiency
## definition
Pulmonary surfactant is secreted by type II alveolar cells and lowers the surface tension of the alveolar lining fluid. That does three things: it facilitates lung expansion, so less effort is needed in inspiration; it prevents alveolar collapse in expiration, because as an alveolus shrinks its surfactant molecules crowd closer and lower the surface tension faster than the falling radius raises the collapsing pressure P = 2T/r; and it prevents pulmonary oedema, because surface tension itself favours filtration of fluid from blood into the alveoli. It is deficient in infant respiratory distress syndrome of prematurity, after long inhalation of 100% oxygen or use of a pump oxygenator in cardiac surgery, when a branch of the pulmonary artery is occluded, in cigarette smoking, in hypothyroidism, in hypocorticism and in hyperinsulinism.
## explicit_objective
Give the three functions of surfactant and at least four conditions in which it is deficient.
## pitfalls
Reading Laplace's law as making the small alveolus empty into the large one. Surfactant is more concentrated in the smaller alveolus, so its surface tension is lower and the law does not apply with a fixed T.

## id

haemoglobin-oxygen-dissociation-curve.factors-shifting-it-to-the-left
## definition
A leftward shift is a lower P50 and a higher affinity of haemoglobin for oxygen, so at any given PO2 more oxygen stays bound and less is delivered. It is produced by a decrease in temperature, a decrease in PCO2 and an increase in pH — the Bohr effect run backwards — by a decrease in 2,3-diphosphoglycerate, by carbon monoxide, which occupies the oxygen-binding sites with more than two hundred times the affinity of oxygen and holds the remaining oxygen more tightly, and by fetal haemoglobin, whose two gamma chains cannot combine with 2,3-DPG.

## pitfalls
Treating a left shift as good for the tissues. High affinity means the blood loads oxygen well and unloads it badly, which is exactly why carbon monoxide poisoning is dangerous at any measured saturation.
