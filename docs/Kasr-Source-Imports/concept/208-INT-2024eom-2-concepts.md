## label
Bethanechol and carbachol are direct-acting choline esters used for post-operative urinary retention and paralytic ileus
## canonical_key
bethanechol-carbachol.direct-agonist.urinary-gi-atony
## aliases

## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Bethanechol and carbachol are direct-acting choline-ester muscarinic agonists, resistant to both true and pseudocholinesterase enzymatic breakdown, which gives them a long enough duration of action for clinical use. Their clinical use is post-operative urinary retention and paralytic ileus (given orally or subcutaneously), stimulating M3 receptors on the detrusor and gut smooth muscle to restore contraction. Bronchial asthma and peptic ulcer are not indications: a muscarinic agonist would worsen bronchospasm, and cholinergic stimulation increases rather than treats gastric acid secretion.
## explicit_objective
Identify bethanechol/carbachol's clinical use as post-operative urinary retention and paralytic ileus, ruling out bronchial asthma and peptic ulcer as indications for a muscarinic agonist.
## pitfalls
Choosing myocardial infarction or bronchial asthma -- a muscarinic agonist has no role in either and would worsen bronchospasm.

## label
Pilocarpine's tertiary-amine lipid solubility lets it cross the blood-brain barrier and act as a first-line glaucoma miotic
## canonical_key
pilocarpine.lipid-solubility.bbb-and-miotic-use
## aliases

## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Pilocarpine is a natural tertiary-amine cholinomimetic alkaloid, and being lipid-soluble it crosses the blood-brain barrier -- unlike the quaternary choline esters (acetylcholine, methacholine) or the charged anticholinesterase neostigmine, none of which cross the BBB appreciably. Pilocarpine is a pure muscarinic agonist and a first-line miotic for glaucoma, contracting the ciliary/iris muscle to open the trabecular drainage angle and lower intraocular pressure. Acetylcholine and methacholine are unstable choline esters with no clinical miotic role (methacholine is instead used diagnostically for bronchial challenge testing), and neostigmine is an anticholinesterase, not a direct agonist, used for myasthenia and post-operative atony rather than as a miotic.
## explicit_objective
Identify pilocarpine, among acetylcholine/methacholine/neostigmine/pilocarpine, as the lipid-soluble, BBB-crossing parasympathomimetic used as a first-line glaucoma miotic.
## pitfalls
Confusing pilocarpine's lipid solubility with the charged, poorly-BBB-penetrant choline esters and anticholinesterases it is being contrasted against.

## label
Post-operative urinary retention must be checked for mechanical obstruction before treating it with a cholinergic agonist
## canonical_key
postop-urinary-retention.rule-out-obstruction-first
## aliases

## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Bethanechol's own clinical-use listing (post-operative urinary retention and paralytic ileus) is not selective to the urinary tract -- it stimulates M3 receptors on detrusor and gut smooth muscle alike, so calling it urinary-tract-selective is incorrect. Acetylcholine itself is not a viable treatment strategy: it is hydrolysed by acetylcholinesterase almost instantly at the synapse and has no useful systemic half-life as an infused drug. Before treating post-operative urinary retention with any cholinergic agonist or anticholinesterase, the safest strategy is to first exclude a mechanical bladder-outlet obstruction, because stimulating a detrusor that cannot empty against a fixed obstruction will not relieve retention and can raise intravesical pressure dangerously.
## explicit_objective
State that post-operative urinary retention must be checked for mechanical obstruction before cholinergic-agonist treatment, and that neither bethanechol nor acetylcholine infusion is a substitute for that first step.
## pitfalls
Assuming bethanechol's urinary-retention listing means it is urinary-tract-selective -- it acts on gut smooth muscle too, and is used for paralytic ileus by the same non-selective mechanism.

## label
Atropine remains the drug of choice over its substitutes specifically for organophosphorus poisoning
## canonical_key
atropine.preferred-over-substitutes.organophosphorus-poisoning
## aliases

## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Atropine's own substitutes are preferred over it in most of its classic uses: tropicamide and cyclopentolate are better than atropine for fundus examination (short-acting, less cycloplegic), ipratropium and tiotropium are the inhaled substitutes used for bronchial asthma and COPD to minimise systemic effects, and M3-selective antimuscarinics (oxybutynin, tolterodine, solifenacin) are preferred for urinary incontinence/overactive bladder. Organophosphorus poisoning is the one setting where atropine itself, not a substitute, remains the drug of choice: its competitive muscarinic blockade directly reverses the life-threatening muscarinic excess (bradycardia, bronchospasm, secretions) of the poisoning, titrated to endpoints such as heart rate above 80 and a clear chest. No listed substitute offers atropine's combination of potency, titratability and established antidotal role in this emergency.
## explicit_objective
Identify organophosphorus poisoning, not fundus exam, asthma or urinary incontinence, as the setting where atropine itself (not one of its substitutes) is preferred.
## pitfalls
Forgetting that atropine has largely been superseded by better-tolerated substitutes in its other classic uses -- poisoning is the exception, not the rule.

## id
CON-FND-94222C01365240
## label
Atropine's muscarinic blockade in organophosphorus poisoning does not block the nicotinic skeletal-muscle effects of the poisoning
## canonical_key
atropine.muscarinic-selectivity.does-not-block-nicotinic-effects
## aliases

## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Organophosphorus poisoning produces both muscarinic signs (bronchoconstriction/bronchospasm, increased lacrimal/salivary/mucus secretion, bradycardia, diarrhoea) and nicotinic signs (muscle fasciculations, initial tachycardia, and eventually skeletal-muscle weakness/paralysis from nicotinic receptor overstimulation at the neuromuscular junction). Atropine acts by competitive muscarinic receptor blockade only, with no effect on nicotinic receptors, so it reverses the muscarinic picture -- blocking bronchoconstriction, secretions and bradycardia -- but does nothing for the nicotinic, neuromuscular-junction effects. Skeletal muscle overstimulation progressing to paralysis is therefore the one listed effect atropine will NOT block; managing it requires an oxime (pralidoxime) to reactivate acetylcholinesterase before the phosphorylated enzyme 'ages'.
## explicit_objective
Identify skeletal-muscle overstimulation/paralysis as the nicotinic effect of organophosphorus poisoning that atropine's purely muscarinic blockade will not reverse.
## pitfalls
Assuming atropine, because it treats organophosphorus poisoning, blocks every effect of the poisoning -- it is muscarinic-selective and leaves the nicotinic (neuromuscular) effects untouched.

## label
An antimuscarinic such as atropine or glycopyrrolate is co-administered with neostigmine to prevent bradycardia from unwanted muscarinic effects
## canonical_key
neostigmine.co-administered-antimuscarinic.prevents-bradycardia
## aliases

## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors such as neostigmine, used in myasthenia gravis to raise acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction, inevitably also raise acetylcholine at muscarinic sites (heart, glands, smooth muscle), risking bradycardia and excess secretions. An antimuscarinic -- atropine or glycopyrrolate -- is therefore given alongside neostigmine specifically to block these unwanted muscarinic effects while leaving neostigmine's therapeutic nicotinic action at the neuromuscular junction untouched. Ephedrine and caffeine are CNS/sympathomimetic stimulants with no antimuscarinic action and no established role in this combination, and azathioprine is an immunosuppressant sometimes used as myasthenia gravis maintenance therapy, not an antidote to neostigmine's muscarinic side effects.
## explicit_objective
Identify atropine as the antimuscarinic co-administered with neostigmine in myasthenia gravis to prevent bradycardia and other unwanted muscarinic effects.
## pitfalls
Confusing azathioprine's role as a disease-modifying immunosuppressant in myasthenia gravis with an acute antidote to neostigmine's muscarinic side effects -- they solve different problems.

## label
Pseudoephedrine is the classic orally-given nasal decongestant, unlike the topical imidazoline decongestants and adrenaline
## canonical_key
pseudoephedrine.oral-route.nasal-decongestant
## aliases

## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Nasal decongestants act as sympathomimetic vasoconstrictors of the nasal mucosa; long-term use of any of them causes rhinitis medicamentosa (rebound congestion), and systemically-absorbed decongestants carry systemic side effects such as hypertension and infant CNS depression. Adrenaline (epinephrine), a catecholamine, is ineffective given orally because of poor, unreliable oral bioavailability from extensive first-pass metabolism and gut-mucosa vasoconstriction. Tetrahydrozoline and xylometazoline are imidazoline-derivative decongestants formulated as long-acting (8-12 hour) topical nasal drops/sprays, not oral agents, while pseudoephedrine is the classic non-catecholamine sympathomimetic with good oral bioavailability, formulated specifically as an oral decongestant.
## explicit_objective
Identify pseudoephedrine as the nasal decongestant given orally, distinguishing it from the topical imidazoline agents (tetrahydrozoline, xylometazoline) and from adrenaline, which is not given orally at all.
## pitfalls
Assuming any nasal decongestant can be given by any route -- the imidazolines are formulated as topical drops and adrenaline is not orally viable, leaving pseudoephedrine as the oral option.

Heparin therapy is monitored by maintaining the activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) at about twice normal
## canonical_key
heparin.monitoring.aptt
## aliases

## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Heparin therapy is controlled by maintaining aPTT (activated partial thromboplastin time) at roughly twice the normal value, reflecting heparin's inhibition of thrombin (factor IIa) and factors IXa, Xa, XIa and XIIa via antithrombin III, which prolongs the intrinsic-pathway-based aPTT assay. This is distinct from warfarin, which is instead monitored by PT/INR (reflecting the extrinsic pathway and vitamin-K-dependent factors), and from sedimentation rate or alpha-2-antiplasmin level, neither of which reflects heparin's anticoagulant effect. Low-molecular-weight heparins, by contrast, do not require routine aPTT monitoring except in specific situations (renal insufficiency, obesity, pregnancy), where anti-factor-Xa assay is used instead.
## explicit_objective
State that heparin therapy is monitored by aPTT (around twice normal), distinguishing this from warfarin's PT/INR monitoring and from unrelated tests (ESR, alpha-2-antiplasmin).
## pitfalls
Confusing heparin's aPTT-based monitoring with warfarin's PT/INR-based monitoring -- the two anticoagulants act on different coagulation pathways and are tracked with different laboratory tests.

## label
Warfarin therapy is monitored by the INR (international normalized ratio), a standardized form of the prothrombin time
## canonical_key
warfarin.monitoring.inr
## aliases

## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Warfarin therapy is controlled by PT (prothrombin time, target about twice normal) standardised as the INR (international normalized ratio), with a typical therapeutic target of 2-3, reflecting warfarin's inhibition of vitamin K epoxide reductase and consequent reduction of the vitamin-K-dependent clotting factors II, VII, IX and X and proteins C and S. This is distinct from heparin, which is monitored by aPTT rather than PT/INR; bleeding time reflects platelet/vessel-wall function, not the coagulation cascade warfarin acts on; and plasma fibrinogen concentration is not the standard warfarin-monitoring test. INR standardisation specifically corrects for variability between different PT reagents/laboratories, which raw PT values do not.
## explicit_objective
State that warfarin therapy is monitored by INR (a standardized PT), distinguishing this from heparin's aPTT, platelet-function bleeding time, or fibrinogen concentration.
## pitfalls
Confusing warfarin's PT/INR monitoring with heparin's aPTT monitoring, or with bleeding time (a platelet-function test unrelated to warfarin's vitamin-K-dependent-factor mechanism).

CON-FND-ED6F922D728F1B
## label
Warfarin is effective only in vivo, not in a test tube, because it works by blocking the hepatic regeneration of active vitamin K-dependent clotting factors
## canonical_key
warfarin.in-vivo-only.mechanism-basis
## aliases

## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Warfarin inhibits vitamin K epoxide reductase in the liver, preventing the regeneration (reactivation) of vitamin K needed to gamma-carboxylate (activate) clotting factors II, VII, IX and X as they are synthesised -- a hepatic, cellular process that only occurs in a living system, which is why warfarin has no anticoagulant effect if simply added to blood in a test tube (in vitro), unlike heparin, whose direct antithrombin-mediated action works both in vivo and in vitro. Heparin, contrary to the other options, has a rapid, not delayed, onset after IV administration, and is not effective orally (it is a large, charged molecule that is not absorbed from the gut) -- it must be given IV or SC. Protamine sulfate is the antidote for heparin, not warfarin, whose own antidote is vitamin K1 (with PCC/FFP for serious bleeding).
## explicit_objective
Identify 'warfarin is effective in vivo only' as the correct statement, because its mechanism requires hepatic vitamin-K-cycle inhibition, unlike heparin's direct in-vitro-and-in-vivo antithrombin action.
## pitfalls
Assuming warfarin and heparin are pharmacologically interchangeable in onset, oral availability or antidote -- protamine sulfate reverses heparin, not warfarin, and heparin (not warfarin) is the one with rapid IV onset and no oral efficacy.

## id
CON-FND-6F103282570DB3
## label
Abciximab blocks the platelet glycoprotein IIb/IIIa receptor, the final common pathway of platelet aggregation
## canonical_key
abciximab.gp-iib-iiia-blockade.final-aggregation-pathway
## aliases

## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Abciximab, eptifibatide and tirofiban are GP IIb/IIIa antagonists, given IV only, that block the glycoprotein IIb/IIIa receptor on platelets -- the final common pathway of platelet aggregation, through which fibrinogen cross-links adjacent platelets regardless of which upstream agonist (ADP, thromboxane, thrombin) activated them. This mechanism is distinct from activating antithrombin III (heparin's mechanism), inhibiting vitamin K epoxide reductase (warfarin's mechanism), and irreversibly blocking platelet ADP (P2Y12) receptors (clopidogrel/prasugrel's mechanism) -- abciximab acts downstream of all of these, at the receptor aggregation itself depends on.
## explicit_objective
Identify GP IIb/IIIa receptor blockade -- the final common pathway of platelet aggregation -- as abciximab's mechanism, distinguishing it from antithrombin activation, vitamin K epoxide reductase inhibition, or ADP-receptor blockade.
## pitfalls
Confusing abciximab's GP IIb/IIIa blockade (the final, receptor-level aggregation step, downstream of every activating pathway) with clopidogrel's more upstream, single-receptor (P2Y12/ADP) antagonism.
