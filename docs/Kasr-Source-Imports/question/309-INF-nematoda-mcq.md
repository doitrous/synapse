## title
Nematode causing different types of anaemia

## question
Different types of anaemia may occur in infection with:

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Diphyllobothrium latum

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Diphyllobothrium latum causes a single, specific anaemia — megaloblastic (vitamin-B12-deficiency) anaemia from competition for B12 — not 'different types'.

## answer_b
Fasciola spp.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Fasciola is a liver fluke; anaemia is not its characteristic haematological feature.

## answer_c
Strongyloides stercoralis

## explanation_c
Correct. Strongyloides stercoralis is the nematode in which SEVERAL types of anaemia may occur: chronic infection produces intestinal malabsorption and chronic blood/protein loss, so an affected patient may develop iron-deficiency (microcytic) anaemia, megaloblastic (folate/B12-malabsorption) anaemia and the anaemia of chronic disease. The breadth of malabsorption and the possibility of hyperinfection in the immunosuppressed is why 'different types of anaemia' points specifically to Strongyloides rather than to a worm that causes one defined anaemia. This distinguishes it from hookworm (iron-deficiency only) and Diphyllobothrium (B12 only).

## answer_d
Necator americanus

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Necator americanus (hookworm) causes one defined picture — iron-deficiency (microcytic hypochromic) anaemia from chronic intestinal blood loss — not different types.

## title
Route of Strongyloides infection

## question
Individuals contract Strongyloides stercoralis via which of the following?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Ingestion of infected freshwater fish

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Ingestion of infected freshwater fish transmits Diphyllobothrium latum (plerocercoid), not Strongyloides.

## answer_b
Skin penetration

## explanation_b
Correct. Strongyloides stercoralis is acquired when infective FILARIFORM larvae in the soil actively penetrate intact skin (typically bare feet), exactly as hookworm larvae do. From the skin the larvae enter the circulation, migrate through the lungs, are swallowed and mature in the small intestine. Skin penetration by soil filariform larvae is therefore the mode of infection, and it also explains larva currens and the risk of autoinfection.

## answer_c
Insect bite

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Strongyloides is not vector-borne; no insect bite is involved.

## answer_d
Ingestion of contaminated beef

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Ingestion of contaminated (undercooked) beef transmits the beef tapeworm Taenia saginata, not Strongyloides.

Not a Trichostrongylus control strategy

## question
Which of the following is not a Trichostrongylus colubriformis prevention and control strategy?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Proper water treatment

## explanation_a
Incorrect (it IS a valid strategy). Trichostrongylus is acquired by swallowing larvae on contaminated water or plants, so proper water treatment does help prevent it.

## answer_b
Appropriate food handling

## explanation_b
Incorrect (it IS a valid strategy). The larvae are ingested on contaminated vegetables, so appropriate food handling and washing of produce reduce transmission.

## answer_c
Use of insect repellent

## explanation_c
Correct. Use of insect repellent is NOT a control strategy for Trichostrongylus colubriformis because the worm is not transmitted by any insect vector — infection is acquired by the faecal-oral route, ingesting infective larvae on contaminated water, soil or plants. Control therefore rests on sanitation, safe water and food hygiene, and repelling insects does nothing to interrupt this non-vector, oral cycle. This is the odd-one-out among the listed measures.

## answer_d
Proper sanitation practices

## explanation_d
Incorrect (it IS a valid strategy). Because transmission is faecal-oral from soil contaminated with faeces, proper sanitation practices break the cycle.

## title
Diagnostic stage of Strongyloides in stool

## question
The typical diagnostic stage of Strongyloides stercoralis seen in stool is:

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Mature egg

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Unlike hookworm, Strongyloides eggs hatch within the intestinal mucosa, so mature eggs are not usually found in stool.

## answer_b
Rhabditiform larva

## explanation_b
Correct. The typical diagnostic stage of Strongyloides stercoralis in fresh stool is the RHABDITIFORM (first-stage) larva. Because the eggs hatch in the intestinal wall, it is the rhabditiform larva — not the egg — that is passed and recovered on stool examination (or by the Baermann/agar-plate techniques). It is distinguished from a hookworm rhabditiform larva by its short buccal cavity and prominent genital primordium, which is the key morphological point tested.

## answer_c
Filariform larva

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The filariform larva is the infective soil/tissue stage (and appears in autoinfection); it is not the usual stool diagnostic stage.

## answer_d
Immature egg

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Immature eggs are not the stool finding for Strongyloides because the eggs hatch in the mucosa before being passed.

Most common feature of visceral larva migrans

## question
The most common characteristic of visceral larva migrans is:

## subject
inf

## status

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Lymphadenitis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Lymphadenitis is not the defining feature of visceral larva migrans.

## answer_b
Chronic eosinophilia

## explanation_b
Correct. The most common and characteristic feature of visceral larva migrans (usually caused by Toxocara canis/cati larvae) is a persistent, marked CHRONIC EOSINOPHILIA. ## explanation_d
Incorrect. A non-specific leucocytosis is far less characteristic than the eosinophilia that defines the syndrome.

## title
Internal and external autoinfection

## question
Internal and external autoinfection occurs with:

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Strongyloides stercoralis

## explanation_a
Correct. Strongyloides stercoralis is the nematode showing BOTH internal and external autoinfection. In internal autoinfection, rhabditiform larvae mature to infective filariform larvae inside the gut and re-penetrate the intestinal mucosa; in external autoinfection they re-penetrate the perianal skin. Both routes let the parasite complete new cycles without leaving the host, which allows lifelong persistence and, in the immunosuppressed, life-threatening hyperinfection. This dual (internal AND external) autoinfection distinguishes Strongyloides from Capillaria, which shows internal autoinfection only.

Capillaria philippinensis

Incorrect. Capillaria philippinensis shows INTERNAL autoinfection (larvipary) only, not external.

## answer_c
Trichuris trichiura

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Trichuris does not autoinfect; its eggs must embryonate in soil.

## answer_d
Trichostrongylus colubriformis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Trichostrongylus is acquired from soil/plants and does not autoinfect.
