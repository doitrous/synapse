## title
Ambient temperature at which sweating begins

## question
Regarding sweat secretion, which one of the following statements is correct?

## subject
endo

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
It is a passive process that does not require energy

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Sweat secretion is an active secretory process: the secretory coil actively pumps out an isotonic primary secretion, and the duct then actively reabsorbs sodium and chloride, both requiring metabolic energy.

## answer_b
Sweat glands are stimulated by sympathetic noradrenergic fibres

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Eccrine sweat glands are an exception among sympathetic targets: they are innervated by sympathetic fibres that release acetylcholine (cholinergic), not noradrenaline.

## answer_c
It starts when the environmental temperature reaches about 32-34 degrees C

## explanation_c
Correct. Thermoregulatory sweating becomes an effective heat-loss mechanism once the environmental temperature rises to roughly 32-34 degrees C, the point at which radiation and convection can no longer dissipate the body's heat load. Below this range heat is lost mainly by radiation and convection, so sweating contributes little. Above it, evaporation of sweat becomes the body's principal avenue for losing heat.

## answer_d
Increased humidity of the air increases the evaporation of sweat

## explanation_d
Incorrect. High ambient humidity reduces, rather than increases, the evaporation of sweat, because the water-vapour gradient between skin and air is small; this is why humid heat feels more oppressive and impairs cooling.

## title
Mediator of tubuloglomerular feedback from the macula densa

## question
In the process of tubuloglomerular feedback, which molecule is released by macula densa cells to constrict the afferent arteriole when the tubular flow (renal blood flow) is increased?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Renin

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Renin is released from the juxtaglomerular granular cells in response to low perfusion; it is not the paracrine signal the macula densa uses to constrict the afferent arteriole.

## answer_b
Adenosine

## explanation_b
Correct. When a rise in GFR delivers more NaCl to the macula densa, the increased NaCl uptake makes these cells release adenosine (with ATP), which acts on A1 receptors of the adjacent afferent arteriolar smooth muscle to cause vasoconstriction. This lowers the glomerular hydrostatic pressure and returns GFR toward normal, the essence of tubuloglomerular feedback. Adenosine is therefore the local vasoconstrictor mediator in this reflex.

## answer_c
Angiotensin II

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Angiotensin II is a circulating/locally generated vasoconstrictor that preferentially constricts the efferent arteriole; it is not the macula densa's paracrine signal onto the afferent arteriole in tubuloglomerular feedback.

## answer_d
Nitric oxide

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Nitric oxide is a vasodilator that opposes tubuloglomerular feedback by relaxing the afferent arteriole, the opposite of the constrictor action being asked about.

## title
Definition of filtration fraction

## question
Which of the following is correct about the filtration fraction?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
It is the ratio of the glomerular filtration rate to the renal plasma flow

## explanation_a
Correct. The filtration fraction is defined as GFR divided by renal plasma flow (FF = GFR/RPF), normally about 0.2, meaning roughly one-fifth of the plasma reaching the glomeruli is filtered. It expresses how efficiently plasma is filtered rather than an absolute volume. A change in efferent tone or plasma protein concentration alters the filtration fraction.

## answer_b
It is the volume of plasma filtered by one kidney per minute

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The volume of plasma filtered per minute is the glomerular filtration rate itself, not the filtration fraction, which is a dimensionless ratio.

## answer_c
It is the volume of plasma filtered by the two kidneys per minute

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This again describes an absolute filtered volume (GFR), not the ratio of filtered plasma to plasma delivered.

## answer_d
It is the percentage of blood converted to glomerular filtrate by one kidney per minute

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The filtration fraction is calculated from plasma, not whole blood, and it is a ratio of GFR to renal plasma flow rather than a percentage of blood filtered.

## question

## subject
renal

## status

## owner
Claude

## vignette

C

## answer_a
The colloid osmotic pressure of the plasma proteins equals approximately 32 mmHg

## explanation_a
Incorrect as the answer (the statement is true). The plasma colloid osmotic (oncotic) pressure opposing filtration is about 32 mmHg, one of the standard Starling forces across the glomerular membrane.

## answer_b
The hydrostatic pressure in Bowman's capsule equals approximately 18 mmHg

## explanation_b
Incorrect as the answer (the statement is true). The hydrostatic pressure within Bowman's capsule is approximately 18 mmHg and opposes filtration.

## answer_c
An increase in the afferent arteriolar tone increases the glomerular filtration rate

## explanation_c
Correct (this is the untrue statement). Increasing the afferent arteriolar tone (constriction) reduces glomerular blood flow and lowers the glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure, so it decreases, not increases, the GFR. Because the question asks for the incorrect statement, this reversed relationship is the answer. Afferent constriction lowers GFR, whereas efferent constriction tends to raise it.

## answer_d
The net filtration pressure equals approximately 10 mmHg

## explanation_d
Incorrect as the answer (the statement is true). The net filtration pressure is about 10 mmHg (roughly 60 glomerular hydrostatic minus 32 oncotic minus 18 capsular), the small positive force that drives filtration.

## title
Nephron segment impermeable to water

## question
Which of the following renal sites is characterized by having no permeability to water?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Collecting duct

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The collecting duct's water permeability is variable but present, becoming high under the influence of ADH through aquaporin-2 insertion.

## answer_b
Thin descending limb of the loop of Henle

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The thin descending limb is highly permeable to water, which passively leaves it into the hyperosmotic medulla, concentrating the tubular fluid.

## answer_c
Late distal tubule

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The late distal tubule and collecting system are water-permeable in an ADH-dependent manner, so they do not qualify as impermeable.

## answer_d
Thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle

## explanation_d
Correct. The thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle is essentially impermeable to water while it actively reabsorbs NaCl through the Na-K-2Cl cotransporter. This 'diluting segment' therefore separates solute from water, generating dilute tubular fluid and depositing solute in the medullary interstitium. Its water impermeability is central to the countercurrent multiplier that concentrates the urine.

## title
Control of water balance and the action of ADH

## question
Regarding the control of water balance by the kidneys, which one of the following statements is correct?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
The renal medulla has an osmotic gradient that decreases from the cortico-medullary border towards the papilla

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The medullary osmotic gradient increases, not decreases, from the cortico-medullary junction (about 300 mOsmol/kg) towards the papilla (up to about 1200 mOsmol/kg).

## answer_b
ADH is secreted by the anterior pituitary in response to a fall in blood osmolality

## explanation_b
Incorrect. ADH is synthesised in the hypothalamus and released from the posterior (not anterior) pituitary, and its secretion is stimulated by a rise in plasma osmolality, not a fall.

## answer_c
A normal person cannot produce urine with an osmolality greater than 300 mOsmol/kg

## explanation_c
Incorrect. With maximal ADH action a normal person can concentrate urine well above plasma, to around 1200 mOsmol/kg, far exceeding 300 mOsmol/kg.

## answer_d
ADH acts on the principal cells of the collecting ducts to increase their permeability to water

## explanation_d
Correct. ADH (vasopressin) binds V2 receptors on the basolateral membrane of collecting-duct principal cells, raising cyclic AMP and inserting aquaporin-2 water channels into the apical membrane. This increases the water permeability of the collecting duct, allowing water to be reabsorbed down the medullary osmotic gradient and the urine to be concentrated. Loss of this action (as in diabetes insipidus) produces large volumes of dilute urine.

## title
Mechanism of proximal tubular hydrogen secretion

## question
Hydrogen ion secretion in the proximal convoluted tubule occurs mainly by:

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Secondary active co-transport with sodium

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Co-transport would move H+ and Na+ in the same direction, whereas proximal H+ secretion moves H+ into the lumen while Na+ moves into the cell, i.e. counter-transport, not co-transport.

## answer_b
Primary active co-transport with sodium

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The proximal H+ secretion is not primary active transport; it does not hydrolyse ATP directly at the exchanger but is driven secondarily by the sodium gradient.

## answer_c
Secondary active counter-transport with sodium

## explanation_c
Correct. In the proximal tubule most H+ is secreted by the apical Na+/H+ exchanger (NHE3), a secondary active antiporter (counter-transport). The inwardly directed sodium gradient, maintained by the basolateral Na-K ATPase, provides the energy that drives H+ out into the lumen in exchange for Na+ entering the cell. This couples sodium reabsorption to hydrogen secretion and to bicarbonate reclamation.

## answer_d
Simple diffusion

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Secretion against the concentration gradient into the lumen cannot occur by simple passive diffusion; it requires the sodium-coupled exchanger.

## title
Acidification of urine and urinary buffers

## question
Regarding acidification of the urine by the renal tubules, which one of the following statements is correct?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The late distal tubules and collecting ducts are responsible for about 10% of bicarbonate reabsorption but cannot generate new bicarbonate

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The distal nephron does generate new bicarbonate as it secretes H+ against buffers; the statement wrongly denies this new-bicarbonate generation that titratable acid and ammonium excretion accomplish.

## answer_b
Secreted H+ is buffered by ammonia (NH3) in the distal tubule and collecting duct, forming ammonium

## explanation_b
Correct. In the distal tubule and collecting duct, secreted H+ combines with ammonia (NH3) that has diffused from the tubular cells to form ammonium (NH4+), which is trapped in the lumen and excreted. This ammonia buffer system, together with phosphate, allows the kidney to excrete a large acid load and generate new bicarbonate. Ammonium excretion is the main adaptive route for eliminating acid in chronic acidosis.

## answer_c
Secreted H+ reacts with NaH2PO4 in the tubular fluid to give Na2HPO4

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The phosphate buffer works the other way round: secreted H+ reacts with Na2HPO4 (dibasic) to form NaH2PO4 (monobasic), the titratable acid, so the reaction as written is reversed.

## answer_d
H+ is secreted into the tubular fluid by the principal cells of the distal tubule and collecting duct

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Distal H+ secretion is performed by the alpha-intercalated cells (via an apical H+ ATPase and H+/K+ ATPase), not by the principal cells, which handle Na+, K+ and water.

## title
Respiratory system in the control of blood pH

## question
Which of the following statements is correct about the respiratory system as a control system for blood pH?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
It is the most rapid of all the pH control systems

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The chemical buffer systems act essentially instantaneously and are the most rapid line of defence; the respiratory system responds within minutes, so it is fast but not the most rapid.

## answer_b
It is the least potent control system of pH

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The respiratory system is actually quite powerful (about one to two times the buffering power of all the chemical buffers combined); the kidney is the slowest but most potent long-term regulator.

## answer_c
It controls pH by adjusting the arterial PCO2

## explanation_c
Correct. The lungs regulate pH by altering alveolar ventilation, which sets the arterial PCO2 and hence the carbonic acid concentration of the blood. Increasing ventilation blows off CO2 and raises pH, while hypoventilation retains CO2 and lowers pH. By adjusting PCO2 the respiratory system provides rapid, second-line control of acid-base balance between the instantaneous buffers and the slow renal response.

## answer_d
It controls pH by adjusting the plasma bicarbonate concentration

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Adjusting the plasma bicarbonate concentration is the role of the kidney, not the lungs; the respiratory system operates on the CO2/carbonic-acid side of the buffer equation.

## title
Condition most likely to cause metabolic alkalosis

## question
Which of the following conditions is most likely to cause metabolic alkalosis?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Salicylate (aspirin) poisoning

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Salicylate poisoning classically produces a mixed picture with a high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis (and an early respiratory alkalosis), not a metabolic alkalosis.

## answer_b
Renal failure

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Renal failure causes a metabolic acidosis because the failing kidney cannot excrete the daily acid load or regenerate bicarbonate.

## answer_c
Diabetic ketoacidosis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Diabetic ketoacidosis is a high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis due to the accumulation of ketoacids.

## answer_d
Hyperaldosteronism (Conn's syndrome)

## explanation_d
Correct. Primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn's syndrome) causes a metabolic alkalosis: aldosterone excess drives distal Na+ reabsorption coupled to increased H+ and K+ secretion, so the urine loses acid while bicarbonate is retained. The resulting hypokalaemia further shifts H+ into cells and enhances renal acid excretion, sustaining the alkalosis. This links a mineralocorticoid excess state to a rise in plasma bicarbonate and pH.
