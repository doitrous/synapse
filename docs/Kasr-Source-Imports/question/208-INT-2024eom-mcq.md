## title
Systemic fungal infection example

## question
Which of the following is an example of systemic fungal infection?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Tinea pedis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Tinea pedis (athlete's foot) is a superficial dermatophyte infection of the skin, not a systemic (internal-organ) fungal infection.

## answer_b
Histoplasmosis

## explanation_b
Correct. Systemic fungal infections are invasive infections of internal organs, uncommon in normally healthy people; Histoplasma capsulatum (histoplasmosis), primarily infecting the lung, is the department book's named example of this type of affection. Systemic fungal infections belong to the broader opportunistic-infection category that also includes protozoal/helminthic, bacterial and viral organisms the immune system normally suppresses without effort. This is why histoplasmosis specifically, rather than a superficial or subcutaneous fungal disease, answers a question about SYSTEMIC fungal infection.

## answer_c
Mycetoma

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Mycetoma is a subcutaneous fungal (or actinomycotic) infection limited to skin and subcutaneous tissue, causing sinuses discharging serosanguinous fluid — not a systemic, internal-organ infection.

## answer_d
Vaginal candidiasis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Vaginal candidiasis is a superficial mucosal fungal infection, not a systemic one, even though Candida can also cause systemic disease in immunocompromised hosts elsewhere in the department book's opportunistic-infection list.

## title
Definition of dysplasia

## question
Dysplasia is:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Neoplastic disordered cellular proliferations

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Dysplasia is explicitly non-neoplastic in the department book's own definition — calling it neoplastic contradicts the source directly.

## answer_b
Non-neoplastic ordered cellular proliferations

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Dysplasia is disordered, not ordered, proliferation — loss of the normal orderly cellular arrangement (polarity) is one of its defining microscopic features.

## answer_c
Non-neoplastic disordered cellular proliferations

## explanation_c
Correct. Dysplasia is non-neoplastic disordered proliferation of cells, usually induced by prolonged cell irritation, seen at sites such as the cervix uteri, bronchi, oral cavity, urinary bladder, colon, gall bladder and epidermis. Microscopically it shows loss of normal orderly arrangement (polarity), cellular pleomorphism with nuclear hyperchromasia, and increased mitosis, and is graded low or high by the degree of atypia. Because it is non-neoplastic, low-grade dysplasia is commonly reversible once the irritating cause is removed — a reversibility neoplasia never shares.

## answer_d
Hyperplastic cellular proliferations

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Hyperplasia is a distinct, ordered increase in cell number by division — not the same process as dysplasia's disordered proliferation.

Incorrect. Myositis ossificans is not an autonomous, disordered neoplastic growth — it is a reactive metaplastic change, typically following a specific trigger (intramuscular hemorrhage).

Anaplastic tumor differentiation

## question
Anaplastic tumor is a tumor in which the tissue is:

## subject
fnd

## vignette

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Well differentiated (grade I) is the LEAST anaplastic grade, the opposite end of the spectrum from an anaplastic (undifferentiated) tumor.

## answer_b
Moderately differentiated

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Moderately differentiated (grade II) sits in the middle of the grading spectrum, still short of the undifferentiated, maximally anaplastic grade IV that the term "anaplastic tumor" specifically names.

Undifferentiated (grade IV) tumors show the greatest anaplasia and grow fastest, and this grade is specifically termed anaplastic carcinoma — the label "anaplastic tumor" names the undifferentiated end of the grading spectrum, not an intermediate point on it.

## question
Presence of mature ectopic pancreatic tissue at the wall of small intestine is called:

## subject
fnd

## status

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A teratoma is a germ-cell tumor composed of tissues from multiple (often all three) germ cell layers, not a single normally organized ectopic tissue nodule.

## answer_b
Choristoma

## explanation_b
Correct. A choristoma is a congenital anomaly consisting of a heterotopic rest of cells — the department book's own worked example is a small nodule of well-developed and normally organized pancreatic tissue found in the mucosa of the stomach, duodenum or (as in this stem) small intestine wall. A hamartoma is a disorganized overgrowth of tissue elements NATIVE to the site where it occurs (e.g. lung hamartoma: cartilage, smooth muscle and bronchial mucosa, all normal lung constituents, just haphazardly arranged) — the opposite situation from ectopic pancreatic tissue, which does not belong in the intestinal wall at all.

## answer_d
Adenoma

## explanation_d
Incorrect. An adenoma is a benign epithelial neoplasm of glandular origin, not a congenital heterotopic tissue rest.
