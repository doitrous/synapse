## title
Extraocular muscle not arising from the orbital apex

## question
All of the following muscles arise from the apex of the orbit, EXCEPT:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Superior oblique muscle

## explanation_a
Incorrect (it does arise at the apex). The superior oblique arises from the body of the sphenoid near the annulus of Zinn at the orbital apex.

## answer_b
Lateral rectus muscle

## explanation_b
Incorrect (it does arise at the apex). The lateral rectus, like the other recti, arises from the annulus of Zinn at the orbital apex.

## answer_c
Inferior oblique muscle

## explanation_c
Correct. The inferior oblique is the exception: it is the only extraocular muscle that arises anteriorly, from the orbital floor near the lacrimal fossa, rather than at the apex. The four recti and the superior oblique all take origin from the annulus of Zinn at the orbital apex, so the inferior oblique is the one that does not.

## answer_d
Inferior rectus muscle

## explanation_d
Incorrect (it does arise at the apex). The inferior rectus is one of the four recti and arises from the annulus of Zinn at the orbital apex.

## title
Action of the superior rectus muscle

## question
What is the action of the superior rectus muscle?

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Elevation, intorsion, abduction

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The superior rectus adducts, not abducts, as its tertiary action, though intorsion is correct.

## answer_b
Elevation, intorsion, adduction

## explanation_b
Correct. The superior rectus runs above the globe at an angle to the visual axis, so its primary action is elevation with secondary intorsion and tertiary adduction. This is why its elevating action is tested with the eye abducted, where the muscle acts as a pure elevator. The combination elevation-intorsion-adduction distinguishes it from the inferior oblique, which elevates but extorts and abducts.

## answer_c
Elevation, extorsion, adduction

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The superior rectus intorts (not extorts); extorsion with elevation belongs to the inferior oblique.

## answer_d
Elevation, extorsion, abduction

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The superior rectus intorts and adducts; extorsion and abduction describe the inferior oblique.

## title
Best management of acute esotropia with limited abduction

## question
A 40-year-old male patient presents with a recent onset of esodeviation in the right eye with diplopia that improved on covering either eye. On examination, there is right esotropia 30 prism diopters and limited abduction. What is the best management plan?

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Prescribing glasses

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Glasses correct a refractive error; they do not address an acquired sixth-nerve palsy causing the esotropia and limited abduction.

## answer_b
Starting systemic antibiotics

## explanation_b
Incorrect. There is no infective process here; antibiotics have no role in an acute cranial nerve palsy.

## answer_c
Asking for brain MRI

## explanation_c
Correct. Recent-onset esotropia with limited abduction and diplopia relieved by covering either eye is an acquired sixth (abducens) nerve palsy. In an adult this is a neurological sign that requires neuroimaging (brain MRI) to exclude a compressive, ischaemic or intracranial cause before any surgical treatment. Investigating the cause takes priority over intervention on the muscle itself.

## answer_d
Scheduling of urgent extraocular muscle surgery

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Muscle surgery is premature and is not done for a recent acquired palsy before the cause is found and a period of observation for recovery has passed.

## title
True statement about amblyopia

## question
Amblyopia:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

C

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Amblyopia develops in early childhood, when the visual system is still plastic, not in adolescence.

## answer_b

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Amblyopia develops in the early years of visual development and must be treated before, not after, that window; it does not begin only after age 9.

## explanation_c
Correct. Congenital cataract and congenital corneal opacity block a clear image during the critical period of visual development, producing stimulus-deprivation amblyopia. ## explanation_d
Incorrect. Amblyopia is a serious, preventable cause of childhood visual loss that should be detected and treated, not ignored.

Cause of exotropia in an elderly hypertensive

## question
Exotropia in a 70 year old male with uncontrolled hypertension can be due to:

## subject
mul

Trigeminal cranial nerve palsy.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The trigeminal (fifth) nerve is sensory to the eye and motor to muscles of mastication; it does not move the globe, so its lesion does not cause a squint.

## title
Refractive error associated with exophoria

## question
Exophoria is commonly associated with which error of refraction?

## subject
mul

## status

## owner
Claude

Incorrect. Hypermetropia is associated with esophoria, because over-accommodation drives over-convergence inward.

Presbyopia.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Presbyopia is age-related loss of accommodation and is not the classic association of exophoria.

## answer_d
Compound astigmatism.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Compound astigmatism is a refractive error of two meridia and is not the recognised association of exophoria.

## title
Correct manifestation of third nerve palsy

## question
The following manifestation of the 3rd nerve palsy is correct:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Lid retraction.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Third-nerve palsy paralyses the levator and causes ptosis (a drooping lid), not lid retraction.

## answer_b
Preserved accommodation.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Accommodation is lost because the parasympathetic supply to the ciliary muscle is interrupted, so it is not preserved.

## answer_c
Convergent squint and diplopia.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Third-nerve palsy causes a divergent squint (exotropia, the eye down and out), not a convergent squint.

## answer_d
Dilated pupil.

## explanation_d
Correct. When the parasympathetic pupillary fibres are affected, the sphincter pupillae is denervated and the pupil is fixed and dilated (mydriasis). This dilated pupil, together with ptosis and a down-and-out eye, is the classic third-nerve palsy picture. The dilated pupil is therefore the correct manifestation.

## title
Commonest cause of bilateral fourth nerve palsy

## question
The most common cause of bilateral 4th nerve palsy is:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Diabetes.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Diabetes causes ischaemic mononeuropathies, more typically of the third or sixth nerve, and usually unilateral.

## answer_b
Aneurysm.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Aneurysm is a classic cause of a painful third-nerve palsy, not of bilateral fourth-nerve palsy.

## answer_c
Head trauma.

## explanation_c
Correct. The trochlear nerve has a long, thin intracranial course and decussates at the dorsal midbrain, where the two nerves are vulnerable together to a blow. Closed head trauma is therefore the commonest cause of bilateral fourth (trochlear) nerve palsy. It presents with vertical and torsional diplopia worse on downgaze.

## answer_d
Hypertension.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Hypertension contributes to microvascular palsies but is not the commonest cause of bilateral fourth-nerve palsy.

## title
Feature of fourth nerve palsy

## question
Fourth nerve palsy:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Closed head trauma is most common cause of unilateral cases.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Closed head trauma is the commonest cause of bilateral, not unilateral, fourth-nerve palsy; unilateral cases are often congenital or vascular.

## answer_b
Diplopia is usually absent.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Diplopia is present in fourth-nerve palsy: it is vertical and torsional and worst on downgaze.

## answer_c
Difficulty in reading is due to lost accommodation.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Accommodation is unaffected in fourth-nerve palsy; the difficulty in reading comes from vertical diplopia on downgaze, not from lost accommodation.

## answer_d
There is limitation of downward and inward movement.

## explanation_d
Correct. The superior oblique depresses the eye when it is adducted, so its palsy limits downward-and-inward movement. This gives vertical and torsional diplopia that is worst on downgaze, as when reading or descending stairs, and a compensatory head tilt. The limitation of depression in adduction is the characteristic sign.

## title
True statement about sixth nerve palsy

## question
Sixth nerve palsy:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
MRI is important for diagnosis.

## explanation_a
Correct. An acquired sixth (abducens) nerve palsy is a neurological sign, so neuroimaging (MRI) is important to look for a compressive, ischaemic, inflammatory or raised-intracranial-pressure cause. Because the nerve has a long intracranial course it can also be a false localising sign of raised pressure. Imaging guides management before any treatment of the squint.

## answer_b
The condition is treated by glasses.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Glasses correct a refractive error; they do not treat the weak lateral rectus of a sixth-nerve palsy, for which prisms or later surgery are used.

## answer_c
Pupil dilatation is a common finding.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The pupil is normal in sixth-nerve palsy, which affects only the lateral rectus; a dilated pupil points to a third-nerve lesion.

## answer_d
Vertical diplopia is present.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The diplopia of sixth-nerve palsy is horizontal, worse on gaze to the affected side, not vertical.
