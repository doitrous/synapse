## title
Foamy macrophages in the arterial wall

## question
The presence of many foamy macrophages in the arterial wall is characteristic of:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Syphilitic arteritis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Syphilitic (luetic) arteritis is an obliterative endarteritis of the vasa vasorum with plasma-cell–rich inflammation, not a foam-cell lesion.

## answer_b
Atherosclerosis

## explanation_b
Correct. Foamy macrophages (‘foam cells’) — lipid-laden macrophages in the intima — are the characteristic cells of ATHEROSCLEROSIS, forming the earliest lesion (the fatty streak) and populating the atheromatous plaque. They arise when intimal macrophages engulf oxidised LDL. Their presence in the arterial wall points specifically to atherosclerosis, not to syphilitic arteritis (plasma cells) or to hypertension (hyaline/fibrinoid arteriolar change).

## answer_c
Hypertension

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Hypertension produces hyaline arteriolosclerosis or fibrinoid necrosis of small vessels, not collections of foam cells.

## answer_d
All of the above

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Because syphilitic arteritis and hypertension do not feature foam cells, ‘all of the above’ is wrong; foamy macrophages are characteristic of atherosclerosis.

## title
Most important complication of atherosclerosis

## question
The most important complication of atherosclerosis is:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Thrombus formation

## explanation_a
Correct. THROMBUS FORMATION over a ruptured or ulcerated plaque is the most important complication of atherosclerosis, because it causes acute arterial occlusion and the major clinical events — myocardial infarction, ischaemic stroke and acute limb ischaemia. Although plaques also ulcerate, calcify and weaken the wall into aneurysms, it is superimposed thrombosis that most often turns a chronic stable plaque into a lethal acute event. This is why plaque rupture with thrombosis dominates the mortality of atherosclerosis.

## answer_b
Ulceration

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Ulceration of the plaque surface occurs and can trigger thrombosis, but it is not itself the most important complication.

## answer_c
Dystrophic calcification

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Dystrophic calcification of plaques is common but is a marker of chronicity rather than the most important (life-threatening) complication.

## answer_d
Aneurysmal dilatation

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Aneurysmal dilatation from medial weakening is an important complication (e.g. abdominal aortic aneurysm) but is less commonly the immediate cause of death than acute thrombotic occlusion.

## title
Cause of ischaemic heart disease in a smoker

## question
A 60 year old man who was a heavy smoker and not practicing exercise presented with ischemic heart disease. The most possible cause of this disease is:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Valvular lesions

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Valvular lesions cause haemodynamic overload and can secondarily reduce coronary flow, but they are not the usual cause of ischaemic heart disease in this risk profile.

## answer_b
Coronary embolism

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Coronary embolism is a rare cause of myocardial ischaemia (e.g. from infective endocarditis or a mural thrombus), far less common than coronary atherosclerosis.

## answer_c
Atherosclerosis

## explanation_c
Correct. In an older heavy smoker with a sedentary lifestyle, ischaemic heart disease is overwhelmingly due to CORONARY ATHEROSCLEROSIS: atheromatous plaques narrow the coronary arteries and, on rupture with thrombosis, occlude them, producing angina and myocardial infarction. Smoking and physical inactivity are classic risk factors accelerating coronary atheroma. Atherosclerosis underlies the vast majority (about 90%) of ischaemic heart disease, which is why it is the most probable cause here.

## answer_d
Aortic aneurysm

## explanation_d
Incorrect. An aortic aneurysm is itself a consequence of atherosclerosis but is not the direct cause of ischaemic heart disease.

## title
Medial calcification of arteries (Monckeberg)

## question
Medial calcification of arteries is seen in:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Monckberg calcification

## explanation_a
Correct. MÖNCKEBERG medial calcific sclerosis is defined by calcification of the MEDIA of medium-sized muscular arteries (classically the radial, ulnar, femoral and uterine arteries), typically in people over 50. The calcification is in the muscular media, does not narrow the lumen, and is usually clinically insignificant — the very definition of medial calcification of arteries being asked for here.

## answer_b
Atherosclerosis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Atherosclerosis calcifies the INTIMAL plaque, not the media; it is intimal (not medial) calcification.

## answer_c
Peusdoxanthoma elasticum

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Pseudoxanthoma elasticum causes fragmentation and calcification of ELASTIC fibres (skin, retina, vessels), a different pattern from Mönckeberg medial calcification.

## answer_d
Idiopathic calcification of infancy

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Idiopathic arterial calcification of infancy is a rare congenital disorder, not the classic ‘medial calcification of arteries’ intended here.

## title
Clinically important changes in atherosclerotic plaques

## question
Atherosclerotic plaques are susceptible to following several clinically important changes:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Rupture, Hemorrhage into a plaque, atheroembolism, aneurysm formation.

## explanation_a
Correct. Established atherosclerotic plaques undergo RUPTURE (fissuring/ulceration, often with overlying thrombosis), HAEMORRHAGE into the plaque (from fragile neovessels, rapidly enlarging it), ATHEROEMBOLISM (cholesterol/atheromatous debris embolising downstream), and ANEURYSM FORMATION (medial weakening beneath the plaque). All four are recognised clinically important complications of the plaque, so this is the correct combination.

## answer_b
Rupture, Hemorrhage into a plaque, atheroembolism, varicose vein formation.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Varicose VEIN formation is a venous disorder unrelated to arterial plaques; it does not belong in this list.

## answer_c
Rupture, Hemorrhage into a plaque, air embolism, aneurysm formation.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. AIR embolism (gas entering the circulation) is not a plaque complication — the plaque produces atheroembolism (cholesterol debris), not air.

## answer_d
Complete resolution, Hemorrhage into a plaque, atheroembolism, aneurysm formation.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Atherosclerotic plaques do NOT undergo complete resolution; they are progressive, so ‘complete resolution’ makes this option wrong.

## title
Lesion presenting as acute myocardial ischaemia

## question
Which of the following lesions may be presented by acute myocardial ischemia?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Fatty streaks.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Fatty streaks are the earliest, clinically silent lesion; they do not narrow the lumen enough to cause ischaemia.

## answer_b
Atheromatous plaque with lumen narrowing more than 75%.

## explanation_b
Correct. Acute myocardial ischaemia requires a critically stenosing lesion: an ATHEROMATOUS PLAQUE narrowing the coronary lumen by MORE THAN ~75% (‘critical stenosis’) limits flow so that oxygen demand outstrips supply on exertion, producing ischaemia. Below this threshold flow is usually adequate at rest and even on moderate exertion, which is why the 75% cut-off is the classic teaching for flow-limiting coronary disease. (An acute event may then be precipitated by rupture/thrombosis of such a plaque.)

## answer_c
Atheromatous plaque with hemorrhage.

## explanation_c
Incorrect as the single best answer. Intraplaque haemorrhage can acutely enlarge a plaque and precipitate an event, but the lesion the question frames as causing ischaemia is the critically (>75%) stenosing plaque.

## answer_d
Atheromatous plaque with excess fibrous tissue.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A fibrous (stable) plaque with excess fibrous tissue is the stable form; excess fibrous tissue alone does not define the ischaemia-producing lesion, which is the >75% stenosis.

## title
Secondary changes in atheromatous plaques

## question
Secondary changes in atheromatous plaques include:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Hypertrophy.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Hypertrophy (increase in cell size) is not a secondary change of a plaque; plaques enlarge by lipid, smooth-muscle and matrix accumulation, not by cellular hypertrophy in this sense.

## answer_b
Calcification.

## explanation_b
Correct. CALCIFICATION (dystrophic calcification of the lipid/necrotic core and fibrous tissue) is a classic secondary/complicated change in atheromatous plaques, along with ulceration, intraplaque haemorrhage, thrombosis and aneurysmal dilatation. Deposition of calcium salts into the degenerate plaque is common in advanced lesions and hardens the arterial wall. It is the only listed option that is a genuine secondary change of a plaque.

## answer_c
Metaplastic changes.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Metaplasia (change of one differentiated tissue type to another) is not a recognised secondary change in atheromatous plaques.

## answer_d
Neoplastic changes

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Neoplastic change (tumour formation) is not a feature of atheromatous plaques.
