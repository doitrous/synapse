## title
Statement that is NOT true of lipoprotein lipase

## question
All the following about lipoprotein lipase are correct EXCEPT:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
It is present in the endothelial cells of extrahepatic tissue

## explanation_a
Incorrect as the answer. Lipoprotein lipase is indeed anchored to the luminal surface of capillary endothelial cells of extrahepatic tissues (adipose, muscle, lactating mammary gland).

## answer_b
Its synthesis is decreased by insulin, and heparin stimulates its release

## explanation_b
Correct. This is the false statement. Insulin INCREASES, not decreases, lipoprotein lipase synthesis (notably in adipose tissue after a meal). The heparin clause is true (heparin releases the enzyme from the endothelium into plasma), but because the insulin clause is wrong the whole statement is incorrect, making it the exception.

## answer_c
It requires the cofactors phospholipids and apo C-II

## explanation_c
Incorrect as the answer. Lipoprotein lipase genuinely requires apo C-II (supplied by circulating lipoproteins) as an activator and phospholipid for its action.

## answer_d
Deficiency of lipoprotein lipase leads to type I hyperlipoproteinaemia

## explanation_d
Incorrect as the answer. Lipoprotein lipase deficiency (or apo C-II deficiency) does cause type I hyperlipoproteinaemia, with fasting chylomicronaemia.

## title
Enzyme class of pancreatic lipase

## question
Pancreatic lipase, which hydrolyses fats, acts as a:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Peptidase

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Peptidases hydrolyse peptide bonds in proteins; pancreatic lipase acts on ester bonds in triacylglycerol, not on peptides.

## answer_b
Hydrolase

## explanation_b
Correct. Pancreatic lipase hydrolyses the ester bonds of triacylglycerol (removing fatty acids from positions 1 and 3 to give 2-monoacylglycerol and free fatty acids). Adding water to cleave a bond makes it a hydrolase.

## answer_c
Carboxylase

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Carboxylases add a carboxyl (CO2) group and require biotin; lipase neither adds CO2 nor uses biotin.

## answer_d
Dehydrogenase

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Dehydrogenases catalyse oxidation-reduction by transferring hydrogen/electrons, which is not what lipase does.

## title
How L-amino acids are absorbed

## question
The L-amino acids are absorbed from the intestine by:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Active transport

## explanation_a
Correct. L-amino acids are absorbed across the enterocyte apical membrane mainly by secondary active transport, coupled to the inward sodium gradient (analogous to SGLT1 for glucose). This lets them be taken up against their concentration gradient.

## answer_b
Passive diffusion

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Simple passive diffusion is negligible for the polar, charged amino acids and cannot move them against a gradient.

## answer_c
Pinocytosis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Pinocytosis of intact protein occurs only transiently in the neonatal gut (e.g. for immunoglobulins), not for routine L-amino-acid absorption in the adult.

## answer_d
Facilitated diffusion

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Some minor carrier-mediated facilitated uptake exists, but the principal, defining mechanism for L-amino acids is sodium-dependent active transport.

## title
Amino acid not needed for glutathione synthesis

## question
Which of the following amino acids is NOT required for the synthesis of glutathione?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Serine

## explanation_a
Correct. Glutathione is the tripeptide gamma-glutamyl-cysteinyl-glycine, built from glutamate, cysteine and glycine. Serine is not one of its constituent amino acids, so it is not required for its synthesis.

## answer_b
Cysteine

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Cysteine is an essential constituent of glutathione and provides its reactive thiol (-SH) group.

## answer_c
Glutamic acid

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Glutamic acid (glutamate) is the N-terminal residue, joined through its gamma-carboxyl in the unusual gamma-peptide bond.

## answer_d
Glycine

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Glycine is the C-terminal residue of the glutathione tripeptide.

## title
Reaction not belonging to phase I detoxification

## question
The major detoxification reactions involved in phase I are the following EXCEPT:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Reduction

## explanation_a
Incorrect as the answer. Reduction is a phase I reaction, introducing or exposing a functional group on the xenobiotic.

## answer_b
Oxidation

## explanation_b
Incorrect as the answer. Oxidation (largely by cytochrome P450) is the archetypal phase I reaction.

## answer_c
Hydroxylation

## explanation_c
Incorrect as the answer. Hydroxylation is a common cytochrome-P450-mediated phase I oxidation.

## answer_d
Methylation

## explanation_d
Correct. Methylation is a conjugation reaction of phase II, not phase I. Phase I comprises oxidation, reduction and hydrolysis (including hydroxylation) that unmask or add a reactive group; phase II then conjugates that group (methylation, sulfation, glucuronidation, acetylation, glutathione conjugation). Methylation therefore does not belong to phase I.
