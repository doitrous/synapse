## title
Basal metabolic rate and its determinants
## question
Which of the following is correct as regards the basal metabolic rate (BMR)?
## subject
endo
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## vignette

## correct_answer
A
## answer_a
Decreased by age due to loss of muscle mass
## explanation_a
Correct. BMR falls progressively with age largely because ageing reduces lean (skeletal muscle) mass, and muscle is the main metabolically active tissue setting resting energy expenditure. As muscle mass declines, resting oxygen consumption falls and BMR drops. This is why the elderly have lower basal energy requirements than young adults of the same weight.
## answer_b
The energy needed to perform the body's chemical reactions during sleep
## explanation_b
Incorrect. BMR is measured awake but resting, in the post-absorptive state (12-14 h fasting), mentally and physically relaxed at a comfortable temperature; during sleep metabolic rate falls a further 10-15% below the basal value.
## answer_c
Decreased in low environmental temperature
## explanation_c
Incorrect. BMR rises, not falls, in cold environments because heat-generating mechanisms such as thyroid hormone release and shivering increase metabolic rate.
## answer_d
Expressed in Kcal/meter/minute
## explanation_d
Incorrect. BMR is expressed as energy per unit body surface area per time (kcal/m2/hour), not kcal/meter/minute, because heat exchange scales with surface area rather than linear length.

## title
Physiological responses to cold exposure
## question
On exposure to cold:
## subject
endo
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## vignette

## correct_answer
C
## answer_a
Maximum shivering occurs to increase the rate of heat production about 10 times
## explanation_a
Incorrect. Maximum shivering increases heat production roughly 4-5 times the basal rate, not 10 times.
## answer_b
There is vasodilation of skin blood vessels to decrease skin temperature
## explanation_b
Incorrect. Cold causes cutaneous vasoconstriction, not vasodilation, to conserve heat; vasodilation increases heat loss and is a response to heat.
## answer_c
Release of thyroid hormones occurs if the exposure to cold is prolonged
## explanation_c
Correct. Prolonged cold exposure activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis, raising TRH, TSH and thyroid hormone secretion. The increased thyroid hormone level raises basal metabolic rate and non-shivering (chemical) thermogenesis for sustained heat production. This adaptive response is slower and longer-lasting than the immediate shivering and vasoconstriction.
## answer_d
Sympathetic stimulation causes coupled oxidative phosphorylation in brown fat
## explanation_d
Incorrect. Sympathetic stimulation of brown fat uncouples oxidative phosphorylation (via uncoupling protein/thermogenin), dissipating the proton gradient as heat rather than producing ATP.

## title
Regulation of body temperature and fever
## question
Concerning regulation of body temperature:
## subject
endo
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## vignette

## correct_answer
D
## answer_a
The thalamus is the center for integrating thermal information
## explanation_a
Incorrect. The hypothalamus (pre-optic/anterior region), not the thalamus, is the integrating center for thermoregulation.
## answer_b
Shivering provides a method of heat gain during prolonged exposure to cold
## explanation_b
Incorrect. Shivering is only a short-term source of heat and cannot be sustained through prolonged cold, where thyroid/brown-fat thermogenesis takes over.
## answer_c
Above the set-point level the rate of heat production is more than the rate of heat loss
## explanation_c
Incorrect. When core temperature is above the set-point the body increases heat loss (sweating, vasodilation) so heat loss exceeds heat production, the reverse of the statement.
## answer_d
During fever prostaglandin E2 triggers the hypothalamus to raise the set-point
## explanation_d
Correct. Pyrogens such as interleukin-1 stimulate the hypothalamus to produce prostaglandin E2, which raises the thermoregulatory set-point above normal. The body then behaves as if cold, using vasoconstriction and shivering until core temperature reaches the new higher set-point, producing fever. Antipyretics such as aspirin inhibit prostaglandin synthesis and lower the set-point back toward normal.

Which of the following renal cells function as baroreceptors?
## subject
renal

## answer_a
Juxtaglomerular cells
## explanation_a
Correct. The juxtaglomerular (granular) cells are modified smooth muscle cells in the wall of the afferent arteriole. They sense wall stretch/pressure and thus act as intrarenal baroreceptors, secreting renin when perfusion pressure falls. This is a key trigger of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system.

Macula densa cells
## explanation_b
Incorrect. The macula densa cells are chemoreceptors sensing the NaCl concentration of distal tubular fluid, not baroreceptors.

Afferent arteriolar endothelial cells
## explanation_c
Incorrect. Afferent arteriolar endothelial cells line the vessel but are not the specialised pressure-sensing, renin-secreting cells; that role belongs to the juxtaglomerular granular cells.
## answer_d
Podocytes
## explanation_d
Incorrect. Podocytes are the visceral epithelial cells forming the filtration slits and have no baroreceptor function.

Definition of renal tubular secretion
## question
Which of the following defines renal tubular secretion?
## subject
renal
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## vignette

## correct_answer
A
## answer_a
Transfer of solutes from the blood (peritubular capillaries) into the tubular lumen
## explanation_a
Correct. Tubular secretion is the movement of substances from the peritubular capillary blood, across the tubular epithelium, into the tubular lumen. It adds solutes such as H+, K+ and organic acids/bases to the filtrate for excretion. It is the opposite direction to reabsorption.

Transfer of plasma from glomerular capillaries into Bowman's capsule
## explanation_b
Incorrect. Transfer of protein-free plasma from glomerular capillaries into Bowman’s capsule is filtration, not secretion.

Movement of solutes from the tubular lumen into the final urine
## explanation_c
Incorrect. Passage of luminal solutes into the final urine describes excretion, the net output; secretion specifically means adding solutes from blood into the lumen.
## answer_d
Transfer of solutes from the tubular lumen into the blood
## explanation_d
Incorrect. Movement of solutes from the lumen back into blood is reabsorption, the reverse of secretion.

Metabolic acidosis is caused by:

## answer_a
Aspirin (salicylate) poisoning
## explanation_a
Correct. Salicylate (aspirin) overdose adds fixed acid and generates organic acids (lactate and ketoacids) by uncoupling oxidative phosphorylation, producing a high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis. It also stimulates the respiratory center, adding a respiratory alkalosis, but the metabolic component is a true acidosis. Aspirin poisoning is therefore a recognised cause of metabolic acidosis.
## answer_b
Cushing syndrome
## explanation_b
Incorrect. Cushing syndrome (gluco/mineralocorticoid excess) causes hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis, not acidosis.
## answer_c
Conn's syndrome
## explanation_c
Incorrect. Conn’s syndrome (primary hyperaldosteronism) drives H+ and K+ loss, producing hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis.
## answer_d
Persistent vomiting
## explanation_d
Incorrect. Persistent vomiting loses gastric HCl, causing hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis, not acidosis.
