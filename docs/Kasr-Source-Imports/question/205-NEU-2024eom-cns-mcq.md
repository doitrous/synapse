## title
Ganglion supplying lacrimal gland secretomotor fibres

## question
A patient complains of dryness of eyes, there was decreased secretion of lacrimal glands, the most likely ganglion involved is?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Otic ganglion

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The otic ganglion relays parasympathetic secretomotor fibres (via the glossopharyngeal nerve and lesser petrosal nerve) to the PAROTID gland, not the lacrimal gland.

## answer_b
Ciliary ganglion

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The ciliary ganglion relays parasympathetic fibres (via the oculomotor nerve) to the sphincter pupillae and ciliary muscle for pupillary constriction and accommodation — it does not supply the lacrimal gland.

## answer_c
Pterygopalatine ganglion

## explanation_c
Correct. The pterygopalatine ganglion is the parasympathetic relay station for lacrimal gland secretomotor fibres: preganglionic fibres travel in the facial nerve, its greater petrosal branch and the nerve of the pterygoid canal to synapse here, and postganglionic fibres reach the lacrimal gland via the zygomatic and lacrimal nerves. A lesion interrupting this pathway therefore reduces tear secretion and produces the dry-eye picture described. This is the reason the pterygopalatine ganglion is often called the "ganglion of hay fever" and the key relay tested for lacrimal (and nasal) gland dysfunction.

## answer_d
Superior cervical ganglion

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The superior cervical ganglion is a SYMPATHETIC ganglion; its fibres do reach the lacrimal gland as vasoconstrictor/hitch-hiking fibres, but the secretomotor (fluid-producing) supply — the one whose loss causes decreased tear secretion — is parasympathetic via the pterygopalatine ganglion.

## title
Cervical plexus cutaneous branch crossing sternocleidomastoid

## question
What is the cutaneous branch of the cervical plexus that crosses over the sternocleidomastoid muscle?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Transverse cervical

## explanation_a
Correct. The transverse (anterior) cutaneous nerve of the neck arises from the cervical plexus (C2-C3), turns around the posterior border of sternocleidomastoid at its midpoint, and runs HORIZONTALLY (transversely) forward across the muscle to supply the skin of the anterior triangle. This transverse course across the front of sternocleidomastoid is exactly what distinguishes it from the other cervical plexus cutaneous branches, which run in different directions.

## answer_b
Great auricular

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The great auricular nerve (C2-C3) ascends VERTICALLY over sternocleidomastoid toward the ear and angle of the mandible, rather than crossing it transversely.

## answer_c
Supraclavicular

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The supraclavicular nerves (C3-C4) descend over the clavicle to supply skin of the lower neck and upper chest/shoulder, not crossing transversely over sternocleidomastoid.

## answer_d
Lesser occipital

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The lesser occipital nerve (C2) ascends along the POSTERIOR border of sternocleidomastoid to supply skin behind the ear, rather than crossing over the muscle's surface transversely.

## title
Embryological origin of a lateral cervical (branchial) fistula

## question
A 2-month-old male infant had a small pit at the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle, with mucus dripping intermittently from the opening. The pit extended to the tonsillar fossa as a branchial fistula. Which of the following embryologic structure(s) is (are) involved in this anomaly?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Second pharyngeal arch

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The second pharyngeal ARCH itself (which overgrows the lower arches to form the cervical sinus) gives rise to muscles and skeletal derivatives, not the epithelial-lined tract of a fistula.

## answer_b
Second pharyngeal pouch and groove

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A persisting second pharyngeal groove alone would form a lateral cervical CYST or a blind sinus opening only externally; a complete fistula opening both externally (on the neck) and internally (at the tonsillar fossa) requires the internal pouch component as well.

## answer_c
Third pharyngeal pouch

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The third pharyngeal pouch forms the inferior parathyroid and thymus; it opens internally near the piriform fossa, not the tonsillar (palatine) fossa, so it does not match a tract ending at the tonsillar fossa.

## answer_d
Second pharyngeal pouch and cervical sinus

## explanation_d
Correct. A complete lateral cervical (branchial) fistula runs from an external opening along the anterior border of sternocleidomastoid to an internal opening in the tonsillar fossa; it forms when the second pharyngeal POUCH (which gives the internal opening at the tonsillar/palatine fossa) persists together with the cervical SINUS (the remnant of the ectodermal grooves buried by the overgrowing second arch, giving the external opening). Because the internal opening is specifically at the tonsillar fossa, the SECOND pouch — not the third — is the pouch involved. The persistence of both the pouch and the cervical sinus is what makes it a through-and-through fistula rather than a blind cyst.

## title
Structures whose fusion failure causes cleft lip

## question
A 2-day-old infant male has a noticeable gap in his upper lip. The diagnosis is a cleft lip. Failure of fusion of which of the following structures is the most likely cause of this anomaly?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Maxillary prominences/processes and the intermaxillary segment

## explanation_a
Correct. The upper lip forms when the maxillary prominence of each side fuses with the intermaxillary segment (the merged medial nasal prominences), which contributes the philtrum and the four incisor-bearing part of the maxilla. Failure of the maxillary prominence to fuse with the intermaxillary segment leaves the characteristic gap of a (lateral) cleft lip. This maxillary-to-intermaxillary fusion is the specific step that closes the upper lip, which is why its failure is the classic embryological basis of cleft lip.

## answer_b
Medial nasal prominences/processes

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The two medial nasal prominences themselves MERGE (they do not simply "fuse" as separate structures) to form the intermaxillary segment; failure of the medial nasal prominences to merge with EACH OTHER would give a rare median cleft lip, not the typical lateral cleft lip described.

## answer_c
Lateral nasal and medial nasal prominences/processes

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Failure of fusion between the lateral nasal and medial nasal prominences produces an oblique facial cleft (running toward the eye along the nasolacrimal groove), not a cleft of the upper lip.

## answer_d
Lateral prominences/processes

## explanation_d
Incorrect. "Lateral prominences" is not the pair whose fusion closes the upper lip; the upper lip specifically requires the maxillary prominence to unite with the intermaxillary segment.

Parietal association area

## title
Structure forming the posterior surface of the midbrain

## question
The posterior surface of midbrain is represented by:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Upper part of 4th ventricle

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The fourth ventricle belongs to the pons and medulla (hindbrain); the midbrain's cavity is the narrow cerebral aqueduct, not part of the fourth ventricle.

## answer_b
Lower part of 4th ventricle

## explanation_b
Incorrect. As with option A, the fourth ventricle is a hindbrain cavity, not the posterior surface of the midbrain.

## answer_c
Cerebral peduncle

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The cerebral peduncles (crura cerebri) form the ANTERIOR (ventral) surface of the midbrain, not the posterior surface.

## answer_d
Corpora quadrigemina

## explanation_d
Correct. The posterior (dorsal) surface of the midbrain is the tectum, formed by the corpora quadrigemina — the paired superior and inferior colliculi. The superior colliculi are visual reflex centres and the inferior colliculi auditory relay centres, and the trochlear nerves emerge just below the inferior colliculi. This dorsal tectal plate of four colliculi is exactly what "corpora quadrigemina" (four bodies) names.

## title
Fissure separating flocculonodular from posterior lobe

## question
The fissure that separates the flocculonodular lobe from posterior lobe of the cerebellum:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Primary fissure

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The primary fissure separates the ANTERIOR lobe from the POSTERIOR lobe of the cerebellum, not the flocculonodular lobe.

## answer_b
Horizontal fissure

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The horizontal fissure runs around the cerebellar margin dividing the superior and inferior surfaces of the posterior lobe; it does not demarcate the flocculonodular lobe.

## answer_c
Posterolateral fissure

## explanation_c
Correct. The posterolateral fissure is the fissure that separates the flocculonodular lobe (the phylogenetically oldest, vestibular part of the cerebellum) from the posterior lobe. It is the first cerebellar fissure to appear in development, which is consistent with the flocculonodular lobe being the oldest part. Recognising this fissure is what allows the three-lobe division of the cerebellum (anterior, posterior, flocculonodular) to be defined.

## answer_d
Lateral fissure

## explanation_d
Incorrect. "Lateral fissure" refers to the Sylvian fissure of the cerebrum, not a cerebellar fissure.

## title
Course of the superficial middle cerebral vein

## question
Concerning the superficial middle cerebral vein choose one best answer:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
It lies in the posterior ramus of the lateral sulcus

## explanation_a
Correct. The superficial middle cerebral vein runs along the posterior ramus of the lateral sulcus (Sylvian fissure), following it over the lateral surface of the hemisphere as it drains the areas around the fissure. From there it passes forward and downward to end in the cavernous sinus (and communicates with the transverse/superior sagittal sinuses via the superior and inferior anastomotic veins). Its position running within the lateral sulcus is the defining anatomical fact this option captures.

## answer_b
It receives the majority of inferior cerebral veins of the medial surface

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Drainage of the inferior/medial surface veins is not the role of the superficial middle cerebral vein; it drains the cortex adjacent to the lateral sulcus on the lateral surface.

## answer_c
Ends in the transverse sinus

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The superficial middle cerebral vein drains chiefly into the CAVERNOUS sinus (via the sphenoparietal sinus), not directly into the transverse sinus — the transverse sinus is reached indirectly through the inferior anastomotic vein (of Labbe).

## answer_d
Ends in the superior sagittal sinus

## explanation_d
Incorrect. It does not end in the superior sagittal sinus; that superior connection is made indirectly through the superior anastomotic vein (of Trolard).

## title
Arterial supply of the lumbar enlargement of the spinal cord

## question
The arterial supply of the lumbar enlargement of the spinal cord:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The arteria radicularis magna

## explanation_a
Correct. The arteria radicularis magna (great anterior radicular artery, or artery of Adamkiewicz) is the dominant segmental feeder that reinforces the anterior spinal artery in the lower thoracic and lumbar region, and is the principal blood supply to the lumbar (lumbosacral) enlargement of the spinal cord. Because this single large radicular artery supplies so much of the lower cord, its interruption (e.g. during aortic surgery) risks infarction of the lumbar enlargement and anterior spinal artery syndrome. This critical, size-based dominance over the lower cord is exactly why it is the specific answer rather than the anterior spinal artery in general.

## answer_b
The anterior spinal artery

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The anterior spinal artery runs the whole length of the cord but is thin and inadequate on its own in the lower regions; it depends on the arteria radicularis magna to reinforce it there — so the artery specifically supplying the lumbar enlargement is the radicularis magna.

## answer_c
The posterior spinal arteries

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The posterior spinal arteries supply only the posterior (dorsal) columns and posterior third of the cord, not the bulk of the lumbar enlargement.

## answer_d
The basilar artery

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The basilar artery supplies the brainstem and cerebellum, not the spinal cord's lumbar enlargement.
