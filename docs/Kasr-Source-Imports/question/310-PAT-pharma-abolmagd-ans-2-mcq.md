## title
Why neostigmine reverses competitive neuromuscular blockers

## question
Which of the following explains the wide use of neostigmine in reversing the action of competitive neuromuscular blockers used in surgical operations?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
A better return of memory after operation

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Neostigmine is a quaternary compound that does not enter the CNS, so it has no effect on postoperative memory.

## answer_b
Its anticholinesterase enzyme activity

## explanation_b
Correct. Competitive (non-depolarising) blockers such as vecuronium work by antagonising acetylcholine at the nicotinic end-plate. Neostigmine inhibits acetylcholinesterase, raising the concentration of acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction so that it out-competes the blocker and restores transmission. This anticholinesterase action is exactly why it reverses the block.

## answer_c
Its good CNS penetration leading to better recovery

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Neostigmine's lack of CNS penetration is a feature, but it is not the mechanism of reversal, which occurs peripherally at the junction.

## answer_d
Anti-muscarinic actions leading to decreased secretions

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Antimuscarinic action is not a property of neostigmine; atropine or glycopyrrolate is added separately to cover its muscarinic effects.

## title
Receptor whose stimulation lowers intracellular cAMP

## question
A decrease in intracellular cAMP results from stimulation of which of the following receptors?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Beta2 receptors

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Beta2 receptors couple to Gs and INCREASE cAMP via adenylyl cyclase.

## answer_b
Alpha1 receptors

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Alpha1 receptors couple to Gq and act through phospholipase C (IP3/DAG and calcium), not by lowering cAMP.

## answer_c
M2 receptors

## explanation_c
Correct. M2 muscarinic receptors couple to the inhibitory Gi protein, which inhibits adenylyl cyclase and so DECREASES intracellular cAMP; they also open potassium channels. This is the basis of the vagal slowing of the heart. Among the options only M2 stimulation lowers cAMP.

## answer_d
Nn receptors

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Nn (neuronal nicotinic) receptors are ligand-gated ion channels; they depolarise the cell rather than acting through cAMP.

## title
Therapeutic use of pilocarpine (sialagogue)

## question
Which of the following describes a therapeutic use of the cholinomimetic alkaloid pilocarpine?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Sialagogue in dryness of mouth

## explanation_a
Correct. Pilocarpine is a direct muscarinic agonist that stimulates exocrine (salivary) glands, so it is used as a sialagogue to relieve xerostomia (dry mouth), for example after head-and-neck irradiation or in Sjogren syndrome. This glandular-secretory action is a recognised therapeutic use. It also lowers intraocular pressure in glaucoma by causing MIOSIS, not mydriasis.

## answer_b
A mydriatic in cases of glaucoma

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Pilocarpine is a MIOTIC (pupil-constricting) agent in glaucoma, not a mydriatic; the term in this option is wrong.

## answer_c
Inhibition of hair growth

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Pilocarpine does not inhibit hair growth; that is eflornithine.

## answer_d
As an add-on therapy for cases of Parkinsonism

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Parkinsonism is treated by reducing cholinergic tone with antimuscarinics, so a muscarinic agonist would worsen it.

## title
Why pralidoxime works in organophosphate poisoning

## question
Which of the following explains the use of pralidoxime in the treatment of organophosphorus poisoning?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Its sedative effect

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Pralidoxime is not a sedative; sedation in organophosphate poisoning is provided by a benzodiazepine such as diazepam.

## answer_b
Its antimuscarinic effect

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The antimuscarinic component of therapy is atropine; pralidoxime has no antimuscarinic action.

## answer_c
Its anticonvulsant effect

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Anticonvulsant cover is again the role of diazepam, not pralidoxime.

## answer_d
It reactivates the cholinesterase enzyme

## explanation_d
Correct. Organophosphates phosphorylate and inactivate acetylcholinesterase. Pralidoxime is an oxime that binds the phosphorylated enzyme and removes the phosphate group, REACTIVATING cholinesterase before irreversible 'ageing' occurs. This restores acetylcholine breakdown and relieves especially the nicotinic (muscle) features that atropine cannot touch, which is why it is given early.

## title
Contraindication of atropine

## question
Contraindications of atropine include which of the following?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Heart block

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Atropine is used to TREAT symptomatic bradycardia and some heart block, so this is an indication, not a contraindication.

## answer_b
Glaucoma

## explanation_b
Correct. Atropine relaxes the ciliary muscle and dilates the pupil, which crowds the iridocorneal angle and impairs aqueous drainage, raising intraocular pressure. In angle-closure glaucoma this can precipitate an acute dangerous rise in pressure, so glaucoma is a classic contraindication to atropine. This is why antimuscarinics are avoided or used with great caution in such patients.

## answer_c
Organophosphorus poisoning

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Organophosphate poisoning is a principal INDICATION for atropine, not a contraindication.

## answer_d
Diarrhoea

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Antimuscarinics reduce gut motility and can be used symptomatically in some diarrhoeas, so diarrhoea is not a contraindication.

## title
Ocular effect of antimuscarinic eye drops

## question
Which of the following is expected when using parasympathetic depressants as eye drops?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Pupillary constriction

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Antimuscarinics block the sphincter pupillae and cause mydriasis (dilatation), not constriction.

## answer_b
Decreased intraocular pressure

## explanation_b
Incorrect. By crowding the drainage angle they tend to RAISE intraocular pressure, not lower it.

## answer_c
Loss of accommodation

## explanation_c
Correct. Antimuscarinic (parasympathetic-depressant) eye drops paralyse the ciliary muscle, producing cycloplegia so the lens cannot accommodate for near vision. Loss of accommodation, together with mydriasis, is the expected ocular effect and is exploited for refraction and fundus examination. It is the correct answer here.

## answer_d
Bronchospasm

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Antimuscarinics cause bronchodilation, not bronchospasm, and topical eye drops have little systemic airway effect anyway.

## title
How hyoscine differs from atropine on the CNS

## question
Compared to atropine, hyoscine is more likely to:

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Hyoscine is used to PREVENT nausea and vomiting (motion sickness), not to induce it.

## answer_b

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Atropine has the stronger cardiac vagolytic effect and is more likely to cause tachycardia; hyoscine affects the heart less.

Incorrect. Central excitation and convulsions at toxic doses are more characteristic of atropine; hyoscine is depressant at therapeutic doses.

Correct. Hyoscine (scopolamine) penetrates the CNS well and at therapeutic doses causes sedation, drowsiness and amnesia, a central DEPRESSANT effect. Atropine, by contrast, tends to be centrally stimulant. This difference is why hyoscine is chosen as a sedative antiemetic and premedicant.

## title
Antimuscarinic precipitating retention in prostatic hypertrophy

## question
An elderly man with benign prostatic hypertrophy recently complained of recurrent intestinal colic. A drug was prescribed but caused acute urine retention. Which of the following could be the prescribed drug?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Atropine

## explanation_a
Correct. Intestinal colic is treated with an antispasmodic antimuscarinic such as atropine, which relaxes gut smooth muscle. However, by also relaxing the bladder detrusor and reducing voiding, an antimuscarinic can precipitate acute retention in a man whose outflow is already narrowed by prostatic hypertrophy. This links the colic treatment to the retention, making atropine the offending drug.

## answer_b
Neostigmine

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Neostigmine increases cholinergic tone and promotes bladder emptying; it would relieve, not cause, retention.

## answer_c
Propranolol

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Propranolol is a beta-blocker used for cardiovascular indications; it is not an antispasmodic for colic and does not typically cause acute retention.

## answer_d
Carbachol

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Carbachol is a cholinergic agonist that stimulates the detrusor and promotes voiding, the opposite of causing retention.

## title
Expected effects of atropine in organophosphate poisoning

## question
In a patient with organophosphorus poisoning, which of the following pharmacological effects would you expect after treatment with atropine?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Bradycardia and bronchodilatation

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Atropine blocks vagal muscarinic tone at the heart, so it produces tachycardia, not bradycardia, though the bronchodilatation is correct.

## answer_b
Tachycardia and bronchodilatation

## explanation_b
Correct. Atropine is a muscarinic antagonist, so in organophosphate poisoning it reverses the excess muscarinic stimulation: blocking cardiac M2 receptors raises heart rate (tachycardia) and blocking airway M3 receptors relaxes bronchial smooth muscle and dries secretions (bronchodilatation). This pairing of tachycardia and bronchodilatation is the expected antimuscarinic response.

## answer_c
Hypotension and bronchoconstriction

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Hypotension and bronchoconstriction are features of the cholinergic crisis itself, which atropine reverses.

## answer_d
Bradycardia and hypotension

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Bradycardia and hypotension are the untreated muscarinic effects, the opposite of what atropine produces.
