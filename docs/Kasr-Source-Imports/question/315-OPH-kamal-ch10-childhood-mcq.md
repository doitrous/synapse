## title
First-line treatment of congenital nasolacrimal duct obstruction
## question
What is the first line of treatment in congenital nasolacrimal duct obstruction?
## subject
mul
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## vignette

## correct_answer
D
## answer_a
Probing.
## explanation_a
Incorrect. Probing is reserved for obstruction that persists beyond about a year, not the first line.
## answer_b
Intubation.
## explanation_b
Incorrect. Intubation (silicone stenting) is a later option when probing fails.
## answer_c
Dacryocystorhinostomy.
## explanation_c
Incorrect. Dacryocystorhinostomy is a surgery for established chronic obstruction, not the infant first line.
## answer_d
Massage.
## explanation_d
Correct. Most congenital obstructions resolve spontaneously, so lacrimal sac massage is the first-line treatment in early infancy.

## title
First step in a newborn with big hazy corneas, lacrimation and photophobia
## question
A newly born presented to you with big, hazy corneas, lacrimation, and photophobia. What is the first step in his management?
## subject
mul
## status
Draft
## owner
Claude
## vignette

## correct_answer
D
## answer_a
Electrophysiological study of the retina
## explanation_a
Incorrect. Electrophysiology assesses retinal function and is irrelevant to this glaucoma triad.
## answer_b
Syringing of nasolacrimal duct
## explanation_b
Incorrect. Syringing addresses tear-duct obstruction, not the raised pressure suggested here.
## answer_c
Fluorescein staining of the cornea
## explanation_c
Incorrect. Fluorescein stains epithelial defects; it does not diagnose congenital glaucoma.
## answer_d
Measuring his intraocular pressure
## explanation_d
Correct. Big hazy corneas with lacrimation and photophobia are the congenital glaucoma triad, so the first step is to measure the intraocular pressure.
