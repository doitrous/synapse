## title
Cause not producing secondary hypertension

## question
The followings may lead to secondary hypertension except:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Polycystic kidney

## explanation_a
Incorrect (this IS a cause of secondary hypertension). Polycystic kidney disease is a renal cause of secondary hypertension, and renal disease is the commonest cause of the secondary form overall.

## answer_b
Cushing syndrome

## explanation_b
Incorrect (this IS a cause of secondary hypertension). Cushing syndrome raises blood pressure through excess cortisol (mineralocorticoid-like sodium and water retention), a recognised endocrine cause of secondary hypertension.

## answer_c
Hypothyroidism

## explanation_c
Correct. Secondary hypertension is high blood pressure with an identifiable underlying cause, and its named causes are renal (e.g. polycystic kidney, chronic glomerulonephritis, renal artery stenosis), endocrine (Cushing syndrome, phaeochromocytoma, hyperaldosteronism, hyperthyroidism), cardiovascular (coarctation of the aorta) and toxaemia of pregnancy. HYPOthyroidism is not on this list — it is if anything associated with a tendency to a low metabolic/cardiac-output state, so it is the exception here. This is exactly the endocrine-cause distinction the question tests: hyperthyroidism can raise pressure, hypothyroidism does not cause secondary hypertension.

## answer_d
Toxemia of pregnancy

## explanation_d
Incorrect (this IS a cause of secondary hypertension). Toxaemia of pregnancy (pre-eclampsia) is a named pregnancy-related cause of secondary hypertension.

Blood diseases

Incorrect. Blood diseases are not among the named causes of secondary hypertension.

## subject

## answer_b

## explanation_b
Incorrect (this IS a cause of renal hypertension). Chronic glomerulonephritis is a classic renal parenchymal cause of secondary hypertension.

Cause of hyaline arteriolar change

## question
Hyaline change in arteriolar wall is due to:

## subject
cvs

## status

## answer_a
Malignant hypertension

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Malignant (accelerated) hypertension produces FIBRINOID necrosis of arteriolar walls and hyperplastic (onion-skin) arteriolosclerosis, not the bland hyaline change of the benign form.

## answer_b
Benign hypertension

## explanation_b
Correct. Benign (long-standing, moderate) hypertension causes hyaline arteriolosclerosis — a homogeneous, glassy pink (hyaline) thickening of the arteriolar wall from leakage of plasma proteins across the injured endothelium and increased smooth-muscle matrix production. This narrows the lumen and is the hallmark small-vessel lesion of benign hypertension (and of diabetes). It contrasts with the fibrinoid necrosis and hyperplastic arteriolosclerosis seen in malignant hypertension.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Polyarteritis nodosa is a necrotising vasculitis of medium-sized arteries with fibrinoid necrosis, not hyaline arteriolar thickening.

## answer_d
Atherosclerosis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Atherosclerosis affects large and medium ELASTIC and muscular arteries (intimal atheromatous plaques), not the hyaline change of small arterioles.

Kidney surface in benign hypertension

## question
The surface of the kidney in benign hypertension is:

## subject
cvs

## status

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Smooth

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A perfectly smooth surface is the normal kidney; benign hypertensive nephrosclerosis makes the surface finely granular, not smooth.

## answer_b
Finely granular

## explanation_b
Correct. Benign nephrosclerosis (the renal lesion of benign hypertension) produces symmetrically contracted kidneys with a FINELY GRANULAR (‘leather-grain’) surface. The fine granularity results from hyaline arteriolosclerosis narrowing arterioles, causing diffuse patchy ischaemic atrophy and scarring of nephrons over a fine scale. This contrasts with the coarse, irregular scars of chronic pyelonephritis and with the petechial ‘flea-bitten’ kidney of the malignant phase.

Coarsely irregular

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A coarsely irregular, deeply scarred surface points to chronic pyelonephritis or infarct scars, not the fine, even granularity of benign nephrosclerosis.

## answer_d
Flea-bitten

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A ‘flea-bitten’ kidney (fine petechial haemorrhages on the surface) is the appearance of MALIGNANT hypertension, not benign.

## explanation_a
Correct. Atherosclerosis is a disease of ARTERIES — specifically the intima of large elastic arteries (aorta, carotid, iliac) and medium muscular arteries (coronary, popliteal) — where lipid-laden atheromatous plaques form. It does not affect veins or the myocardium directly; its damage to the heart is indirect, through coronary-artery narrowing causing ischaemia. Naming the artery as the target vessel is the basic definition being tested.

## answer_b
Veins

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Veins are not affected by atherosclerosis (they may undergo other changes such as phlebosclerosis or varicosity, but not atheroma).

## answer_c
Myocardium

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The myocardium is injured only SECONDARILY (ischaemia/infarction) when coronary arteries are diseased; atherosclerosis is not a primary myocardial disease.

## answer_d
All of the above

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Because veins and myocardium are not primary targets, ‘all of the above’ is wrong; atherosclerosis is an arterial disease.

## subject

## answer_a
Inflammatory disease

## explanation_a
Incorrect per this source. Modern pathology does increasingly describe atherosclerosis as having a chronic inflammatory component (the response-to-injury hypothesis), but the answer keyed in this department-style bank is ‘degenerative disease’ — so ‘inflammatory’ is not the intended answer here.

## answer_b
Degenerative disease

## explanation_b
Correct (per this source). This bank classifies atherosclerosis as a DEGENERATIVE arterial disease — a chronic, age-related degeneration of the intima with lipid deposition, fibrosis and calcification of the vessel wall. The term captures the slow, wear-and-deposition nature of plaque build-up in large and medium arteries. (Note for the learner: contemporary teaching also emphasises a chronic inflammatory/response-to-injury element, but the classification asked for here is degenerative.)

## answer_c
Autoimmune disease

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Atherosclerosis is not an autoimmune disease; there is no autoantibody-mediated attack on the artery driving it.

## answer_d
Embolic lesion

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Atherosclerosis is not an embolic lesion — emboli (e.g. cholesterol emboli) are a COMPLICATION of established plaques, not the nature of the disease itself.

The most important risk factor for atherosclerosis in patients under 45 years is:

## subject

## status

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Smoking is an important modifiable risk factor for atherosclerosis, but in the under-45 group hyperlipidaemia is singled out as the most important.

## answer_b
Male sex

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Male sex is a non-modifiable risk factor, but it is not the single most important factor in the young.

## answer_c
Lack of physical exercise

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Physical inactivity contributes to risk but is a lesser, indirect factor compared with hyperlipidaemia in young patients.

## answer_d
Hyperlipidemia

## explanation_d
Correct. In patients under 45 years, HYPERLIPIDAEMIA (especially familial/genetic dyslipidaemias with very high LDL) is the most important risk factor for premature atherosclerosis, because a lifelong high LDL burden drives early, aggressive intimal lipid deposition. Severe inherited hypercholesterolaemia can cause clinically significant atherosclerosis and coronary disease at an unusually young age. This is why a young patient with premature atherosclerosis is investigated for a lipid disorder first.

Not a major atherosclerosis risk factor

## question
Major risk factors for the development of atherosclerosis include all of the following lesions except:

## subject
cvs

## status

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Cigarette smoking

## explanation_a
Incorrect (this IS a major risk factor). Cigarette smoking is a major modifiable risk factor, promoting endothelial injury and atherogenesis.

## answer_b
Hypocholesterolemia

## explanation_b
Correct. HYPOcholesterolaemia (a LOW blood cholesterol) is not a risk factor for atherosclerosis — it is HYPERcholesterolaemia (high cholesterol, especially high LDL) that drives plaque formation. Naming a low cholesterol as a risk factor reverses the actual lipid relationship, so it is the exception. The genuine major risk factors are hypertension, diabetes mellitus, smoking and hyperlipidaemia.

## answer_c
Hypertension

## explanation_c
Incorrect (this IS a major risk factor). Hypertension is a major modifiable risk factor for atherosclerosis.

## answer_d
DM

## explanation_d
Incorrect (this IS a major risk factor). Diabetes mellitus is a major risk factor, accelerating atherosclerosis through dyslipidaemia and endothelial damage.
