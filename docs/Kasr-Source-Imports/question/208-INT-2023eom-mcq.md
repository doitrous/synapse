## title
Chemotaxis definition

## question
Chemotaxis is:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Phagocytosis of infective agent

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Phagocytosis is the recognition, engulfment and killing of a particle once a leukocyte has already reached it — a separate step of leukocyte activation, not the movement that gets the leukocyte there in the first place.

## answer_b
Rolling of leukocytes on endothelial surface

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Rolling is the loose, tumbling adhesion of leukocytes to activated endothelium, mediated by selectins, and it happens while the leukocyte is still inside the vessel — chemotaxis happens afterwards, once the leukocyte is already in the tissue.

## answer_c
Adhesion of leukocytes to endothelium

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Adhesion to endothelium (pavementing) is the firm attachment step mediated by integrins, occurring before emigration — it describes a leukocyte still at the vessel wall, not one already moving through extravascular tissue.

## answer_d
Movement of extravasated neutrophils toward infective agent

## explanation_d
Correct. After extravasating from the blood, leukocytes move toward sites of infection or injury along a chemical gradient — this directed movement is chemotaxis, driven by bacterial products, chemokines, complement C5a and leukotriene B4. This ordered handoff — margination and rolling, then firm adhesion, then emigration, then chemotaxis — is what actually delivers a leukocyte to the site of injury, and chemotaxis is specifically its final, directional step.

## title
Macrophage fate after inflammation

## question
Which is FALSE about macrophages:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
They influence process of repair

## explanation_a
Incorrect (this is true, not the FALSE option). Macrophages secrete growth factors — epidermal growth factor, fibroblast growth factor, transforming growth factor beta — that are important in the process of repair, so they genuinely influence healing.

## answer_b
After the end of inflammation they die and wander off into blood stream

## explanation_b
Correct — this is the FALSE statement. Once the initiating stimulus is eliminated and the inflammatory reaction ends, macrophages die or wander off into the lymphatics, not into the blood stream; only in chronic inflammatory sites, where the stimulus persists, does macrophage accumulation continue through ongoing recruitment and local proliferation. The distractor swaps "lymphatics" for "blood stream," which reverses the correct route macrophages actually take once their job is done.

## answer_c
They are derived from monocytes

## explanation_c
Incorrect (this is true, not the FALSE option). Macrophages are derived from blood monocytes, which leave the vessels at the site of inflammation and transform into active, motile, phagocytic macrophages.

## answer_d
Capable of phagocytosis

## explanation_d
Incorrect (this is true, not the FALSE option). Macrophages are capable of phagocytosis, although in most acute inflammation they play a lesser phagocytic role than neutrophils until later stages, when they predominate.

## title
Diffuse suppurative inflammation example

## question
One of the following is an example of diffuse suppurative inflammation:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Carbuncle

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A carbuncle is multiple communicating deep subcutaneous abscesses opening by multiple sinuses, caused by Staphylococcus aureus's coagulase, which walls the infection off — it is a localized, not diffuse, suppurative pattern.

## answer_b
Cellulitis

## explanation_b
Correct. Cellulitis is diffuse suppurative inflammation occurring in loose tissues (subcutaneous tissue, orbit, scrotum), commonly caused by Streptococcus pyogenes, whose fibrinolysin dissolves fibrin and lets the infection spread rather than being walled off. Recognising the streptococcal-fibrinolysin mechanism behind cellulitis is what explains why it spreads through tissue planes rather than staying contained like a staphylococcal abscess.

## answer_c
Boil

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A boil (furuncle) is staphylococcal infection of a hair follicle, walled off by coagulase in the same way as an abscess or carbuncle — a localized, not diffuse, pattern.

## answer_d
Abscess

## explanation_d
Incorrect. An abscess is the defining example of localized suppurative inflammation — a discrete, walled-off collection of pus, the opposite of a spreading, diffuse process.

## title
Pseudomembranous inflammation

## question
Pseudomembranous inflammation is:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Mild form of acute suppurative inflammation

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Pseudomembranous inflammation is a severe form of acute inflammation of mucous membranes, not mild — a necrotizing toxin kills the surface epithelium outright, which is a more destructive process than ordinary suppuration.

## answer_b
Caused by toxin producing bacteria

## explanation_b
Correct. It is caused by bacteria that produce a powerful necrotizing toxin — diphtheria bacilli in the throat and larynx, and shigella bacilli in the large intestine causing bacillary dysentery — and the mucosa becomes covered by a false membrane of precipitated fibrin, necrotic epithelium and leukocytes. This toxin-driven necrosis of the surface epithelium, not simple pus formation, is what makes pseudomembranous inflammation a severe pattern in its own right.

## answer_c
Seen in serous cavities

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Involvement of serous cavities (pleura, pericardium, peritoneum) with a fibrin coating giving a 'bread and butter' appearance describes fibrinous inflammation, a different pattern from pseudomembranous inflammation, which affects mucous membranes.

## answer_d
Caused by pyogenic bacteria

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Pyogenic (pus-forming) bacteria such as staphylococci and streptococci cause suppurative inflammation; pseudomembranous inflammation is driven by a specific necrotizing toxin, not simply by pus formation.

## title
Granulation tissue

## question
Granulation tissue is:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Avascular scar tissue describes the late, remodelled scar, which granulation tissue matures into — granulation tissue itself is actively vascular, not avascular.

## answer_b

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Granulation tissue is the early stage of healing by fibrosis (scar formation), not regeneration — regeneration replaces cells with the same cell type and does not require new granulation tissue.

Incorrect. Granulation tissue is moist, red and bleeds easily, the opposite of pale and dry, which describes the mature scar after remodelling.

## explanation_d
Correct. Granulation tissue is formed of proliferated capillaries and fibroblasts (some transformed into myofibroblasts) — the granular appearance of the new capillaries at the base of a healing wound gives the tissue its name. This composition is also why granulation tissue bleeds so readily on contact, a bedside sign clinicians use to recognise it in a healing wound bed.

## title
Tissues that do not heal by regeneration

## question
All of the following can heal by regeneration EXCEPT:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Liver cells

## explanation_a
Incorrect (this tissue DOES regenerate, making it not the exception). Hepatocytes are a stable population with strong regenerative capacity — injury confined to the parenchymal cells alone is followed by regeneration and complete restitution.

## answer_b
Endometrial lining

## explanation_b
Incorrect (this tissue DOES regenerate, making it not the exception). Endometrial lining is labile surface epithelium with continuous replicating activity, so it regenerates readily — it is shed and rebuilt every cycle.

Nerve cells

## explanation_c
Correct. Nerve cells (neurons) are permanent, terminally differentiated cells that are irreversibly post-mitotic in postnatal life — the department book states plainly that neurons do not effectively regenerate, so injury to them results in a scar rather than restitution. This is exactly why central nervous system injury, unlike injury to labile epithelium or bone marrow, always heals by scar rather than by restoring functioning neurons.

## answer_d
Hematopoietic cells

## explanation_d
Incorrect (this tissue DOES regenerate, making it not the exception). Hematopoietic cells are classic labile tissue with continuous mitotic division, giving them strong regenerative capacity.

## title
Liver cirrhosis mechanism

## question
In liver healing by fibrosis (liver cirrhosis) occurs if:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Injury affects parenchymal cells only

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Injury confined to the parenchymal (hepatocyte) cells alone is followed by regeneration and complete restitution — the department book states this explicitly as the case that heals without scarring.

## answer_b
Injury affects portal tracts only

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Portal tract involvement alone is not the department book's stated cirrhosis mechanism — cirrhosis requires destruction of both the hepatocytes and the surrounding connective-tissue architecture together, not the portal tracts in isolation.

## answer_c
Injury affects the peritoneal covering

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The peritoneal (serosal) covering of the liver is not the site the department book names for the cirrhosis mechanism — the relevant structures are the hepatocytes and the internal tissue framework, not the outer covering.

## answer_d
Injury affects liver cells and the framework

## explanation_d
Correct. When chronic injury destroys both the hepatocytes and the connective-tissue architecture — as in chronic hepatitis — the hepatocytes regenerate but fail to reconstruct the original architecture, forming nodules surrounded by fibrous scarring: cirrhosis. This is the mechanistic reason cirrhosis is irreversible once established — the regenerated nodules can restore cell mass but never the original architecture.

Chemotactic factors

## question
Chemotactic factors include the following:

## subject
fnd

## status

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Histamine is a vasoactive amine that causes arteriolar dilation and rapidly increases vascular permeability — it is a mediator of the vascular phase of inflammation, not a chemotactic factor that directs leukocyte movement.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Prostaglandins potentiate the effect of histamine and contribute to pain and fever — they are arachidonic acid metabolites with vascular and systemic effects, not chemotactic agents.

Recognising C5a specifically (rather than histamine or prostaglandins) as chemotactic is what separates the vascular mediators of inflammation from the ones that actually summon leukocytes to the site.

## answer_d
Lysosomal enzymes

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Lysosomal enzymes (collagenase, cathepsin) destroy phagocytosed material and can cause tissue damage once released — they act after a leukocyte has already arrived, not as a signal that draws it there.

## title
Cellular events of acute inflammation

## question
As regard the cellular events of acute inflammation:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Neutrophils then monocytes accumulate within the extravascular space

## explanation_a
Correct. Leukocyte recruitment proceeds neutrophils first, then monocytes, accumulating within the extravascular space — this order (not the reverse) is stated directly in the department book's account of cellular events. Getting this order right also explains why a blood film taken early in acute inflammation is neutrophil-predominant, with monocytes rising only later as the reaction matures.

## answer_b
The adhesion of neutrophils to the vascular endothelium at the sites of acute inflammation is called 'rolling'

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The firm adhesion of leukocytes to the endothelium is called pavementing, mediated by integrins; rolling is the earlier, loose, tumbling adhesion mediated by selectins — the two terms name different steps, and this option applies the wrong one.

## answer_c
Integrin play a role in margination

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Margination is the physical shift of neutrophils out of the axial blood stream into the peripheral plasmatic zone, caused by slowed flow and increased plasma viscosity — it precedes any adhesion molecule engagement; integrins act at the later pavementing (firm adhesion) step, not margination.

## answer_d
Emigration of leucocytes is followed by leucopenia

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Emigration of leukocytes out of the vessel depletes the local marginating pool, not the total circulating white cell count — the systemic response to acute inflammation is typically leukocytosis (driven by cytokine-stimulated colony-stimulating factors), not leucopenia.

## title
Key cells of chronic inflammation

## question
The key cells in chronic inflammation:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Lymphocytes

## explanation_a
This is also textbook-correct (the department book's own formative-assessment answer names lymphocytes, plasma cells and macrophages together as the chronic-inflammation cell types), but the paper's own printed key marks (c) as the answer, and macrophages are singled out as THE key cell because they drive both the tissue damage and the fibrotic repair that define the chronic picture.

## answer_b
Plasma cells

## explanation_b
This is also textbook-correct for the same reason as (a) — plasma cells (activated B lymphocytes) are named among the chronic inflammation cell types — but the keyed answer is macrophages specifically, the cell the department book calls 'a key cell in chronic inflammation' in its own dedicated subsection.

Macrophages

## explanation_c
Correct. The macrophage is the key cell in chronic inflammation: it is activated by microbial products or T-cell cytokines to proliferate, persist at the site, and secrete both the proteolytic enzymes that cause ongoing tissue damage and the growth factors that drive fibrotic repair. Recognising macrophages as the single defining cell — rather than any one of the several mononuclear types present — is what lets a student distinguish chronic inflammation from the neutrophil-dominated picture of an acute process.

## answer_d
Eosinophils

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Eosinophils are characteristically found in parasitic infections and IgE-mediated allergic reactions, not as the general key cell of chronic inflammation, which is dominated by macrophages, lymphocytes and plasma cells.

Incorrect. Loss of normal polarity (architectural orientation) is a defining feature of anaplasia, not something anaplastic cells retain — this is the reverse of the true relationship.

## explanation_b
This statement is also textbook-true — anaplastic cells do show cytologic abnormalities (pleomorphism, hyperchromatism, abnormal mitoses), which is what anaplasia means — but the paper's own printed key marks (c) as the intended answer, and grading by differentiation is the more specific fact this question is built to test.

Correct. Better differentiated tumors tend to show great cellular anaplasia and grow faster

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The relationship runs the opposite way — better-differentiated tumors show LESS cellular anaplasia and grow SLOWER, while undifferentiated tumors show the greatest anaplasia and grow fastest.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. An increase in the number of cells by division describes hyperplasia, the companion adaptation to hypertrophy but mechanistically distinct from it.

## subject

## explanation_a
Correct — this is the EXCEPT. The department book lists increased hydrostatic pressure, not decreased, as a cause of edema: impaired venous return (as in congestive heart failure) or venous obstruction (by thrombus, or external compression such as pregnancy) raises the pressure pushing fluid out of vessels. Lymphatic obstruction — congenital hypoplasia (Milroy disease), inflammatory lymphadenitis or filariasis, neoplastic permeation, or post-surgical/post-irradiation fibrosis — is one of the book's listed causes, since lymphatics are what drain the excess interstitial fluid that hydrostatic and osmotic forces do not reabsorb.

Renal hypoperfusion

## explanation_d
Incorrect (this is a true cause, not the EXCEPT). Sodium retention, including from renal hypoperfusion, is one of the book's listed causes of edema — retained sodium retains water with it, expanding the interstitial fluid volume.

## explanation_a

Increased blood inflow describes hyperemia, an active process due to arteriolar and capillary vasodilatation (as in exercising skeletal muscle or at sites of inflammation), not congestion — the book keeps these two red-tissue conditions apart precisely because one is active (inflow) and the other passive (outflow).

Active vasodilatation of arterioles and capillaries

## subject

## explanation_a

## title
Major source of pulmonary emboli

## question
The major source of pulmonary emboli is:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Mural thrombi complicating myocardial infarcts of left side of heart

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Left-sided mural thrombi from myocardial infarcts embolize down the systemic arterial tree, not into the pulmonary circulation — the book places them among the sources of systemic embolization, since blood on the left side of the heart flows out to the aorta and its branches, not through the lungs.

## answer_b
Deep leg vein thrombi

## explanation_b
Correct. The department book states that most pulmonary emboli come from the deep leg veins, and may also arise from thrombi in the iliac and pelvic veins or the right side of the heart. Venous thrombi almost always occlude the vein and embolize easily, because veins are thin-walled, superficial and therefore easily injured, and blood flow within them is slow — all factors that favour both thrombus formation and its later dislodgement. ## title
Contraindicated combination with propranolol in angina

## question
It is contraindicated to prescribe Propranolol for a patient suffering from angina pectoris who is already receiving:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Nifedipine

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Combining a beta-blocker with a dihydropyridine such as nifedipine is a favourable combination: the dihydropyridine's arteriolar vasodilation is offset by the beta-blocker preventing the reflex tachycardia it would otherwise cause.

## answer_b
Amlodipine

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Amlodipine is also a dihydropyridine calcium-channel blocker, and pairing a dihydropyridine with a beta-blocker is favourable for the same reflex-tachycardia-blunting reason as nifedipine.

## answer_c
Verapamil

## explanation_c
Correct. Verapamil, like diltiazem, is a non-dihydropyridine calcium-channel blocker with its own negative chronotropic (decreased AV conduction) and negative inotropic effects; combining it with a beta-blocker such as propranolol is unfavourable, carrying a severe risk of cardiac inhibition -- bradycardia, heart failure, or even asystole -- because both drugs suppress the sinus and AV nodes and myocardial contractility together. This is precisely why verapamil and diltiazem, unlike the dihydropyridines, are singled out as not to be combined with beta-blockers or digoxin.

## answer_d
Isosorbide mononitrate

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Combining a beta-blocker with a nitrate is favourable: the nitrate's venodilation and reflex tachycardia are offset by the beta-blocker, while the beta-blocker's tendency to raise preload (from bradycardia-driven longer diastolic filling) is offset by the nitrate.

Molecular action of isosorbide mononitrate in angina

## question
A 53-year-old man diagnosed with exertional angina was prescribed oral isosorbide mononitrate. Which of the following molecular actions most likely mediate the therapeutic efficacy of this drug in the patient's disease?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Decreased synthesis of guanylyl cyclase

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Organic nitrates activate, rather than decrease the synthesis of, guanylyl cyclase -- and the mechanism is enzyme activation, not a change in how much of the enzyme is made.

## answer_b
Phosphorylation of the myosin light chain kinase

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Organic nitrates cause dephosphorylation, not phosphorylation, of myosin light chain kinase, which reduces active MLCK and leads to failed actin-myosin interaction and smooth-muscle relaxation -- the opposite direction from this option.

## answer_c
Increased intracellular Ca2+ concentration

## explanation_c
Incorrect. High intracellular cGMP from nitrate action tends to reduce, not increase, calcium entry into vascular smooth muscle, contributing further to relaxation.

## answer_d
Nitric oxide release and stimulated formation of cGMP

## explanation_d
Correct. Organic nitrates such as isosorbide mononitrate are de-nitrated in the body, releasing nitric oxide, which activates guanylyl cyclase to form cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP). Elevated cGMP causes dephosphorylation of myosin light chain kinase and reduced calcium entry, producing vascular smooth-muscle relaxation -- powerful venodilation that reduces preload and myocardial oxygen demand, which is the mechanistic basis for nitrates' efficacy in exertional angina.

Which of the following combinations of drugs reduce both preload and afterload?

## explanation_a

Hydralazine and diazoxide

Incorrect. Hydralazine and diazoxide are both predominantly arteriolar vasodilators, both reducing afterload without a significant venodilating, preload-reducing partner drug in this pairing.

## question

## subject
fnd

## status

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Occurs more commonly with carcinomas than sarcomas

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The department book states this carcinoma-over-sarcoma frequency specifically for LYMPHATIC spread, not hematogenous (blood) spread -- the two routes are not interchangeable in this teaching, and this option misapplies a fact from the wrong route.

## answer_b
Most tumor emboli are destroyed by immune mechanisms

## explanation_b
Correct. Not every tumor cell that enters the circulation as an embolus survives to seed a metastasis: most tumor emboli are destroyed by host immune mechanisms. The survivors are protected in part by adhering to platelets, which shields them from antitumor host immune cells, and only these survivors go on to become impacted in small vessels, cross the endothelium and basement membrane, and "home" into a new site to form a secondary (metastatic) deposit. This immune-clearance step is why blood-borne tumor cell entry into the circulation, which happens constantly once a tumor invades a vessel wall, does not translate into metastasis anywhere near as often as the raw number of circulating tumor cells would suggest.

## answer_c
The most common sites of metastases include the muscles, spleen, pancreas & intestine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The department book states the opposite: the most common sites of metastases are the liver, lungs, bones and brain, while metastases are specifically RARE in muscles, spleen, pancreas and intestine.

## answer_d
Prostatic carcinoma preferentially spreads to brain

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The department book's paravertebral-venous-plexus mechanism explains why prostatic (and thyroid) carcinoma shows frequent VERTEBRAL metastases, not preferential spread to the brain.
