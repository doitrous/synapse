## title
Disease with Roth spots and splinter haemorrhages

## question
What is the disease characterized by infection of diseased cardiac valves and embolic manifestations in the form of Roth spots and splinter haemorrhage?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Acute rheumatic fever

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Acute rheumatic fever produces small sterile warty vegetations along the lines of valve closure, not the embolic Roth spots and splinter haemorrhages of an infected valve.

## answer_b
Acute infective endocarditis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Acute infective endocarditis is a rapidly destructive infection of previously NORMAL valves by virulent organisms; the picture of infection seeded onto already-DISEASED valves with peripheral embolic stigmata is the subacute form.

## answer_c
Subacute infective endocarditis

## explanation_c
Correct. SUBACUTE infective endocarditis characteristically infects previously DAMAGED or abnormal valves with low-virulence organisms (e.g. Streptococcus viridans). Its peripheral embolic and immune-complex phenomena are classic: Roth spots (retinal haemorrhages with pale centres), splinter (subungual) haemorrhages, Osler nodes, Janeway lesions and microscopic haematuria. This combination of infected diseased valves plus embolic manifestations defines subacute infective endocarditis.

## answer_d
Cardiomyopathy

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Cardiomyopathy is a primary myocardial disease and does not produce valvular vegetations or embolic Roth spots and splinter haemorrhages.

## question
The immune reaction of rheumatic heart is:

## subject
cvs

Draft

## vignette

## correct_answer

## answer_a
Type IV hypersensitivity

## explanation_a
Incorrect (per the source key). A delayed T-cell (type IV) component does contribute to rheumatic carditis, but the reaction is classified here as antibody-mediated type II; see the author note.

## answer_b
Type II hypersensitivity

## explanation_b
Correct. Rheumatic heart disease is an antibody-mediated (TYPE II) hypersensitivity: antibodies raised against group A streptococcal M protein cross-react with structurally similar antigens in the heart valves and myocardium (molecular mimicry), binding host tissue and triggering complement and inflammatory injury. This anti-tissue antibody cross-reactivity is why it is grouped with type II reactions. It is a post-streptococcal immune sequel, not a direct infection of the heart.

## answer_c
Type III hypersensitivity

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Type III (immune-complex) hypersensitivity underlies post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis and polyarteritis nodosa, not the cross-reactive antibody injury of rheumatic carditis.

## answer_d
Type I hypersensitivity

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Type I (IgE, immediate) hypersensitivity mediates atopy and anaphylaxis and has no role in rheumatic heart disease.

Non-bacterial thrombotic endocarditis may be associated with the following conditions except:

## subject

## answer_a
Carcinoma of pancreas

## explanation_a

## answer_d
Disseminated intravascular coagulation

## explanation_d
Incorrect (not the exception). Disseminated intravascular coagulation is a hypercoagulable, thrombosis-prone state and is a recognised association of non-bacterial thrombotic endocarditis.

Organism related to rheumatic fever

## question
Rheumatic fever is related to:

## subject
cvs

## status

## owner

## answer_a
Group A alpha-haemolytic streptococci

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Group A streptococci are BETA-haemolytic, not alpha; alpha-haemolytic streptococci (e.g. Strep viridans) cause subacute infective endocarditis, not rheumatic fever.

## answer_b
Group B beta-haemolytic streptococci

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Group B beta-haemolytic streptococci (Strep agalactiae) cause neonatal sepsis and meningitis, not rheumatic fever.

## answer_c
Group A beta-haemolytic streptococci

## explanation_c
Correct. Rheumatic fever follows pharyngitis by GROUP A BETA-HAEMOLYTIC streptococcus (Streptococcus pyogenes). Antibodies against its M protein cross-react with cardiac and other host tissues (molecular mimicry) two to four weeks after the throat infection. This specific organism, group A beta-haemolytic streptococcus, is the trigger.

## answer_d
Group B alpha-haemolytic streptococci

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Group B alpha-haemolytic streptococci are not the cause; rheumatic fever is specifically linked to group A beta-haemolytic streptococcal pharyngitis.

## title
Commonest valve affected by rheumatic fever

## question
Most common cardiac valve affected by rheumatic fever is:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Aortic valve

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The aortic valve is the SECOND most commonly involved valve in rheumatic heart disease, frequently together with the mitral, but it is not the commonest.

## answer_b
Mitral valve

## explanation_b
Correct. The MITRAL valve is the valve most commonly affected by rheumatic fever, involved in the great majority of cases (often with the aortic valve). Its high left-sided pressures and mechanical stress make it most vulnerable to the recurrent rheumatic valvulitis that leads to mitral stenosis and regurgitation. Mitral, then aortic, is the usual order of frequency.

## answer_c
Tricuspid valve

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Tricuspid involvement in rheumatic disease is uncommon and almost always accompanies mitral and aortic disease.

## answer_d
Pulmonary valve

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The pulmonary valve is very rarely affected by rheumatic fever.
