## title
What decides a transmitter's excitatory or inhibitory action

## question
The excitatory or inhibitory action of a neurotransmitter is determined by which of the following?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The function of its postsynaptic receptor

## explanation_a
Correct. Whether a transmitter excites or inhibits the postsynaptic cell is set not by the transmitter itself but by the receptor (and the ion channel it gates) on the postsynaptic membrane. The same transmitter can do either job depending on the receptor: acetylcholine excites at nicotinic receptors of skeletal muscle yet slows the heart through muscarinic (M2) receptors, and glutamate is generally excitatory while GABA is inhibitory because of the channels their receptors open. A receptor that opens Na+ (or Ca2+) channels produces an EPSP; one that opens Cl-/K+ channels produces an IPSP.

## answer_b
Its molecular composition

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The molecular structure of the transmitter determines which receptors it can bind, but it does not by itself dictate an excitatory versus inhibitory outcome — the same molecule can be either, depending on the postsynaptic receptor engaged.

## answer_c
The shape of the synaptic vesicle in which it is contained

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Vesicle shape/size relates to how transmitter is packaged and released, not to whether the postsynaptic response is excitatory or inhibitory.

## answer_d
The distance between the pre- and post-synaptic membranes

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The synaptic cleft width (~20 nm) affects diffusion time but not the sign of the response; excitation versus inhibition is decided at the postsynaptic receptor.

## title
How synaptic transmission is terminated

## question
Synaptic transmission is terminated by which of the following?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Block of postsynaptic receptors

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Blocking the postsynaptic receptor (as a drug/toxin might) prevents a response, but it is not the physiological mechanism that clears released transmitter and ends normal synaptic signalling.

## answer_b
Elevation of Ca2+ in the presynaptic knobs

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A rise in presynaptic Ca2+ triggers and sustains transmitter RELEASE (and underlies post-tetanic potentiation); it starts transmission rather than terminating it.

## answer_c
Reuptake of neurotransmitter by the postsynaptic neuron

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Reuptake does terminate signalling for many transmitters, but it is carried out by the PRESYNAPTIC terminal (and neighbouring glia) via membrane transporters — not by the postsynaptic neuron, so this option is wrong as stated.

## answer_d
Degradation of neurotransmitter by specific enzymes

## explanation_d
Correct. Once released, transmitter action is ended by three routes: enzymatic degradation, reuptake into the presynaptic terminal/glia, and simple diffusion out of the cleft. Enzymatic degradation is the option that is correct here — the classic example is acetylcholinesterase, which hydrolyses acetylcholine in the cleft (monoamine oxidase and catechol-O-methyltransferase play the analogous role for monoamines). Rapid removal is what restores the synapse for the next signal and prevents continuous receptor activation.

## title
Mechanism of tetanus toxin

## question
Tetanus toxin leads to:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Flaccid paralysis due to inhibition of acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Inhibition of acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction, causing FLACCID paralysis, is the action of BOTULINUM toxin — the opposite toxin to tetanus.

## answer_b
Convulsions due to competitive inhibition with excitatory transmitters

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Tetanus toxin does not act by competing with excitatory transmitters; it blocks the release of INHIBITORY transmitters, and the resulting picture is sustained muscle spasm rather than a simple competitive block.

## answer_c
Spastic paralysis due to inhibition of acetylcholine release

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Tetanus does cause spastic paralysis, but not by inhibiting acetylcholine release; acetylcholine transmission at the motor end-plate is preserved (indeed unopposed).

## answer_d
Spastic paralysis due to inhibition of inhibitory (GABA/glycine) transmitter release

## explanation_d
Correct. Tetanospasmin is taken up by motor terminals, transported retrogradely to the spinal cord, and blocks the release of the inhibitory transmitters glycine (and GABA) from Renshaw cells and other inhibitory interneurons. Losing this inhibition leaves the alpha motor neurons unopposed, producing continuous muscle contraction — spastic paralysis with the characteristic lockjaw (trismus) and opisthotonus. This disinhibition mechanism is exactly the mirror image of botulinum toxin, which blocks excitatory acetylcholine release to cause flaccid paralysis.
