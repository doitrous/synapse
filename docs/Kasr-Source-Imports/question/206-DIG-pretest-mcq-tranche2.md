## title
GI hormone whose receptor erythromycin activates

## question
A 17-year-old man treated with the macrolide antibiotic erythromycin complains of nausea, intestinal cramping and diarrhoea. These effects result from the antibiotic binding receptors on gut enteric nerves and smooth muscle that recognise which gastrointestinal hormone?

## subject
gi

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Gastrin

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Gastrin stimulates acid secretion and mucosal growth; it does not trigger the interdigestive motor pattern that erythromycin mimics.

## answer_b
Secretin

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Secretin drives pancreatic bicarbonate secretion in response to duodenal acid and is unrelated to the macrolide effect.

## answer_c
Cholecystokinin

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Cholecystokinin causes gallbladder contraction and pancreatic enzyme secretion, not the migrating motor complex activity erythromycin provokes.

## answer_d
Motilin

## explanation_d
Correct. Motilin is the gut peptide that initiates the migrating motor complex during the interdigestive period, exciting smooth muscle directly and through enteric nerves. Erythromycin acts as a motilin-receptor agonist, so it stimulates strong gut contractions. This is why the antibiotic commonly causes nausea, cramping and diarrhoea, and is used clinically as a prokinetic.

## answer_e
Enterogastrone

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Enterogastrone is an inhibitory signal that slows gastric function; it does not mediate the prokinetic action of erythromycin.

## title
Pancreatic enzyme whose loss causes steatorrhoea in pancreatitis

## question
A 67-year-old man with alcohol abuse presents with severe epigastric pain, hypotension, abdominal distension and diarrhoea with steatorrhoea; serum amylase and lipase are raised, confirming pancreatitis. The steatorrhoea is best explained by a fall in the intraluminal concentration of which pancreatic enzyme?

## subject
gi

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Amylase

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Amylase digests starch; its loss causes carbohydrate maldigestion, not fat in the stool.

## answer_b
Trypsin

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Trypsin is a protease; deficiency impairs protein digestion rather than producing steatorrhoea.

## answer_c
Chymotrypsin

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Chymotrypsin is also a protease and does not account for fat malabsorption.

## answer_d
Lipase

## explanation_d
Correct. Pancreatic lipase is the major lipolytic enzyme that completes fat digestion in the proximal small intestine. When acinar secretion of lipase falls in pancreatitis, triglycerides are not hydrolysed. The undigested fat then passes into the stool as steatorrhoea, whereas the other enzymes listed act on carbohydrate or protein.

## answer_e
Colipase

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Colipase is only a cofactor that anchors lipase at the lipid interface; the enzyme whose intraluminal concentration primarily determines fat digestion is lipase itself.

## title
Primary genesis of lower oesophageal sphincter pressure

## question
A 53-year-old man with chronic cough and heartburn is found on manometry to have a hypotensive lower oesophageal sphincter with mild reflux. What is the primary genesis of lower oesophageal sphincter pressure in adults?

## subject
gi

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Tonic excitatory sympathetic nerve input to the smooth muscle

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Sympathetic input modulates but does not generate the resting sphincter tone.

## answer_b
Tonic excitatory parasympathetic nerve input to the smooth muscle

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Excitatory vagal (parasympathetic) input contributes to tone but is not its principal source.

## answer_c
Circulating gastrin

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Circulating gastrin has only a minor, non-primary influence on resting sphincter pressure in adults.

## answer_d
Myogenic properties of the sphincter smooth muscle

## explanation_d
Correct. The lower oesophageal sphincter is a high-pressure zone whose resting tone is chiefly an intrinsic (myogenic) property of its circular smooth muscle. Excitatory vagal input and hormones modulate this baseline rather than generate it. When the myogenic tone is lost, gastric contents reflux and cause heartburn.

## answer_e
Local production of nitric oxide by enteric nerves

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Nitric oxide from inhibitory enteric nerves relaxes the sphincter; it lowers rather than generates the pressure.

## title
Hormone elevated in Zollinger-Ellison acid hypersecretion

## question
A 42-year-old man with refractory peptic ulcer disease has data suggestive of Zollinger-Ellison syndrome. His raised basal and maximal acid output is best explained by an increase in the plasma concentration of which of the following?

## subject
gi

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Gastrin-releasing peptide

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Gastrin-releasing peptide is a neurotransmitter that stimulates G cells; it is not the circulating hormone measured to be elevated in this syndrome.

## answer_b
Secretin

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Secretin inhibits acid; indeed it paradoxically raises gastrin in gastrinoma but is not itself the cause of hypersecretion.

## answer_c
Somatostatin

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Somatostatin inhibits acid secretion, so its increase would lower, not raise, acid output.

## answer_d
Gastrin

## explanation_d
Correct. Zollinger-Ellison syndrome is a gastrinoma that secretes large amounts of gastrin outside normal feedback control. The high circulating gastrin drives parietal-cell acid hypersecretion. This produces the characteristic triad of refractory peptic ulceration, raised basal and maximal acid output, and an elevated serum gastrin.

## answer_e
Histamine

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Histamine is the local paracrine amplifier of acid secretion but is not the hormone elevated in the plasma in this syndrome.

Vitamin both made by gut bacteria and absorbed in quantity

## question
Which of the following vitamins is both synthesised by intestinal bacteria and absorbed by the host in significant quantities?

## subject
gi

## status

## owner

## vignette

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Vitamin B6 is obtained from the diet, not from significant host absorption of bacterial product.

## answer_b

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Vitamin K is synthesised by intestinal bacteria, but in this bank's framing folic acid is the vitamin taken up by the host in significant quantities; dietary vitamin K intake remains necessary.

Incorrect. Thiamine is a dietary requirement and is not appreciably supplied by gut flora.

Folic acid

## explanation_e
Correct. Several vitamins, including folic acid, vitamin K and some of the B complex, are synthesised by intestinal bacteria. Among the options, folic acid is the one the host absorbs from the intestine in significant quantities. Dietary intake of the others remains necessary because their bacterial product is not efficiently absorbed.

## title
Stimulus for gallbladder contraction after a meal

## question
A patient's abdominal pain eases after eating. Which statement about gallbladder contraction following a meal is correct?

## subject
gi

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
It is inhibited by a fat-rich meal

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A fat-rich meal is the strongest stimulus to gallbladder emptying, not an inhibitor.

## answer_b
It is inhibited by the presence of amino acids in the duodenum

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Amino acids in the duodenum release cholecystokinin and promote, rather than inhibit, gallbladder contraction.

## answer_c
It is stimulated by atropine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Vagal cholinergic input aids gallbladder contraction, so atropine blocks it rather than stimulating it.

## answer_d
It occurs in response to cholecystokinin

## explanation_d
Correct. Cholecystokinin is released from the upper small intestine by dietary fat and partly digested protein. It contracts gallbladder smooth muscle directly and through a vagovagal reflex, while inhibitory enteric nerves relax the sphincter of Oddi. Together these deliver concentrated bile into the duodenum for fat digestion.

## answer_e
It occurs simultaneously with contraction of the sphincter of Oddi

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Gallbladder contraction is coupled with relaxation of the sphincter of Oddi, not its simultaneous contraction, so bile can flow into the duodenum.

## title
Cause of malabsorption in gluten-sensitive enteropathy

## question
A 43-year-old woman with bulky diarrhoea, weight loss, microcytic anaemia, low serum calcium and low albumin is diagnosed with gluten-sensitive enteropathy. Her generalised fall in intestinal absorption is best attributed to which of the following?

## subject
gi

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Decreased intestinal motility

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Coeliac malabsorption is not caused by slowed motility; transit changes do not explain the global nutrient loss.

## answer_b
Increased migrating motor complexes

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Migrating motor complexes are a fasting motor pattern and are not the mechanism of coeliac malabsorption.

## answer_c
Decreased intestinal surface area

## explanation_c
Correct. Gluten-sensitive enteropathy causes autoimmune destruction of intestinal villi, sharply reducing the absorptive surface area. The surviving villous-tip cells also have impaired or absent transporters. As a result absorption of nutrients, minerals, electrolytes and water all fall together, giving the generalised malabsorption seen here.

## answer_d
Increased enterohepatic circulation of bile

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The enterohepatic circulation is not increased; if anything bile-salt handling suffers, and this does not explain generalised malabsorption.

## answer_e
Decreased gastric emptying

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Gastric emptying is not the limiting step; the defect is in the small-intestinal mucosa itself.

## title
Mechanism of diarrhoea in lactose intolerance

## question
An 18-year-old student has severe bloating and diarrhoea within an hour of dairy products, and her hydrogen breath test is abnormal. The diarrhoea and bloating are best explained by which of the following?

## subject
gi

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
A deficiency of the brush-border enzyme lactase

## explanation_a
Correct. Lactase, a brush-border enzyme, splits lactose into glucose and galactose. When it is deficient, unabsorbed lactose stays in the lumen and osmotically draws in water, causing diarrhoea. Colonic bacteria then ferment the sugar to gas, producing bloating and the abnormal hydrogen breath test.

## answer_b
Carbohydrate-induced secretory diarrhoea

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The diarrhoea is osmotic, not secretory: it is driven by the unabsorbed sugar's osmotic pull and ceases when lactose is withdrawn.

## answer_c
Decreased intestinal surface area

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The absorptive surface is normal; the specific enzyme lactase is lacking rather than the mucosa being reduced.

## answer_d
Decreased carbohydrate absorption in general

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Only lactose is malabsorbed; general carbohydrate absorption is intact, so this is too broad.

## answer_e
A decrease in exocrine pancreatic secretion

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Pancreatic exocrine secretion is normal; lactose is digested at the brush border, not by pancreatic enzymes.
