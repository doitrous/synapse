## title
False statement about viral conjunctivitis

## question
In viral conjunctivitis, all of the following are true, EXCEPT:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
It is self-limiting, resolving in about 14 days

## explanation_a
Incorrect (a true statement). Viral (adenoviral) conjunctivitis is self-limiting and resolves over about two weeks.

## answer_b
It is characterised by enlarged tender pre-auricular lymph nodes

## explanation_b
Incorrect (a true statement). A tender enlarged preauricular lymph node is characteristic of viral conjunctivitis.

## answer_c
It is usually non-contagious

## explanation_c
Correct. This is the false statement: viral conjunctivitis is highly CONTAGIOUS, spreading readily by contact and causing epidemics, so calling it non-contagious is wrong. The other statements are all true of it. Its contagiousness is exactly why hand hygiene and isolation are advised.

## answer_d
It is one of the causes of follicular conjunctivitis

## explanation_d
Incorrect (a true statement). Viral conjunctivitis produces a follicular conjunctival reaction.

## title
Contraindicated drop in acute anterior uveitis

## question
A 37-year-old man presents with aching pain and blurred vision in the left eye for 5 days. Slit-lamp examination shows fine keratic precipitates, aqueous cells and flare. Which of the following medications is CONTRAINDICATED?

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Topical corticosteroid eye drops

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Topical corticosteroids are a mainstay of treating anterior uveitis, to suppress the inflammation.

## answer_b
Topical pilocarpine 2% eye drops

## explanation_b
Correct. Pilocarpine, a miotic, is contraindicated in acute anterior uveitis: constricting the inflamed pupil promotes posterior synechiae and worsens pain and inflammation. The pupil should instead be dilated with a cycloplegic such as atropine to rest the ciliary body and prevent synechiae. So the miotic is the contraindicated drop.

## answer_c
Topical atropine 1% eye drops

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Atropine, a cycloplegic, is exactly what is indicated to dilate the pupil and prevent synechiae.

## answer_d
Topical timolol 0.5% eye drops

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Timolol may be added if the pressure rises, and is not contraindicated.

## title
Complication of trachoma

## question
Complications of trachoma include:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Corneal descemetocele

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A descemetocele is a complication of a deep corneal ulcer, not of trachoma.

## answer_b
Cicatricial entropion

## explanation_b
Correct. Trachoma scars the upper tarsal conjunctiva, and the contracting scar rolls the lid margin inward as a cicatricial entropion with trichiasis. The inturned lashes abrade and scar the cornea and can blind the eye. So cicatricial entropion is a classic trachoma complication.

## answer_c
Subconjunctival haemorrhage

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Subconjunctival haemorrhage is unrelated to trachoma.

## answer_d
Ptosis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Trachoma causes cicatricial entropion rather than ptosis.

## title
Disease that does not present with panuveitis

## question
Panuveitis is a common presentation in the following diseases, EXCEPT:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Behcet's disease

## explanation_a
Incorrect (it causes panuveitis). Behcet disease classically causes a panuveitis with hypopyon.

## answer_b
Rheumatoid arthritis

## explanation_b
Correct. Rheumatoid arthritis is the exception: its ocular disease is scleritis and keratoconjunctivitis sicca (dry eye), not panuveitis. Behcet, VKH and sarcoidosis are the systemic causes of panuveitis. So rheumatoid arthritis does not present as panuveitis.

## answer_c
Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada syndrome

## explanation_c
Incorrect (it causes panuveitis). Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada syndrome is a bilateral granulomatous panuveitis.

## answer_d
Sarcoidosis

## explanation_d
Incorrect (it causes panuveitis). Sarcoidosis is a classic cause of granulomatous panuveitis.

## title
Danger of topical steroids in a viral corneal ulcer

## question
Topical steroids are contraindicated in a case of viral corneal ulcer for fear of:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Secondary uveitis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Secondary uveitis is not the feared consequence of steroids in a herpetic ulcer.

## answer_b
Cortical cataract

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Cataract is a long-term steroid effect, not the acute danger in a viral ulcer.

## answer_c
Corneal perforation

## explanation_c
Correct. Topical steroids in a viral (herpes simplex) corneal ulcer suppress local immunity, allowing the dendritic ulcer to enlarge into a geographic ulcer and to deepen with stromal melting toward corneal perforation. This is the feared complication. So steroids are withheld for fear of perforation.

## answer_d
Corneal opacity

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Some scarring may occur, but the dangerous acute risk driving the contraindication is perforation.

Incorrect. It is treated with a topical antiviral, not antibiotics.

## title
Best vital stain for a corneal ulcer

## question
The best vital stain to detect a corneal ulcer is:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Fluorescein stain

## explanation_a
Correct. Fluorescein is the best vital stain to detect a corneal ulcer: it pools in and stains areas of denuded epithelium bright green under cobalt-blue light, outlining the ulcer and its dendritic or geographic shape. It is the standard bedside stain for an epithelial defect. So fluorescein is the answer.

## answer_b
Rose Bengal stain

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Rose Bengal stains devitalised cells and mucus (useful in dry eye), not the first choice for an ulcer.

## answer_c
Trypan blue stain

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Trypan blue is used to stain the anterior capsule during cataract surgery, not to detect an ulcer.

## answer_d
Lissamine green stain

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Lissamine green stains devitalised cells like Rose Bengal, not the ulcer bed.

## title
Correct treatment of a corneal ulcer

## question
A corneal ulcer should be treated as follows:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Topical antimicrobial eye drops, cycloplegia, eye patching

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The correct treatment uses topical drops but the eye must NOT be patched.

## answer_b
Topical antimicrobial eye drops, cycloplegia, no eye patching

## explanation_b
Correct. An infective corneal ulcer is treated with intensive topical antimicrobial drops and a cycloplegic for comfort and to prevent synechiae, and the eye is NOT patched. Patching creates a warm moist closed space that encourages microbial growth and can worsen the infection. So topical antimicrobials, cycloplegia and no patch is correct.

## answer_c
Oral antibiotics, cycloplegia, eye patching

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Treatment is by topical, not oral, antimicrobials, and the eye is not patched.

## answer_d
Oral antibiotics, cycloplegia, no eye patching

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The mainstay is topical antimicrobials rather than oral antibiotics.
