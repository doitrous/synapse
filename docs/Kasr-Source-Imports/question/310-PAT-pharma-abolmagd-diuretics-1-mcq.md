## title
Site of action of thiazide diuretics

## question
The site of action of thiazide diuretics is:

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The proximal convoluted tubule

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The proximal convoluted tubule is where carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (acetazolamide) act, not thiazides.

## answer_b
The distal convoluted tubule

## explanation_b
Correct. Thiazides act in the early DISTAL CONVOLUTED TUBULE, where they inhibit the Na+/Cl- cotransporter (NCC) on the luminal membrane, reducing sodium and chloride reabsorption and producing diuresis. This distal site also explains their enhancement of calcium reabsorption. It is the defining location of thiazide action.

## answer_c
The thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The thick ascending limb is the site of loop diuretics (furosemide), which block the Na-K-2Cl cotransporter.

## answer_d
The collecting tubule

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The collecting tubule is where potassium-sparing diuretics (aldosterone antagonists, ENaC blockers) act.

## title
Diuretic to lower intracranial pressure in cerebral oedema

## question
Which of the following diuretics would be most useful to lower intracranial pressure in a comatose patient with brain injury and cerebral oedema?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Amiloride

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Amiloride is a weak potassium-sparing diuretic and has no role in acutely reducing cerebral oedema.

## answer_b
Ethacrynic acid

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Ethacrynic acid is a loop diuretic; it reduces plasma volume but does not osmotically dehydrate the brain.

## answer_c
Furosemide

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Furosemide can adjunctively lower intracranial pressure but is not the most useful single agent for cerebral oedema.

## answer_d
Mannitol

## explanation_d
Correct. Mannitol is an OSMOTIC diuretic that is filtered but not reabsorbed; it raises plasma osmolality and draws water out of the brain across the intact blood-brain barrier, rapidly reducing cerebral oedema and intracranial pressure. This osmotic dehydration of the brain is why it is the agent of choice here.

Diuretic that decreases calcium excretion

## question
Which of the following diuretics decreases calcium excretion and could be used in idiopathic hypercalciuria and recurrent renal calcium stones?

## subject
pharm

## status

## owner
Claude

Spironolactone

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Spironolactone is a potassium-sparing aldosterone antagonist without a useful calcium-retaining effect for stone prevention.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Mannitol is an osmotic diuretic and does not selectively reduce calcium excretion.

## title
How triamterene differs from spironolactone

## question
Although both spironolactone and triamterene are potassium-sparing diuretics, triamterene differs in that:

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
It has a greater natriuretic action

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Neither drug is strongly natriuretic; the potassium-sparing agents produce only mild sodium loss.

## answer_b
It induces powerful anti-androgenic effects

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Anti-androgenic effects (gynaecomastia) are a feature of SPIRONOLACTONE, not triamterene.

## answer_c
Its potassium-retaining action is not dependent on the presence of aldosterone

## explanation_c
Correct. Triamterene (like amiloride) blocks the epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) in the collecting duct DIRECTLY, so its potassium-sparing action does NOT require aldosterone. Spironolactone, by contrast, works only by antagonising the aldosterone receptor and so depends on aldosterone being present. This aldosterone-independence is the key difference.

## answer_d
It acts from the luminal membrane side of the proximal tubular cells

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Triamterene acts in the collecting duct, not from the proximal tubule luminal side.

## title
Effect of low-dose dopamine infusion

## question
Dopamine used at a low infusion rate (2-3 micrograms/kg/min) is used to:

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Decrease heart rate

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Dopamine does not slow the heart; at higher doses it tends to increase heart rate via beta-1 receptors.

## answer_b
Elevate blood pressure

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A pressor effect appears at HIGH doses through alpha-1 vasoconstriction, not at low renal-dose infusion.

## answer_c
Increase cardiac output

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Increased cardiac output is a mid-dose beta-1 effect, not the low-dose action.

## answer_d
Increase renal blood flow

## explanation_d
Correct. At low doses (about 2-3 micrograms/kg/min) dopamine preferentially stimulates dopamine D1 receptors in the renal vasculature, causing renal (and splanchnic) vasodilation and INCREASING renal blood flow. This dose-dependent, receptor-specific effect is why low-dose dopamine is described as the renal dose.
