## title
Characteristic of presynaptic inhibition

## question
Presynaptic inhibition is characterized by:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Decreased Cl- influx into presynaptic terminals

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Presynaptic inhibition is associated with an INCREASE in Cl- (and K+) conductance at the presynaptic terminal, not a decrease in Cl- influx.

## answer_b
Hyperpolarization of presynaptic terminals

## explanation_b
Correct. Presynaptic inhibition acts through an axo-axonic synapse in which an inhibitory (usually GABAergic) interneuron hyperpolarises/less-depolarises the presynaptic terminal of an excitatory neuron. This reduces opening of voltage-gated Ca2+ channels when the action potential arrives, so less transmitter is released. The effect selectively damps that one input without altering the postsynaptic cell's overall excitability, which is what distinguishes it from postsynaptic inhibition.

## answer_c
Increased Ca2+ influx into post-synaptic neuron

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Increased Ca2+ entry would ENHANCE transmitter release; and the Ca2+ change in presynaptic inhibition is at the presynaptic terminal, not the postsynaptic neuron.

## answer_d
A 3rd neuron secreting serotonin

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A third serotonergic neuron is not the mechanism of presynaptic inhibition; presynaptic inhibition is mediated by a GABAergic axo-axonic synapse reducing presynaptic Ca2+ entry.

## title
Property of synaptic transmission

## question
Concerning synaptic transmission:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Depends on presence of voltage-gated Ca 2+ channels in the postsynaptic membrane

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The voltage-gated Ca2+ channels that trigger release are in the PREsynaptic membrane, not the postsynaptic membrane.

## answer_b
Presynaptic knobs release only an excitatory transmitter

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Presynaptic terminals can release excitatory OR inhibitory transmitters (e.g. glutamate vs GABA/glycine), depending on the neuron.

## answer_c
It is physiologically terminated by block of postsynaptic receptors

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Transmission is terminated physiologically by removal of transmitter from the cleft (reuptake, enzymatic breakdown, diffusion), not by blocking postsynaptic receptors.

## answer_d
It is inhibited by hypoglycemia and acidosis

## explanation_d
Correct. Synaptic transmission is the most metabolically demanding and chemically sensitive step in a neural pathway, because transmitter synthesis, vesicle recycling and ion pumping are energy-dependent. It is therefore readily depressed by hypoxia, hypoglycaemia and acidosis, whereas alkalosis tends to increase excitability. This metabolic vulnerability at the synapse is why such disturbances rapidly impair CNS function and consciousness.

## title
Requirement for CNS neurotransmitter release

## question
The release of neurotransmitter at a chemical synapse in the CNS is dependent on:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Synthesis of acetylcholinesterase

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Acetylcholinesterase degrades acetylcholine to TERMINATE its action; it is not required to release transmitter (and most CNS synapses are not cholinergic).

## answer_b
Hyperpolarization of the synaptic terminal

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Release requires DEPOLARISATION of the terminal to open Ca2+ channels; hyperpolarisation reduces release.

## answer_c
Opening of ligand-gated calcium channel

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The Ca2+ channels that trigger release are VOLTAGE-gated, opened by the depolarisation of the arriving action potential, not ligand-gated.

## answer_d
Influx of calcium into the presynaptic terminal

## explanation_d
Correct. When the action potential depolarises the presynaptic terminal it opens voltage-gated Ca2+ channels, and the resulting Ca2+ influx triggers vesicle fusion and exocytosis of transmitter into the cleft. Release is graded with Ca2+ entry and is abolished if extracellular Ca2+ is removed or the channels are blocked. This makes presynaptic Ca2+ influx the essential, rate-limiting trigger for chemical transmission.

## title
Nature of the inhibitory postsynaptic potential

## question
Inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSPs):

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Interacts with other potentials to move the membrane potential of postsynaptic neuron away or toward firing level

## explanation_a
Correct. An IPSP is a graded hyperpolarising potential that summates algebraically with excitatory inputs (EPSPs) on the postsynaptic neuron. Through this interaction it shifts the membrane potential relative to the firing threshold — moving it away from threshold (or opposing movement toward it) — so the cell fires only if the net effect still reaches threshold. This integrative interaction of postsynaptic potentials is the essence of how a neuron decides to fire.

## answer_b
Is produced by decreased Cl- conductance

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The IPSP is produced by INCREASED Cl- conductance (or increased K+ conductance), not decreased Cl- conductance.

## answer_c
Can be produced by an increase in Na+ conductance

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Increased Na+ conductance is DEPOLARISING and produces an EPSP, not an inhibitory (hyperpolarising) potential.

## answer_d
Is produced as a result of the presence of an inhibitory 3rd neuron

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The IPSP is a postsynaptic event generated by inhibitory transmitter on the neuron's own membrane; it does not require a separate 'third neuron' (that description confuses it with pathway-level inhibitory circuits).

## title
Mechanism of post-tetanic potentiation

## question
Post-tetanic potentiation in synapses:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Is due to activation of AMPA and NMDA receptors by glutamate

## explanation_a
Incorrect. AMPA/NMDA receptor activation underlies long-term potentiation (a postsynaptic mechanism), which is distinct from post-tetanic potentiation.

## answer_b
Is produced by an excitatory 3rd neuron secreting serotonin

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A serotonergic 'third neuron' is not the mechanism; post-tetanic potentiation is intrinsic to the stimulated presynaptic terminal.

## answer_c
Is due to opening of transmitter gated Cl- channels

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Opening transmitter-gated Cl- channels is an inhibitory (IPSP) event, not the cause of potentiation.

## answer_d
Is due to accumulation of calcium in the presynaptic terminal

## explanation_d
Correct. After a rapid train (tetanus) of presynaptic stimuli, Ca2+ enters the terminal faster than it can be pumped out, so residual Ca2+ accumulates in the presynaptic terminal. Subsequent single stimuli then release more transmitter, producing larger postsynaptic responses for seconds to minutes. This makes post-tetanic potentiation a presynaptic form of short-term synaptic plasticity.

## title
Basis of facilitation in a neuronal pool

## question
Facilitation:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Is due to stimulation of 2 input neurons away from each other

## explanation_a
Correct. Each input fibre has a central discharge (liminal) zone surrounded by a facilitated subliminal fringe of neurons brought only part-way to threshold. When two input fibres are stimulated far enough APART that their discharge zones do not overlap but their subliminal fringes do, the subthreshold potentials summate and extra fringe neurons now reach threshold, so the combined response exceeds the sum of the separate responses. That supra-additive gain from overlapping fringes is facilitation.

## answer_b
Is due to stimulation of 2 input neurons close to each other

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Inputs close together have overlapping DISCHARGE zones, which gives occlusion (a sub-additive response), not facilitation.

## answer_c
Number of action potentials released is less than if each input neuron is stimulated alone

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Fewer impulses than the sum of the separate responses describes OCCLUSION, the opposite of facilitation.

## answer_d
Occurs when the two afferent neurons have a common discharge zone

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A common (shared) discharge zone underlies occlusion; facilitation depends on overlap of the subliminal fringes of spaced inputs.

## title
Property of the receptor potential

## question
The receptor potential:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Has a long absolute refractory period

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A long absolute refractory period is a property of the action potential; the receptor potential is a graded local potential without this refractory behaviour.

## answer_b
Amplitude is not related to the strength of the stimulus

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The receptor potential is GRADED — its amplitude increases with stimulus strength.

## answer_c
Always generates an action potential in the receptor

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The receptor potential triggers action potentials only if it reaches threshold; a subthreshold receptor potential fires no spike.

## answer_d
Its duration is longer than action potential duration

## explanation_d
Correct. The receptor (generator) potential is a local, non-propagated, graded depolarisation whose time course is longer than that of the action potential it may trigger. Because it outlasts the brief all-or-none spike, a maintained stimulus can drive repetitive firing during the receptor potential. This longer duration and graded, non-propagated nature distinguish it from the action potential.

## title
Regarding sensory receptors

## question
Regarding sensory receptors:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Stimulus energy is converted into a local depolarization

## explanation_a
Correct. A sensory receptor transduces its adequate stimulus into a local graded depolarisation — the receptor potential — by opening ion channels in the receptor membrane. This conversion of stimulus energy into a local electrical change is the defining event of sensory transduction, occurring before any action potentials are generated. The receptor potential then spreads electrotonically to the spike-initiating zone.

## answer_b
The receptor potential is graded and self-propagating

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The receptor potential is graded but NOT self-propagating; it spreads passively (electrotonically), unlike the self-propagating action potential.

## answer_c
The receptor potential can be produced by only one form of energy

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A receptor is most sensitive (low threshold) to its adequate stimulus, but strong energy of ANOTHER form can also excite it, so it is not limited to only one energy form.

## answer_d
The frequency of action potentials generated doubles when the strength of the stimulus doubles

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Impulse frequency rises with stimulus strength but follows a power/logarithmic function, not a simple linear doubling.

## title
How receptors code increased stimulus intensity

## question
Receptors detect increased stimulus intensity by:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Lowering the threshold for receptor stimulation

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Threshold is a fixed property of the receptor; intensity is not signalled by lowering it.

## answer_b
By generating receptor potentials having higher magnitudes

## explanation_b
Correct. A stronger stimulus produces a receptor potential of greater magnitude, which in turn raises the frequency of action potentials in the sensory fibre (frequency coding) and recruits more receptors (population coding). The larger-amplitude receptor potential is the receptor-level event that encodes increased intensity. Thus intensity is coded by receptor-potential amplitude and impulse frequency, not by any change in conduction speed.

## answer_c
By generating nerve impulses that are transmitted along sensory fibers at higher velocities

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Conduction velocity is fixed by fibre diameter and myelination and does not change with stimulus strength.

## answer_d
By enhancing the central effects of sensory impulses

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Central 'enhancement' is not how the receptor itself signals intensity; the receptor encodes intensity peripherally by receptor-potential amplitude and firing frequency.

Correct. Each intrafusal fibre has a central sensory (equatorial) region and contractile polar ends; the polar ends are supplied by gamma (fusimotor) efferents. Gamma activation shortens the poles and keeps the central sensory region taut, resetting spindle sensitivity as the whole muscle shortens. This gamma supply to the peripheral (polar) parts is a defining feature of the spindle.

## answer_c
Nuclear chain fibers show dynamic response

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The dynamic response is a property of nuclear-BAG fibres (via the primary ending); nuclear-chain fibres give a more static response.

## answer_d
Afferent discharge decreased with muscle stretch

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Afferent discharge from the spindle INCREASES with muscle stretch (the spindle is a stretch receptor), not decreases.

## title
Response elicited by spindle Ia afferents on sudden stretch

## question
When a muscle is suddenly stretched, a signal is transmitted over Ia sensory fibers from muscle spindles. Which of the following statements best describes the response elicited by these spindle afferent signals?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Contraction of the muscle in which the active spindles are located

## explanation_a
Correct. The stretch (myotatic) reflex is monosynaptic: sudden stretch excites the spindle primary endings, whose Ia afferents synapse directly on the alpha motor neurons of the SAME (homonymous) muscle, causing it to contract and oppose the stretch (e.g. the knee jerk). This resists lengthening and maintains muscle tone and posture. Hence Ia activity produces contraction of the muscle containing the active spindles.

## answer_b
Relaxation of the muscle in which the active spindles are located

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The stretched muscle CONTRACTS, not relaxes; relaxation would not oppose the stretch.

## answer_c
Relaxation of intrafusal fibers in the active spindles

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Intrafusal fibres are controlled by gamma efferents; the Ia stretch signal does not relax them, and this is not the reflex output.

## answer_d
Direct synaptic activation of gamma motor neurons

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Ia afferents act on ALPHA motor neurons (monosynaptically) to contract the muscle; they do not directly drive gamma motor neurons.
