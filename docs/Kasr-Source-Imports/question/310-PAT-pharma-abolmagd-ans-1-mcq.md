## title
How paediatric pharmacokinetics differ from adults

## question
Paediatric patients differ from adults in:

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Having more body water

## explanation_a
Correct. Neonates and infants have a higher proportion of total body water (about 70-80% of body weight versus roughly 60% in adults) and a larger extracellular fluid compartment. This raises the apparent volume of distribution of water-soluble drugs, so weight-based doses of hydrophilic drugs are relatively higher in children. It is the single true statement here.

## answer_b
Having more fat content

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Neonates have LESS body fat than adults, not more; fat as a proportion of body weight rises through infancy but is low at birth, which is why lipophilic-drug distribution differs.

## answer_c
Having higher plasma protein levels

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Plasma protein (albumin) concentrations are LOWER in neonates, increasing the free fraction of highly protein-bound drugs rather than being higher than in adults.

## answer_d
Having a less permeable blood-brain barrier

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The neonatal blood-brain barrier is MORE permeable, not less, allowing greater CNS penetration of drugs such as opioids and bilirubin.

## title
Factors affecting drug dosage — the incorrect statement

## question
Regarding the factors affecting dosages of drugs, which of the following is INCORRECT?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Females need smaller doses than males

## explanation_a
Correct statement (so not the answer). Women on average have smaller lean body mass and a higher fat fraction than men, so standard doses are often relatively larger per kilogram and modest dose reduction can be appropriate.

## answer_b
Irritant drugs must be given after meals

## explanation_b
Correct statement (so not the answer). Gastric-irritant drugs (for example NSAIDs, iron, metronidazole) are given after meals to buffer the mucosa and reduce dyspepsia.

## answer_c
Renal glomerular filtration rate is decreased in geriatrics

## explanation_c
Correct statement (so not the answer). Glomerular filtration rate declines with age, so renally cleared drugs accumulate and doses of these must be reduced in the elderly.

## answer_d
Paediatrics have less body water and a less permeable BBB than adults

## explanation_d
Correct. This is the INCORRECT statement, and therefore the keyed answer: paediatric patients actually have MORE total body water and a MORE permeable blood-brain barrier than adults, the reverse of what is written. The other three statements are valid dosage-modifying principles.

## title
Therapeutic use of cholinergic agonists

## question
Which of the following is a therapeutic use of cholinergic agonists?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Bronchial asthma

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Cholinergic agonists cause bronchoconstriction and increased bronchial secretion, so bronchial asthma is a contraindication, not a use.

## answer_b
Myocardial infarction

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Muscarinic stimulation slows the heart and lowers blood pressure; it is not used in and is hazardous after myocardial infarction.

## answer_c
Paralytic ileus

## explanation_c
Correct. Cholinergic agonists stimulate gastrointestinal smooth muscle and restore propulsive motility, so a direct agonist such as bethanechol (or the anticholinesterase neostigmine) is used to treat postoperative paralytic ileus and non-obstructive urinary retention. This exploits the parasympathomimetic action on gut and bladder detrusor muscle. It is the recognised therapeutic use among the options.

## answer_d
Peptic ulcer

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Cholinergic agonists increase gastric acid secretion and would worsen a peptic ulcer, so this is a contraindication.

## title
Effect of anticholinesterases absent with parasympathetic nerve stimulation

## question
Anticholinesterases increase endogenous acetylcholine and mimic parasympathetic stimulation. Which of the following actions will appear ONLY with anticholinesterases but NOT with parasympathetic nerve stimulation?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Bradycardia

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Bradycardia results from muscarinic action on the SA node and is produced both by vagal (parasympathetic) nerve stimulation and by anticholinesterases.

## answer_b
Increased gastric acid secretion

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Increased gastric acid secretion is a direct vagal muscarinic effect and occurs with parasympathetic nerve stimulation as well as with anticholinesterases.

## answer_c
Miosis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Miosis follows muscarinic stimulation of the sphincter pupillae and appears with both parasympathetic nerve stimulation and anticholinesterases.

## answer_d
Hypotension

## explanation_d
Correct. Most resistance vessels have NO parasympathetic innervation, so nerve stimulation cannot dilate them; however their endothelium bears M3 muscarinic receptors that release nitric oxide. When an anticholinesterase raises circulating acetylcholine, it reaches these uninnervated endothelial receptors and produces vasodilation and hypotension, an effect the nerves alone cannot reproduce. Hence hypotension is the action seen only with anticholinesterases.

## title
Lipid-soluble parasympathomimetic used as a miotic

## question
Which of the following parasympathomimetics has the greatest lipid solubility, passes the blood-brain barrier, and is suitable for use as a miotic?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Acetylcholine

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Acetylcholine is a charged ester that is hydrolysed within seconds by cholinesterase and penetrates membranes poorly, so it is unsuitable as a topical miotic.

## answer_b
Methacholine

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Methacholine is also a quaternary choline ester with poor lipid solubility and little CNS penetration; it is used mainly as a bronchial-challenge agent, not a miotic.

## answer_c
Neostigmine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Neostigmine is a lipid-insoluble quaternary anticholinesterase that does not cross the blood-brain barrier or penetrate the cornea well.

## answer_d
Pilocarpine

## explanation_d
Correct. Pilocarpine is a naturally occurring cholinomimetic alkaloid and a tertiary amine, so it is lipid-soluble, crosses the blood-brain barrier and penetrates the cornea. This lets it be applied topically as a miotic that contracts the ciliary muscle and lowers intraocular pressure in glaucoma. Among the options it uniquely combines lipid solubility with a direct muscarinic action suitable for ocular use.

## title
Best first step for postoperative urinary retention

## question
A 35-year-old woman recovering from abdominal surgery fails to pass urine. Which of the following is the best strategy?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Give an infusion of acetylcholine because it has a very short half-life

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Acetylcholine's ultra-short half-life makes it useless systemically, and giving any cholinomimetic before excluding obstruction is unsafe.

## answer_b
Give neostigmine because it will not have CNS side effects

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Neostigmine can be used for retention, but the justification is wrong and, more importantly, obstruction must be excluded first.

## answer_c
Give bethanechol because it is selective to the urinary tract

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Bethanechol has relative selectivity for bladder and gut but is not exclusively urinary, and it is still contraindicated if outlet obstruction exists.

## answer_d
Make sure she does not have urinary tract obstruction before giving any medication

## explanation_d
Correct. The essential first step is to confirm there is no mechanical bladder-outlet obstruction before giving any cholinergic drug. Stimulating detrusor contraction against an obstruction can raise intravesical pressure dangerously and risk rupture or reflux. Only once obstruction is excluded is a cholinomimetic such as bethanechol appropriate, so safety adjudication precedes drug therapy.

## title
When atropine is preferred over its substitutes

## question
In which of the following situations is it better to use atropine rather than one of its substitutes?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Bronchial asthma

## explanation_a
Incorrect. For airway disease an inhaled, poorly absorbed antimuscarinic such as ipratropium or tiotropium is preferred over systemic atropine to limit side effects.

## answer_b
Eye fundus examination

## explanation_b
Incorrect. For fundus examination a short-acting mydriatic such as tropicamide is chosen so that cycloplegia and mydriasis wear off quickly, unlike long-acting atropine.

## answer_c
Treatment of organophosphorus poisoning

## explanation_c
Correct. In organophosphorus (anticholinesterase) poisoning atropine is the antidote of choice because a broad, potent systemic antimuscarinic action is needed to reverse life-threatening bronchorrhoea, bronchospasm, bradycardia and secretions. The organ-selective substitutes are designed to limit systemic effect, which is exactly the opposite of what an antidote requires. Atropine, titrated to drying of secretions, is therefore preferred here over any substitute.

## answer_d
Treatment of urinary incontinence

## explanation_d
Incorrect. For bladder overactivity a uroselective antimuscarinic (for example tolterodine or oxybutynin) is used rather than non-selective atropine.

## title
Best use of hyoscine (scopolamine)

## question
Hyoscine would be most useful in the treatment of which of the following conditions?

## subject
pharm

## status

## owner

Incorrect. Antimuscarinics reduce gut motility and would worsen paralytic ileus, which instead responds to cholinergic stimulation.

## title
Organophosphate effect not blocked by atropine

## question
A farmer exposed to insecticides (irreversible cholinesterase inhibitors) was given atropine. Which of the following effects of the cholinesterase inhibitor will NOT be blocked by atropine?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Bronchoconstriction/bronchospasm

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Bronchospasm is a muscarinic (M3) effect on airway smooth muscle and is blocked by atropine.

## answer_b
Increased bowel motility (diarrhoea)

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Increased gut motility and diarrhoea are muscarinic effects and are antagonised by atropine.

## answer_c
Increased lacrimal, salivary and mucous secretions

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Glandular hypersecretion is muscarinic and is reversed by atropine, which is titrated to drying of secretions.

## answer_d
Skeletal-muscle overstimulation leading to paralysis

## explanation_d
Correct. Atropine blocks only muscarinic receptors, so it cannot reverse the effects at the skeletal-muscle neuromuscular junction, which are mediated by NICOTINIC receptors. The accumulated acetylcholine causes fasciculations, weakness and eventually depolarising paralysis of skeletal muscle, and these persist despite atropine. Managing the nicotinic component requires a cholinesterase reactivator such as pralidoxime and respiratory support.

## title
Drug added to neostigmine to reduce muscarinic effects in myasthenia

## question
The following drug is used with neostigmine to decrease the unwanted muscarinic effects in myasthenia gravis:

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Ephedrine

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Ephedrine is a sympathomimetic and does not antagonise muscarinic side effects.

## answer_b
Caffeine

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Caffeine is a CNS stimulant/phosphodiesterase inhibitor and has no role in blocking neostigmine's muscarinic actions.

## answer_c
Azathioprine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Azathioprine is an immunosuppressant used to modify the underlying autoimmune disease, not to counter neostigmine's acute muscarinic effects.

## answer_d
Atropine

## explanation_d
Correct. Neostigmine raises acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction to improve strength, but the same rise stimulates muscarinic receptors and causes salivation, colic, sweating and bradycardia. Atropine, a muscarinic antagonist, is co-administered to block these unwanted muscarinic effects while leaving the beneficial nicotinic action at the muscle end-plate intact. It is therefore the correct partner drug.
