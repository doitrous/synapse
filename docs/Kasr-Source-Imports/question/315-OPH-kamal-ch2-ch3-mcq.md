## title
Normal horizontal corneal diameter in adults

## question
The normal horizontal diameter of the cornea in adults is about:

## subject
mul

## status

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
8 mm

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A horizontal diameter of about 8 mm would represent microcornea, well below the adult norm.

## answer_b
10 mm

## explanation_b
Incorrect. 10 mm is smaller than the normal adult horizontal corneal diameter.

## answer_c
12 mm

## explanation_c
Correct. The adult cornea measures about 11.5–12 mm horizontally and slightly less (~10.5–11 mm) vertically, giving it a mildly oval outline. This measurement is used clinically to recognise microcornea (below 10 mm) and megalocornea or buphthalmos (above 13 mm). About 12 mm is therefore the expected normal horizontal diameter.

## answer_d
14 mm

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A 14 mm cornea is larger than normal and suggests megalocornea or congenital glaucoma.

## title
Method of examining the anterior chamber angle

## question
The method of examination of the anterior chamber angle is called:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Direct ophthalmoscopy gives a magnified view of the fundus, not the angle.

## answer_b
Retinoscopy

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Retinoscopy is an objective measurement of refractive error.

## answer_c
Gonioscopy

## explanation_c
Correct. The anterior chamber angle cannot be seen directly because light from it undergoes total internal reflection at the cornea, so a gonioscopy contact lens with mirrors is used to visualise it. Gonioscopy is essential for classifying glaucoma as open- or closed-angle. It is therefore the standard method of examining the angle.

Incorrect. Indirect ophthalmoscopy is used to examine the peripheral fundus.

Slit lamp with accessory lenses for fundus examination

## question

## subject
mul

## status

## owner

## vignette

B

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The slit lamp is the most frequently used instrument in the routine eye examination.

## answer_b
With accessory lenses it can be used for fundus examination.

## explanation_b
Correct. The slit lamp by itself images the anterior segment, but with an auxiliary condensing lens (such as a +78/+90 D lens or a fundus contact lens) it also gives a magnified stereoscopic view of the fundus. Incorrect. A dense vitreous haemorrhage blocks the view of the fundus; ultrasound is used instead.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Angle examination requires a gonioscopy contact lens, not the slit lamp alone.

## title
Next step when Snellen letters cannot be read

## question
On testing visual acuity, if the patient is unable to identify any letters on the Snellen chart you should next test the ability to:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Count fingers.

## explanation_a
Correct. When a patient cannot read even the largest Snellen letter, acuity is graded down a standard ladder: counting fingers, then hand movements, then perception and projection of light. Counting fingers is the next step tested immediately after Snellen failure. This ordered sequence quantifies very low vision.

## answer_b
Identify the direction of hand movements.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Hand movements are tested only after counting fingers has failed.

## answer_c
Identify the position of a light.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Light projection (position of a light) is assessed near the bottom of the ladder.

## answer_d
Identify the presence of a light.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Light perception (presence of a light) is the very last step, after hand movements.

## title
Term for incomplete lid closure

## question
Incomplete closure of the palpebral fissure when the eyelids are shut is called:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Lagophthalmos

## explanation_a
Correct. Lagophthalmos is the inability to close the eyelids completely, leaving part of the palpebral fissure open when closure is attempted. It results from facial nerve palsy, severe proptosis or lid scarring, and predisposes to exposure keratopathy. Hence incomplete lid closure is called lagophthalmos.

## answer_b
Ptosis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Ptosis is drooping of the upper eyelid.

## answer_c
Bell's phenomenon

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Bell's phenomenon is the protective upward rolling of the globe on attempted lid closure.

## answer_d
Blepharochalasis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Blepharochalasis is recurrent lid oedema leaving lax, redundant lid skin.

## title
Features of ulcerative blepharitis

## question
In ulcerative blepharitis:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
The lid margin is not inflamed.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. In ulcerative blepharitis the lid margin is inflamed.

## answer_b
Scaly dandruff-like material is seen between the cilia.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Scaly dandruff-like material describes squamous (seborrhoeic) blepharitis.

## answer_c
Treatment is by antifungal drugs.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Ulcerative blepharitis is staphylococcal and is treated with antibiotics and lid hygiene, not antifungals.

## answer_d
There are yellow crusts and ulcers on the lid margin.

## explanation_d
Correct. Ulcerative (staphylococcal) blepharitis is characterised by hard yellow crusts at the lash bases which, when removed, reveal small bleeding ulcers on an inflamed lid margin. Chronic disease leads to lash loss, trichiasis and lid-margin scarring. Hence yellow crusts and ulcers on the lid margin typify ulcerative blepharitis.

## title
Cause not associated with ptosis

## question
The well-known causes of ptosis include the following EXCEPT:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Blunt trauma to the eyelid

## explanation_a
Incorrect (it is a cause). Blunt trauma can disinsert the levator aponeurosis, producing aponeurotic ptosis.

## answer_b
Hypertension

## explanation_b
Correct. This is the exception: systemic hypertension is not a recognised cause of ptosis. The genuine causes are neurogenic (third-nerve palsy, Horner's syndrome), myogenic (myasthenia, myopathies), aponeurotic (age, trauma) and mechanical. Hence hypertension is the odd one out.

## answer_c
Myasthenia gravis

## explanation_c
Incorrect (it is a cause). Fatigable ptosis is a classic feature of myasthenia gravis.

## answer_d
Horner's syndrome

## explanation_d
Incorrect (it is a cause). Loss of Muller's-muscle tone in Horner's syndrome gives a mild ptosis.
