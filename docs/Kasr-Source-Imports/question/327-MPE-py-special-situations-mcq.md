## title
Communicating with an aggressive patient

## question
An aggressive patient was admitted to the hospital by his family members. How can the resident communicate with him?

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Give him a depressant drug to calm him

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Sedating a patient purely to control behaviour, without a medical indication and consent, is chemical restraint and is not a first-line communication approach.

## answer_b
Leave the clinic until he becomes quiet

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Abandoning the encounter neither addresses the patient's needs nor de-escalates the situation; the clinician should engage, not withdraw.

## answer_c
Talk to him politely with sympathy

## explanation_c
Correct. The appropriate way to communicate with an aggressive patient is to talk to him politely and with sympathy, using calm, respectful, de-escalating engagement. Acknowledging his feelings and maintaining a non-confrontational manner reduces agitation and preserves the therapeutic relationship. Direct, empathetic communication is preferred over sedation, avoidance, or bypassing the patient.

## answer_d
Talk to his family and avoid the patient

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Speaking only to the family and avoiding the patient bypasses him and disrespects his autonomy; the patient himself should be engaged.

Confidentiality request from a pregnant minor

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Where the procedure would be performed is not the point tested; the reason the doctor cannot honour her request is her status as a minor, which requires parental involvement.

No, parental consent is a must as she is a minor

## explanation_b
Correct. Because she is a 15-year-old minor, she cannot give valid independent consent and her parents must be involved, so the doctor cannot keep the matter secret from them as she asks. Incorrect. Her being physically fit does not remove the need for parental consent; a minor still cannot decide this alone or bind the doctor to secrecy.

## question

## title
Respecting refusal of HIV testing

## question
A 22 year-old woman is admitted to the hospital with headache and photophobia but with intact mental status. LAB test reveal cryptococcal meningitis (an infection commonly associated with HIV infection). When she was told that, she refused to be tested for HIV. What would you do?

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Do not test for HIV

## explanation_a
Correct. Because she is a competent adult who has refused, you do not test her for HIV: testing requires her informed consent, and a mentally intact patient's refusal must be respected. Overriding her would violate her autonomy and the consent requirement for HIV testing. The physician should continue to treat her meningitis and may counsel her, but must not test against her will.

## answer_b
Report the patient's condition to public health department and ask the department to test the patient for HIV

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Enlisting the public-health department to test her against her refusal still overrides her autonomy and is not justified by her declining a test.

## answer_c
Test her for HIV without her approval and tell her the result

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Testing without her approval violates her right to consent; a competent patient's refusal is binding even if you disclose the result.

## answer_d
Test the patient for HIV without telling her

## explanation_d

## title
Mental-health care principle: liberty

## question
The following principle should be taken into account during treatment of mentally ill patients "Patients should be free from interventions that inhibit the ability to enjoy life and treatment options should be the least possible restrictive." this refers to as:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Bodily integrity

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Bodily integrity protects against unwanted physical interference, not freedom from restrictive interventions and enjoyment of life.

## answer_b
Confidentiality

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Confidentiality concerns the patient's information, not the restrictiveness of interventions.

## answer_c
Dignity

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Dignity concerns respectful and courteous treatment, not the least-restrictive principle described here.

## answer_d
Liberty

## explanation_d
Correct. Keeping patients free from interventions that inhibit enjoyment of life and choosing the least restrictive treatment is the principle of liberty. Liberty protects the mentally ill patient's freedom, permitting only the minimum necessary restriction. It is distinct from dignity, confidentiality, and bodily integrity, each of which guards a different interest.
