## title
Sign not seen in dysthyroid eye disease

## question
The following are signs of dysthyroid (thyroid) eye disease EXCEPT:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Upper lid retraction

## explanation_a
Incorrect (it is a sign). Upper-lid retraction from sympathetic overactivity and muscle fibrosis is a classic sign of thyroid eye disease.

## answer_b
Uveitis

## explanation_b
Correct. This is the exception: thyroid eye disease is an orbital and adnexal disorder (muscle and fat swelling) and does not inflame the uveal tract, so uveitis is not one of its signs. Uveitis points instead to an intraocular inflammatory cause. Hence uveitis is the odd one out.

## answer_c
Unilateral or bilateral proptosis

## explanation_c
Incorrect (it is a sign). Swelling of the extraocular muscles and orbital fat produces unilateral or bilateral proptosis.

## answer_d
Lid lag

## explanation_d
Incorrect (it is a sign). Lid lag on downgaze (von Graefe sign) is a recognised feature.

Muscle paralysed in Horner's ptosis

## question
Ptosis in Horner's syndrome is due to paralysis of:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Riolan's muscle

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Riolan's muscle is the marginal part of orbicularis and is not responsible for the Horner ptosis.

## answer_b
Horner's muscle

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Horner's muscle is the lacrimal part of orbicularis, unrelated to lid elevation.

## answer_c
Muller's muscle

## explanation_c
Correct. Horner's syndrome interrupts the ocular sympathetic supply and so paralyses Muller's muscle (the smooth superior tarsal muscle), giving a mild ptosis. The levator palpebrae muscle

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Levator paralysis is a third-nerve problem and causes a much greater ptosis than that of Horner's syndrome.

## title
Cause of congenital ptosis

## question
Congenital ptosis is due to:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Increased weight of the upper lid

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Increased lid weight (mechanical ptosis) is an acquired, not a congenital, mechanism.

## answer_b
Levator tendon injury

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Levator tendon (aponeurotic) injury causes acquired ptosis, typically after trauma or surgery.

## answer_c
3rd nerve palsy

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A third-nerve palsy is a neurogenic acquired cause, usually with other ocular motility signs.

Dystrophy of the levator muscle

## explanation_d
Correct. Congenital ptosis results from a developmental dystrophy of the levator palpebrae superioris, in which the muscle is maldeveloped and partly replaced by fibro-fatty tissue. Because the dystrophic muscle also relaxes poorly, there is lid lag on downgaze. Hence congenital ptosis is due to dystrophy of the levator muscle.

## title
Structure not part of the lacrimal secretory system

## question
The lacrimal secretory system consists of the following parts EXCEPT:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
The lacrimal gland

## explanation_a
Incorrect (it is secretory). The main lacrimal gland secretes the aqueous layer of reflex tears.

## answer_b
The accessory lacrimal glands

## explanation_b
Incorrect (it is secretory). The accessory lacrimal glands of Krause and Wolfring provide basal tear secretion.

## answer_c
The goblet cells

## explanation_c
Incorrect (it is secretory). Conjunctival goblet cells secrete the mucin layer of the tear film.

## answer_d
The lacrimal sac

## explanation_d
Correct. This is the exception: the lacrimal sac belongs to the excretory (drainage) apparatus that carries tears from the puncti and canaliculi to the nasolacrimal duct, not to the tear-secreting system. The secretory system is the glands and goblet cells. Hence the lacrimal sac is the odd one out.

## title
Function of the orbital portion of the lacrimal gland

## question
The orbital portion of the lacrimal gland is responsible for:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Reflex tear secretion

## explanation_a
Correct. The main (orbital and palpebral) lacrimal gland produces reflex tears in response to ocular irritation or emotion. Basal, resting secretion comes mainly from the accessory lacrimal glands of Krause and Wolfring. Hence the orbital portion of the lacrimal gland is responsible for reflex tear secretion.

## answer_b
Basic (basal) tear secretion

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Basal tear secretion is largely the job of the accessory lacrimal glands.

## answer_c
Mucin layer secretion

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The mucin layer is secreted by conjunctival goblet cells.

## answer_d
None of the above

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The orbital lacrimal gland has a definite role, namely reflex tear secretion.

## title
Meaning of a positive regurgitation test

## question
A positive regurgitation test is diagnostic of:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Nasolacrimal duct obstruction

## explanation_a
Correct. In the regurgitation test, pressure over the lacrimal sac refluxes mucoid or mucopurulent material back through the punctum. A positive result means the canaliculus is patent but the nasolacrimal duct is blocked, so fluid has accumulated in the sac (chronic dacryocystitis). Hence a positive regurgitation test indicates nasolacrimal duct obstruction.

## answer_b
Canalicular obstruction

## explanation_b
Incorrect. In canalicular obstruction the passage to the sac is blocked, so nothing can reflux and the test is negative.

## answer_c
Punctal occlusion

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Punctal occlusion prevents fluid from re-emerging at the punctum, so no regurgitation is seen.

## answer_d
Ectropion

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Ectropion causes watering from lid malposition and does not give sac regurgitation.
