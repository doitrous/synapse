## title
Passive congestion of the lungs

## question
Passive congestion of the lungs is commonly caused by:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Left-sided heart failure

## explanation_a
Correct. The department book names mitral stenosis or left-sided heart failure as the cause of chronic venous congestion of the lung: blood dams up behind the failing left ventricle into the pulmonary circulation, producing lungs that are early dark red, moist and heavy, later brown and indurated, with alveolar heart-failure cells (hemosiderin-laden macrophages).

## answer_b
Right-sided heart failure

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Right-sided heart failure instead backs blood up into the systemic venous circulation, causing systemic congestion and congestion of the liver (nutmeg liver) -- the book's own figure (4.2) pairs left heart failure with lung congestion and right heart failure with liver/systemic congestion, not the reverse.

## answer_c
Pulmonary embolism

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Pulmonary embolism obstructs pulmonary arterial flow, causing ischemia or infarction distal to the clot -- an arterial occlusion problem, not the passive venous outflow obstruction that defines congestion.

## answer_d
Hypovolemic shock

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Hypovolemic shock is a state of reduced circulating blood volume and tissue hypoperfusion -- the physiological opposite of a congested, blood-engorged tissue.

## title
Non-pitting edema

## question
Which of the following conditions is associated with non-pitting edema:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Cardiac edema

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Cardiac edema results from increased venous hydrostatic pressure (impaired venous return in heart failure); the resulting transudate is protein-poor and produces classic pitting edema.

## answer_b
Liver cirrhosis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Liver cirrhosis causes edema through reduced plasma oncotic pressure (hypoalbuminemia); like cardiac edema, this low-protein transudate pits on finger pressure.

## answer_c
Lymphatic obstruction

## explanation_c
Correct. The department book states specifically that non-pitting edema occurs in inflammatory edema and edema due to lymphatic obstruction, because in both cases the accumulated fluid is protein-rich rather than the low-protein transudate of hydrostatic/oncotic-pressure causes, so it does not readily displace under pressure.

## answer_d
Renal edema

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Renal (nephrotic) edema follows the same hydrostatic/oncotic-pressure mechanisms (sodium retention, hypoalbuminemia) as cardiac and hepatic edema, producing a protein-poor transudate that pits.

## subject

Incorrect. Degree of acinar (gland-forming) differentiation is the book's grading criterion for adenocarcinoma, not squamous cell carcinoma -- squamous cell carcinoma has no glandular acini to grade.

Percent of cell nest formation

## explanation_b
Correct. The department book grades squamous cell carcinoma specifically by the percentage of the tumor formed of "cell nests" (nests of malignant cells recapitulating normal epidermal maturation, with keratin pearls at their centre): Grade I is 75-100% cell nests, down to Grade IV at 0-25% (anaplastic carcinoma may show no cell nests at all).

Extent of stromal invasion

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Extent of stromal invasion is not the book's named criterion for squamous cell carcinoma grading; the book's grading system is defined purely by the proportion of cell nests present.

## answer_d
The presence of distant spread

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The presence of distant spread is a staging (TNM, "M" for metastasis) concept, answering how far the cancer has spread -- the book explicitly separates staging from grading, which answers how differentiated the tumor tissue is.

## title
Healing by first intention

## question
Wound healing by first intention is characterized by:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Large scar followed by wound contraction

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A large scar with subsequent wound contraction (via myofibroblasts) describes healing by SECOND intention, used for open gaping or infected wounds with tissue loss -- first intention's small, well-apposed incision instead produces a fine, pale linear scar with no contraction phase.

## answer_b
Extensive tissue damage

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The department book specifically defines first intention as occurring where there is little tissue damage on either side of the cut (a clean incised wound); extensive tissue damage instead necessitates healing by second intention, with its abundant granulation tissue filling a real defect.

## answer_c
Closure of the gap by epithelial regeneration followed by granulation tissue formation

## explanation_c
Correct. In first intention, basal epidermal cells begin proliferating and migrating to bridge the gap within 24-48 hours (epithelial regeneration), while only a small amount of granulation tissue forms from the wound edges to repair the dermis beneath -- together restoring the full epidermal thickness and uniting the edges with collagen by about day 7.

## answer_d
Severe cosmetic deformities

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The book states the end result of healing by first intention is a fine, pale linear scar -- not a severely deforming one; extensive scarring/deformity is a recognised complication of wound healing in general, not a defining feature of first intention specifically.
