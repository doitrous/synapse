## title
Management of traumatic tympanic membrane perforation

## question
A 17-year-old male suffered a blow to the right side of the head by a blunt object, then noticed a few drops of blood from the right ear with diminished hearing. Examination shows a perforation of the right tympanic membrane with ragged, irregular edges and surrounding blood clots. Management of this case is:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Nothing per ear (keep water out of the ear) until spontaneous healing

## explanation_a
Correct. Most traumatic tympanic membrane perforations heal spontaneously within a few weeks if the ear is kept dry and free of infection. The essential management is to keep water and instruments out of the ear canal and to avoid ear drops or syringing, which introduce infection and displace the healing edges. Only a perforation that fails to heal after adequate time is later considered for myringoplasty.

## answer_b
Ear wash to remove any blood clots or debris

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Ear washing or syringing forces water and organisms through the perforation into the middle ear and risks converting a clean traumatic perforation into a suppurative otitis media.

## answer_c
Hearing aid fitting

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A hearing aid treats a fixed hearing loss, not an acute traumatic perforation that is expected to heal on its own with restoration of hearing.

## answer_d
Immediate myringoplasty

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Myringoplasty is reserved for a persistent perforation that has failed to heal; it is not done immediately, when spontaneous closure is the rule.

## answer_e
Ear exploration under general anaesthesia

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Exploration under general anaesthesia is not indicated for a simple traumatic perforation with no ossicular or facial-nerve injury.

## title
Treatment of auricular haematoma

## question
Treatment of auricular haematoma is:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Excision

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The pinna cartilage is not excised; excision would deform the auricle and is not the treatment of a collection of blood.

## answer_b
Cautery

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Cautery has no role; the problem is a blood collection between perichondrium and cartilage, not bleeding to be cauterised.

## answer_c
Tympanoplasty

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Tympanoplasty is a middle-ear/ear-drum reconstruction and is unrelated to a haematoma of the pinna.

## answer_d
Incision and evacuation

## explanation_d
Correct. An auricular haematoma is drained promptly by incision and evacuation of the clot, followed by a pressure dressing to prevent re-accumulation. Evacuation restores contact between the perichondrium and cartilage, preserving the cartilage's blood supply. If it is left undrained the cartilage is deprived of nutrition and fibroses, producing the deformed cauliflower ear.

## title
Watery ear discharge increased by straining

## question
A watery ear discharge that increases by straining is:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Serous otitis media

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Serous (secretory) otitis media is a middle-ear effusion behind an intact drum; it does not discharge a watery fluid that varies with straining.

## answer_b
CSF otorrhoea

## explanation_b
Correct. A clear, watery discharge from the ear that increases on straining, coughing or bending is cerebrospinal fluid otorrhoea, driven by the rise in CSF pressure these manoeuvres cause. It follows a breach of the dura and the temporal bone, usually after trauma or surgery, and can be confirmed by testing the fluid for beta-2 transferrin. Recognising it matters because it signals a communication with the subarachnoid space and a risk of meningitis.

## answer_c
Acute otitis media

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Acute otitis media discharges mucopurulent, not clear watery, fluid and is not pressure-dependent in this way.

## answer_d
Malignant otitis externa

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Malignant otitis externa produces a purulent, often granulation-associated discharge in an elderly diabetic, not a clear watery leak that changes with straining.

## title
Commonest presenting symptom of acoustic neuroma

## question
The commonest presenting symptom of acoustic neuroma is:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Unilateral tinnitus with progressive sensorineural hearing loss

## explanation_a
Correct. Acoustic neuroma (vestibular schwannoma) most often presents with unilateral tinnitus and a slowly progressive sensorineural hearing loss, because the tumour arises on the vestibulocochlear nerve in the internal auditory canal. Any adult with asymmetric or unilateral sensorineural hearing loss and tinnitus should be investigated for it, typically with MRI. The insidious cochlear symptoms come long before balance or cranial-nerve signs.

## answer_b
Vertigo and loss of balance

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Although the tumour is on the vestibular nerve, slow growth allows central compensation, so frank vertigo is uncommon and not the usual presenting complaint.

## answer_c
Facial and trigeminal paralysis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Facial and trigeminal involvement are late features of a large tumour compressing adjacent nerves, not the commonest presentation.

## answer_d
Sudden sensorineural hearing loss with tinnitus

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Sudden sensorineural hearing loss can occur but is far less common than the slowly progressive unilateral loss with tinnitus.
