## title
Management of traumatic tympanic membrane perforation

## question
A 17-year-old male suffered a blow to the right side of the head by a blunt object, then noticed a few drops of blood from the right ear with diminished hearing. Examination shows a perforation of the right tympanic membrane with ragged, irregular edges and surrounding blood clots. Management of this case is:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Nothing per ear (keep water out of the ear) until spontaneous healing

## explanation_a
Correct. Most traumatic tympanic membrane perforations heal spontaneously within a few weeks if the ear is kept dry and free of infection. The essential management is to keep water and instruments out of the ear canal and to avoid ear drops or syringing, which introduce infection and displace the healing edges. Only a perforation that fails to heal after adequate time is later considered for myringoplasty.

## answer_b
Ear wash to remove any blood clots or debris

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Ear washing or syringing forces water and organisms through the perforation into the middle ear and risks converting a clean traumatic perforation into a suppurative otitis media.

## answer_c
Hearing aid fitting

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A hearing aid treats a fixed hearing loss, not an acute traumatic perforation that is expected to heal on its own with restoration of hearing.

## answer_d
Immediate myringoplasty

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Myringoplasty is reserved for a persistent perforation that has failed to heal; it is not done immediately, when spontaneous closure is the rule.

## answer_e
Ear exploration under general anaesthesia

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Exploration under general anaesthesia is not indicated for a simple traumatic perforation with no ossicular or facial-nerve injury.

## title
Treatment of auricular haematoma

## question
Treatment of auricular haematoma is:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Excision

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The pinna cartilage is not excised; excision would deform the auricle and is not the treatment of a collection of blood.

## answer_b
Cautery

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Cautery has no role; the problem is a blood collection between perichondrium and cartilage, not bleeding to be cauterised.

## answer_c
Tympanoplasty

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Tympanoplasty is a middle-ear/ear-drum reconstruction and is unrelated to a haematoma of the pinna.

## answer_d
Incision and evacuation

## explanation_d
Correct. An auricular haematoma is drained promptly by incision and evacuation of the clot, followed by a pressure dressing to prevent re-accumulation. Evacuation restores contact between the perichondrium and cartilage, preserving the cartilage's blood supply. If it is left undrained the cartilage is deprived of nutrition and fibroses, producing the deformed cauliflower ear.

## title
Watery ear discharge increased by straining

## question
A watery ear discharge that increases by straining is:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Serous otitis media

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Serous (secretory) otitis media is a middle-ear effusion behind an intact drum; it does not discharge a watery fluid that varies with straining.

## answer_b
CSF otorrhoea

## explanation_b
Correct. A clear, watery discharge from the ear that increases on straining, coughing or bending is cerebrospinal fluid otorrhoea, driven by the rise in CSF pressure these manoeuvres cause. It follows a breach of the dura and the temporal bone, usually after trauma or surgery, and can be confirmed by testing the fluid for beta-2 transferrin. Recognising it matters because it signals a communication with the subarachnoid space and a risk of meningitis.

## answer_c
Acute otitis media

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Acute otitis media discharges mucopurulent, not clear watery, fluid and is not pressure-dependent in this way.

## answer_d
Malignant otitis externa

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Malignant otitis externa produces a purulent, often granulation-associated discharge in an elderly diabetic, not a clear watery leak that changes with straining.

## title
Commonest presenting symptom of acoustic neuroma

## question
The commonest presenting symptom of acoustic neuroma is:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Unilateral tinnitus with progressive sensorineural hearing loss

## explanation_a
Correct. Acoustic neuroma (vestibular schwannoma) most often presents with unilateral tinnitus and a slowly progressive sensorineural hearing loss, because the tumour arises on the vestibulocochlear nerve in the internal auditory canal. Any adult with asymmetric or unilateral sensorineural hearing loss and tinnitus should be investigated for it, typically with MRI. The insidious cochlear symptoms come long before balance or cranial-nerve signs.

## answer_b
Vertigo and loss of balance

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Although the tumour is on the vestibular nerve, slow growth allows central compensation, so frank vertigo is uncommon and not the usual presenting complaint.

## answer_c
Facial and trigeminal paralysis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Facial and trigeminal involvement are late features of a large tumour compressing adjacent nerves, not the commonest presentation.

## answer_d
Sudden sensorineural hearing loss with tinnitus

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Sudden sensorineural hearing loss can occur but is far less common than the slowly progressive unilateral loss with tinnitus.

Investigations in malignant otitis externa

Investigations in malignant otitis externa include:

## subject

Incorrect. Acute mastoiditis presents with post-auricular swelling and fever, not itching and canal debris.

## title
Treatment of mastoid abscess

## question
Mastoid abscess is treated by:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Simple incision

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A simple incision drains the pus but leaves the infected mastoid air cells, so the abscess recurs.

## answer_b
Cortical mastoidectomy

## explanation_b
Correct. A mastoid (subperiosteal) abscess complicating acute mastoiditis is treated by cortical (simple) mastoidectomy, which exenterates the infected air cells and drains the antrum while preserving the middle-ear structures and canal wall. Antibiotics alone will not clear coalescent mastoid disease. Cortical mastoidectomy is therefore the operation of choice.

## answer_c
Radical mastoidectomy

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Radical mastoidectomy is reserved for cholesteatoma (unsafe disease), not for acute mastoid abscess.

## answer_d
Tympanoplasty

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Tympanoplasty reconstructs the drum and ossicles; it does not drain an acute mastoid abscess.

## title
Symptoms of CSOM — the exception

## question
All are symptoms of chronic suppurative otitis media EXCEPT:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Discharge

## explanation_a
Incorrect (this IS a symptom). Ear discharge (otorrhoea) is the leading symptom of chronic suppurative otitis media.

## answer_b
Pain

## explanation_b
Correct. Uncomplicated chronic suppurative otitis media is characteristically painless; the perforation vents the middle ear so pressure does not build up. The appearance of pain in a known CSOM is a warning sign of a developing complication such as mastoiditis or an intracranial abscess. So pain is the exception among its usual symptoms.

## answer_c
Deafness

## explanation_c
Incorrect (this IS a symptom). A conductive hearing loss (deafness) is expected from the perforation and ossicular damage.

## answer_d
Tinnitus

## explanation_d
Incorrect (this IS a symptom). Tinnitus commonly accompanies the hearing loss.

## title
Vertigo with sensorineural hearing loss

## question
Vertigo with sensorineural hearing loss occurs in:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Labyrinthitis

## explanation_a
Correct. Labyrinthitis inflames the whole membranous labyrinth, so it affects both the vestibular apparatus (causing vertigo) and the cochlea (causing sensorineural hearing loss). The combination of vertigo with hearing loss localises the lesion to the labyrinth. This is what separates it from a purely vestibular disorder.

## answer_b
Vestibular neuritis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Vestibular neuritis affects only the vestibular nerve, giving vertigo with normal hearing.

## answer_c
Cerebellar lesions

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Cerebellar lesions cause imbalance and ataxia but not sensorineural hearing loss.

## answer_d
Benign positional paroxysmal vertigo

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo causes brief positional vertigo with no hearing loss.

## title
Cranial complication of otitis media

## question
A cranial (intratemporal) complication of otitis media is:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
External otitis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. External otitis is a disease of the outer ear canal, not a complication of middle-ear otitis media.

## answer_b
Petrositis

## explanation_b
Correct. Petrositis, infection of the petrous temporal bone, is an intratemporal ("cranial") complication of otitis media. It shares this category with mastoiditis, labyrinthitis and facial palsy — all confined to the temporal bone. Brain abscess and lateral sinus thrombosis, by contrast, are intracranial complications.

## answer_c
Brain abscess

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A brain abscess is an intracranial complication, not an intratemporal one.

## answer_d
Lateral sinus thrombosis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Lateral sinus thrombosis is likewise an intracranial complication.
