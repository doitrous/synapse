## title
Toxidrome of coma, respiratory depression and miosis

## question
A triad of coma, severe respiratory depression and miosis is common with:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Fentanyl

## explanation_a
Correct. The triad of depressed consciousness, severe respiratory depression and pinpoint (miotic) pupils is the classic OPIOID toxidrome, and fentanyl is a potent synthetic opioid. Opioids act on central mu receptors to depress the brainstem respiratory centre and the level of consciousness while stimulating the Edinger-Westphal nucleus to constrict the pupils. This distinctive triad, reversible with naloxone, is the reason it points to an opioid rather than any other class.

## answer_b
Theophylline

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Theophylline is a CNS and cardiac stimulant; overdose causes agitation, seizures, tachyarrhythmia and vomiting, not coma with miosis and respiratory depression.

## answer_c
Tricyclic antidepressants

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Tricyclic antidepressants produce an anticholinergic picture with mydriasis (dilated pupils), seizures and a wide-QRS tachyarrhythmia, the opposite pupillary sign to this triad.

## answer_d
Verapamil

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Verapamil is a calcium-channel blocker; overdose causes bradycardia and hypotension, not the opioid triad of miosis and respiratory depression.

## title
Activated charcoal is effective in barbiturate poisoning

## question
Activated charcoal is used in:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Alcohol poisoning

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Small, highly water-soluble alcohols (methanol, ethanol, ethylene glycol) are poorly adsorbed by activated charcoal, so charcoal is not used for alcohol poisoning.

## answer_b
Barbiturate poisoning

## explanation_b
Correct. Activated charcoal adsorbs many organic drugs onto its large porous surface, and barbiturates are well adsorbed, so it reduces their gastrointestinal absorption. It is most useful given early after ingestion of a charcoal-binding drug such as a barbiturate. This is why barbiturate poisoning is the listed indication, whereas alcohols and metals are not adsorbed.

## answer_c
Heavy metal poisoning

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Heavy metals are poorly bound by activated charcoal; they require specific chelating agents rather than charcoal.

## answer_d
Lead poisoning

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Lead is a heavy metal and is not effectively adsorbed by charcoal; it is managed with chelation, not activated charcoal.

## title
Tinnitus is not an indication for haemodialysis in salicylate poisoning

## question
Which of the following clinical manifestation of salicylate poisoning is not indication of hemodialysis?

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Acidosis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Severe or refractory metabolic acidosis is a recognised indication for haemodialysis in salicylate poisoning because it reflects serious systemic toxicity.

## answer_b
Renal failure

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Renal failure impairs salicylate excretion and is an accepted indication for haemodialysis.

## answer_c
Tinnitus

## explanation_c
Correct. Tinnitus is an early, minor feature of mild salicylism reflecting an ototoxic effect, and it resolves as levels fall; it does not by itself signify the life-threatening toxicity that warrants dialysis. The indications for haemodialysis are severe acidosis, renal failure, a very high blood level, pulmonary or cerebral oedema and altered mental status. Because tinnitus is not in that severity group, it is the one that is NOT an indication.

## answer_d
Toxic blood level

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A very high (toxic) blood salicylate level is a standard indication for haemodialysis to enhance elimination.

## title
Haemodialysis is most effective for methanol

## question
Hemodialysis would have the greatest effect on reducing blood levels of which of the following?

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Digitalis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Digitalis has a very large volume of distribution and is extensively tissue-bound, so little is in the blood to remove; haemodialysis is ineffective.

## answer_b
Methadone

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Methadone is highly protein-bound with a large volume of distribution, making it a poor candidate for haemodialysis.

## answer_c
Methanol

## explanation_c
Correct. Haemodialysis removes toxins that are small, water-soluble, minimally protein-bound and have a low volume of distribution, and methanol fits all of these. Because most of the methanol stays in the blood and readily crosses the dialyser membrane, dialysis clears it efficiently (and also removes its toxic formic-acid metabolite). This is why methanol, not the large-volume or tissue-bound drugs, is best cleared by dialysis.

## answer_d
Verapamil

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Verapamil is highly protein-bound with a large volume of distribution, so haemodialysis does not effectively reduce its blood level.

Correct drug-toxicity pairing: paracetamol and hepatotoxicity

## question
Which of the following is a CORRECT pairing of drug and toxic action?

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Amphetamine / Autotoxicity

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Amphetamine is a sympathomimetic causing agitation, hyperthermia and cardiovascular effects; "autotoxicity" is not its recognised toxic action.

## answer_b
Digitalis / Nephrotoxicity

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Digitalis is cardiotoxic (arrhythmias and conduction block), not primarily nephrotoxic.

Iron / Pulmonary toxicity

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Iron toxicity targets the gastrointestinal tract, liver and metabolism (metabolic acidosis, shock), not the lungs.

Correct. This is the basis for N-acetylcysteine treatment, which replenishes glutathione. The other pairings misattribute the target organ, making this the only correct match.

## subject

## title
Weak acids worsen alkaline corrosive injury

## question
Weak acids cannot be used with alkaline corrosives because they may:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Produce more corrosive action

## explanation_a
Correct. Neutralising a swallowed alkali with an acid is contraindicated because the acid-base reaction is strongly exothermic; the heat liberated adds a thermal burn to the existing chemical injury, producing MORE corrosive tissue damage. For this reason chemical neutralisation is never attempted and dilution or supportive care is used instead. This worsening of injury is why weak acids cannot be given with alkaline corrosives.

## answer_b
Produce CO2 gas

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Gas (CO2) liberation is a concern specific to neutralising with carbonates/bicarbonate, not the principal reason weak acids are avoided with alkalis.

## answer_c
Induce acidosis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A small oral weak acid does not induce systemic acidosis; the danger is local heat and corrosion, not an acid-base derangement.

## answer_d
Induce vomiting

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Vomiting is undesirable with corrosives (re-exposing the oesophagus) but is not the reason acids are withheld; the reason is the exothermic, injury-worsening reaction.

## title
Organophosphate delayed neuropathy causes distal limb paralysis

## question
Delayed peripheral neuropathy in organophosphorus poisoning is characterized by:

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Develops within 3 days

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Organophosphate-induced delayed neuropathy appears 1-3 WEEKS after exposure, not within 3 days; an onset within 3 days would describe the acute cholinergic or intermediate phase.

## answer_b
Causes paralysis of distal limb muscles

## explanation_b
Correct. Organophosphate-induced delayed neuropathy (OPIDN) is a distal, length-dependent sensorimotor polyneuropathy that develops one to three weeks after exposure and produces weakness and paralysis of the DISTAL limb muscles, often with foot- and wrist-drop. It results from inhibition and ageing of neuropathy target esterase rather than acetylcholinesterase. Distal limb involvement is therefore its defining feature.

## answer_c
Causes paralysis of neck flexor muscles

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Paralysis of neck flexor, proximal limb and respiratory muscles a few days after poisoning describes the intermediate syndrome, not the delayed distal neuropathy.

## answer_d
All of the above

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Because the early-onset and neck-flexor descriptions belong to other syndromes, "all of the above" cannot be correct.
