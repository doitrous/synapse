## title
Defining feature of endocrine glands

## question
Which of the following statements describes endocrine glands best?

## subject
endo

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
They have no trabeculae

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Many endocrine glands do have connective-tissue trabeculae/septa (for example the adrenal and pituitary), so "no trabeculae" does not define them.

## answer_b
They have no follicles

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The thyroid is an endocrine gland built entirely of follicles, so absence of follicles is not a defining feature.

## answer_c
They have no ducts

## explanation_c
Correct. Endocrine glands are DUCTLESS glands: they lost their connection to a surface during development and release their hormones directly into the blood rather than through a duct. This is the defining structural feature that separates them from exocrine glands, whose secretion travels along a duct.

## answer_d
They are avascular

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Endocrine glands are in fact HIGHLY vascular — a rich capillary network carries the hormone away — so "avascular" is the opposite of the truth.

## title
Development of the adenohypophysis

## question
Which structure gives rise to the adenohypophysis?

## subject
endo

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Ebner's glands

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Ebner's (von Ebner's) glands are serous glands of the tongue, unrelated to pituitary development.

## answer_b
Rathke's pouch

## explanation_b
Correct. The adenohypophysis (anterior pituitary) develops from Rathke's pouch, an ectodermal diverticulum that grows upward from the roof of the primitive oral cavity (stomodeum). This oral ectodermal origin is why the anterior lobe is glandular epithelium, in contrast to the neurohypophysis, which grows down from the diencephalon as neural tissue.

## answer_c
Diencephalon

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The diencephalon gives rise to the neurohypophysis (posterior pituitary), not the adenohypophysis.

## answer_d
Hypothalamus

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The hypothalamus supplies the axons and releasing hormones that control the pituitary but does not form the adenohypophysis itself.

## title
The hypothalamic-hypophyseal portal system

## question
In the hypothalamic-hypophyseal portal system, we have:

## subject
endo

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Two arterial capillary networks

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Only the first (primary) plexus is fed directly by an artery; the second plexus is supplied by portal veins, so the two beds are not both arterial.

## answer_b
One capillary network

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A portal system by definition has TWO capillary beds in series, not one.

## answer_c
Two venous capillary networks

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The primary plexus is arterial-fed, so the two beds are not both venous.

## answer_d
One arterial and one venous capillary network

## explanation_d
Correct. The hypophyseal portal system links two capillary beds by portal veins. The primary (upper) plexus in the median eminence/infundibulum is supplied by the superior hypophyseal artery and picks up hypothalamic releasing/inhibiting hormones; hypophyseal portal veins then carry that blood to the secondary (lower) plexus in the pars distalis, which drains into veins. So there is one arterial-fed and one venous-fed capillary network connected in series.

## title
Ultrastructure of adrenal cortex cells

## question
Cells of the cortex of the suprarenal gland have a conspicuous morphological property. Which one?

## subject
endo

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Their rough endoplasmic reticulum is well developed

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Prominent rough ER is a feature of protein-secreting cells; the steroid-secreting cortical cells are dominated by SMOOTH ER instead.

## answer_b
Their cytoplasm is devoid of fat droplets

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Adrenal cortical cells are packed with lipid droplets (the cholesterol substrate for steroidogenesis), which is why they look pale and foamy ('spongiocytes'); they are not devoid of fat.

## answer_c
Their smooth endoplasmic reticulum is well developed

## explanation_c
Correct. The cortical cells of the suprarenal gland are steroid-secreting cells, so their conspicuous feature is abundant smooth endoplasmic reticulum, together with mitochondria that have tubular cristae and numerous lipid droplets. The smooth ER and these mitochondria house the enzymes that convert cholesterol into the corticosteroids.

## answer_d
Their nucleus has two lobes

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A bilobed nucleus is a feature of some granulocytes, not of adrenal cortical cells.

## title
Origin of adrenal chromaffin cells

## question
Which statement about the suprarenal gland is true?

## subject
endo

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
There are no androgen-producing cells in the suprarenal gland

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The zona reticularis of the cortex does produce androgens (e.g. DHEA), so it is false that the gland has no androgen-producing cells.

## answer_b
Aldosterone-producing cells are under the control of ACTH

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Aldosterone-producing cells of the zona glomerulosa are controlled mainly by the renin-angiotensin system and plasma potassium, not by ACTH.

## answer_c
Cortex and medulla are of the same embryonic origin

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The cortex and medulla have DIFFERENT embryonic origins: the cortex from coelomic (mesodermal) epithelium and the medulla from the neural crest.

## answer_d
Chromaffin cells are derived from the neural crest

## explanation_d
Correct. The chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla are modified postganglionic sympathetic neurons derived from the NEURAL CREST. This explains why they are innervated by preganglionic sympathetic fibres and secrete catecholamines (adrenaline, noradrenaline) directly into the blood, and why the medulla is embryologically distinct from the mesodermal cortex.

## title
Composition of thyroid colloid

## question
Which molecule is the main compound of the colloid in the thyroid gland?

## subject
endo

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
A glycoprotein called thyroglobulin

## explanation_a
Correct. The colloid that fills the thyroid follicle is composed mainly of thyroglobulin, a large iodinated GLYCOPROTEIN made by the follicular cells. It is the stored, inactive precursor from which the thyroid hormones T3 and T4 are later cleaved after endocytosis and lysosomal digestion.

## answer_b
A lipoprotein called thyroglobulin

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Thyroglobulin is a glycoprotein, not a lipoprotein.

## answer_c
A glycoprotein called thyroxin

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Thyroxin (T4) is the active hormone released into blood, not the bulk molecule of the stored colloid.

## answer_d
A lipoprotein called thyroxin

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The colloid is neither a lipoprotein nor thyroxin; it is the glycoprotein thyroglobulin.

## title
Epithelium of thyroid follicular cells

## question
Which type of epithelium do the follicular cells of the thyroid gland form?

## subject
endo

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Stratified squamous to simple columnar epithelium

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The follicle is lined by a SIMPLE (single-layered) epithelium, never a stratified one.

## answer_b
Simple squamous to simple columnar epithelium

## explanation_b
Correct. Thyroid follicular cells form a simple epithelium whose height varies with activity: flattened (simple squamous) when the gland is inactive and the follicle is distended with colloid, cuboidal when normally active, and simple columnar when strongly stimulated. So the range is simple squamous to simple columnar, always a single layer.

## answer_c
Pseudostratified columnar non-ciliated epithelium

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The follicular epithelium is a simple layer, not pseudostratified columnar.

## answer_d
Neuroepithelium

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Neuroepithelium is sensory epithelium (e.g. in taste buds or the retina), not the lining of a thyroid follicle.

## title
Endocrine tissue that does not store its secretion

## question
Which of the following does NOT store its secretion?

## subject
endo

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Pars nervosa

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The pars nervosa stores oxytocin and ADH in the terminals of hypothalamic axons (seen as Herring bodies), so it does store secretion.

## answer_b
Pars distalis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The pars distalis cells store their peptide/protein hormones in cytoplasmic secretory granules until released.

## answer_c
Suprarenal cortex

## explanation_c
Correct. The suprarenal (adrenal) cortex secretes STEROID hormones, and steroid-secreting cells do not store their product; they synthesise it on demand from cholesterol and release it immediately by simple diffusion. So the cortex is the tissue that does not store its secretion, unlike the granule- or colloid-storing endocrine tissues.

## answer_d
Thyroid gland

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The thyroid is unique among endocrine glands in storing a large extracellular reserve of hormone as colloid (thyroglobulin) in its follicles.
