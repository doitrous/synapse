## title
Cholinergic agonist for paralytic ileus

## question
Which of the following is a therapeutic use of cholinergic agonists?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Bronchial asthma

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A muscarinic agonist would contract bronchial smooth muscle and increase secretions, worsening rather than treating bronchial asthma.

## answer_b
Myocardial infarction

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Cholinergic agonists have no established therapeutic role in myocardial infarction; if anything, unopposed vagal/muscarinic stimulation (bradycardia) is a risk to manage, not a treatment goal, after MI.

## answer_c
Paralytic ileus

## explanation_c
Correct. Bethanechol and carbachol are direct-acting choline esters resistant to cholinesterase breakdown, giving them a long enough duration for clinical use; both are used, orally or subcutaneously, for post-operative urinary retention and paralytic ileus, stimulating M3 receptors on gut and detrusor smooth muscle to restore contraction and motility. This is why it is bethanechol/carbachol, not a bronchodilator-worsening or acid-secretion-raising agent, that appears on the paralytic-ileus/urinary-retention indication list.

## answer_d
Peptic ulcer

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Cholinergic stimulation increases gastric acid secretion via M3 receptors on parietal cells, which would worsen rather than treat peptic ulcer disease.

## title
Pilocarpine BBB penetration and miotic use

## question
Which of the following parasympathomimetic has the greatest lipid solubility, passes the blood brain barrier, and is suitable for use as a miotic?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Acetylcholine

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Acetylcholine is a charged, unstable quaternary choline ester, poorly lipid-soluble and not clinically used as a miotic; it is also hydrolysed almost instantly by cholinesterase.

## answer_b
Methacholine

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Methacholine is likewise a charged choline ester with poor BBB penetration; its clinical role is diagnostic (bronchial hyperreactivity challenge testing), not as a miotic.

## answer_c
Neostigmine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Neostigmine is a charged, quaternary-amine anticholinesterase that does not cross the blood-brain barrier appreciably; it is used for myasthenia gravis and post-operative atony, not as a miotic.

## answer_d
Pilocarpine

## explanation_d
Correct. Pilocarpine is a natural tertiary-amine cholinomimetic alkaloid, and being lipid-soluble it crosses the blood-brain barrier, unlike the charged choline esters and neostigmine. It is a pure muscarinic agonist and a first-line miotic for glaucoma, contracting ciliary/iris muscle to open the trabecular drainage angle and lower intraocular pressure.

## title
Post-operative urinary retention management

## question
A 35-year-old lady is recovering from abdominal surgery. She fails to pass urine. Which of the following is the best strategy?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Give her an infusion of acetyl choline because it has a very short half-life

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Acetylcholine is hydrolysed by acetylcholinesterase almost instantly at the synapse; it has no useful systemic half-life as an infused drug and is not used this way clinically, regardless of any CNS consideration.

## answer_b
Give her neostigmine because it will not have CNS side effects

## explanation_b
Incorrect. While neostigmine's lack of CNS side effects is true, offering a reason to give a cholinergic drug does not address the more urgent, prior clinical question of whether the bladder can safely be stimulated at all.

## answer_c
Give her bethanechol because it is selective to the urinary tract

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Bethanechol's own clinical-use listing is post-operative urinary retention AND paralytic ileus -- it is not selective to the urinary tract, since it stimulates M3 receptors on gut smooth muscle just as it does on the detrusor.

## answer_d
Make sure she does not have urinary tract obstruction before giving any medication

## explanation_d
Correct. Before treating post-operative urinary retention with any cholinergic agonist or anticholinesterase, the safest strategy is to first exclude a mechanical bladder-outlet obstruction: stimulating a detrusor that cannot empty against a fixed obstruction will not relieve retention and can dangerously raise intravesical pressure. Only once obstruction is excluded does it become safe to try a cholinergic strategy such as bethanechol or neostigmine.

## title
Atropine's advantage in organophosphorus poisoning

## question
In which of the following situations will it be better to use atropine rather than one of its substitutes?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Bronchial asthma

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Ipratropium and tiotropium are the preferred inhaled antimuscarinic substitutes for bronchial asthma/COPD, minimising systemic effects compared with atropine.

## answer_b
Eye fundus examination

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Tropicamide and cyclopentolate are preferred over atropine for fundus examination, being short-acting with a shorter cycloplegic effect.

## answer_c
Treatment of organophosphorus poisoning

## explanation_c
Correct. Organophosphorus poisoning is the one setting where atropine itself remains the drug of choice: its competitive muscarinic blockade directly and titratably reverses the life-threatening muscarinic excess (bradycardia, bronchospasm, secretions) of the poisoning, an emergency role no listed substitute is established for. In every other listed setting, atropine has been superseded by a better-tolerated, more selective substitute.

## answer_d
Treatment of urinary incontinence

## explanation_d
Incorrect. M3-selective antimuscarinics such as oxybutynin, tolterodine and solifenacin are preferred over atropine for urinary incontinence/overactive bladder, reducing systemic antimuscarinic side effects.

## title
Atropine and the nicotinic effects of organophosphorus poisoning

## question
A farmer exposed to insecticides (irreversible cholinesterase inhibitors) was given atropine. Which of the following effects of the cholinesterase inhibitor will NOT be blocked by the atropine?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Bronchoconstriction/bronchospasm

## explanation_a
Incorrect (this IS blocked). Bronchoconstriction is a muscarinic effect of organophosphate poisoning, and atropine's competitive muscarinic blockade reverses it.

## answer_b
Increased bowel motility (i.e., diarrhea)

## explanation_b
Incorrect (this IS blocked). Increased bowel motility/diarrhea is a muscarinic (SLUDGE) sign of the poisoning, blocked by atropine's antimuscarinic action.

## answer_c
Increased secretions of lacrimal fluid, saliva, mucus

## explanation_c
Incorrect (this IS blocked). Increased lacrimal, salivary and mucus secretion are classic muscarinic signs, reversed by atropine's competitive blockade at muscarinic receptors.

## answer_d
Skeletal muscle overstimulation, eventually leading to skeletal muscle paralysis

## explanation_d
Correct. Skeletal muscle overstimulation progressing to paralysis is a NICOTINIC effect, from nicotinic receptor overstimulation at the neuromuscular junction. Atropine acts by muscarinic blockade only, with no nicotinic receptor action, so it reverses the muscarinic picture but leaves this nicotinic, neuromuscular effect untouched -- managing it instead requires an oxime (pralidoxime) to reactivate acetylcholinesterase before the phosphorylated enzyme 'ages.'

## subject

## vignette

## correct_answer

## answer_a

## explanation_a

## answer_b

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Caffeine is a CNS stimulant (methylxanthine) with no antimuscarinic action relevant to blocking neostigmine's cholinergic side effects.

Which of the following nasal decongestants is given orally?

## subject

## explanation_a

## answer_b
Tetrahydrozoline

Systemic absorption of an oral decongestant like pseudoephedrine is also why oral decongestants carry systemic side effects such as hypertension and infant CNS depression, and long-term use of any of these causes rebound congestion (rhinitis medicamentosa).

Statins raise HDL

## question
Statins are most likely to increase which of the following?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Hepatic intra-cellular cholesterol

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Statins DEPLETE hepatic intracellular cholesterol -- that depletion is exactly what upregulates hepatic LDL-receptor expression and clears plasma LDL.

## answer_b
Plasma HDL

## explanation_b
Correct. Beyond their primary LDL-lowering mechanism, statins modestly increase HDL (and decrease triglycerides) as an additional lipid effect. This modest HDL-raising, triglyceride-lowering profile is an additional benefit layered on top of statins' primary LDL-lowering action.

## answer_c
Plasma triglycerides

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Statins lower, not raise, plasma triglycerides.

## answer_d
Vascular inflammation

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Statins' pleiotropic effects include reduced, not increased, vascular inflammation, alongside improved endothelial function and plaque stabilisation.

## title
Bempedoic acid mechanism

## question
Inhibition of ATP citrate lyase, decreased endogenous cholesterol synthesis, indirectly increase in the expression of LDL receptors is the mechanism of action of which of the following?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Bempedoic acid

## explanation_a
Correct. Bempedoic acid inhibits ATP-citrate lyase, an enzyme upstream of HMG-CoA reductase in hepatic cholesterol synthesis; blocking it decreases cholesterol synthesis, indirectly increasing hepatic LDL-receptor expression, the same compensatory upregulation statins rely on. It is activated in the liver and used as an oral statin adjunct.

## answer_b
Ezetimibe

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Ezetimibe blocks intestinal cholesterol absorption via the NPC1L1 transporter, not hepatic ATP-citrate lyase.

## answer_c
Fibrates

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Fibrates are PPAR-alpha agonists mainly lowering triglycerides via lipoprotein lipase, not ATP-citrate lyase inhibitors.

## answer_d
Niacin

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Niacin inhibits adipose lipolysis via the GPR109A receptor, a different mechanism from ATP-citrate lyase inhibition.

Clopidogrel indication

## question
Which of the following is an indication of clopidogrel?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Prophylaxis of deep venous thrombosis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Deep venous thrombosis prophylaxis is an anticoagulant indication (e.g. heparin, a DOAC), not an antiplatelet indication.

## answer_b
Prophylaxis of transient ischemic attacks and ischemic stroke

## explanation_b
Correct. Clopidogrel is an irreversible P2Y12 ADP-receptor antagonist on platelets (a prodrug requiring hepatic CYP2C19 bioactivation), and its established indication is secondary prophylaxis of ischemic events such as TIA and ischemic stroke, by preventing ADP-driven platelet aggregation. This arterial, antiplatelet role is distinct from the venous, anticoagulant indications the other options describe.

## answer_c
Treatment of pulmonary embolism

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Treatment of an established pulmonary embolism is an anticoagulant/thrombolytic indication, not an antiplatelet one.

## answer_d
Treatment of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia is managed with a direct thrombin inhibitor such as argatroban, not an antiplatelet drug.

## title
Warfarin's in-vivo-only mechanism

## question
Which of the following statements is correct regarding anticoagulant therapy?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Heparin has delayed onset

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Heparin has a RAPID onset after IV administration (delayed 20-60 minutes only after SC dosing), not a delayed onset.

## answer_b
Heparin is effective orally.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Heparin is a large, charged molecule not absorbed from the gut, so it is NOT effective orally -- it must be given IV or SC.

## answer_c
Warfarin is effective in vivo only

## explanation_c
Correct. Warfarin inhibits vitamin K epoxide reductase in the liver, preventing regeneration of active vitamin K needed to activate clotting factors -- a hepatic, cellular process occurring only in a living system, so warfarin has no anticoagulant effect if simply added to blood in a test tube, unlike heparin's direct antithrombin-mediated action, which works both in vivo and in vitro. This in-vivo dependency is a direct consequence of warfarin needing living hepatocyte machinery to exert its effect.

## answer_d
Warfarin is antagonized by protamine sulfate.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Protamine sulfate is the antidote for HEPARIN, not warfarin -- warfarin's antidote is vitamin K1 (with PCC/FFP for serious bleeding).

## title
Abciximab mechanism

## question
Which of the following is the mechanism of action of abciximab?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Activate antithrombin III.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Activating antithrombin III is heparin's mechanism, not abciximab's.

## answer_b
Inhibition of vitamin K epoxide reductase enzyme.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Inhibiting vitamin K epoxide reductase is warfarin's mechanism, not abciximab's.

## answer_c
Block of platelet glycoprotein IIb/IIIa receptors.

## explanation_c
Correct. Abciximab, eptifibatide and tirofiban are GP IIb/IIIa antagonists, given IV only, that block the glycoprotein IIb/IIIa receptor on platelets -- the final common pathway of platelet aggregation, through which fibrinogen cross-links adjacent platelets regardless of which upstream agonist activated them. This final-pathway blockade is why GP IIb/IIIa antagonists are effective regardless of which upstream platelet agonist is driving aggregation.

## answer_d
Block irreversibly platelet ADP receptors.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Irreversibly blocking platelet ADP (P2Y12) receptors is clopidogrel/prasugrel's mechanism, a more upstream, single-receptor action distinct from abciximab's final-pathway blockade.
