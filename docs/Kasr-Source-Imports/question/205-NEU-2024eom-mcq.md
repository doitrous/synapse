## title
Nerve supply of the upper lateral eyelid skin

## question
Skin over the upper lateral eyelid is supplied by the:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Lacrimal nerve

## explanation_a
Correct. The lacrimal nerve is the most lateral branch of the ophthalmic division (V1) of the trigeminal nerve; after running along the upper border of lateral rectus and through the lacrimal gland, its terminal cutaneous twigs supply the skin of the upper eyelid's lateral part and adjoining conjunctiva. This lateral position, distinct from the more medial frontal-nerve branches, is exactly what makes it the answer for the LATERAL upper eyelid specifically.

## answer_b
Supraorbital nerve

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The supraorbital nerve (a frontal nerve branch of V1) supplies the skin of the upper eyelid's medial/central part, forehead and scalp — the middle of the eyelid, not its lateral part.

## answer_c
Supratrochlear nerve

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The supratrochlear nerve (also a frontal nerve branch of V1) supplies the skin near the medial angle of the eye and lower medial forehead — the medial, not lateral, side.

## answer_d
Zygomaticofacial nerve

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The zygomaticofacial nerve (a branch of the zygomatic nerve, V2/maxillary division) supplies skin over the zygomatic (cheek) prominence, below and lateral to the eye — not the eyelid itself.

## title
Effect of a stab wound in the submandibular triangle

## question
Stab wound directed into submandibular triangle may lead to:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Ptosis of the eye

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Ptosis follows sympathetic chain or oculomotor nerve injury in the neck/orbit region, not a structure that runs through the submandibular triangle.

## answer_b
Drop of the shoulder

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Drop of the shoulder follows accessory nerve (CN XI) injury, which crosses the POSTERIOR triangle of the neck, not the submandibular triangle.

## answer_c
Deviation of the mouth

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Deviation of the mouth follows facial nerve (CN VII) injury; the facial nerve's cervical branch does cross near this region but the submandibular triangle's most exposed named nerve is the hypoglossal, not the facial trunk.

## answer_d
Deviation of the tongue

## explanation_d
Correct. The hypoglossal nerve (CN XII) loops through the floor of the submandibular triangle on its way to the tongue, making it the structure most vulnerable to a penetrating stab wound there. Hypoglossal nerve injury paralyses the ipsilateral genioglossus and related tongue muscles. The tongue's own unopposed contralateral muscles then push it over toward the injured side on protrusion, producing the deviation this option describes.

Facial nerve exit from the skull

## question
The facial nerve:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Marginal mandibular branch supplies muscles of the upper and lower lips

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The marginal mandibular branch supplies the muscles of the LOWER lip only (depressor labii inferioris, depressor anguli oris, mentalis) — the upper lip is supplied by the buccal and zygomatic branches instead.

## answer_b
Emerges through the stylomastoid foramen

## explanation_b
Correct. After leaving the brainstem and traversing the facial canal within the temporal bone, the facial nerve exits the skull through the stylomastoid foramen, immediately posterior to the styloid process, before entering the parotid gland and dividing into its terminal branches. This exit point also explains the classic surgical landmark: a parotid or mastoid-region approach must identify the nerve as it emerges here before it fans out into its branches within the gland.

Has four main branches that exit the parotid gland

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The facial nerve classically divides into FIVE terminal branches (temporal, zygomatic, buccal, marginal mandibular, cervical) within the parotid gland, not four.

## answer_d
Supplies the anterior belly of digastric

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The facial nerve supplies the POSTERIOR belly of digastric (via its digastric branch); the anterior belly of digastric is supplied by the mylohyoid nerve, a branch of the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve (CN V3).

Muscle that opens the jaw

## question
Which muscle helps to open the jaw?

## subject
neuro

## status

## owner

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Medial pterygoid is a muscle of MASTICATION that elevates (closes) the mandible and assists in protrusion, not opening.

## answer_b
Lateral pterygoid

## explanation_b
Correct. Lateral pterygoid is the only one of the four muscles of mastication that acts to open (depress) the mandible, by pulling the condyle and articular disc forward as the mouth opens; the other three (masseter, temporalis, medial pterygoid) all elevate/close the jaw. This is why a lateral pterygoid palsy classically causes the jaw to deviate TOWARD the weak side on opening, since the healthy contralateral muscle pulls the mandible over unopposed.

Masseter

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Masseter is a powerful jaw-CLOSING (elevator) muscle.

## answer_d
Temporalis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Temporalis is also a jaw-CLOSING (elevator) muscle, with its posterior fibres additionally retracting the mandible.

## title
Tongue muscle supplied by the pharyngeal plexus

## question
The ___ muscle of the tongue is innervated by the pharyngeal plexus:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Hyoglossus

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Hyoglossus, like almost all other tongue muscles, is supplied by the hypoglossal nerve (CN XII), not the pharyngeal plexus.

## answer_b
Styloglossus

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Styloglossus is supplied by the hypoglossal nerve (CN XII), not the pharyngeal plexus.

## answer_c
Palatoglossus

## explanation_c
Correct. Palatoglossus is the single exception among the tongue's muscles: although it acts on the tongue, it is developmentally a palatal muscle and is supplied by the pharyngeal plexus (vagal fibres, CN X) rather than the hypoglossal nerve, which supplies every other tongue muscle (extrinsic and intrinsic). This split explains why an isolated vagal lesion affecting the pharyngeal plexus can weaken palatoglossus specifically while every other tongue muscle, still supplied by an intact hypoglossal nerve, stays unaffected.

## answer_d
Intrinsic vertical

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The intrinsic vertical (and all other intrinsic) tongue muscles are supplied by the hypoglossal nerve (CN XII), not the pharyngeal plexus.

## question
Ptosis (dropping of the upper eyelid) is due to paralysis of the smooth muscle part of the ___ muscle:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Superior rectus

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Superior rectus is an extraocular muscle that elevates the globe; it has no eyelid-elevating smooth-muscle component.

## answer_b
Superior oblique

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Superior oblique is an extraocular muscle that depresses/rotates the globe, unrelated to eyelid elevation.

## answer_c
Levator palpebrae superioris

## explanation_c
Correct. Levator palpebrae superioris has two parts: a voluntary skeletal-muscle component (oculomotor nerve, CN III) providing most eyelid elevation, and a smooth-muscle component — the superior tarsal muscle (Müller's muscle) — supplied by sympathetic fibres, which provides the extra few millimetres of lid elevation. Loss of sympathetic supply paralyses this smooth part specifically, producing the mild ptosis classically seen in Horner's syndrome (as opposed to the complete ptosis of an oculomotor nerve palsy, which paralyses the skeletal part instead).

## answer_d
Depressor anguli oris

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Depressor anguli oris is a muscle of facial expression around the mouth, unrelated to the eyelid.

Muscle that rotates the face upward and to the left

## question
Moving the head so that the face looks upwards and to the left is a movement produced by the:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Platysma

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Platysma is a thin subcutaneous muscle of facial expression/neck skin tension, not a mover of head rotation.

## answer_b
Left trapezius only

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Trapezius contributes to head/neck extension and, with sternocleidomastoid, to rotation, but acting alone on the LEFT it does not produce this specific upward-and-left-facing turn the way the paired sternocleidomastoid action does.

## answer_c
Right geniohyoid only

Right sternocleidomastoid

This paired, opposite-side rotation action is why testing sternocleidomastoid strength — turning the head against resistance — evaluates the accessory nerve on the side OPPOSITE to the direction the patient turns.

## title
Nerve supplying skin over the angle of the mandible

## question
A patient had lost cutaneous sensation over the angle of the mandible, which nerve is most likely damaged?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Mandibular

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The mandibular nerve (CN V3) supplies skin over the lower face/jaw via its own branches, but the specific patch of skin overlying the angle of the mandible and the parotid/lower ear region is instead supplied by a cervical plexus branch, not the trigeminal mandibular division.

## answer_b
Ophthalmic

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The ophthalmic nerve (CN V1) supplies the forehead, upper eyelid and nose — nowhere near the angle of the mandible.

## answer_c
Great auricular

## explanation_c
Correct. The great auricular nerve, a branch of the cervical plexus (C2, C3) that ascends over sternocleidomastoid, supplies skin over the angle of the mandible, parotid gland region and lower auricle — its territory is exactly the area described, making it the classic answer for sensory loss here. This is also why great auricular nerve injury during parotidectomy, where it lies superficially and is at particular surgical risk, produces numbness over the parotid region and mandibular angle rather than the mouth or upper face.

## answer_d
Auriculotemporal

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The auriculotemporal nerve (a branch of CN V3) supplies skin over the temporal region and anterior/superior auricle, adjacent to but not covering the angle of the mandible itself.
