# Item

ART-MSK-MUST-PED501-BONE-AND-JOINT-INFECTION

## title
Paediatric bone and joint infection/inflammation — osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, JIA and reactive arthritis

## subject
msk

## topic
Musculoskeletal system

## summary
A febrile, systemically unwell child with a single hot swollen joint or a painful limb needs osteomyelitis, septic arthritis and systemic JIA actively differentiated from each other and from the far more common, afebrile reactive arthritis/transient synovitis — the same core skill (severity-and-pattern recognition) tested from four different angles.

## sections
### Definition
Osteomyelitis (most often Staphylococcus aureus) presents with bone pain, fever and local swelling following a preceding skin infection. Septic arthritis presents as a single red, hot, swollen joint with fever, refusal to move it and severe pain on any passive movement. Systemic-onset JIA presents with fever, polyarthritis (more than four joints) and a salmon-pink evanescent rash, with negative ANA/dsDNA/ASOT distinguishing it from SLE and post-streptococcal arthritis. Reactive arthritis (transient synovitis) is the most common cause of acute hip pain in children 2-12 years, typically post-viral, with decreased range of movement but no fever and no systemic illness.

### Mechanism
The differentiating axis across all four is systemic illness and fever: septic arthritis and osteomyelitis are both acutely febrile and unwell; systemic JIA is febrile but with a more chronic, multi-joint, rash-associated picture; reactive arthritis/transient synovitis is characteristically afebrile and the child remains otherwise well. Investigation choice follows from this — joint aspiration and blood markers to confirm/exclude septic arthritis urgently, autoimmune/streptococcal serology to support a JIA diagnosis, and often just clinical observation (normal ultrasound/CRP) to support transient synovitis.

### Key determinants
Fever plus single joint plus severe pain on any movement = septic arthritis until excluded. Fever plus multiple joints plus rash plus negative autoimmune serology = systemic JIA. No fever plus reduced external rotation post-viral illness = reactive arthritis/transient synovitis. Bone (not joint) pain plus fever plus preceding skin infection = osteomyelitis.

### Clinical significance
Missing septic arthritis by assuming transient synovitis risks permanent joint damage; over-treating every limping child as septic arthritis over-investigates and delays discharge. The systemic-JIA serology pattern (ANA/dsDNA/ASOT all negative) is a recurring exam trap that inverts students' instinct to associate any inflammatory arthritis with a positive autoimmune marker.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming transient synovitis without excluding septic arthritis first in a febrile child. Expecting a positive ANA for systemic JIA, when it is characteristically negative. Confusing osteomyelitis (bone) with septic arthritis (joint) tenderness patterns.

## hold_these
Septic arthritis: single hot swollen joint + fever + refusal to move it, until excluded.
Systemic JIA: fever + polyarthritis + salmon-pink rash + negative ANA/dsDNA/ASOT.
Reactive arthritis/transient synovitis: afebrile, decreased hip external rotation, post-viral, most common cause of acute childhood hip pain.
Osteomyelitis: most often Staphylococcus aureus, bone pain + fever + preceding skin infection.

## lose_the_mark
Diagnosing transient synovitis in a febrile, systemically unwell child without excluding septic arthritis first.
Expecting a positive ANA to diagnose systemic-onset JIA.
Confusing bone (osteomyelitis) versus joint (septic arthritis) tenderness patterns.

# Item

## id
ART-MSK-MUST-PED501-GROWTH-PLATE-AND-OVERUSE

## title
Overuse and growth-plate disorders — Osgood-Schlatter disease and red-flag back pain

## subject
msk

## topic
Musculoskeletal system

## summary
Osgood-Schlatter disease's tibial-tuberosity presentation in a physically active adolescent, and the red-flag pattern of nocturnal back pain pointing toward tumour (osteoid osteoma), are both "location and pattern, not just symptom" recognition tasks.

## sections
### Definition
Osgood-Schlatter disease is an osteochondritis of the patellar tendon insertion at the tibial tuberosity, affecting physically active adolescents (classically footballers/basketball players), with knee pain worse after exercise, localised tibial-tuberosity swelling and tenderness, and often hamstring tightness — bilateral in 25-50% of cases. Osteoid osteoma is a benign tumour that classically presents with back pain worse at night in a young child; back pain in young children is uncommon and should be taken seriously, with red flags including young age, night waking, fever, weight loss and focal neurological signs.

### Mechanism
Both conditions are recognised by their specific pain pattern (exercise-related tibial-tuberosity pain; nocturnal back pain) rather than by a single defining investigation — Osgood-Schlatter is usually a clinical diagnosis, and osteoid osteoma prompts imaging because of its red-flag pattern rather than a screening test applied to every child with any back pain.

### Key determinants
The exact anatomical location (tibial tuberosity vs vertebral) and the specific timing pattern (worse after exercise vs worse at night, waking from sleep) are what differentiate these two conditions from other, more common or more benign causes of adolescent limb/back pain.

### Clinical significance
Osgood-Schlatter is a reassurance-and-activity-modification diagnosis; osteoid osteoma (and back pain red flags generally) is an imaging-now diagnosis. Confusing the urgency of these two very different-looking, but both "pain related to activity/time" presentations is the trap.

### Common misconceptions
Treating any adolescent knee pain the same way regardless of exact location (tibial tuberosity is specific to Osgood-Schlatter). Dismissing nocturnal back pain in a young child as growing pains or muscular strain without checking red flags.

## hold_these
Osgood-Schlatter disease: tibial-tuberosity pain/swelling, worse after exercise, in a physically active adolescent.
Nocturnal back pain in a young child is a red flag; osteoid osteoma is a specific benign-tumour cause.

## lose_the_mark
Diagnosing Osgood-Schlatter disease without the specific tibial-tuberosity location.
Reassuring nocturnal back pain in a young child without checking red-flag features.

# Item

## id
ART-MSK-MUST-PED501-LIMP-AND-PAIN-DIFFERENTIALS

## title
Paediatric limp and limb pain — non-accidental injury, growing pains, CRPS and chondromalacia patellae

## subject
msk

## topic
Musculoskeletal system

## summary
Four causes of paediatric limb pain/limp are distinguished by their pattern rather than pain severity alone: an unexplained fracture in a pre-mobile infant (non-accidental injury), bilateral nocturnal pain with a normal exam (growing pains), disproportionate pain with allodynia after minor trauma (complex regional pain syndrome), and activity-related anterior knee pain in an adolescent female (chondromalacia patellae).

## sections
### Definition
A femur fracture in an infant not yet independently mobile, with no consistent trauma history and no signs of infection, is a non-accidental injury red flag. Growing pains present as bilateral, nocturnal leg pain in a well child with an entirely normal examination and no daytime limitation. Complex regional pain syndrome often presents in a single foot or ankle after minor trauma, with severe disproportionate pain, allodynia and a limb that may be cool and mottled. Chondromalacia patellae is patellofemoral cartilage softening, most often in adolescent females, causing pain when the patella is loaded — stairs, or standing from sitting.

### Mechanism
Non-accidental injury is identified by the absence of a plausible mechanical explanation, not by the fracture type itself. Growing pains is a diagnosis of exclusion built on a reassuringly normal, bilateral, nocturnal pattern. CRPS reflects an exaggerated pain-processing response disproportionate to the inciting minor trauma. Chondromalacia patellae reflects a biomechanical loading pattern, often with coexisting hypermobility or flat feet.

### Key determinants
Age and mobility status (pre-mobile infant) plus an implausible or absent trauma history point to non-accidental injury. Bilateral, nocturnal, normal-exam pain points to growing pains. Disproportionate pain, allodynia and a cool/mottled limb after minor trauma points to CRPS. Female adolescent, stairs/sitting-to-standing anterior knee pain points to chondromalacia patellae.

### Clinical significance
Each of these four is a "pattern recognition prevents both under- and over-investigation" concept: missing NAI risks ongoing harm; over-investigating growing pains burdens a well child; missing CRPS delays specific pain-management referral; and confusing chondromalacia patellae with Osgood-Schlatter misdirects management toward the wrong structure.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming a fracture always needs a specific traumatic mechanism to explain it, rather than recognising the absence of one as itself the red flag. Over-investigating classic growing pains. Confusing chondromalacia patellae's diffuse patellofemoral pain with Osgood-Schlatter's specific tibial-tuberosity swelling.

## hold_these
Unexplained femur fracture, pre-mobile infant, no infection signs = non-accidental injury red flag.
Growing pains: bilateral, nocturnal, normal exam, no daytime limitation.
CRPS: disproportionate pain + allodynia + cool/mottled limb after minor trauma.
Chondromalacia patellae: adolescent female, anterior knee pain on stairs/rising from sitting.

## lose_the_mark
Requiring a specific traumatic mechanism before considering non-accidental injury.
Over-investigating a classic, normal-exam growing-pains presentation.
Confusing chondromalacia patellae's pain pattern with Osgood-Schlatter disease's tibial-tuberosity swelling.

ART-MSK-MUST-PED501-CONGENITAL-AND-DEVELOPMENTAL

## title
Congenital and developmental limb presentations — talipes, osteogenesis imperfecta, DDH, rickets and normal variants

## subject
msk

## topic
Musculoskeletal system

## summary
This article covers the "test-it-to-tell-it-apart" congenital/developmental limb presentations: positional talipes (passively correctable), osteogenesis imperfecta (recurrent fracture + blue sclera), late-presenting DDH (painless limp + asymmetric folds), rickets (bow legs + swollen wrists) versus two genuinely normal variants — toddler flat feet and idiopathic toe-walking, each confirmed benign by a specific bedside test.

## sections
### Definition
Positional talipes equinovarus is a normal-sized, passively-correctable-to-neutral newborn foot deformity from intrauterine compression. Osteogenesis imperfecta presents with recurrent low-trauma fractures, osteoporotic-looking bone on X-ray, and a subtle blue scleral tinge, often with a similarly affected sibling given its typically autosomal dominant inheritance. Developmental dysplasia of the hip can present late as a painless limp with asymmetric thigh skin folds and reduced abduction, especially after breech birth, since newborn screening is not 100% sensitive. Bow legs with swollen wrists (rather than bow legs alone) suggests rickets over the normal genu varum variant of early childhood.

### Mechanism
Flat feet are a normal toddler variant from a flattened medial arch plus a fat pad that resolves with age — confirmed benign when an arch appears on tiptoe or big-toe extension. Idiopathic toe-walking is a normal variant confirmed by the child being able to heel-walk on request with normal tone and reflexes, distinguishing it from mild cerebral palsy (increased tone) or, in older boys, Duchenne muscular dystrophy.

### Key determinants
Each of these six presentations is confirmed or excluded by a specific bedside manoeuvre or associated finding: passive correction to neutral (positional talipes), blue sclera plus affected sibling (OI), asymmetric folds plus breech history (late DDH), swollen wrists (rickets vs normal bow legs), arch on tiptoe (normal flat feet), and heel-walk on request with normal tone (normal toe-walking).

### Clinical significance
Non-accidental-injury concerns must run in parallel with, not instead of, investigating for osteogenesis imperfecta when the clinical picture supports it. Recognising the two genuinely benign normal variants (flat feet, toe-walking) by their specific confirmatory test avoids unnecessary referral and parental anxiety, while missing late DDH or rickets has lasting orthopaedic consequences.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming any newborn foot deformity is a fixed structural problem without testing passive correction. Treating OI and non-accidental injury as mutually exclusive rather than parallel considerations. Reassuring bow legs without checking for the wrist-swelling clue that distinguishes rickets. Referring every flat-footed or toe-walking toddler without first performing the specific bedside test that confirms the benign variant.

## hold_these
Positional talipes equinovarus: normal-sized, passively correctable to neutral.
Osteogenesis imperfecta: recurrent low-trauma fracture + blue sclera + often an affected sibling; investigate NAI in parallel, not instead.
Late DDH: painless limp + asymmetric thigh folds + reduced abduction, especially post-breech.
Rickets vs normal bow legs: swollen wrists is the distinguishing clue for rickets.
Normal flat feet: arch appears on tiptoe/big-toe extension. Normal toe-walking: can heel-walk on request, normal tone.

## lose_the_mark
Diagnosing a fixed talipes deformity without testing passive correction to neutral.
Treating osteogenesis imperfecta and non-accidental injury concerns as mutually exclusive.
Reassuring bow legs as normal without checking for swollen wrists (rickets).
Referring normal-variant flat feet/toe-walking without performing the specific confirmatory bedside test.
