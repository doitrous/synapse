ART-MUL-MUST-PED501-RESUSCITATION-ALGORITHMS

## title
Paediatric resuscitation — GCS scoring, fluid calculations and the ABCDEFG sequence

## subject
mul

## topic
Multisystem and emergencies

## summary
Paediatric resuscitation follows an ordered sequence — stimulate and shout, then airway, breathing, circulation, disability (including a mandatory glucose check) — with specific calculated fluid volumes and age-adjusted technique choices (GCS scoring, hands-encircling chest compressions, hand-encircling versus one-hand technique) tested from a different angle across the Lissauer emergencies chapter.

## sections
### Definition
The paediatric life support algorithm opens with checking safety, then stimulating the child and shouting for help, before airway, breathing and circulation are assessed and managed in sequence; "ABCDEFG" extends this with disability and a mandatory glucose check, since hypoglycaemia is a readily reversible cause of collapse, seizure or altered consciousness. The Glasgow Coma Scale (or its under-4 Children's Coma Scale variant) scores eye, verbal and motor responses independently, each with a floor of 1 (never 0), summing to a 3-15 total; below 8 signals an airway at risk.

### Mechanism
Fluid resuscitation in shock uses a 20 mL/kg crystalloid bolus, repeated as needed (smaller aliquots in trauma or DKA, where full boluses risk complications); once stabilised, the 24-hour fluid requirement is deficit (%dehydration x weight x 10 mL) plus maintenance (~100 mL/kg/24h, Holliday-Segar) plus continuing losses. Chest compression technique in infants (heart rate under the resuscitation threshold, airway/breathing already managed) is the hands-encircling method, needing a second rescuer for simultaneous ventilation; an unconscious child with abnormal breathing sounds (gasping, moaning) needs airway repositioning before bag-mask ventilation is attempted, since an unopened airway makes any ventilation technique fail. Once airway/breathing/circulation/conscious level are checked, pupils are examined next, screening for a structural or toxidrome cause.

### Key determinants
The glucose check applies whenever consciousness is altered or a seizure has occurred, whether the trigger is a treated seizure (check as part of the resuscitation sequence) or a known diabetic becoming confused and sweaty during exercise (check to confirm exertional hypoglycaemia) — same investigation, different clinical trigger, tested as two separate concepts in this module. SIDS risk is reduced by several factors, but supine sleeping position is singled out as the dominant one behind the epidemiological decline.

### Clinical significance
Correctly sequencing "stimulate and shout" before compressions, "check glucose" before further seizure treatment, and "open the airway" before bag-mask ventilation reflects that resuscitation is an ordered algorithm, not a menu of correct-but-unordered actions — getting the right action in the wrong order is a common exam trap this article's concepts are built to catch.

### Common misconceptions
Scoring a GCS/CCS domain as 0 for no response instead of the floor value of 1. Confusing the acute 20 mL/kg shock bolus with the 24-hour maintenance-plus-deficit calculation, or with the smaller trauma/DKA aliquot. Jumping to chest compressions or ventilation before completing the earlier steps in sequence.

## hold_these
GCS/CCS total is the sum of eye+verbal+motor, each floored at 1, never 0.
20 mL/kg crystalloid bolus for shock; 24h total = deficit + maintenance + continuing losses.
Glucose is checked as a routine resuscitation step, not only when specifically suspected.
Infant chest compressions (HR under threshold) use the hands-encircling technique.
Supine sleeping is the single most important SIDS-risk-reduction factor.

## lose_the_mark
Scoring "no response" as 0 instead of the GCS/CCS floor of 1 per domain.
Confusing the shock bolus (20 mL/kg) with the 24-hour maintenance/deficit total.
Starting ventilation or compressions before completing the earlier sequence steps.

ART-IMM-MUST-PED501-ALLERGY-AND-KAWASAKI

## title
Paediatric allergic reaction severity and Kawasaki disease

## subject
imm

## topic
Immune system

## summary
Allergic reaction management is graded by severity (mild reaction — oral antihistamine; anaphylaxis — intramuscular adrenaline first), and Kawasaki disease is tested from both its diagnostic-criteria angle and its treatment angle (IVIG, to prevent coronary artery aneurysm) — related immune-mediated presentations, each with a specific, non-interchangeable management answer.

## sections
### Definition
A mild allergic reaction (urticaria, facial flushing, normal vital signs, no airway/breathing compromise) is managed with an oral antihistamine and observation. Anaphylaxis is defined by upper airway obstruction and/or wheeze and/or shock — any one of these is sufficient — and its first-line treatment is intramuscular adrenaline, with high-flow oxygen, ahead of any antihistamine or steroid in any route. Kawasaki disease is diagnosed clinically on fever over 5 days with a set of features (cervical lymphadenopathy, conjunctival injection, injected pharynx/lips, finger/toe peeling), and treated with intravenous immunoglobulin specifically to reduce the risk of coronary artery aneurysm.

### Mechanism
The severity spectrum from mild allergic reaction to anaphylaxis is defined by whether airway, breathing or circulatory compromise is present, not by rash severity alone — a dramatic-looking urticarial rash with normal vital signs is still a mild reaction, while a less visually dramatic wheeze with normal-looking skin can be anaphylaxis. Kawasaki disease's coronary risk is the reason treatment (IVIG) is not optional supportive care; the diagnostic criteria exist specifically to catch the disease before that complication develops.

### Key determinants
Route and drug both matter in allergy management: intramuscular adrenaline (not IV, not antihistamine) for anaphylaxis; oral (not intramuscular) antihistamine for a mild reaction able to tolerate oral medicine. For Kawasaki, the fever-duration-plus-feature-count diagnostic pattern is distinguished from infectious mononucleosis (enlarged "slimy" tonsils but no peeling) and staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome (denuded skin but a different overall pattern).

### Clinical significance
Under-treating anaphylaxis (choosing an antihistamine or steroid route because it is "standard allergy treatment") is a life-threatening trap this module tests directly; missing Kawasaki disease, or treating it as a self-limiting fever, exposes the child to a preventable coronary complication.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming any facial swelling or dramatic-looking rash automatically means anaphylaxis, rather than checking for actual airway/breathing/circulatory compromise. Reaching for antihistamine or steroid before adrenaline in true anaphylaxis. Treating Kawasaki disease as resolved once the fever settles, without recognising IVIG's specific coronary-protective role.

## hold_these
Mild allergic reaction: oral antihistamine. Anaphylaxis (airway/breathing/circulatory compromise): IM adrenaline first.
Kawasaki disease diagnosis: fever over 5 days plus lymphadenopathy/conjunctivitis/peeling-type features.
Kawasaki disease treatment: IVIG, specifically to prevent coronary artery aneurysm.

## lose_the_mark
Choosing an antihistamine or IV steroid ahead of IM adrenaline for true anaphylaxis.
Treating a dramatic rash alone (without airway/breathing/circulatory compromise) as anaphylaxis.
Managing Kawasaki disease as self-limiting without IVIG.

## id
ART-INF-MUST-PED501-CHILDHOOD-INFECTIONS

## title
Meningococcal disease, CSF interpretation and childhood exanthem/toxin syndromes

## subject
inf

## topic
Infection and tropical medicine

## summary
A febrile child with a purpuric rash is meningococcal septicaemia until proven otherwise, its definitive investigation once antibiotics are started is PCR, and CSF findings distinguish bacterial from tuberculous meningitis; toxic shock syndrome and parvovirus B19 round out this article's set of infection-recognition patterns from the Lissauer emergencies/infection chapters.

## sections
### Definition
A febrile, unwell child with a purpuric, non-blanching rash of variable lesion size is meningococcal septicaemia until proven otherwise, whether or not meningism is present. Once antibiotics have already been given, meningococcal PCR (not blood culture, which loses sensitivity after antibiotic exposure) is the definitive-diagnosis investigation, and treatment is never delayed awaiting its result. Bacterial meningitis CSF shows a markedly raised, neutrophil-predominant white cell count with raised protein and a reduced CSF:blood glucose ratio; tuberculous meningitis is lymphocyte-predominant like viral meningitis but with markedly raised protein and very low glucose, over a subacute multi-week course.

### Mechanism
Toxic shock syndrome (usually Staphylococcus aureus, sometimes group A streptococcus) is a toxin-mediated syndrome triggered by a local infected lesion (e.g. an infected wound), producing fever, hypotension, a diffuse erythematous rash and mucositis — systemic toxin effects, not simple spreading bacteraemia, drive the picture. Parvovirus B19 causes erythema infectiosum ("slapped-cheek syndrome"), usually mild, but temporarily suppresses red cell production, which is dangerous in a child with a shortened red cell lifespan (spherocytosis, sickle cell disease) or an infected pregnant mother.

### Key determinants
Cell type (neutrophil vs lymphocyte) plus protein and glucose levels — not white cell count alone — separate bacterial, viral and TB meningitis on CSF. A rash's clinical context (well child vs septic-looking, isolated vs following an infected wound, facial "slapped cheek" vs diffuse erythema) is what distinguishes these infections from each other and from non-infectious mimics (HSP, ITP).

### Clinical significance
Recognising the purpuric-rash-plus-fever pattern before waiting for meningism, choosing PCR over culture once antibiotics are already on board, and correctly reading a CSF result's cell-type/protein/glucose triad are all decisions that change how urgently and with what a child is treated.

### Common misconceptions
Waiting for neck stiffness before suspecting meningococcal disease. Sending blood culture as "the" definitive test regardless of prior antibiotic exposure. Calling any lymphocyte-predominant CSF "viral" without checking protein and glucose for a TB pattern instead.

## hold_these
Purpuric non-blanching rash + fever = meningococcal septicaemia until proven otherwise.
Meningococcal PCR is the definitive test once antibiotics have already been given.
Bacterial meningitis CSF = neutrophils + raised protein + low CSF:blood glucose ratio; TB meningitis = lymphocytes + very high protein + very low glucose, subacute course.
Toxic shock syndrome = toxin-mediated, usually staphylococcal, triggered by a local infected lesion.
Parvovirus B19 = slapped-cheek rash; dangerous anaemia risk in spherocytosis/sickle cell/pregnancy.

## lose_the_mark
Requiring meningism before suspecting meningococcal septicaemia.
Sending blood culture instead of PCR as the definitive test after antibiotics have been given.
Calling a lymphocyte-predominant CSF "viral" without checking protein/glucose for TB.

ART-GIT-MUST-PED501-ABDOMINAL-EMERGENCIES

## title
Paediatric abdominal emergencies — intussusception and gastroenteritis dehydration

## subject
gi

## topic
Gastrointestinal system

## summary
Two abdominal presentations tested from the same "recognise severity, then act" lens: intussusception (episodic crying, leg-drawing, progressing to shock) as a surgical emergency, and gastroenteritis dehydration without shock, which is managed orally rather than intravenously.

## sections
### Definition
Intussusception classically presents in infancy with episodic inconsolable crying and drawing the legs up to the abdomen, progressing to lethargy and shock if untreated; it is managed with an ABC approach, senior and radiology involvement, and often confirmed on ultrasound. Gastroenteritis with clinical dehydration (dry mucous membranes, sunken eyes, reduced skin turgor) but no shock (normal capillary refill, warm extremities) is managed with oral rehydration solution first, under a period of observation, with intravenous fluids avoided where possible.

### Mechanism
Intussusception is bowel telescoping into an adjacent segment, causing intermittent obstruction and ischaemic pain; the differential (strangulated hernia, malrotation/volvulus, Meckel diverticulum) is distinguished on exam findings and vomiting character rather than the pain pattern alone. Dehydration without shock reflects fluid loss the child's circulation is still compensating for; shock indicates decompensation and needs an IV bolus instead of oral rehydration.

### Key determinants
For intussusception, the combination of episodic crying-with-leg-drawing plus progression to shock (not bile-stained vomiting, which points to malrotation, and not a palpable hernia, which should be visible) is the pattern to recognise. For gastroenteritis, capillary refill time and extremity warmth are what separate "dehydrated, manage orally" from "shocked, needs IV bolus" — not degree of diarrhoea alone.

### Clinical significance
Both concepts test the same underlying skill: matching management intensity (oral vs IV, observation vs urgent surgical referral) to an accurately assessed severity, rather than defaulting to the more aggressive option for every unwell child.

### Common misconceptions
Expecting bile-stained vomiting as the intussusception clue, when it is more typical of malrotation/volvulus. Starting IV fluids as the automatic first step for any dehydrated child with gastroenteritis, rather than reserving it for shock or failed oral/NG rehydration.

## hold_these
Intussusception: episodic crying + leg-drawing + progressive shock in an infant.
Gastroenteritis with dehydration but no shock: oral rehydration solution first, observed.

## lose_the_mark
Waiting for bile-stained vomiting before considering intussusception.
Starting IV fluids by default for dehydration without checking for shock first.

## id
ART-RES-MUST-PED501-RESPIRATORY-CARE

## title
Paediatric respiratory infection — recognition, triggers and management thresholds

## subject
resp

## topic
Respiratory system

## summary
Rhinovirus dominates both URTI and asthma-trigger epidemiology; hypoxia in a talking child needs immediate high-flow oxygen; and pneumonia's management (oral vs IV antibiotics) and URTI's (antipyretic only, no antibiotics) both hinge on a severity threshold rather than the diagnosis alone.

## sections
### Definition
Rhinovirus is the most common cause of both paediatric URTI and asthma exacerbations (over 80% of the latter). A talking child (patent airway) with a low oxygen saturation needs high-flow oxygen promptly, which also reduces anxiety and work of breathing. An uncomplicated viral URTI is managed with antipyretic/analgesic medication for comfort, not antibiotics, and fever itself is only treated when the child is distressed by it.

### Mechanism
Pneumonia management follows a severity threshold: mild disease (no oxygen requirement, no effusion signs, only mild distress) is treated with oral antibiotics at home with safety-netting advice; disease complicated by effusion, marked distress or borderline saturation needs intravenous antibiotics initially, stepping down to oral once afebrile for 48 hours.

### Key determinants
Oxygen requirement, degree of distress, and presence of an effusion are what separate the oral-antibiotic and IV-antibiotic pneumonia pathways — not the diagnosis of "pneumonia" alone. For hypoxia, the fact a child can still talk does not lower the priority of correcting oxygenation.

### Clinical significance
These thresholds prevent both under-treatment (sending a child with effusion and borderline saturation home on oral antibiotics) and over-treatment (admitting and IV-treating every mild pneumonia or prescribing antibiotics for a viral URTI).

### Common misconceptions
Assuming a "sicker-sounding" virus like RSV or influenza is the leading URTI/asthma-trigger cause rather than rhinovirus. Deferring oxygen correction because a hypoxic child can still talk. Treating every paediatric pneumonia the same way regardless of severity markers.

## hold_these
Rhinovirus is the commonest cause of both URTI and asthma exacerbations in children.
A hypoxic, talking child needs high-flow oxygen immediately, not deferred assessment.
Mild pneumonia (no O2 need, no effusion): oral antibiotics at home. Complicated pneumonia (effusion, marked distress, borderline SpO2): IV antibiotics, step down at 48h afebrile.
Viral URTI: antipyretic/analgesia only, not antibiotics; fever treated only if the child is distressed by it.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming RSV or influenza, not rhinovirus, is the leading URTI/asthma-trigger cause.
Delaying oxygen correction in a hypoxic child because they can still talk.
Prescribing antibiotics for an uncomplicated viral URTI.

ART-POP-MUST-PED501-IMMUNISATION

## title
Immunisation counselling — the individual-plus-community rationale

## subject
pop

## topic
Population health

## summary
The most complete counselling answer to vaccine hesitancy names both the direct benefit to the individual child and the community-level (herd immunity) benefit — not one alone, and not a deferral of the decision without explanation.

## sections
### Definition
Immunisation protects the individual child from serious infection and, at sufficient population uptake, interrupts community transmission (herd immunity), protecting those who cannot themselves be vaccinated. Herd immunity is not guaranteed and can fail when uptake drops, as shown historically by measles outbreaks following adverse (and since-discredited) publicity about vaccine safety.

### Mechanism
Community-level protection depends on a high enough proportion of the population being immune to interrupt onward transmission; below that threshold, susceptible individuals (including the unimmunized) remain at risk even if most others are vaccinated.

### Key determinants
The strongest counselling response names the individual benefit and the community benefit together, rather than leaning on only one, and rather than treating the decision as purely personal without offering the actual evidence.

### Clinical significance
A parent's decision not to vaccinate does not only affect their own child — it affects the achievable herd-immunity threshold for the whole community, which is why balanced, complete counselling matters.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming an unimmunized child is protected simply because most other children are immunized. Giving a community-only answer that omits the child's own direct benefit, or deferring the conversation without providing the evidence a parent needs to decide.

## hold_these
Immunisation protects the individual child directly and the community via herd immunity — the complete counselling answer names both.
Herd immunity is not guaranteed; it fails below a sufficient uptake threshold.

## lose_the_mark
Answering with only the community-benefit framing, omitting the individual child's direct benefit.
Assuming an unimmunized child is automatically protected because most peers are vaccinated.
