## summary
Arterial blood pressure is not a single fixed number across the population; it shifts with several normal, non-pathological factors.

## sections
### Definition
Arterial blood pressure is not a single fixed number across the population; it shifts with several normal, non-pathological factors.

### Mechanism
In young children, resting blood pressure runs well below the adult range — a typical value for a 4-year-old is about 100/65 mmHg, against an adult norm nearer 120/80 mmHg — because vessel calibre, cardiac output and sympathetic tone are all still maturing. After about age 45, blood pressure in women tends to rise more than it does in men of the same age, attributed to the hormonal changes of the perimenopausal and postmenopausal transition rather than to any single disease process. Blood pressure, especially the systolic component, also rises acutely and reversibly during strong emotional states (fear, anger, excitement), driven by a burst of sympathetic outflow that raises heart rate, cardiac output and vasomotor tone together.

### Key determinants
Treating every one of these physiological shifts as pathological hypertension. The source's own framing groups age, sex/hormonal status and emotional state under "physiological variations", distinct from the disease processes (renal, endocrine, essential) that raise blood pressure abnormally.

## summary
Dynamic muscular exercise raises cardiac output sharply, through both a rise in heart rate and a rise in stroke volume, which drives systolic arterial pressure up.

## sections
### Definition
Dynamic muscular exercise raises cardiac output sharply, through both a rise in heart rate and a rise in stroke volume, which drives systolic arterial pressure up.

### Mechanism
At the same time, exercising skeletal muscle vasodilates markedly (local metabolite accumulation and sympathetic cholinergic vasodilator activity), which lowers total peripheral resistance enough that diastolic pressure falls or stays roughly level even as systolic pressure climbs. The net haemodynamic picture during dynamic exercise is therefore a widened pulse pressure — systolic up, diastolic down or unchanged — distinguishing it from the pattern seen with, for example, sustained isometric exercise or emotional pressor responses, where both systolic and diastolic pressure tend to rise together.

### Key determinants
Assuming exercise raises both systolic and diastolic pressure together, by analogy with the emotional pressor response. Dynamic exercise's skeletal-muscle vasodilation is what makes its diastolic response different from a simple global sympathetic surge.

### Clinical significance

## summary
Arterial blood pressure, as measured by a manometer or intra-arterial line, is not uniform along the vertical length of the body; it is modified by the hydrostatic pressure of the column of blood between the point of measurement and the heart.

## sections
### Definition
Arterial blood pressure, as measured by a manometer or intra-arterial line, is not uniform along the vertical length of the body; it is modified by the hydrostatic pressure of the column of blood between the point of measurement and the heart.

### Mechanism
For each centimetre a measurement point sits below the level of the heart, the local arterial pressure is higher than the pressure at heart level by about 0.77 mmHg, purely from the added weight of that extra column of blood, and the converse subtraction applies above heart level. This hydrostatic effect is why blood pressure is conventionally measured with the cuff and the arm at heart level, and why pressures recorded in a dependent limb (for example, the leg while standing) read higher than a simultaneous reading at the arm.

### Key determinants
Confusing this static, gravity-driven hydrostatic correction with the dynamic fall in pressure along the vascular tree from resistance (as in the Starling-forces or Poiseuille's-law facts); the hydrostatic column effect is a purely physical, position-dependent addition or subtraction, not a consequence of flow or resistance.

### Clinical significance

## summary

## sections
### Definition
Painful stimulation of the skin activates a reflex arc that raises, rather than lowers, systemic arterial pressure and heart rate — a cutaneous pain pressor reflex.

### Mechanism
Afferent nociceptive input from the skin reaches the brainstem and reflexly increases sympathetic outflow to the heart and vasculature, producing both hypertension and tachycardia, the opposite of the hypotension and bradycardia that would follow a purely vagal or depressor reflex. This pressor pattern places cutaneous pain alongside other excitatory afferent inputs to the vasomotor centre (peripheral chemoreceptor discharge, the Cushing reflex, the Alam-Smirk reflex) rather than with the inhibitory, pressure-lowering baroreceptor reflexes.

### Key determinants
Assuming any strong somatic stimulus produces a vagal, blood-pressure-lowering response (as in vasovagal syncope from certain visceral or emotional triggers). Cutaneous pain specifically drives a sympathetic pressor reflex, hypertension with tachycardia, in this source's own teaching.

### Clinical significance

## summary
Among the mechanisms that help restore arterial blood pressure after it falls (for example, following haemorrhage) is a shift of fluid across the capillary wall itself.

## sections
### Definition
Among the mechanisms that help restore arterial blood pressure after it falls (for example, following haemorrhage) is a shift of fluid across the capillary wall itself.

### Mechanism
A drop in arterial pressure lowers capillary hydrostatic pressure, which tips the Starling balance in favour of absorption over filtration, so fluid moves from the interstitial (tissue) space back into the capillaries, expanding plasma volume and partially restoring the fall in arterial pressure. This capillary fluid shift acts over minutes, more slowly than the near-instantaneous baroreceptor reflex but faster than the renal fluid-retention mechanisms that dominate longer-term restoration of blood volume.

### Key determinants
Reversing the direction of fluid movement — the shift runs from tissue into capillary specifically when arterial (and so capillary) pressure has fallen; the opposite direction (capillary into tissue) is what happens when capillary hydrostatic pressure rises, as in the oedema-producing mechanisms.

### Clinical significance

## summary

## sections
### Definition
The afferent inputs that regulate the medullary vasomotor centre can be classified by whether raising their discharge rate excites or inhibits vasomotor (sympathetic) outflow.

### Mechanism
Peripheral chemoreceptors are excitatory: a rise in their discharge (from hypoxia, hypercapnia or acidosis) increases sympathetic outflow and raises arterial pressure. Arterial baroreceptors, atrial (volume) receptors and left ventricular receptors are, by contrast, inhibitory: a rise in their discharge (from raised pressure, volume or ventricular filling) reduces sympathetic outflow and lowers arterial pressure, which is why raised baroreceptor discharge is a depressor signal even though raised chemoreceptor discharge is a pressor one.

### Key determinants
Assuming all cardiovascular sensory receptors act in the same direction on the vasomotor centre. This source's bank draws the excitatory/inhibitory line specifically between the chemoreceptors (excitatory) and the mechanoreceptor-based reflexes — baroreceptor, atrial and left-ventricular (inhibitory).

## summary

## sections
### Definition
The capillary fragility test (the tourniquet or Rumpel-Leede test) checks whether small skin capillaries rupture under a standardised increase in venous pressure, producing petechiae distal to an inflated cuff.

### Mechanism
A positive test reflects a structurally or functionally fragile capillary wall rather than a platelet-number defect, and classically follows from non-thrombocytopenic purpura (normal platelet count, defective vessel wall), vitamin C deficiency (scurvy, where inadequate collagen synthesis weakens the capillary basement membrane and perivascular connective tissue), or an allergic/hypersensitivity vasculitis. In a patient with liver cirrhosis and a positive fragility test, the most likely explanation taught by this source is vitamin C deficiency, reflecting the malnutrition and impaired vitamin storage/absorption that often accompanies chronic liver disease.

### Key determinants
Assuming a positive capillary fragility test always implies a platelet disorder. The test specifically isolates vessel-wall fragility from platelet-number causes of bleeding, which is why "non-thrombocytopenic" purpura, not thrombocytopenic purpura, is the named cause here.

## sections
### Definition
The law of Laplace relates the wall tension of a cylindrical vessel to its internal pressure and its radius (tension is proportional to pressure multiplied by radius).

### Mechanism
Although a capillary wall is extremely thin — a single layer of endothelium with little supporting connective tissue — it can withstand the pressures generated within it because its radius is so small: for a given internal pressure, a smaller radius produces far less wall tension than a larger vessel would experience at the same pressure. This is why capillaries, not large thin-walled veins, are able to tolerate their intraluminal pressure without a thick muscular or elastic wall, while a vessel with a much larger radius would need a correspondingly thicker or stronger wall to withstand the same pressure without rupturing.

### Key determinants
Attributing the capillary wall's pressure tolerance to a thick basement membrane or to fenestrations, rather than to its small radius. This source's bank isolates the radius term of Laplace's law as the specific correct explanation.

## summary

## sections
### Definition
Firmly stroking the skin produces the "white-line reaction": a pale line along the stroked path, appearing within seconds.

### Mechanism
This pallor is caused by local constriction of the precapillary sphincters supplying that strip of skin, mechanically triggered by the stroke itself, which transiently shuts off capillary inflow and empties the superficial capillary bed of blood. It is the mirror-image component of the skin's local vascular responses to mechanical/injurious stimulation: where the white-line reaction is constriction producing pallor, the axon-reflex flare that can follow a firmer or more sustained stimulus is instead vasodilation producing redness, and the two should not be conflated as the same mechanism acting in the same direction.

### Key determinants
Confusing the white-line reaction with the flare or the wheal, the other two components of the skin's local vascular responses to stimulation. The white-line reaction is constriction (pallor); the flare is vasodilation via the axon reflex (redness); the wheal is histamine-driven oedema (swelling).

## summary

## sections
### Definition
Along a typical capillary, slightly more fluid is filtered out at the arterial end than is reabsorbed back in at the venous end, because the Starling forces do not balance to exactly zero net movement.

### Mechanism
In a healthy individual this small net excess of filtered fluid does not accumulate as oedema; instead it is picked up by the lymphatic capillaries in the interstitium and returned to the venous circulation via the lymphatic vessels and thoracic duct, keeping interstitial fluid volume constant. This lymphatic return is the physiological safety valve that makes the small, everyday imbalance between capillary filtration and reabsorption compatible with a stable interstitial fluid volume, and it is only when lymph flow itself is inadequate, or filtration rises well beyond this small excess, that fluid accumulates as clinically evident oedema.

### Key determinants
Assuming any un-reabsorbed filtrate simply accumulates as tissue fluid indefinitely. In health this small excess does not accumulate at all — it is continuously drained by the lymphatics; oedema only appears once filtration outpaces lymphatic drainage capacity, not from the normal small imbalance itself.
