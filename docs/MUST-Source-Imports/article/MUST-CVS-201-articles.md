## summary
Amiodarone is a broad-spectrum class III anti-arrhythmic that blocks potassium, calcium, alpha- and beta-receptors, at the cost of toxicity across the lung, liver, thyroid, cornea and skin.

## sections
### Definition
Amiodarone is a broad-spectrum class III anti-arrhythmic drug, used for both atrial and ventricular arrhythmias, named for its dominant potassium-channel-blocking action but pharmacologically wider than that one class.

### Mechanism
Amiodarone prolongs the effective refractory period (ERP) and action potential duration (APD), mainly by blocking potassium (K+) channels. It also blocks calcium (Ca2+) channels and alpha- and beta-adrenergic receptors, so it is described as a broad-spectrum anti-arrhythmic rather than a pure class III agent.

### Key determinants
Its adverse-effect profile follows directly from that multi-channel, multi-receptor action and from the drug's high lipid solubility and long tissue half-life, which is why toxicity accumulates with cumulative dose rather than daily dose alone.

### Clinical significance
Amiodarone's adverse effects span multiple organs: pulmonary toxicity, hepatic cirrhosis, corneal micro-deposits, thyroid dysfunction (an enlarged thyroid with hypo- or hyperthyroidism), photosensitivity, GIT upset and neurological side effects — each is separately examinable and separately monitored.

## published_summary


## published_sections


## hold_these
Amiodarone blocks K+ channels (class III action) plus Ca2+ channels and alpha/beta receptors — broad-spectrum, not pure class III.
Its adverse effects reach the lung, liver, thyroid, cornea and skin, not only the heart.

## lose_the_mark
Naming only the potassium-channel action and missing the calcium-channel and adrenergic-receptor blockade that makes it broad-spectrum.
Forgetting any one organ system in the adverse-effect list — each is separately examinable.

## sections

Atherosclerosis is a disease of large and medium arteries, characterised by atheromatous plaques in the intima.

## summary
Malignant hypertension is severe, progressive hypertension with acute target-organ damage — retinal, renal and arteriolar — whose leading cause of death is renal failure.

## sections
### Definition
Malignant hypertension is severe, progressive hypertension, classically above 280/180 mmHg, with retinal haemorrhage, papilloedema and renal impairment.

### Mechanism
The characteristic renal arteriolar changes are fibrinoid necrosis and onion-skin (concentric laminar) thickening of the arteriolar wall, driven by the very high pressure itself rather than by a separate disease process.

### Key determinants
The diagnosis rests on acute target-organ damage — retinal, renal and arteriolar — not on the blood pressure figure alone.

### Clinical significance
In order of frequency, death occurs from renal failure, heart failure, coronary heart disease, and cerebral haemorrhage.

## summary
Smooth muscle fibres are spindle-shaped, non-striated, non-branched cells with one central nucleus and no T-tubule system — the set of features that separates them from skeletal and cardiac muscle on a slide.

## sections
### Definition
Smooth muscle is the involuntary, non-striated muscle type lining hollow organs and vessel walls, distinguished from skeletal and cardiac muscle by a fixed set of histological features.

### Mechanism
Smooth muscle contracts by actin-myosin sliding as in other muscle types, but the filaments are not arranged into the regular sarcomeric lattice that produces visible cross-striations — hence 'non-striated'.

### Key determinants
Smooth muscle fibres are spindle-shaped; non-striated; non-branched; each fibre has a single, centrally placed nucleus; and they have no T-tubule system.

### Clinical significance
These five features together are what a student uses to identify smooth muscle on a histology slide and to distinguish it from skeletal muscle (striated, multinucleated, peripheral nuclei) and cardiac muscle (striated, branched, intercalated discs).

## published_sections

## lose_the_mark

## summary
Fibrinous pericarditis is the commonest type of pericarditis, giving a rough 'bread and butter' gross appearance, and follows both infective and sterile insults to the pericardium.

## sections
### Definition
Fibrinous pericarditis is the commonest type of pericarditis. Fibrin deposition over the visceral and parietal pericardium produces a rough, shaggy 'bread and butter' gross appearance.

### Mechanism
An inflammatory or irritant insult to the pericardium increases vascular permeability, allowing fibrinogen to leak out and polymerise into fibrin over the serosal surface, roughening the normally smooth, glistening pericardial lining.

### Key determinants
The causes are a mix of infective and sterile insults: rheumatic fever, lobar pneumonia, tuberculosis, uraemia, and as a complication over a myocardial infarction.

### Clinical significance
Because both infective and sterile causes give the same bread-and-butter appearance, the gross finding alone does not tell the underlying cause — that requires the clinical context.

## sections
### Definition
The fatty streak is the first grossly visible lesion in developing atherosclerosis, seen as flat yellow streaks in the arterial intima.

### Mechanism
Lipoproteins that enter the injured intima are taken up by macrophages and smooth muscle cells, which become lipid-laden foam cells — the microscopic substance of the fatty streak.

### Key determinants
Microscopically it is composed of foam cells (lipid-laden macrophages and smooth muscle cells); it occurs characteristically at the aortic valve and in the thoracic aorta.

### Clinical significance
The fatty streak is reversible and can occur even in childhood; it is taught as the earliest stage of atherosclerosis rather than as disease itself, which is why the risk-factor exposures that follow it matter for whether it progresses to a fibrous plaque.

## summary
Long-term arterial blood pressure is set by renal and endocrine control of ECF volume, through three levers on the same variable: the renin-angiotensin system, atrial natriuretic peptide, and vasopressin.

## sections
### Definition
Long-term arterial blood pressure (ABP) is set mainly by renal and endocrine mechanisms that control extracellular fluid (ECF, mainly plasma) volume, distinct from the fast neural reflexes that buffer beat-to-beat pressure.

### Mechanism
A fall in ABP causes renal ischaemia, which triggers renin secretion and angiotensin II formation. Angiotensin II raises ABP by systemic vasoconstriction, catecholamine secretion, and ADH and aldosterone secretion.

### Key determinants
An increased ECF volume stretches atrial muscle fibres, triggering ANP secretion, which increases renal Na+ excretion and enhances pressure natriuresis, returning ECF volume and blood pressure to normal. Conversely, a decreased ECF volume reduces atrial low-pressure receptor discharge, increasing vasopressin secretion, which decreases renal water excretion to limit any further fall.

### Clinical significance
RAS, ANP and vasopressin are three levers on the same variable — ECF volume — rather than three unrelated systems; recognising which lever a drug or disease pulls (e.g. an ACE inhibitor blocking RAS) depends on holding this shared framework.

## published_summary


## published_sections


## hold_these
Long-term ABP regulation works through renal and endocrine control of ECF volume, not through the fast neural reflexes that buffer beat-to-beat pressure.
RAS and vasopressin both respond to a fall in ECF volume by retaining fluid/vasoconstricting; ANP is the opposing, natriuretic response to a rise in ECF volume.

## lose_the_mark
Treating RAS, ANP and vasopressin as three unrelated systems rather than three levers on one variable, ECF volume.
Confusing this long-term, systemic, hormonal control with short-term local autoregulation of blood flow — they answer different questions.
