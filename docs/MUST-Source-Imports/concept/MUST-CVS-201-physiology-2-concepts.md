# Item

## label
Arterial blood pressure varies physiologically with age, sex/hormonal status and emotional state

## id
CON-CVS-04186B68A522B8

## canonical_key
abp.physiological-variations-age-sex-emotion

## definition
Arterial blood pressure is not a single fixed number across the population; it shifts with several normal, non-pathological factors. In young children, resting blood pressure runs well below the adult range — a typical value for a 4-year-old is about 100/65 mmHg, against an adult norm nearer 120/80 mmHg — because vessel calibre, cardiac output and sympathetic tone are all still maturing. After about age 45, blood pressure in women tends to rise more than it does in men of the same age, attributed to the hormonal changes of the perimenopausal and postmenopausal transition rather than to any single disease process. Blood pressure, especially the systolic component, also rises acutely and reversibly during strong emotional states (fear, anger, excitement), driven by a burst of sympathetic outflow that raises heart rate, cardiac output and vasomotor tone together.

## explicit_objective
State three physiological (non-pathological) sources of arterial blood pressure variation — age, the post-45 rise in women linked to hormonal change, and acute elevation with strong emotion — and give the typical BP figure for each where the source specifies one.

## pitfalls
Treating every one of these physiological shifts as pathological hypertension. The source's own framing groups age, sex/hormonal status and emotional state under "physiological variations", distinct from the disease processes (renal, endocrine, essential) that raise blood pressure abnormally.

## label
During muscular exercise, systolic pressure rises while diastolic pressure falls

## id
CON-CVS-FF330EB96D20EC

## canonical_key
abp.exercise-effect-systolic-diastolic

## definition
Dynamic muscular exercise raises cardiac output sharply, through both a rise in heart rate and a rise in stroke volume, which drives systolic arterial pressure up. At the same time, exercising skeletal muscle vasodilates markedly (local metabolite accumulation and sympathetic cholinergic vasodilator activity), which lowers total peripheral resistance enough that diastolic pressure falls or stays roughly level even as systolic pressure climbs. The net haemodynamic picture during dynamic exercise is therefore a widened pulse pressure — systolic up, diastolic down or unchanged — distinguishing it from the pattern seen with, for example, sustained isometric exercise or emotional pressor responses, where both systolic and diastolic pressure tend to rise together.

## explicit_objective
State that dynamic muscular exercise raises systolic pressure while lowering diastolic pressure, and attribute this to the combination of increased cardiac output and skeletal-muscle vasodilation.

## pitfalls
Assuming exercise raises both systolic and diastolic pressure together, by analogy with the emotional pressor response. Dynamic exercise's skeletal-muscle vasodilation is what makes its diastolic response different from a simple global sympathetic surge.

## label
Arterial pressure rises by about 0.77 mmHg for each centimetre below heart level, from the added hydrostatic column of blood

## id
CON-CVS-3E457083BE5148

## canonical_key
abp.hydrostatic-column-effect-on-arterial-pressure

## definition
Arterial blood pressure, as measured by a manometer or intra-arterial line, is not uniform along the vertical length of the body; it is modified by the hydrostatic pressure of the column of blood between the point of measurement and the heart. For each centimetre a measurement point sits below the level of the heart, the local arterial pressure is higher than the pressure at heart level by about 0.77 mmHg, purely from the added weight of that extra column of blood, and the converse subtraction applies above heart level. This hydrostatic effect is why blood pressure is conventionally measured with the cuff and the arm at heart level, and why pressures recorded in a dependent limb (for example, the leg while standing) read higher than a simultaneous reading at the arm.

## explicit_objective
State the approximate hydrostatic correction (about 0.77 mmHg per centimetre below heart level) and explain why blood pressure measurement is standardised to heart level.

## pitfalls
Confusing this static, gravity-driven hydrostatic correction with the dynamic fall in pressure along the vascular tree from resistance (as in the Starling-forces or Poiseuille's-law facts); the hydrostatic column effect is a purely physical, position-dependent addition or subtraction, not a consequence of flow or resistance.

## label
Stimulation of cutaneous pain receptors reflexly raises blood pressure and heart rate (a pressor reflex)

## id
CON-CVS-FD51B226A4986D

## canonical_key
abp.cutaneous-pain-receptor-pressor-reflex

## definition
Painful stimulation of the skin activates a reflex arc that raises, rather than lowers, systemic arterial pressure and heart rate — a cutaneous pain pressor reflex. Afferent nociceptive input from the skin reaches the brainstem and reflexly increases sympathetic outflow to the heart and vasculature, producing both hypertension and tachycardia, the opposite of the hypotension and bradycardia that would follow a purely vagal or depressor reflex. This pressor pattern places cutaneous pain alongside other excitatory afferent inputs to the vasomotor centre (peripheral chemoreceptor discharge, the Cushing reflex, the Alam-Smirk reflex) rather than with the inhibitory, pressure-lowering baroreceptor reflexes.

## explicit_objective
State that stimulation of cutaneous pain receptors produces a pressor response — hypertension and tachycardia — not a depressor one.

## pitfalls
Assuming any strong somatic stimulus produces a vagal, blood-pressure-lowering response (as in vasovagal syncope from certain visceral or emotional triggers). Cutaneous pain specifically drives a sympathetic pressor reflex, hypertension with tachycardia, in this source's own teaching.

## label
The Alam-Smirk reflex, a pressor reflex triggered by exercising-muscle proprioceptors, raises arterial blood pressure

## id
CON-CVS-70A247C343C46D

## canonical_key
abp.alam-smirk-exercise-pressor-reflex

## definition
The Alam-Smirk reflex is a rise in arterial blood pressure triggered by stimulation of proprioceptors in actively contracting skeletal muscle during exercise. Afferent signals from these muscle proprioceptors are relayed centrally and reflexly raise sympathetic outflow, contributing a rapid, exercise-linked pressor drive that is separate from, and additive to, the central command and metabolite-driven components of the overall exercise pressor response. It is one of several named excitatory reflexes (alongside the cutaneous pain pressor reflex and the Cushing reflex) that raise, rather than lower, systemic arterial pressure.

## explicit_objective
Name proprioceptors in exercising muscle as the trigger for the Alam-Smirk reflex, and place it among the excitatory (pressor) reflexes controlling arterial blood pressure.

## pitfalls
Confusing the Alam-Smirk reflex's proprioceptor trigger with the chemoreceptor- or baroreceptor-mediated reflexes; this source's bank distinguishes it explicitly as proprioceptor-driven.

# Item

## label
In health, the small excess of fluid filtered at the capillary's arterial end but not reabsorbed at its venous end is returned to the circulation by the lymphatics

## id
CON-CVS-0FA0A2CA166B19

## canonical_key
capillary.lymphatic-return-of-unabsorbed-filtrate

## definition
Along a typical capillary, slightly more fluid is filtered out at the arterial end than is reabsorbed back in at the venous end, because the Starling forces do not balance to exactly zero net movement. In a healthy individual this small net excess of filtered fluid does not accumulate as oedema; instead it is picked up by the lymphatic capillaries in the interstitium and returned to the venous circulation via the lymphatic vessels and thoracic duct, keeping interstitial fluid volume constant. This lymphatic return is the physiological safety valve that makes the small, everyday imbalance between capillary filtration and reabsorption compatible with a stable interstitial fluid volume, and it is only when lymph flow itself is inadequate, or filtration rises well beyond this small excess, that fluid accumulates as clinically evident oedema.

## explicit_objective
State that lymphatic vessels normally return the small excess of capillary-filtered fluid that is not reabsorbed at the venous end, keeping interstitial fluid volume stable.

## pitfalls
Assuming any un-reabsorbed filtrate simply accumulates as tissue fluid indefinitely. In health this small excess does not accumulate at all — it is continuously drained by the lymphatics; oedema only appears once filtration outpaces lymphatic drainage capacity, not from the normal small imbalance itself.
