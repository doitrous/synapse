QST-MUSTPED501-LISSAUER-CH28-CH28-01-OSTEOMYELITIS

## title
Fever and arm pain after a finger abscess

## question
Amir is a 5-year-old boy who presents with a high temperature and pain in his right upper arm for 2 days, worse when he moves the arm. He had an abscess on his right finger 2 weeks ago, treated with oral antibiotics. On examination he has a temperature of 39°C, with redness and swelling over the right upper arm, painful to touch or movement. White cell count and C-reactive protein are raised. What is the most likely causative organism?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Haemophilus influenzae

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Haemophilus influenzae may cause osteomyelitis, particularly in unimmunised children, but it is an uncommon causative organism compared with Staphylococcus aureus.

## answer_b
Mycobacterium tuberculosis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Mycobacterium tuberculosis may cause osteomyelitis but is an uncommon cause in a setting like this, and presents more insidiously than this acute 2-day history.

## answer_c
Salmonella typhi

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Salmonella osteomyelitis has an increased risk specifically in sickle cell anaemia, which is not mentioned here; it is not the typical organism in an otherwise well child.

## answer_d
Staphylococcus aureus

## explanation_d
Correct. Amir most likely has osteomyelitis following haematogenous spread from his recent finger abscess, and the most common causative organism is Staphylococcus aureus. Other pathogens include Streptococcus species, and in sickle cell anaemia there is an increased risk of Staphylococcus and Salmonella osteomyelitis specifically.

## answer_e
Streptococcus viridans

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Most infections are caused by Staphylococcus aureus, with group A Streptococcus as another recognised pathogen; Streptococcus viridans is not the typical organism in paediatric osteomyelitis.

## title
Footballer with knee pain and tibial swelling

## question
Zain is a 14-year-old boy, a good footballer, who complains of pain in his left knee for a while, worse when he exercises, after training today. On examination there is swelling over the left tibial tuberosity. He is afebrile, with no night pain or pain on waking. What is the most likely diagnosis?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Osteomyelitis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Osteomyelitis would be a very unusual cause here, particularly given the absence of fever, which would be expected in a bone infection.

## answer_b
Osgood-Schlatter disease

## explanation_b
Correct. This is osteochondritis of the patellar tendon insertion at the knee, often affecting physically active adolescent males (particularly footballers or basketball players). It usually presents with knee pain after exercise, localised tenderness and swelling over the tibial tuberosity, often with hamstring tightness, and is bilateral in 25-50% of cases.

## answer_c
Perthes disease

## explanation_c
Incorrect. While pain might be referred to the knees from the hips in Perthes disease, this is unlikely to explain localised tibial-tuberosity swelling in this boy.

## answer_d
Septic arthritis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Fever is absent, and he can still weight bear and exercise, both of which argue strongly against septic arthritis.

## answer_e
Slipped capital femoral epiphysis

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Slipped capital femoral epiphysis affects the hip, and would not present with swelling over the tibial tuberosity.

## title
Toddler with a red swollen knee and fever

## question
Edward, aged 3 years, woke up complaining of pain in his right leg. He has become more unwell and lethargic, with a temperature of 39°C, heart rate 180/min, respiratory rate 25/min. His right knee is red and swollen, and he cries if it is extended or flexed. There is a small healing insect bite on his right ankle. He has no other medical history. What is the most likely diagnosis?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Henoch-Schönlein purpura arthritis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The arthritis of Henoch-Schönlein purpura would usually be accompanied by its characteristic purpuric rash, and a high fever like this would not be typical.

## answer_b
Juvenile idiopathic arthritis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Juvenile idiopathic arthritis does not usually present so acutely, with a single red, swollen joint appearing over hours; it is a more chronic, often multi-joint process.

## answer_c
Osteomyelitis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Osteomyelitis is the main differential diagnosis to distinguish from septic arthritis, but the joint itself (rather than the bone) is what is red and swollen here, with pain on movement of the joint pointing to the joint as the primary site.

## answer_d
Septic arthritis

## explanation_d
Correct. Edward has septic arthritis, which needs to be differentiated from osteomyelitis. His knee joint itself is red and swollen with pain on movement, making septic arthritis more likely; an ultrasound and aspiration of the joint should be performed urgently to confirm this and guide treatment.

## answer_e
Trauma

## explanation_e
Incorrect. There is no history of trauma given in this vignette, making a traumatic cause unlikely as the explanation for this acute, febrile, red swollen joint.

## title
Polyarthritis with a salmon-pink rash

## question
Karen, aged 5 years, presents with 2 weeks of lethargy, fever and painful wrists and knees. She has a temperature of 38.5°C and a subtle erythematous rash on her trunk. Several joints are swollen, warm and painful, including wrists, right elbow, knees, left ankle and left hip, with cervical lymphadenopathy and a palpable spleen. Blood tests show: Hb 85 g/L, WBC 17×10⁹/L, neutrophils 10.4×10⁹/L, platelets 366×10⁹/L, normal blood film, ESR 70 mm/hour, ANA negative, double-stranded DNA negative, antistreptolysin O titre normal. What is the most likely diagnosis?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia needs to be considered given the anaemia, but is less likely as Karen has numerous joints affected in an inflammatory pattern and her blood film is normal, without blast cells.

## answer_b
Epstein-Barr virus infection

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Epstein-Barr virus infection is unlikely as it rarely causes widespread polyarthritis of this pattern.

## answer_c
Post-streptococcal arthritis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Post-streptococcal arthritis is unlikely as the antistreptolysin O titre is normal, although a repeat blood test would be needed to confirm the titre remains low over time.

## answer_d
Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)

## explanation_d
Incorrect. SLE may have similar clinical features but is unlikely as the ANA and double-stranded DNA are both negative; SLE is also very uncommon at this young age.

## answer_e
Systemic-onset juvenile idiopathic arthritis

## explanation_e
Correct. Systemic-onset juvenile idiopathic arthritis is the most likely diagnosis, given her systemic illness, polyarthritis (more than four joints) and salmon-pink coloured rash. The raised neutrophil count and markedly raised ESR are consistent with this diagnosis, and the negative ANA/dsDNA/normal ASOT help exclude SLE and post-streptococcal arthritis respectively.

## title
Acute limp after a coryzal illness

## question
Dominika is a 3-year-old girl who presents with an acute onset limp not present the previous day. She was unwell 2 weeks ago with a coryzal illness. The pain is in her right leg, present only on walking. Her temperature is 37°C, the hip and leg look normal, but passive movement of her right hip shows decreased external rotation. What is the most likely diagnosis?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Bone tumour

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A bone tumour is rare at this age and would usually present more insidiously, not with this acute-onset limp following a recent viral illness.

## answer_b
Perthes disease

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Perthes disease usually occurs in slightly older children (aged 5-10 years), rather than a 3-year-old with this acute post-viral presentation.

## answer_c
Reactive arthritis (transient synovitis)

## explanation_c
Correct. This is the most common cause of acute hip pain in children, occurring in those aged 2-12, often following or accompanying a viral infection. Presentation is with sudden onset pain in the hip or a limp; there is no pain at rest, but decreased range of movement, particularly external rotation, as seen here.

## answer_d
Septic arthritis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Septic arthritis can be difficult to differentiate from reactive arthritis, but is far less common, and children with septic arthritis are usually systemically unwell with fever, unlike Dominika's normal temperature.

## answer_e
Slipped capital femoral epiphysis

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Slipped capital femoral epiphysis is more common in overweight teenage girls, not a 3-year-old with a recent coryzal illness.

## title
Newborn with inward-turning feet

## question
Huw is a baby who is 24 hours old, born by normal vaginal delivery following a normal pregnancy. The midwife notes the baby's feet look abnormal — turned inwards but of normal size. On passive movement the foot can be manipulated so that it can be fully dorsiflexed to touch the front of the lower leg (the neutral position). What is the most likely diagnosis?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Positional talipes equinovarus

## explanation_a
Correct. This is a common problem caused by intrauterine compression. The foot is of normal size, the deformity is mild, and it can be corrected to the neutral position with passive manipulation, as described here — this passive correctability is the key distinguishing feature from fixed structural deformities.

## answer_b
Talipes calcaneovalgus

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Talipes calcaneovalgus is a fixed bony deformity, in which the foot is everted and dorsiflexed, not correctable to neutral in the way described here.

## answer_c
Talipes equinovarus

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Talipes equinovarus is a fixed bony deformity in which the foot is inverted and supinated; passive movement to a normal, neutral position is not possible, unlike this case.

## answer_d
Tarsal coalition

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Tarsal coalition is a diagnosis made in adolescent years, not in a newborn.

## answer_e
Vertical talus

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Vertical talus is seen in children with rocker-bottom feet, who often have other congenital abnormalities, not this passively correctable inward-turned foot.

## title
Recurrent fractures with blue sclera

## question
Harry is an 18-month-old boy brought to the emergency department because he will not weight bear and cries when his left leg is moved. He is afebrile with an otherwise normal examination, except his sclera has a slight blue tinge. An X-ray shows a fracture of his femur, with the bones looking osteoporotic; his mother cannot give a history of trauma. He broke his left arm 4 months ago, similarly unexplained. His older sister, present with them, has her arm in a splint having recently fractured it too. What is the most likely diagnosis for Harry?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Accidental injury (unwitnessed)

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Accidental injury is possible in principle, but does not explain the recurrent pattern (a previous unexplained arm fracture) or the blue sclera and similarly affected sibling.

## answer_b
Non-accidental injury

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Although non-accidental injury needs to be considered and addressed in parallel, the abnormality of the bones on X-ray (osteoporotic appearance) makes osteogenesis imperfecta more likely; child-protection concerns should be investigated alongside, not instead of, the medical work-up.

## answer_c
Osteogenesis imperfecta

## explanation_c
Correct. Harry is likely to have osteogenesis imperfecta (type 1). It presents with fractures in early childhood, with osteoporotic-looking bones on X-ray. Children often have blue sclera, though this can be difficult to identify with confidence. The condition is autosomal dominant, and it is likely his older sister Amy has it as well, given her similar unexplained fracture history.

## answer_d
Osteomyelitis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Osteomyelitis does present with a painful leg, but would usually be associated with fever and redness over the area, and the X-ray would usually be normal at presentation, unlike the osteoporotic bone appearance described here.

## answer_e
Osteopetrosis

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Osteopetrosis is also an autosomal dominant disease that can present in its milder form with fractures, but the bones look dense (not osteoporotic) on X-ray, which is the opposite of what is described here.

## title
Toddler with a swollen, held-flexed thigh and fever

## question
Emma, aged 3½ years, presents to the Emergency Department with a 24-hour history of a limp and pain in her left thigh, with no history of trauma. Her temperature is 38.9°C and her pulse is 150 beats/min. On examination the left thigh is swollen and held flexed and abducted, and she cries if it is moved. The upper leg is slightly red and warmer to touch than the right leg. Paracetamol has had no effect. What is the most likely diagnosis?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Cellulitis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Cellulitis would show more diffuse skin erythema without the specific hip-flexed-and-abducted positioning suggestive of joint irritation seen here.

## answer_b
Fractured femur

## explanation_b
Incorrect. There is no history of trauma, and a fractured femur would not typically present with fever and this positioning suggestive of joint pathology.

## answer_c
Osteomyelitis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Osteomyelitis is an important differential, but this presentation, with the joint held flexed and abducted (the position that minimises pressure within an inflamed hip joint) and severe pain on any movement, is more suggestive of an intra-articular process.

## answer_d
Perthes disease

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Perthes disease usually presents in slightly older children with a more insidious limp, not this acute, febrile, systemically unwell picture with a hot swollen thigh.

## answer_e
Septic arthritis

## explanation_e
Correct. It can be difficult to clinically differentiate between osteomyelitis and septic arthritis, but in this child, the very acute history and flexed-and-abducted position of the thigh at rest suggest septic arthritis. Ultrasound (and diagnostic aspiration), blood cultures and inflammatory markers should be performed and intravenous antibiotics started, without delaying treatment for urgent orthopaedic review.

## title
Flat feet in a walking toddler

## question
Rachel, a 2-year-old girl, is seen for a developmental check by the health visitor. She is walking on her own but has flat feet while walking. When asked to stand on tiptoes she cannot, but when the health visitor passively extends her big toe an arch is demonstrated. What is the most likely diagnosis?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Developmental dysplasia of the hip

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Developmental dysplasia of the hip presents with a limp or reduced hip abduction, not this bilateral flat-foot appearance with a demonstrable arch on toe extension.

## answer_b
Normal variant of childhood

## explanation_b
Correct. This is a normal variant of childhood. Toddlers learning to walk usually have flat feet due to flatness of the medial longitudinal arch and the presence of a fat pad, which disappears as the child gets older. An arch can usually be demonstrated on standing on tiptoe, or by passively extending the big toe, as shown here. Rachel is unable to stand on tiptoes not because of a pathological condition, but because children are typically unable to do so at this young age.

## answer_c
Positional talipes equinovarus

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Positional talipes equinovarus is a newborn foot deformity correctable to neutral, not this later-presenting flat-foot appearance in a walking toddler.

## answer_d
Rickets

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Rickets would typically show additional findings such as bowed legs and swollen wrists, not isolated flat feet with a demonstrable arch.

## answer_e
Tarsal coalition

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A fixed flat foot that cannot demonstrate an arch, often painful, may indicate tarsal coalition and needs orthopaedic opinion — but Rachel's arch is demonstrable, making this an unlikely diagnosis here.

## title
Persistent toe-walking since first steps

## question
Alliah is a 2½-year-old girl seen in the paediatric outpatient department because her mother is worried about her walking on tiptoes. She was born by normal vaginal delivery at 37 weeks, started walking at 15 months, never crawled but did bottom shuffle. On examination of her gait she mostly walks and runs on tiptoes, and her mother confirms she has done this since she started walking; however, she can walk on her heels if asked to do so. General examination of her legs shows normal tone and reflexes. What is the most likely diagnosis?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Cerebral palsy

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Children with mild cerebral palsy can present with toe walking, but an increase in tone would be found on examination, which is explicitly absent here (normal tone and reflexes).

## answer_b
Duchenne muscular dystrophy

## explanation_b
Incorrect. In older boys, Duchenne muscular dystrophy should be excluded when toe-walking persists, but Alliah is a girl with normal tone and reflexes, making this diagnosis unlikely.

## answer_c
Growing pains

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Growing pains cause nocturnal leg pain rather than a gait abnormality like toe-walking.

## answer_d
Normal variant of childhood

## explanation_d
Correct. Toe walking is common in young children and may become persistent, usually from habit. The child can walk normally (on her heels) when asked, as Alliah demonstrates. Bottom-shuffling rather than crawling is also a recognised normal variant of the pre-walking developmental pathway, and with normal tone and reflexes on examination, this fits a benign, idiopathic pattern.

## answer_e
Osteomyelitis

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Osteomyelitis would present with pain, fever and localised tenderness, not this painless, voluntary-appearing gait pattern with a normal general examination.
