# Item

QST-MUSTPED501-LISSAUER-CH6-CH15-CH6-02-MENINGOCOCCAL

## title
Purpuric rash with fever in a toddler

## question
David, a 15-month-old boy, is seen with a fever of 38.5°C. He has had a runny nose, cough and fever for 3 days. Since this morning he has been difficult to wake, and his heart rate is raised. He has a rash scattered over his legs and abdomen which does not disappear with pressure, with lesions of variable size. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia can cause a purpuric rash through marrow-crowding thrombocytopenia, but it typically has a longer, more insidious history and other features such as pallor, bone pain or lymphadenopathy, not the short, acutely febrile, rapidly drowsy course described here. The 3-day febrile prodrome culminating in reduced consciousness is far more in keeping with an acute septic process than a haematological malignancy.

## answer_b
Henoch-Schönlein purpura

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Henoch-Schönlein purpura's purpuric rash is classically confined to the legs, buttocks and extensor surfaces and is accompanied by abdominal pain and joint pain, but the child remains systemically well rather than becoming drowsy with a raised heart rate. The severity of this child's systemic illness argues against HSP, which is a vasculitis rather than a septic process.

## answer_c
Immune thrombocytopenia

## explanation_c
Incorrect. In immune thrombocytopenia the child is usually otherwise well, with petechiae/bruising from a low platelet count but no fever or systemic illness. This vignette's fever, tachycardia and reduced consciousness point to sepsis, not an isolated platelet disorder.

## answer_d
Non-accidental injury

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Non-accidental injury is not suggested by an acutely evolving febrile illness with a spreading purpuric rash; there is no bruising pattern or history inconsistency here to raise that concern, and the clinical picture is fully explained by an infectious cause.

## answer_e
Septicaemia

## explanation_e
Correct. This is meningococcal septicaemia. A purpuric, non-blanching rash of variable lesion size in a febrile, increasingly drowsy child with a raised heart rate is the classic presentation, and meningococcal disease should be suspected and treated on this basis alone, without waiting for neck stiffness or other signs of meningitis to appear.

## title
Glasgow Coma Scale scoring

## question
A 3-year-old boy is unresponsive to voice but, on stimulation, his eyes open, he cries, and he raises his hand and pushes the examiner's hand away. Using the Glasgow Coma Scale (with the under-4 Children's Coma Scale equivalents), what is this child's total score?

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
8

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A total of 8 would require lower individual scores than described; this child scores more than 8, since localising to pain (a motor score of 5) alone already contributes more than a bare minimum response would.

## answer_b
9

## explanation_b
Correct. The GCS/Children's Coma Scale total is the sum of three independently scored components: best motor response, best verbal response and best eye opening. Here the child localises pain (motor = 5, pushing the hand away shows localisation, not just withdrawal) and scores 2 each for verbal (cries only, to pain) and eye opening (opens to pain), giving 5 + 2 + 2 = 9.

## answer_c
10

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A score of 10 would follow if the verbal or eye-opening component were higher – for example if the child had vocalised words/sounds or opened eyes to voice rather than only to pain – which is not what is described here.

## answer_d
11

## explanation_d
Incorrect. 11 overstates the verbal and eye scores for a child who only cries and opens eyes in response to a painful stimulus; there is also a separate scoring system for children under 4, which this vignette's age (3 years) requires using rather than the adult GCS descriptors.

## answer_e
12

## explanation_e
Incorrect. This score corresponds to a child who is essentially only responsive to pain across all domains (AVPU 'P', roughly GCS 8-9), which does not match the higher motor score (localising, not merely responding) described in the case.

## title
Next step after a submersion injury

## question
A 10-month-old boy is rushed to the emergency department after being found submerged in the bath. His mother is distraught. What is the next most appropriate step in his initial management?

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Commence chest compressions in a ratio of 15:2

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Chest compressions may well be needed, but only later in the resuscitation sequence, after the earlier steps (stimulate/shout for help, then airway) have been completed and found inadequate, not as the very first action.

## answer_b
If the child is not breathing, commence bag and mask ventilation

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Bag and mask ventilation is done after the child has been stimulated, help has been called for, and the airway has been opened — it is not the opening step, even though breathing does need urgent attention in this scenario.

## answer_c
Place the child onto the examination couch and put his head into the neutral position

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Positioning the airway will need to be done, but is not the first step in the resuscitation sequence; it follows stimulation and calling for help.

## answer_d
Remove wet clothing/towels and dry the baby vigorously

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Drying and removing wet clothing is an essential step for a newborn infant to prevent heat loss, but for this 10-month-old the priority sequence starts with stimulation and calling for help, not drying.

## answer_e
Stimulate the baby and shout for help

## explanation_e
Correct. All paediatric resuscitation algorithms begin with a sequential approach: check safety, stimulate the child and shout for help, then proceed through airway, breathing and circulation. Calling for help early is essential because effective resuscitation of a collapsed child needs multiple rescuers working together.

QST-MUSTPED501-LISSAUER-CH6-CH15-CH6-06-INTUSSUSCEPTION

## title
Infant with episodic crying and shock

## question
Mohammed, aged 8 months, has been vomiting and off his feeds for 2 days. He initially had episodes of crying uncontrollably, drawing his legs up into his abdomen as if in pain, and appeared fractious. His mother gave him oral rehydration solution but his vomiting continued and he has become lethargic. On admission to hospital he is in shock. What is the most likely diagnosis?

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Gastroenteritis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Gastroenteritis is less likely because the episodes of uncontrollable crying with drawing the legs up into the abdomen, as if in colicky pain, are characteristic of intussusception rather than simple infective gastroenteritis.

## answer_b
Intussusception

## explanation_b
Correct. Intussusception — one segment of bowel telescoping into an adjacent segment — is the most likely cause of this episodic colicky pain progressing to shock. The combination of leg-drawing crying episodes, ongoing vomiting despite oral rehydration, and progression to lethargy and shock is the classic pattern; ultrasound often makes the diagnosis obvious and management follows an airway-breathing-circulation approach with early senior and radiology input.

## answer_c
Malrotation and volvulus

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Malrotation and volvulus remains a differential that must be considered, but it more classically presents with bile-stained vomiting, which is not described here; the leg-drawing colicky pattern is more typical of intussusception.

## answer_d
Meckel diverticulum

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Meckel diverticulum tends to present with rectal bleeding rather than this pain-and-shock pattern, and blood loss from it is rarely severe enough to cause shock on its own.

## answer_e
Strangulated hernia

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A strangulated hernia is a differential, but should be clinically evident on examination (a palpable, tender groin/scrotal swelling); nothing in this history points to a hernia rather than intussusception.

## title
Initial fluid bolus volume in shock

## question
Mohammed, aged 8 months, presents in shock (see the clinical scenario of episodic crying, drawing his legs up, and lethargy after 2 days of vomiting). He weighs 8 kg and needs an initial bolus of 0.9% normal saline to treat his shock. What volume of fluid would be given initially?

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
40 ml

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This volume is too small for a 20 mL/kg bolus at 8 kg; it corresponds to only 5 mL/kg, well below the standard resuscitation dose.

## answer_b
160 ml

## explanation_b
Correct. The initial fluid bolus for a shocked child is 20 mL/kg of an isotonic crystalloid such as 0.9% saline, repeated as necessary if shock persists. For an 8 kg infant this is 20 x 8 = 160 mL, given as a rapid bolus rather than trickled in over hours.

## answer_c
320 ml

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This is double the correct bolus volume (40 mL/kg); smaller aliquots than a full repeated bolus, not larger single ones, are used in situations like trauma or diabetic ketoacidosis where over-resuscitation is a specific risk.

## answer_d
680 ml

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This volume is far larger than the standard 20 mL/kg bolus and does not correspond to any standard paediatric resuscitation calculation for this weight.

## answer_e
800 ml

## explanation_e
Incorrect. 800 mL is actually the correct answer for this child's total maintenance fluid requirement over 24 hours (100 mL/kg/24h at 8 kg), not the initial resuscitation bolus, which is a much smaller, immediate volume.

## title
Total 24-hour fluid requirement after resuscitation

## question
Mohammed, aged 8 months and weighing 8 kg, has received a fluid bolus for shock which has improved his condition. He is now assessed as 10% dehydrated. His plasma sodium is normal (138 mmol/L) and his continuing losses from vomiting are now small and can be ignored. What is his total fluid requirement for the initial 24 hours?

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
160 ml

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This volume is far too small; it does not account for either the fluid deficit or the maintenance requirement for an 8 kg child.

## answer_b
320 ml

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This volume covers roughly the deficit alone at a lower percentage dehydration than stated, but omits maintenance fluid entirely.

## answer_c
800 ml

## explanation_c
Incorrect. 800 mL correctly represents only one of the two main components (either the deficit or the maintenance requirement alone, both of which happen to equal 800 mL here) — the total 24-hour requirement needs both added together, not either alone.

## answer_d
880 ml

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This is close to but not the correct total; it does not match the sum of the deficit (800 mL) and maintenance (800 mL) components calculated correctly for this child's weight and dehydration percentage.

## answer_e
1600 ml

## explanation_e
Correct. Total 24-hour fluid requirement is deficit plus maintenance plus continuing losses. Deficit is 10% of 8 kg = 800 mL; maintenance is approximately 100 mL/kg/24h = 800 mL; continuing losses are negligible (0 mL) as stated. Total = 800 + 800 + 0 = 1600 mL over the first 24 hours.

QST-MUSTPED501-LISSAUER-CH6-CH15-CH6-09-CHECK-GLUCOSE

## title
Next step after treating a prolonged seizure

## question
A 6-year-old child has been having a generalised seizure for 15 minutes. The ambulance crew gave a dose of buccal midazolam 5 minutes ago. The emergency doctor has maintained the airway and applied oxygen. His capillary refill time is under 2 seconds and his heart rate is 120 beats/min. What is the next most appropriate management step?

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Administer further anticonvulsant

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A further dose of anticonvulsant may be appropriate if the seizure is still ongoing after 10 minutes from the first dose, but glucose is checked first since a reversible metabolic cause should not be missed.

## answer_b
Check blood glucose level

## explanation_b
Correct. Checking blood glucose is the next most appropriate step, because hypoglycaemia is a rapidly reversible cause of ongoing or recurrent seizure and the only definitive treatment is glucose. This reflects the resuscitation teaching of "ABCDEFG" — don't ever forget glucose — checked as a routine part of the primary survey once airway and breathing are secured, ideally with intravenous glucose if access allows, or buccal glucose gel if not.

## answer_c
Gain intravenous access

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Gaining intravenous access is difficult during an ongoing seizure and, while helpful, is often done in parallel by other team members rather than being the single next priority step ahead of the glucose check.

## answer_d
Request senior review

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Requesting senior review is important, but should follow the clinician's own ABC (plus glucose) assessment rather than precede it — don't seek senior review before completing your own primary survey.

## answer_e
Start bag and valve mask ventilation

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Bag and valve mask ventilation is reserved for when breathing is inadequate or stops; oxygen administration is usually sufficient here, and this step is not indicated ahead of the glucose check in this scenario.

## title
Widespread urticaria with normal vital signs

## question
Seb, a 2-year-old boy, was at a birthday party when his mother noticed he suddenly developed a widespread urticarial rash and became flushed in the face. His vital signs are normal and he has no respiratory compromise. Which medication would be given?

## subject
mul

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Intramuscular adrenaline

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Intramuscular adrenaline is reserved for anaphylaxis (airway obstruction, wheeze or shock); it is not needed here since there is no respiratory compromise and vital signs are normal.

## answer_b
Intramuscular antihistamine

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Intramuscular antihistamine is not indicated when the child can tolerate oral medicine — it is painful to administer and offers no advantage over the oral route in a reaction of this mildness.

## answer_c
Intravenous hydrocortisone

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Intravenous hydrocortisone is not needed for a mild allergic reaction without respiratory compromise; it is an unnecessarily aggressive step for this severity.

## answer_d
Oral antihistamine

## explanation_d
Correct. In children, the most common triggers of acute food allergy are nuts, egg, milk or seafood; urticaria and facial flushing/swelling without respiratory or circulatory compromise are mild reactions. Immediate management is an oral antihistamine (e.g. chlorphenamine) with observation for around 2 hours for possible progression to a more severe reaction.

## answer_e
Oral corticosteroid

## explanation_e
Incorrect. An oral corticosteroid is not needed for a mild allergic reaction without respiratory compromise; the reaction should respond adequately to an oral antihistamine alone.

Facial swelling with noisy breathing and wheeze

## question
Jenny, a 3-year-old girl, was at a village fete when she suddenly developed swollen cheeks and lips and a widespread urticarial rash. ## title
Kawasaki disease treatment

## question
Michael is a 2-year-old boy whose parents were born in Taiwan. He has been unwell for 6 days with fever, sore eyes and throat. He has red eyes, an injected throat, cervical lymphadenopathy, a generalised maculopapular rash and peeling skin of the hands and feet, consistent with Kawasaki disease. What is the most appropriate next step in his management?

## subject

## status
Draft

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Antipyretic/analgesia

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Antipyretic/analgesia may provide symptomatic comfort, but does not address the underlying disease process or its coronary artery risk.

## answer_b
Combined anti-tuberculosis medication

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Anti-tuberculosis medication is for tuberculosis, which does not fit this clinical picture of fever, conjunctivitis, lymphadenopathy, rash and peeling.

## answer_c
Highly active antiretroviral therapy

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Antiretroviral therapy treats HIV infection, which is not the diagnosis here.

## answer_d
Intravenous antibiotics

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Kawasaki disease is not a bacterial infection responsive to antibiotics; intravenous antibiotics do not address its underlying vasculitic process.

## answer_e
Intravenous immunoglobulin

## explanation_e
Correct. Michael has Kawasaki disease and is at risk of developing coronary artery aneurysm; he requires treatment with intravenous immunoglobulin, which reduces this specific risk. This is the disease-modifying treatment, not supportive care alone.
