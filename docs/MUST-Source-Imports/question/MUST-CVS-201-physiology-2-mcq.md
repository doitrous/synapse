Effect of muscular exercise on blood pressure

## question
During muscular exercise, what happens to blood pressure?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

C

## answer_a
Systolic decreases and diastolic increases

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Exercise raises, not lowers, systolic pressure, and does not typically raise diastolic pressure either.

## answer_b
Both systolic and diastolic increase

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Diastolic pressure characteristically falls or stays level during dynamic exercise rather than rising alongside systolic pressure.

## answer_c
Systolic increases and diastolic decreases

## explanation_c
Correct. During dynamic muscular exercise, systolic pressure rises (driven by increased cardiac output) while diastolic pressure falls (driven by marked vasodilation in the exercising skeletal muscle, which lowers total peripheral resistance). ## explanation_d
Incorrect. Exercise raises cardiac output and systolic pressure; both pressures falling together does not match the exercise response.

Hydrostatic effect on arterial pressure below heart level

## question
For each centimeter below the level of the heart, arterial blood pressure:

## subject

## status

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Pressure increases, not decreases, below heart level, from the added weight of the hydrostatic column of blood.

## answer_b

## explanation_b
Correct. For each centimetre a measurement point sits below heart level, arterial pressure is higher by about 0.77 mmHg, purely from the hydrostatic pressure of the extra column of blood between that point and the heart. The Alam-Smirk reflex is triggered by stimulation of:

## subject
cvs

Claude

C

## explanation_a

It is named specifically for this proprioceptor trigger, distinguishing it from chemoreceptor- or baroreceptor-mediated reflexes.

Visceral pain receptors

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Visceral pain receptors are not the trigger this source names; the Alam-Smirk reflex is specifically a proprioceptor-driven exercise pressor reflex.

## title
Capillary fluid shift after a drop in arterial pressure

## question
In the capillary fluid shift mechanism, a drop in arterial blood pressure causes:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Fluid to shift from capillaries to tissues

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Fluid shifting from capillaries into tissues is the direction seen when capillary hydrostatic pressure rises (as in oedema-producing mechanisms), the opposite of what a fall in arterial pressure produces.

## answer_b
Fluid to shift from tissues to capillaries

## explanation_b
Correct. A fall in arterial pressure lowers capillary hydrostatic pressure, tipping the Starling balance in favour of absorption over filtration, so fluid moves from the interstitial (tissue) space back into the capillaries. This capillary fluid shift expands plasma volume and partially restores the fall in arterial pressure, acting over minutes — more slowly than the near-instantaneous baroreceptor reflex but faster than renal fluid-retention mechanisms.

## answer_c
No fluid movement

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Fluid movement does occur; the Starling balance shifts toward net absorption when capillary pressure falls, it does not simply stop.

## answer_d
Increased filtration from capillaries

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Filtration from the capillaries would raise, not restore, a fall in arterial pressure by moving fluid out of the vessels; the compensatory direction is absorption into the capillaries.
