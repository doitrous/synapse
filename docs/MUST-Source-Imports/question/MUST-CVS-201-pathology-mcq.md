## title
Pericardial effusion classification from lymphatic obstruction

## question
Pericardial effusion due to obstruction of lymphatic drainage is classified as:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Exudative

## explanation_a
Incorrect. An exudative effusion is protein-rich and driven by inflammation or infection, not by a mechanical drainage problem.

## answer_b
Transudative

## explanation_b
Correct. Pericardial effusion is classified by the character of its fluid, and a transudate is the low-protein fluid produced by a mechanical or hydrostatic disturbance rather than inflammation of the pericardium itself. Obstruction of the pericardial lymphatic drainage prevents normal fluid clearance and lets low-protein fluid accumulate, the same mechanism (impaired drainage or raised hydrostatic pressure, as in heart failure or hypoproteinaemia) that produces a transudate elsewhere in the body. This distinguishes it from an exudate, which requires inflamed, more permeable pericardial vessels.

## answer_c
Hemorrhagic

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A haemorrhagic effusion contains blood, typically from trauma, malignant invasion, or aortic dissection into the pericardium, not from lymphatic obstruction.

## answer_d
Suppurative

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A suppurative effusion is frank pus from direct pyogenic bacterial infection of the pericardium, an inflammatory process rather than a drainage problem.

## title
Bread and butter pericarditis appearance

## question
The "bread and butter" appearance is characteristic of which type of pericarditis?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Suppurative

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Suppurative pericarditis shows frank pus on the pericardial surface, not the rough, shaggy bread-and-butter texture.

## answer_b
Fibrinous

## explanation_b
Correct. Fibrinous pericarditis is the commonest type of pericarditis and produces the classic "bread and butter" gross appearance — rough, shaggy fibrin strands coating the visceral and parietal pericardial surfaces, resembling two slices of bread pulled apart after being buttered. It follows causes such as rheumatic fever, lobar pneumonia, tuberculosis, uraemia, or as a complication overlying a myocardial infarction.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Serous pericarditis produces a clear, watery effusion with a smooth pericardial surface, not the rough fibrinous texture described.

## answer_d
Hemorrhagic

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Haemorrhagic pericarditis is characterised by blood-stained fluid, typically from tuberculosis or malignant invasion, not the fibrin-coated bread-and-butter surface.

## title
First grossly visible lesion in atherosclerosis

## question
The first grossly visible lesion in atherosclerosis is:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Fibrous plaque

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The fibrous (atheromatous) plaque is the basic, more advanced lesion of established atherosclerosis, developing after the fatty streak, not before it.

## answer_b
Complicated plaque

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A complicated plaque — with ulceration, calcification, haemorrhage or thrombosis — is the most advanced stage, far later than the first visible change.

## answer_c
Fatty streak

## explanation_c
Correct. The fatty streak is the earliest grossly visible lesion of developing atherosclerosis, seen as a flat, yellow streak in the arterial intima. Microscopically it is made of foam cells — lipid-laden macrophages and smooth muscle cells — and it occurs characteristically at the aortic valve and in the thoracic aorta. It can be found even in children in some populations and is potentially reversible, which is why it is taught as the earliest lesion rather than as established disease.

## answer_d
Atheroma

## explanation_d
Incorrect. "Atheroma" describes the lipid-rich core of an established plaque, a later structure than the first visible fatty streak.

Basic lesion of atherosclerosis

## question
The basic lesion of atherosclerosis is the:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner

B

Fatty streak

## explanation_a

Fibrous atheromatous plaque

## explanation_b
Correct. The fibrous atheromatous plaque is the basic lesion of atherosclerosis, developing from the fatty streak within the tunica intima. Calcified nodule

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Calcified nodule is a specific, less common plaque morphology, not the term for the basic atherosclerotic lesion.

## title
Complication not caused by atherosclerosis

## question
Complications of atherosclerosis include all EXCEPT:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Aneurysms

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This is a recognised complication. Weakening of the arterial wall by atherosclerosis is the classic mechanism for an atherosclerotic (typically abdominal aortic) aneurysm, so it is not the exception.

## answer_b
Limb claudication

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This is a recognised complication. Atherosclerotic narrowing of the peripheral (classically popliteal) arteries produces intermittent limb claudication, so it is not the exception.

## answer_c
Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

## explanation_c
Correct. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is a primary disease of the sarcomere, most often caused by an inherited mutation in a gene encoding a sarcomere protein, and is not a downstream complication of atherosclerosis. Atherosclerosis instead causes complications through vessel-wall weakening (aneurysm), luminal narrowing (claudication, infarction) and plaque instability (thromboembolism, haemorrhage), none of which produces the primary myocardial hypertrophy that defines HCM.

## answer_d
Cerebral infarction

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This is a recognised complication. Atherosclerotic occlusion of a cerebral vessel is a classic cause of cerebral infarction (stroke), so it is not the exception.

## title
Kidney change in benign hypertension

## question
In benign hypertension, the kidney may show:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Fibrinoid necrosis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Fibrinoid necrosis of the arterioles is the change specific to malignant, not benign, hypertension.

## answer_b
Hyaline arteriolosclerosis

## explanation_b
Correct. Benign hypertension produces hyaline arteriolosclerosis in the kidney — pink, structureless, homogeneous thickening of the arteriolar wall from deposition of an abnormal (plasma) protein. This is the same hyaline change seen in diabetes and, to a lesser degree, as an ageing change in otherwise normotensive elderly patients, and it is distinct from the hyperplastic ("onion-skin") change with fibrinoid necrosis specific to malignant hypertension.

## answer_c
Onion-skin thickening

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Onion-skin (hyperplastic) arteriolar thickening is the change specific to malignant, not benign, hypertension.

## answer_d

## title
Circumferential extent of a saccular aneurysm

## question
A saccular aneurysm involves:

## subject
cvs

## status

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Entire circumference

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Involvement of the entire circumference describes a fusiform, not a saccular, aneurysm.

## answer_b
Part of circumference

## explanation_b
Correct. A saccular aneurysm dilates only part of the circumference of the vessel, forming a focal, sac-like outpouching. This contrasts with a fusiform aneurysm, which dilates the entire circumference symmetrically over a length of the vessel. The shape distinction matters clinically because a saccular aneurysm's narrow neck and eccentric sac carry a different rupture risk profile from a fusiform one.

## answer_c
Dissection of wall

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Dissection of the wall, with blood tracking within the media, describes a dissecting aneurysm, a mechanistically distinct entity from a saccular true aneurysm.

## answer_d
False lumen

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A false lumen is the hallmark of a dissecting aneurysm, not of a saccular true aneurysm.

## title
Condition most associated with mycotic aneurysms

## question
Mycotic aneurysms are most associated with:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Atherosclerosis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Atherosclerosis is the classic association for a fusiform, degenerative aneurysm, a different mechanism from an infected, mycotic one.

## answer_b
Syphilis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Syphilis is the classic association for syphilitic (thoracic aortic) aneurysm from tertiary aortitis, a different, non-embolic mechanism.

## answer_c
Infective endocarditis

## explanation_c
Correct. A mycotic aneurysm is a localised arterial dilation caused by infection of the vessel wall, most commonly from septic embolisation of infected material — classically vegetations shed from infective endocarditis, which seed and weaken a distal artery wall. This embolic, infective mechanism is the specific association this source is testing, distinguishing it from the degenerative (atherosclerotic) or connective-tissue (Marfan/dissecting) mechanisms tested elsewhere in this paper.

## answer_d
Trauma

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Trauma is the classic association for a false (pseudo-) aneurysm, not the infective, embolic mechanism of a mycotic one.

Vessels affected by Takayasu arteritis

Takayasu arteritis affects the:

## subject
cvs

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Small vessels only

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Takayasu arteritis is a large-vessel, not a small-vessel, vasculitis.

## answer_b
Aorta and main branches

## explanation_b
Correct. Takayasu arteritis is a large-vessel vasculitis involving the aorta and its major branches, with granulomatous inflammation leading to wall thickening, stenosis and, over time, diminished or absent peripheral pulses ("pulseless disease"). This large-vessel distribution, together with the typical young-female demographic, is what distinguishes it from other vasculitides of different vessel calibre.

## answer_c
Coronary arteries only

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Isolated coronary artery involvement is not the characteristic distribution of Takayasu arteritis, which affects the aorta and its major branches broadly.

## answer_d
Pulmonary arteries

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The pulmonary arteries can occasionally be involved in Takayasu arteritis, but they are not the primary vessels this source names; the aorta and its main branches are.

Effect of bone erosion by a descending aortic aneurysm

In descending aortic aneurysm, bone erosion may cause:

## subject

Chest pain

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Chest pain is the classic presenting symptom of the aneurysm itself (or its dissection), not the specific symptom of adjacent bone erosion by a descending aortic lesion.

## answer_b
Back pain

## explanation_b
Correct. A descending aortic aneurysm lies directly against the vertebral bodies. As it enlarges, it can erode the adjacent bone by sustained mechanical pressure over time. Abdominal pain

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Abdominal pain is more typical of an abdominal, not a descending thoracic, aortic aneurysm eroding adjacent structures.

## answer_d
Headache

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Headache is not the expected symptom of vertebral bone erosion by a descending aortic aneurysm.
