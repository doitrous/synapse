# Item

## id
QST-MUSTMED501-SOURCE-A-A01-ANTI-CCP

## title
Anti-CCP antibody specificity

## question
Anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) antibodies are most frequently associated with:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Rheumatoid arthritis

## explanation_a
Correct. Anti-CCP (anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide) antibody is a specific serological marker for rheumatoid arthritis. Unlike rheumatoid factor, which is also seen in several unrelated diseases, anti-CCP's high specificity makes it valuable for confirming an RA diagnosis and for identifying RA early, before rheumatoid factor may have become positive. It is now a standard part of the rheumatoid arthritis diagnostic serology panel alongside rheumatoid factor.

## answer_b
Seronegative spondyloarthritis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The seronegative spondyloarthritides (ankylosing spondylitis, reactive arthritis, psoriatic arthropathy) are defined by a NEGATIVE rheumatoid factor and are associated with HLA-B27, not with anti-CCP antibodies.

## answer_c
Wegener's granulomatosis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Wegener's granulomatosis (granulomatosis with polyangiitis) is associated with cytoplasmic ANCA (PR3-ANCA/cANCA), not anti-CCP antibodies.

## answer_d
Progressive systemic sclerosis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Progressive systemic sclerosis is associated with anti-centromere antibodies (limited form) or anti-Scl-70/topoisomerase I antibodies (diffuse form), not anti-CCP.

QST-MUSTMED501-SOURCE-A-A02-MORNING-STIFFNESS

## title
RA typical feature

## question
The typical feature of rheumatoid arthritis is:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Always associated with a positive rheumatoid factor

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Roughly 20-30% of RA patients are seronegative (rheumatoid-factor negative), particularly early in disease, so a positive RF is not universal and is not the defining clinical feature.

## answer_b
Prolonged morning stiffness

## explanation_b
Correct. Prolonged morning stiffness — classically lasting at least one hour — is a hallmark clinical feature of rheumatoid arthritis, reflecting overnight accumulation of inflammatory synovial fluid and synovitis. It is one of the ACR 1987 classification criteria and is used clinically to distinguish inflammatory from mechanical joint pain, since mechanical/degenerative joint stiffness is typically brief.

## answer_c
Affects the distal interphalangeal joint

## explanation_c
Incorrect. RA characteristically involves the proximal interphalangeal and metacarpophalangeal joints while sparing the distal interphalangeal joints; DIP involvement instead suggests osteoarthritis or psoriatic arthritis.

## answer_d
Migratory polyarthritis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. RA is a persistent, additive symmetrical polyarthritis, not a migratory one; migratory polyarthritis is more typical of rheumatic fever or gonococcal arthritis.

QST-MUSTMED501-SOURCE-A-A04-RA-ANEMIA

## title
Anaemia types in RA

## question
Which of the following types of anaemia could be caused by rheumatoid arthritis?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Normocytic normochromic anaemia

## explanation_a
Partially correct, but incomplete on its own. Normocytic normochromic anaemia of chronic disease, from marrow suppression by ongoing inflammation, is the default RA-associated anaemia, but it is not the only mechanism RA produces — see option E.

## answer_b
Microcytic hypochromic anaemia

## explanation_b
Partially correct, but incomplete on its own. Microcytic hypochromic anaemia occurs in RA from chronic NSAID-induced gastrointestinal blood loss, but this is only one of several mechanisms — see option E.

## answer_c
Megaloblastic anaemia

## explanation_c
Partially correct, but incomplete on its own. Megaloblastic anaemia occurs in RA from methotrexate-induced folate deficiency, but this is only one of several mechanisms — see option E.

## answer_d
Aplastic anaemia

## explanation_d
Partially correct, but incomplete on its own. Marrow-suppressive drug toxicity (e.g. gold, methotrexate) or hypersplenism in Felty syndrome can produce more severe cytopenias including an aplastic picture, but this is only one of several mechanisms — see option E.

## answer_e
All of the above

## explanation_e
Correct. Rheumatoid arthritis can cause anaemia through multiple distinct mechanisms depending on the clinical context: normocytic normochromic anaemia of chronic disease from ongoing inflammation (the default pattern), microcytic hypochromic anaemia from NSAID-induced GI bleeding, megaloblastic anaemia from methotrexate-induced folate deficiency, and more severe marrow suppression (including an aplastic-range picture) from disease-modifying drug toxicity or hypersplenism in Felty syndrome. All of the listed types are recognised in RA, so "all of the above" is correct.

## title
Acute gout — case diagnosis

## question
A 30-year-old overweight, hyperactive male, taking a thiazide diuretic and smoking, develops acute pain in his left foot around the big toe. What is the most likely diagnosis?

## subject
msk

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Deep vein thrombosis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. DVT presents with unilateral calf/leg swelling, pain and warmth, not with acute pain localised specifically around the first metatarsophalangeal joint (the great toe).

## answer_b
Tendonitis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Tendonitis produces pain along a tendon on movement or palpation rather than acute joint-centred pain, and does not fit this constellation of gout risk factors.

## answer_c

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Osteoarthritis is a chronic, gradually progressive degenerative condition, not an acute presentation, and does not typically strike the great toe acutely in this way.

Incorrect. Cellulitis is a skin/soft-tissue infection with diffuse erythema and systemic signs, not classically centred on a single joint with this specific risk-factor profile.

Acute gout arthritis

## explanation_e
Correct. This vignette is the classic presentation of acute gouty arthritis: a man with the recognised risk factors — obesity, a thiazide diuretic (which reduces renal urate excretion), and smoking/lifestyle factors — presenting with sudden, acute pain around the first metatarsophalangeal joint of the great toe (podagra). Thiazide diuretics are a well-known precipitant of hyperuricaemia and acute gout attacks.

## id
QST-MUSTMED501-SOURCE-A-A09-GOUT-GOLD-STANDARD

## title
Gout — gold-standard investigation

## question
The most diagnostic investigation of gout is:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Serum uric acid greater than 7 mg

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Serum uric acid is a supportive investigation, but it is neither diagnostic on its own (levels can be normal during an acute attack, or high in asymptomatic hyperuricaemia without gout) nor as specific as visualising the crystals directly.

## answer_b
Joint aspiration and polarized microscope analysis

## explanation_b
Correct. Joint aspiration with polarized-light microscopy is the gold-standard investigation for gout, demonstrating needle-shaped, negatively birefringent monosodium urate crystals within the synovial fluid (often within neutrophils), which definitively confirms the diagnosis and distinguishes gout from pseudogout (rhomboid, positively birefringent calcium pyrophosphate crystals). This directly visualises the crystals responsible for the disease, rather than relying on an indirect marker.

## answer_c
X-ray of the first metatarsophalangeal joint

## explanation_c
