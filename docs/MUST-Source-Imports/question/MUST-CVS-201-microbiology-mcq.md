## title
Most common cause of acute infective endocarditis in an IV drug user

## question
A 32-year-old IV drug user is admitted with high fever, chills and a rapidly progressive new regurgitant murmur. Which organism is most likely responsible for acute endocarditis in this setting?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Viridans streptococci

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Viridans streptococci cause the slower, subacute form of endocarditis on an already-damaged valve, not this fulminant, rapidly progressive picture.

## answer_b
Staphylococcus aureus

## explanation_b
Correct. Staphylococcus aureus is the most common cause of acute infective endocarditis, and in an intravenous drug user it classically seeds the tricuspid valve directly from injection-site bacteraemia. Its high virulence produces a fulminant illness within days -- high fever, rigors and rapid valve destruction with a new regurgitant murmur -- unlike the indolent course typical of lower-virulence organisms. This combination of an IV-drug-use risk factor with a rapidly evolving picture is the classic clinical fingerprint of acute S. aureus endocarditis.

## answer_c
Staphylococcus epidermidis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Staphylococcus epidermidis is a low-virulence skin commensal that causes indolent prosthetic-valve infection, not a fulminant acute presentation on a native valve.

## answer_d
Enterococcus faecalis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Enterococcus faecalis is more typically associated with endocarditis following genitourinary or GI procedures in older patients, not this IV-drug-use acute presentation.

## title
Virulence factor letting Viridans streptococci adhere to a damaged valve

## question
Which virulence property explains why Viridans streptococci adhere to damaged valves?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Coagulase production

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Coagulase is a Staphylococcus aureus virulence factor that clots plasma; Viridans streptococci do not produce it.

## answer_b
Dextran (biofilm) synthesis from glucose

## explanation_b
Correct. Viridans streptococci synthesise dextran, a sticky extracellular polysaccharide, from dietary glucose. This dextran forms a biofilm that lets the organism adhere firmly to the fibrin-platelet vegetation already present on a damaged or abnormal heart valve, anchoring it in place against the flow of blood. This dextran-dependent adherence is exactly why Viridans streptococci need a pre-damaged valve to establish infection, rather than being able to seed a normal one the way S. aureus can.

## answer_c
Protein A expression

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Protein A is a Staphylococcus aureus surface protein that binds immunoglobulin Fc regions; it plays no role in Viridans streptococcal adherence.

## answer_d
Novobiocin resistance

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Novobiocin resistance is a laboratory identification feature of Staphylococcus saprophyticus, unrelated to valve adherence by Viridans streptococci.

## title
Best initial diagnostic test for suspected infective endocarditis

## question
A patient with suspected infective endocarditis -- best initial diagnostic specimen/test is:

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Throat swab culture

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A throat swab samples the oropharynx, not the bloodstream, and cannot identify the organism infecting the heart valve.

## answer_b
One blood culture after antibiotics

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A single blood culture risks a false negative, and drawing it after antibiotics have already started can suppress bacterial growth and cause the culture to miss the causative organism.

## answer_c
Serial blood cultures before antibiotics

## explanation_c
Correct. Serial blood cultures -- at least three, from separate venepuncture sites, drawn before any antibiotic is given -- are the best initial investigation for suspected infective endocarditis. Because the bacteraemia of endocarditis is continuous, sampling on more than one occasion before treatment starts maximises the chance of isolating the organism and its susceptibilities. Starting empirical antibiotics first can sterilise the blood and permanently obscure the diagnosis, which is why cultures should be drawn first whenever the patient is stable enough to allow it.

## answer_d
Urine culture

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Urine culture identifies a urinary pathogen, not the organism responsible for an infected heart valve.

## title
Micro-embolic lesion on the palms/soles in acute endocarditis

## question
Which clinical sign is described as a micro-embolic lesion on the palms/soles in acute endocarditis?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Janeway lesions

## explanation_a
Correct. Janeway lesions are small, painless, erythematous or haemorrhagic macules on the palms and soles caused by septic micro-emboli that break off an infected valve and lodge in cutaneous small vessels. Because they are painless and flat, they are easy to overlook on examination, unlike the tender nodules of Osler nodes. Their presence in a febrile patient is a specific peripheral sign of infective endocarditis, especially the more embolising acute form.

## answer_b
Osler nodes

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Osler nodes are painful, raised nodules on the finger and toe pads caused by immune-complex deposition, not painless embolic macules on the palms/soles.

## answer_c
Roth spots

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Roth spots are retinal haemorrhages with a pale centre, seen on fundoscopy, not a palm/sole skin finding.

## answer_d
Splinter hemorrhages only under nails

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Splinter haemorrhages are linear haemorrhages under the nail beds, a different peripheral sign from the palmar/plantar macules described here.

## title
Organism NOT covered by empiric endocarditis antimicrobial therapy

## question
Empiric antimicrobial coverage for suspected endocarditis should include activity against each of the following EXCEPT:

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA)

## explanation_a
Incorrect (this IS covered). MRSA is one of the leading bacterial causes of endocarditis, so empirical regimens include an agent active against it, such as vancomycin.

## answer_b
Viridans streptococci

## explanation_b
Incorrect (this IS covered). Viridans streptococci are the classic cause of subacute endocarditis, so empirical therapy is chosen to be active against them as well.

## answer_c
Enterococci

## explanation_c
Incorrect (this IS covered). Enterococci are a recognised cause of endocarditis, particularly after genitourinary procedures, and empirical regimens are designed to cover them too.

## answer_d
Coxsackievirus B

## explanation_d
Correct. Empirical antimicrobial therapy for suspected endocarditis is built to cover the bacteria that overwhelmingly cause the disease -- S. aureus/MRSA, Viridans streptococci and enterococci. Because endocarditis is essentially always a bacterial disease, this empirical cover has no antiviral activity, and Coxsackievirus B (a cause of a different disease, viral myocarditis) is not a target of it. Recognising this distinction keeps empirical treatment focused on the organisms that actually cause endocarditis rather than broadening it unnecessarily.

## title
Antibiotic prophylaxis before dental extraction with a prosthetic valve

## question
A patient with a history of prosthetic valve placement is scheduled for dental extraction. What is the recommended prophylaxis:

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
No prophylaxis required

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A prosthetic valve is a recognised high-risk indication for endocarditis prophylaxis; omitting it would leave the patient unprotected during the procedure's bacteraemia.

## answer_b
Oral amoxicillin before the procedure

## explanation_b
Correct. A single dose of oral amoxicillin given shortly before a dental procedure is the recommended prophylaxis for a patient with a prosthetic valve. Dental extraction releases oral flora, chiefly Viridans streptococci, into the bloodstream, and amoxicillin covers this organism during the brief window of bacteraemia the procedure causes. This targeted, single-dose approach is reserved for patients whose valve is already abnormal or artificial, since it is the damaged or prosthetic surface that lets a transient bacteraemia progress to endocarditis.

## answer_c
Long-acting benzathine penicillin IM monthly

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Monthly benzathine penicillin is the secondary-prophylaxis regimen used to prevent recurrent rheumatic fever, not single-procedure endocarditis prophylaxis.

## answer_d
Erythromycin before the procedure

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Erythromycin before a procedure is not the standard first-line dental prophylaxis; it is used as an alternative in patients with a penicillin allergy.

## title
Pathogenesis of prosthetic-valve endocarditis due to S. epidermidis

## question
Which of the following best describes the pathogenesis of prosthetic-valve endocarditis due to S. epidermidis?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Toxin-mediated myocardial necrosis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. S. epidermidis does not produce a potent myocardial-necrosing toxin; its pathogenesis is a device-surface colonisation process, not toxin-mediated tissue destruction.

## answer_b
Glycocalyx formation on biomedical devices

## explanation_b
Correct. Staphylococcus epidermidis, a coagulase-negative skin commensal, colonises a prosthetic valve by forming a glycocalyx (slime) biofilm on the artificial surface. This biofilm cements the organism and subsequent generations to the device, shielding it from both host immune defences and antibiotics. Because this is a surface-biofilm process rather than tissue invasion, S. epidermidis prosthetic-valve infection is often indolent and difficult to eradicate with antibiotics alone.

## answer_c
Molecular mimicry with valve antigens

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Molecular mimicry with valve antigens describes the immune-mediated mechanism of rheumatic fever, not S. epidermidis prosthetic-valve infection.

## answer_d
Invasion through adjacent lung infection

## explanation_d
Incorrect. S. epidermidis reaches a prosthetic valve via bacteraemia introduced at the time of surgery or device handling, not by direct spread from an adjacent lung infection.

Most common viral cause of myocarditis

A 24-year-old presents with dyspnea and signs of heart failure after a recent viral illness. Which organism is listed as the most common viral cause of myocarditis?

## subject
inf

## status

Coxsackie virus

## title
Diagnostic features emphasized for myocarditis

## question
Which diagnostic features are emphasized for myocarditis?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Elevated cardiac enzymes and heart-failure presentation

## explanation_a
Correct. Myocarditis is diagnosed from a combination of elevated cardiac enzymes (such as troponin), which rise as inflamed myocytes are injured, together with a clinical presentation of heart failure -- dyspnoea, fatigue and reduced cardiac output. This combination, rather than a murmur or a positive blood culture, is the pattern that should point the clinician toward myocarditis as the underlying diagnosis. Direct viral injury and the accompanying immune response damage myocytes enough both to release troponin and to impair contractile function.

## answer_b
Positive blood cultures and new murmur

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Positive blood cultures and a new murmur are the hallmark findings of infective endocarditis, a valve infection, not myocarditis, which is inflammation of the heart muscle itself.

## answer_c
Optochin sensitivity on blood agar

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Optochin sensitivity is a laboratory test used to identify Streptococcus pneumoniae on culture, unrelated to the clinical diagnosis of myocarditis.

## answer_d
Non-specific skin lesions only

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Myocarditis is not diagnosed from skin findings; those belong to other conditions such as endocarditis (Janeway lesions) or rheumatic fever.

## title
Causative organism of cardiac manifestations weeks after streptococcal pharyngitis

## question
A patient presents with cardiac manifestations developing 2-4 weeks after an episode of streptococcal pharyngitis. Laboratory investigation focuses on a pathogen whose virulence factor triggers an immune-mediated cross-reaction with cardiac tissue. Serologic testing is used to demonstrate a recent infection. Which of the following is the most likely causative organism?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Staphylococcus Aureus

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Staphylococcus aureus causes acute infective endocarditis directly, not a delayed immune-mediated cardiac disease following pharyngitis.

## answer_b
Streptococcus Viridans

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Viridans streptococci cause subacute endocarditis by direct valve colonisation, not a delayed immune-mediated syndrome after pharyngitis.

## answer_c
Staphylococcus Epidermidis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Staphylococcus epidermidis causes indolent prosthetic-device infection, unrelated to a preceding streptococcal throat infection.

## answer_d
Streptococcus Pyogenes

## explanation_d
Correct. Streptococcus pyogenes (group A Streptococcus) is the organism responsible: acute rheumatic fever follows pharyngitis when antibodies raised against the organism's M protein cross-react with joint, heart and brain antigens, producing cardiac and other manifestations some 2-4 weeks later. Because the heart is not directly infected at this stage, serologic testing (such as the antistreptolysin O titer) rather than a positive culture is used to demonstrate the antecedent streptococcal infection. This delayed, immune cross-reactive pattern -- not direct bacterial invasion -- is the hallmark that identifies S. pyogenes as the trigger organism.

## explanation_a
Correct. Corynebacterium diphtheriae produces an exotoxin that, once absorbed systemically from the local throat infection, can damage the myocardium, producing toxin-mediated myocarditis as a serious complication of diphtheria. Vasculitis

Incorrect. Vasculitis is not a recognised complication of diphtheria toxin; the toxin's recognised target organs are the myocardium and peripheral nerves.

## title
Treatment for endocarditis

## question
Which of the following is the treatment for endocarditis?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Penicillin + aminoglycosides

## explanation_a
Correct. Standard antimicrobial treatment for infective endocarditis combines a cell-wall-active agent such as penicillin (or vancomycin) with an aminoglycoside. Penicillin weakens the bacterial cell wall, which lets the aminoglycoside -- normally excluded by an intact cell wall -- enter the cell and act on the ribosome, producing synergistic, more rapid bactericidal killing than either drug achieves alone. This synergy is important because the vegetation in endocarditis is a dense, poorly vascularised mass that is harder for antibiotics and host defences to penetrate than an ordinary soft-tissue infection.

Penicillin alone

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Penicillin alone lacks the synergistic bactericidal speed of the combination and is not the standard approach for a poorly vascularised vegetation.

## answer_c
Ceftriaxone only

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Ceftriaxone alone is used in specific circumstances but is not the general standard combination described in this teaching.

## answer_d
None of the above

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A recognised standard combination (penicillin/vancomycin plus an aminoglycoside) does exist, so 'none of the above' is not correct.
