## title
Basal vasomotor tone maintenance

## question
The continuous, partial vasoconstriction of blood vessels is maintained by:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Parasympathetic tone

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Parasympathetic vasodilator fibres supply only a limited set of vascular beds, such as the salivary glands and genital erectile tissue, and are not the source of generalised arteriolar tone.

## answer_b
Sympathetic tone

## explanation_b
Correct. Arterioles are held in a state of continuous partial constriction, called vasomotor tone, by ongoing tonic discharge from sympathetic vasoconstrictor fibres releasing norepinephrine onto alpha-adrenergic receptors, even at rest. This resting sympathetic outflow gives arterioles a baseline degree of constriction that can be increased by further sympathetic discharge or decreased by its withdrawal, which is how the nervous system adjusts vascular resistance and blood pressure moment to moment. Because the tone is continuous rather than intermittent, withdrawing it produces vasodilation just as effectively as an active vasodilator signal would.

## answer_c
Autoregulation

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Autoregulation is a local, intrinsic mechanism that keeps blood flow to a specific tissue constant despite changes in perfusion pressure; it is not the source of the body's generalised, sympathetically-driven baseline vessel tone.

## answer_d
Basal metabolic rate

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Basal metabolic rate reflects the body's resting energy expenditure and influences local tissue blood-flow demand, but it does not itself maintain the sympathetic vasoconstrictor discharge that produces vasomotor tone.

## title
Epinephrine and skeletal muscle vasodilation

## question
During a "fight or flight" response, epinephrine causes vasodilation in skeletal muscle by acting on:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Alpha-adrenergic receptors

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Alpha-adrenergic receptors, which predominate in skin, splanchnic and renal vessels, are the receptor epinephrine binds to cause vasoconstriction, not the skeletal-muscle vasodilation this question asks about.

## answer_b
Beta-adrenergic receptors

## explanation_b
Correct. In skeletal muscle, beta2-adrenergic receptors predominate over alpha receptors, so epinephrine released from the adrenal medulla binds beta2 receptors there and causes vasodilation rather than the vasoconstriction it produces almost everywhere else. This increases blood flow to skeletal muscle during a fight-or-flight response, precisely when the body needs to shunt blood away from the skin and splanchnic circulation and toward the muscles that will do the fighting or fleeing. Norepinephrine, by contrast, acts almost exclusively on alpha receptors and is a vasoconstrictor in essentially every vascular bed it reaches, which is why epinephrine's dual, receptor-bed-dependent action is the distinguishing fact tested here.

## answer_c
Muscarinic receptors

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Muscarinic receptors mediate parasympathetic (acetylcholine) signalling, not the adrenal-medulla catecholamine action this question describes.

## answer_d
Nicotinic receptors

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Nicotinic receptors mediate fast synaptic transmission at autonomic ganglia and the neuromuscular junction, not epinephrine's direct vascular smooth-muscle action.

RAS activation trigger

## question
The Renin-Angiotensin System (RAS) is primarily activated by:

## subject
cvs

## status

C

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Increased blood pressure suppresses, rather than activates, renin secretion, through the same feedback logic in reverse.

## answer_b
Increased sodium delivery to the kidney

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Increased sodium delivery to the distal tubule decreases renin secretion; it is decreased sodium delivery that raises it, not increased delivery.

## answer_c
Decreased blood pressure

## explanation_c
Correct. A drop in arterial blood pressure causes renal ischaemia, which triggers renin secretion from the juxtaglomerular apparatus. Renin then cleaves hepatic angiotensinogen to angiotensin I, which angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) converts to angiotensin II, and angiotensin II raises arterial pressure by systemic vasoconstriction, catecholamine secretion, and ADH and aldosterone secretion. This renal-ischaemia trigger is what makes the RAS a major long-term regulator of arterial pressure, activated specifically by a fall, not a rise, in pressure.

## answer_d
Increased blood volume

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Increased blood volume raises atrial stretch and favours atrial natriuretic peptide secretion, the opposing, pressure-lowering system, not renin secretion.

## subject

Incorrect. Hypotension is typically associated with reduced venous return and atrial filling, which would reduce atrial stretch and ANP secretion, not stimulate it.

## title
ANP action versus angiotensin II

## question
A key action of Atrial Natriuretic Peptide (ANP) is to:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Promote sodium and water reabsorption

## explanation_a
Incorrect. ANP promotes sodium excretion, the opposite of sodium and water reabsorption, which is instead an action of aldosterone and angiotensin II.

## answer_b
Stimulate aldosterone secretion

## explanation_b
Incorrect. ANP does not stimulate aldosterone secretion; angiotensin II is the hormone that stimulates aldosterone release from the adrenal cortex, an action ANP works against rather than reinforces.

## answer_c
Oppose the actions of Angiotensin II

## explanation_c
Correct. ANP is secreted when increased extracellular fluid volume stretches the atrial muscle fibres, and it acts by increasing renal sodium excretion and enhancing pressure natriuresis, returning blood pressure and volume toward normal. Because angiotensin II raises blood pressure by vasoconstriction, aldosterone-driven sodium retention and ADH secretion, ANP's natriuretic, vasodilator and pressure-lowering actions are its direct physiological opposite. This opposing relationship is why the two hormones are taught together as the two counterbalancing arms of long-term blood-pressure regulation.

## answer_d
Cause potent vasoconstriction

## explanation_d
Incorrect. ANP itself does not cause vasoconstriction; if anything it has a mild vasodilator action, again the opposite of the vasoconstriction angiotensin II produces.

Kinins and pain sensation

## subject

## answer_d
Endothelins (ET)

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Endothelins are potent vasoconstrictors released by endothelium in response to stretch or injury; their defining action is vasoconstriction, not pain stimulation.

## title
Flare reaction mechanism

## question
The "flare reaction" in the skin is an example of vasodilation mediated by:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Sympathetic cholinergic fibers

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Sympathetic cholinergic fibres mediate sweating and some skeletal-muscle vasodilation during exercise, not the localised skin flare produced by injury or firm stimulation.

## answer_b
Antidromic vasodilator fibers

## explanation_b
Correct. When skin is injured or firmly stimulated, the red flare that spreads beyond the directly stimulated site is produced by the axon reflex: an impulse travelling centrally along one branch of a sensory afferent fibre can also travel antidromically, backwards, down neighbouring branches of that same afferent neuron that innervate adjacent skin. This antidromic conduction triggers local release of vasodilator neuropeptides from the nerve terminals themselves, producing vasodilation without involving any efferent autonomic outflow, which is what gives the flare its characteristic spread beyond the point of direct injury.

## answer_c
Parasympathetic fibers

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Parasympathetic vasodilator fibres supply only a limited set of vascular beds, such as salivary glands and genital erectile tissue, and are not the mechanism behind the skin's local flare response.

## answer_d
Circulating catecholamines

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Circulating catecholamines act systemically on distant vascular beds via the bloodstream; the flare is a local, peripherally-generated response confined to skin near the site of stimulation.

## subject

## title
Adrenal medulla hormone with dual vascular action

## question
Which hormone is released from the adrenal medulla and can cause both vasoconstriction and vasodilation?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Vasopressin

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Vasopressin is released from the posterior pituitary, not the adrenal medulla, and acts principally as a vasoconstrictor and water-retaining hormone rather than showing a dual, bed-dependent action.

## answer_b
Aldosterone

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Aldosterone is released from the adrenal cortex, not the medulla, and its principal action is renal sodium retention rather than a direct dual vascular effect.

## answer_c
Epinephrine

## explanation_c
Correct. Epinephrine, released from the adrenal medulla, is unusual among circulating vasoactive hormones because its net vascular effect depends on which adrenergic receptor subtype predominates in a given vascular bed. Acting on alpha-adrenergic receptors, which predominate in skin, splanchnic and renal vessels, epinephrine causes vasoconstriction, but in skeletal muscle, where beta2-adrenergic receptors predominate, it instead causes vasodilation. Norepinephrine, by contrast, acts almost exclusively on alpha receptors and is a vasoconstrictor essentially everywhere, which is why epinephrine, not norepinephrine, is the adrenal medulla hormone capable of both effects.

## answer_d
Renin

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Renin is released from the kidney's juxtaglomerular apparatus, not the adrenal medulla, and acts as an enzyme initiating the renin-angiotensin cascade rather than directly on vascular smooth muscle.

## question
Vasopressin (ADH) helps regulate blood pressure by:

## subject

## status

## answer_a
Causing vasodilation

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Vasopressin's vascular action, at higher concentrations, is vasoconstriction, not vasodilation.

## answer_b
Increasing sodium excretion

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Vasopressin decreases, rather than increases, renal water excretion, and its principal renal action concerns water rather than sodium handling.

## answer_c
Water retention

## explanation_c
Correct. A decreased extracellular fluid volume reduces the discharge of atrial low-pressure receptors, and this leads to increased secretion of vasopressin, which decreases renal water excretion, that is, promotes water retention, to limit any further fall in extracellular fluid volume and arterial pressure. This water-conserving action is vasopressin's principal contribution to blood-pressure regulation, working alongside the renin-angiotensin system and ANP as one of the three renal/endocrine mechanisms of long-term arterial pressure control.

## answer_d
Increasing heart rate

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Vasopressin's regulatory action on blood pressure is via water retention and, at higher levels, direct vasoconstriction, not via a direct effect on heart rate.
