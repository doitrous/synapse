## title
Cause of subacute endocarditis after dental extraction in prior rheumatic valve disease

## question
A 45-year-old with prior rheumatic mitral disease develops low-grade fever, weight loss and splinter hemorrhages after a dental extraction. The most likely causative organism is:

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Staphylococcus aureus

## explanation_a
Incorrect. S. aureus causes acute, rapidly destructive endocarditis, not this indolent low-grade picture following a dental procedure.

## answer_b
Coxsackie B virus

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Coxsackie B virus causes myocarditis, not valve-based endocarditis.

## answer_c
Viridans streptococci

## explanation_c
Correct. Streptococcus viridans, part of the normal oral flora, is the classic cause of subacute infective endocarditis following a dental procedure such as tooth extraction, when transient bacteraemia lets the organism seed a susceptible, already-damaged valve -- here, a valve scarred by prior rheumatic heart disease. The indolent course (low-grade fever, weight loss, splinter haemorrhages developing gradually) matches the lower virulence of Viridans streptococci compared with S. aureus. This is the textbook link between rheumatic valve disease, dental bacteraemia and subacute Viridans endocarditis.

## answer_d
Mycobacterium tuberculosis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Mycobacterium tuberculosis is not a typical cause of infective endocarditis following a dental procedure.

## title
Organism identified by non-hemolytic, catalase+/coagulase-, novobiocin-sensitive colonies on a prosthetic valve

## question
A patient with a prosthetic valve develops subacute bacteremia. The organism grows as non-hemolytic, catalase positive, coagulase negative colonies and is novobiocin-sensitive. What is the organism?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Staphylococcus saprophyticus

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Staphylococcus saprophyticus is distinguished from the other coagulase-negative staphylococci precisely by being novobiocin-RESISTANT, the opposite of this organism's novobiocin-sensitive result.

## answer_b
Staphylococcus epidermidis

## explanation_b
Correct. Catalase-positive, coagulase-negative cocci that are novobiocin-sensitive identify Staphylococcus epidermidis. The novobiocin test is the specific laboratory step that separates the coagulase-negative staphylococci from one another: S. saprophyticus is novobiocin-resistant, while S. epidermidis (and most other coagulase-negative staphylococci) are novobiocin-sensitive. On a prosthetic valve, S. epidermidis is a leading cause of indolent, subacute infection via glycocalyx (biofilm) formation on the device surface, fitting this patient's picture exactly.

## answer_c
Viridans streptococci

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Viridans streptococci are streptococci, not staphylococci, and would not be described as catalase-positive cocci (streptococci are catalase-negative).

## answer_d
Staphylococcus aureus

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Staphylococcus aureus is coagulase-POSITIVE, the opposite of this organism's coagulase-negative result.

## title
Organism causing subacute IE after wisdom-tooth extraction in a patient with congenital heart disease

## question
After extraction of a wisdom tooth, an 18-year-old male student he start to complain with a fever, cough, shortness of breath, Fatigue, aching joints, muscles and chills. He has a congenital heart disease that has been under control. Blood culture showed Gram positive cocci. On blood agar the organism produces alpha hemolytic colonies that are catalase negative. Which the following is the most likely organism causing the infection?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Staphylococcus aureus

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Staphylococcus aureus is catalase-POSITIVE, the opposite of this catalase-negative culture result, and it is a cluster-forming coccus, not the alpha-haemolytic streptococcal pattern described.

## answer_b
Staphylococcus epidermidis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Staphylococcus epidermidis is also catalase-positive, not catalase-negative, so it does not fit this culture result either.

## answer_c
Streptococcus pneumoniae

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Streptococcus pneumoniae is alpha-haemolytic and catalase-negative like Viridans, but it is not the classic organism linked to dental-procedure endocarditis in congenital heart disease, and it is optochin-sensitive and bile-soluble, distinguishing it from Viridans.

## answer_d
Streptococcus viridians

## explanation_d
Correct. Streptococcus viridans, part of normal oral flora, is the classic cause of infective endocarditis following a dental procedure such as wisdom-tooth extraction, and it matches this culture picture exactly: catalase-negative (all streptococci are), alpha-haemolytic on blood agar. A pre-existing congenital heart disease, like rheumatic valve disease, provides the abnormal valve surface that a transient dental bacteraemia can seed. The subacute onset (fever, fatigue, joint and muscle aches developing after the procedure) fits the lower-virulence, indolent course typical of Viridans streptococcal endocarditis.

## answer_e
Enterococcus faecalis

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Enterococcus faecalis is more typically linked to genitourinary or GI-procedure bacteraemia, not a dental extraction, and it is not the classic organism in this vignette.

## title
Long-term prophylaxis to prevent recurrent rheumatic fever attacks

## question
A patient with history of rheumatic fever is asking about preventing recurrent attacks. The most appropriate long-term prophylaxis is:

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Monthly long-acting penicillin injections

## explanation_a
Correct. Benzathine penicillin, given as a monthly long-acting intramuscular injection, is the standard secondary prophylaxis used to prevent recurrent attacks of rheumatic fever. Because each further streptococcal pharyngitis risks triggering another bout of immune-mediated cardiac damage, sustained antibiotic levels between doses are needed to prevent the organism from re-establishing itself in the throat; the long-acting depot formulation maintains this cover for about a month per injection, which is why it is dosed monthly rather than daily. This regimen is continued for years, often into adulthood, particularly when carditis has occurred.

## answer_b
Daily oral amoxicillin forever

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Daily oral amoxicillin is not the standard secondary-prophylaxis regimen for rheumatic fever; the long-acting monthly injectable penicillin regimen is preferred for reliable adherence and sustained cover.

## answer_c
No prophylaxis necessary after first attack

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Prophylaxis is very much necessary after a first attack, since recurrent rheumatic fever carries a high risk of cumulative, worsening cardiac damage.

## answer_d
Single dose erythromycin yearly

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A single yearly dose of erythromycin would leave long stretches without adequate antimicrobial cover against recurrent streptococcal infection, unlike the sustained monthly penicillin regimen.

## title
Long-term RF prophylaxis in a penicillin-allergic patient with recurrent pharyngitis

## question
A 16-year-old boy with a history of acute rheumatic fever complicated by mild mitral regurgitation presents to the clinic for follow-up. He reports recurrent episodes of sore throat over the past year. His medical history is significant for a documented immediate hypersensitivity reaction to penicillin. Physical examination is unremarkable except for a soft holosystolic murmur at the apex. Which of the following is the most appropriate long-term management to prevent recurrent rheumatic fever and progressive cardiac damage?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Monthly intramuscular benzathine penicillin

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Benzathine penicillin is the standard first-line prophylaxis, but this patient has a documented immediate (type I) hypersensitivity reaction to penicillin, making it contraindicated here.

## answer_b
Oral erythromycin for secondary prophylaxis

## explanation_b
Correct. Oral erythromycin is the recommended alternative for long-term secondary prophylaxis against recurrent rheumatic fever in a patient with a documented penicillin allergy. Continuous prophylaxis, rather than treatment only during symptomatic episodes, is needed because streptococcal pharyngitis can be subclinical and still trigger a further rheumatic attack, and this patient already has established mild mitral regurgitation that further attacks would worsen. Substituting erythromycin preserves the same principle of sustained antimicrobial cover against group A Streptococcus while avoiding the contraindicated drug.

## answer_c
Immediate surgical replacement of the mitral valve

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Mild mitral regurgitation on examination does not itself warrant immediate surgical valve replacement; the priority here is preventing further rheumatic damage with prophylaxis, not surgery.

## answer_d
Short courses of antibiotics only during symptomatic pharyngitis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Treating only symptomatic episodes would miss subclinical streptococcal infections capable of triggering another rheumatic attack, which is why continuous, not intermittent, prophylaxis is required.

## title
Organism identified by poor dental hygiene, rheumatic heart disease and alpha-hemolytic, bile-insoluble, optochin-resistant colonies

## question
A patient with poor dental hygiene and a history of rheumatic heart disease presents with low-grade fever, night sweats, and splinter hemorrhages. Blood cultures show alpha-hemolytic colonies that are bile-insoluble and optochin-resistant. Which organism is most likely responsible?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Staphylococcus aureus

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Staphylococcus aureus is not alpha-haemolytic in this classic streptococcal pattern, and it produces an acute, not this subacute, indolent picture.

## answer_b
Streptococcus pneumoniae

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Streptococcus pneumoniae is alpha-haemolytic but is bile-SOLUBLE and optochin-SENSITIVE, the reverse of the culture results given here.

## answer_c
Viridans streptococci

## explanation_c
Correct. Streptococcus viridans, part of the normal oral flora, is the classic cause of subacute infective endocarditis in a patient with poor dental hygiene and a pre-existing damaged valve such as rheumatic heart disease. The laboratory clues confirm it: Viridans streptococci are alpha-haemolytic, bile-insoluble and optochin-resistant, the pattern that distinguishes them from Streptococcus pneumoniae (bile-soluble, optochin-sensitive). The indolent course -- low-grade fever, night sweats and splinter haemorrhages -- matches the lower virulence of Viridans streptococci compared with acute pathogens such as S. aureus.

## answer_d
Enterococcus faecalis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Enterococcus faecalis is more typically linked to genitourinary or GI-procedure bacteraemia and does not match this optochin-resistant, bile-insoluble alpha-haemolytic laboratory pattern as the classic dental-hygiene-associated organism.

## title
Organism that is normal flora of the human oropharynx

## question
Which of the following organisms is a normal flora of human oropharynx?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Candida albicans

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Candida albicans can be found in the oral cavity opportunistically but is not considered a defining member of normal oropharyngeal bacterial flora in this context.

## answer_b
Streptococcus pyogenes

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Streptococcus pyogenes is a pathogen that can transiently colonise or infect the pharynx (causing streptococcal pharyngitis); it is not itself part of normal resident flora.

## answer_c
Viridans Streptococci

## explanation_c
Correct. Streptococcus viridans, part of the normal oral flora, resides harmlessly in the oropharynx of essentially everyone, contributing to the local microbial balance without causing disease under normal conditions. It becomes clinically significant only when transient bacteraemia -- classically from a dental procedure -- delivers it to an already-damaged or abnormal heart valve, where it can then cause subacute infective endocarditis. This dual identity, harmless commensal in the mouth versus classic cause of subacute endocarditis once in the bloodstream, is the key teaching point linking oral flora to cardiovascular disease.

## answer_d
Mycoplasma pneumoniae

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Mycoplasma pneumoniae is a respiratory pathogen causing atypical pneumonia, not a member of normal oropharyngeal flora.
