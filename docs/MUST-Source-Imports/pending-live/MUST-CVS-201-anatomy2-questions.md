## title
Origin of the posterior interventricular branch

## question
The posterior interventricular branch is a branch of:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Left coronary artery

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The left coronary artery's terminal branches are the anterior interventricular and circumflex arteries; the posterior interventricular artery is not among them.

## answer_b
Right coronary artery

## explanation_b
Correct. In the majority (right-dominant) pattern, the right coronary artery continues around the heart's inferior border and turns into the posterior coronary sulcus to give off the posterior (inferior) interventricular artery, which descends in the posterior interventricular groove to supply the posterior third of the septum and anastomose with the anterior interventricular artery. This vessel typically also supplies the AV node and bundle as it passes near them, which is why right coronary artery obstruction here can produce both an inferior infarct and conduction block. Because right coronary dominance is the commonest pattern, the posterior interventricular artery is properly described as a right coronary artery branch unless the heart is left-dominant.

## answer_c
Circumflex artery

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The circumflex artery is the left coronary artery's other terminal branch, running towards the posterior surface, but it does not itself give rise to the posterior interventricular artery.

## answer_d
Left marginal artery

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The left marginal artery is a branch of the circumflex artery running along the heart's rounded left margin, an entirely separate vessel from the posterior interventricular artery.

Fetal remnant forming the oblique vein of the left atrium

## question
Which vessel is a remnant of the left common cardinal vein of the fetus?

## subject
cvs

## status

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The great cardiac vein is the adult continuation of the anterior cardiac venous drainage, not a fetal common cardinal vein remnant.

## answer_b
Oblique vein of left atrium

## explanation_b
Correct. In the embryo, the sinus venosus's left horn receives a left common cardinal vein among its tributaries; as the sinus venosus is progressively drawn into the developing right atrium and its left horn shrinks to become the coronary sinus, the vein draining into that shrinking horn persists as the oblique vein of the left atrium — a small, functionally minor vessel descending on the back of the left atrium to join the coronary sinus near its left end. Its small calibre and clinical insignificance in the adult are themselves a clue to its origin as the remnant of a once-larger fetal channel that lost its function once the left horn stopped being a major venous return route. This is a classic embryology-to-adult-anatomy correlation, tested because the vessel's adult appearance gives no obvious hint of its fetal significance.

Incorrect. The small cardiac vein develops alongside the right marginal artery on the sternocostal surface, unrelated to the common cardinal venous system.

Venae cordis minimi are small veins that drain directly into:

## title
Anatomical site of the SA node

## question
What is the anatomical site of the SA node?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Lower part of interatrial septum

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The lower part of the interatrial septum is where the AV node lies, not the SA node.

## answer_b
Upper part of sulcus terminalis

## explanation_b
Correct. The sino-atrial node sits in the right atrial wall at the upper end of the sulcus terminalis, the shallow external groove marking where the smooth sinus venarum meets the pectinate right atrial wall, close to the superior vena cava opening. Its position at this surface landmark is a standard way anatomists and surgeons locate the node without seeing it directly through the atrial wall, much as the coronary sinus orifice is used to locate the nearby AV node. Because the sulcus terminalis corresponds internally to the crista terminalis, the node's site can also be described relative to that internal ridge.

If the primitive heart tube bends to the left instead of its normal rightward direction during cardiac looping, the result is dextrocardia, a mirror-image reversal of the heart's own chambers and vessels; when this cardiac mirror-imaging is accompanied by a corresponding complete mirror-image reversal of the abdominal viscera as well (so that, for example, the liver lies on the left and the spleen on the right), the combined whole-body mirror-image condition is called situs inversus. Situs inversus is therefore best understood as dextrocardia occurring as part of a global, whole-body laterality reversal, rather than as an isolated cardiac finding — a distinction that matters clinically because isolated dextrocardia (without visceral mirroring) carries a much higher rate of associated structural heart defects than situs inversus, where the mirrored organ arrangement is otherwise internally consistent. This extension from the purely cardiac looping mechanism taught for dextrocardia to the whole-body term "situs inversus" is the added layer this question tests beyond the basic looping fact.

## answer_b
Situs solitus

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Situs solitus is the term for the normal, non-mirrored arrangement of the thoracic and abdominal organs, the opposite of what is being described.

## answer_c
Mesocardia

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Mesocardia describes a heart positioned in the midline rather than displaced fully to either side; it is not the term for a complete mirror-image reversal of the heart and abdominal organs together.

## answer_d
Levocardia

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Levocardia simply describes a heart in its normal left-sided position (which can occur with or without abnormal visceral arrangement elsewhere), not a mirror-image reversal.

## question

## subject

## answer_a
Septum primum and septum secundum

## explanation_a
Correct. The foramen ovale is the oblique passage that persists between the free, mobile edge of septum primum on one side and the firm lower margin of septum secundum on the other, once septum secundum has grown down to overlap the earlier ostium secundum opening in septum primum. This two-septum, overlapping-flap arrangement is exactly what allows the foramen ovale to function as a one-way valve in fetal life — blood can push septum primum away from septum secundum to flow right-to-left, but back-flow presses the two septa together and seals the gap — and it is also why postnatal pressure reversal, rather than any new tissue growth, is enough to close it functionally at birth. Locating the foramen ovale precisely between these two named septa, rather than vaguely "in the atrial septum," is the level of detail this question is testing.

Incorrect. The muscular and membranous parts of the interventricular septum are ventricular structures; the foramen ovale is an atrial-level structure, unrelated to that boundary.

## title
Vessel carrying mixed blood to the fetal lower body

## question
Which structure carries mixed blood to the lower body in the fetus?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Ascending aorta

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The ascending aorta, receiving blood almost entirely from the left ventricle (itself filled mainly with better-oxygenated blood that crossed the foramen ovale), carries the fetus's most highly oxygenated blood, directed preferentially to the head, neck and upper limbs, not the "mixed" blood of the lower body.

## answer_b
Pulmonary trunk

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The pulmonary trunk carries deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle, most of which is diverted through the ductus arteriosus rather than reaching the still-non-functional fetal lungs.

## answer_c
Descending aorta

## explanation_c
Correct. Beyond the point where the ductus arteriosus empties its right-ventricular, poorly oxygenated blood into it, the descending aorta carries a mixture of that ductal blood together with the better-oxygenated blood that already passed through the aortic arch, so the descending aorta supplies the fetal lower body, and returns to the placenta via the umbilical arteries, with blood of intermediate, "mixed" oxygen saturation — lower than the ascending aorta's supply to the head and upper limbs. This head-to-lower-body oxygenation gradient in the fetus, higher above the ductus arteriosus and lower below it, is a distinctive feature of fetal circulation with no equivalent after birth, and it is thought to preferentially direct the best-oxygenated blood to the rapidly developing fetal brain. Recognising the descending aorta specifically as the "mixed blood" vessel, downstream of the ductus arteriosus's contribution, is the key mechanistic point this question is testing.

## answer_d
Ductus venosus

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The ductus venosus carries oxygenated umbilical venous blood from the placenta towards the IVC, upstream of the heart entirely, not blood destined for the lower body.
