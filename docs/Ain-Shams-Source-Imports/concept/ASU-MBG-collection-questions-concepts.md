## definition
Penetrance and expressivity are two separate ways a genotype can fail to produce a uniform, fully predictable phenotype, and this course's own item bank routinely builds one term as the distractor for the other. Penetrance is a yes/no, population-level measure: the proportion of individuals who carry a particular disease-causing genotype and show any detectable phenotype at all, whatever its severity. Complete penetrance means every carrier is affected to some degree; incomplete (reduced) penetrance means some carriers show no phenotype whatsoever despite carrying the genotype — which is exactly why a dominant condition can appear to "skip a generation" in a pedigree, without any new mutation or non-paternity being involved. Expressivity, by contrast, is a within-affected-individuals measure of severity or degree: among carriers who are affected, how mild or severe the phenotype is in each one. A single genotype can therefore show complete penetrance (everyone who carries it is affected) with highly variable expressivity (some of those affected are mildly affected, others severely), or incomplete penetrance with uniform expressivity among the subset who are affected — the two properties vary independently of one another.

## explicit_objective
Define penetrance as the proportion of a particular genotype's carriers who show any phenotype at all, and distinguish it from expressivity (the severity of the phenotype among those who are affected), recognising incomplete penetrance as the reason a dominant condition can appear to skip a generation.

## pitfalls
Confusing penetrance (does the phenotype appear at all, a population-level yes/no proportion) with expressivity (how severe the phenotype is among those who are affected) — the two terms are the standard distractor pair for one another in this course's item bank, and neither is the same thing as a brand-new mutation or genomic imprinting, which are separate mechanisms tested alongside them in the same option lists.

## definition
Eukaryotic gene expression can be regulated at the translational level itself, downstream of transcription and mRNA processing, without changing the mRNA's own sequence, splicing or stability. One standard mechanism for this is translational repression by a sequence-specific regulatory protein: the protein binds directly to the 5' end of the target mRNA — typically at or near the 5' cap or the 5' untranslated region (5'-UTR) upstream of the start codon — and this binding physically blocks the small ribosomal subunit and initiation factors from loading onto the message, preventing translation initiation. Because the mRNA itself is left structurally intact (it is neither degraded nor spliced differently), the cell can rapidly de-repress translation later simply by removing or inactivating the bound protein, without needing to synthesise a fresh transcript. This is one of five broad levels at which eukaryotic gene expression is regulated — pre-transcriptional, transcriptional (cis-/trans-acting elements), post-transcriptional (capping, splicing, polyadenylation), translational (this mechanism, alongside initiation-factor phosphorylation), and post-translational — and this course's own item bank tests recognising which named mechanism belongs at which of these five levels.

## explicit_objective
Identify a regulatory protein binding the 5' end of an mRNA (its cap region or 5'-UTR) as a translational-level mechanism that inhibits translation by blocking ribosome loading, and place this correctly among eukaryotic gene expression's other regulatory levels (pre-transcriptional, transcriptional, post-transcriptional, post-translational).

## pitfalls
Assuming any mechanism involving mRNA automatically counts as post-transcriptional regulation — a protein binding an already fully processed, mature mRNA to block its translation is a translational-level event, not a post-transcriptional processing event (which covers capping, splicing and polyadenylation of the primary transcript itself). Also confusing this mechanism with mRNA-destabilising regulation (which would shorten the message's half-life) or with transcriptional silencing (cis-/trans-acting elements, DNA methylation, chromatin remodelling), which act earlier in the pathway and are separately tested in the same item.
