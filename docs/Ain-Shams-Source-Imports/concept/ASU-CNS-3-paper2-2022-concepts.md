## label
Rupture of a congenital berry (saccular) aneurysm at the circle of Willis is the classic cause of subarachnoid haemorrhage

## id
CON-NEU-A5EE0C82D04EFB

## canonical_key
circleofwillis.berry-aneurysm.subarachnoid-haemorrhage

## definition
Congenital (berry/saccular) aneurysms form at branch points of the circle of Willis, most often at the anterior communicating artery, the origin of the posterior communicating artery from the internal carotid, or the middle cerebral artery bifurcation, where the vessel wall's muscular and elastic layers are congenitally deficient. Their rupture is the leading cause of spontaneous (non-traumatic) subarachnoid haemorrhage, classically presenting as a sudden, severe ("thunderclap") headache, sometimes precipitated by exertion, with blood found in the cerebrospinal fluid on lumbar puncture. This differs from Charcot-Bouchard microaneurysms, which form on small penetrating vessels deep within the brain parenchyma as a consequence of chronic hypertension and rupture to cause intracerebral, not subarachnoid, haemorrhage.

## explicit_objective
State that rupture of a berry aneurysm at the circle of Willis is the classic cause of subarachnoid haemorrhage.

## label
Decorticate posturing (bilateral upper-limb flexion with lower-limb extension) localises a lesion above the level of the red nucleus

## id
CON-NEU-D194F6586756F2

## canonical_key
decorticateposturing.localisation.lesion-above-red-nucleus

## definition
Decorticate (decortication) posturing — bilateral flexion and adduction of the upper limbs at the elbow and wrist with extension of the lower limbs — results from a severe lesion at or above the level of the red nucleus (e.g. in the cerebral hemispheres, internal capsule or upper midbrain) that interrupts the corticospinal and corticorubral pathways while sparing the rubrospinal tract, which continues to drive upper-limb flexor tone. This contrasts with decerebrate (decerebration) posturing — extension of all four limbs — which results from a lower, pontine-level lesion that additionally interrupts the rubrospinal tract itself, leaving only the extensor-promoting vestibulospinal and pontine reticulospinal tracts active; decerebrate posturing therefore indicates a more caudal, generally worse-prognosis lesion than decorticate posturing.

## explicit_objective
State that decorticate posturing (upper-limb flexion, lower-limb extension) localises a lesion above the red nucleus, in contrast to the more caudal lesion of decerebrate posturing.

## id

## canonical_key
pilocyticastrocytoma.pathology.rosenthal-fibres-childhood-cerebellar

## definition
Pilocytic astrocytoma (WHO grade 1) is the most common paediatric brain tumour and characteristically arises in the cerebellum, presenting with signs of raised intracranial pressure and cerebellar dysfunction (ataxia, dysmetria) in a child. Histologically it shows a biphasic pattern of compact, elongated ("hair-like", pilocytic) bipolar cells alternating with looser microcystic areas, and its hallmark feature is the Rosenthal fibre — an eosinophilic, corkscrew-shaped, degenerating astrocytic process. Unlike glioblastoma, it is a slow-growing, well-circumscribed tumour with an excellent prognosis after surgical resection.

## label
The stria terminalis connects the amygdala with the hypothalamus

## id
CON-NEU-110FA281F7FBFF

## canonical_key
striaterminalis.pathway.amygdala-hypothalamus-connection

## definition
The stria terminalis is a thin, C-shaped fibre bundle that follows the curve of the lateral ventricle from the amygdala, in the temporal lobe, forward to the hypothalamus (chiefly its anterior and ventromedial nuclei) and the septal area, carrying the amygdala's principal efferent output to these targets. It is the amygdala's own major projection pathway, distinct from the Papez circuit's hippocampus-fornix-mammillary body-cingulate loop, and mediates the autonomic and neuroendocrine (hypothalamic) components of emotional and fear responses generated in the amygdala.

## id

## canonical_key
alzheimerdisease.pathology.neuritic-plaques-neurofibrillary-tangles

## definition
Alzheimer disease, the most common cause of dementia, is characterised histologically by two hallmark lesions: extracellular neuritic (senile) plaques, composed of a core of beta-amyloid protein surrounded by degenerating neuronal processes, and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles, composed of paired helical filaments of hyperphosphorylated tau protein. These lesions accumulate progressively in the hippocampus, entorhinal cortex and neocortex, driving progressive cortical atrophy and the disease's characteristic short-term memory loss and cognitive decline.
