## definition
RNA differs from DNA and its own three main species differ from one another in several superlative facts that are commonly tested together. First, of the three major RNA types, transfer RNA (tRNA) carries by far the highest percentage of chemically modified nucleotides — bases such as dihydrouridine, pseudouridine, inosine and ribothymidine are added post-transcriptionally, concentrated in tRNA's D-arm, anticodon arm and TψC arm, supporting its compact cloverleaf fold and precise codon reading. Second, messenger RNA (mRNA) is the most heterogeneous RNA species in the cell — its unprocessed nuclear precursor was historically named "heterogeneous nuclear RNA" (hnRNA) for exactly this reason — because every different protein-coding gene produces its own uniquely sequenced and uniquely sized transcript, unlike the comparatively few, highly conserved rRNA and tRNA sequences. Third, a human cell needs at least roughly 20 distinct tRNA species, one dedicated (via its own aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase) to charging each of the 20 standard amino acids, even though the genetic code's 61 sense codons and wobble-position pairing let a single tRNA species often recognise more than one codon. Finally, of the RNA species routinely listed in an introductory course — rRNA, tRNA, small nuclear RNA (snRNA) and long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) — only mRNA is translated into protein; the rest are classified as non-coding RNA, meaning they act directly as RNA molecules (structural, catalytic, splicing or regulatory) rather than as a template read out into an amino acid sequence.

## explicit_objective
State that tRNA has the highest percentage of modified nucleotides among RNA types, that mRNA is the most heterogeneous ("hnRNA") RNA species, that a human cell needs at least ~20 tRNA species (one per amino acid), and that mRNA is the one coding RNA among rRNA/tRNA/snRNA/lncRNA.

## pitfalls
Confusing "most heterogeneous" (greatest sequence diversity, i.e. mRNA) with "most abundant" (rRNA, by mass) or "most modified" (tRNA, by modified-nucleotide percentage) — three different superlatives about three different RNA types, routinely built as distractors for one another. Also assuming the "except" framing of the coding/non-coding question is testing whether tRNA is non-coding (it is, uncontroversially) rather than testing whether the student can correctly spot mRNA as the single coding RNA in the list.

## definition
RNA's ribose sugar carries a 2'-hydroxyl (2'-OH) group that DNA's deoxyribose lacks. Under alkaline conditions this 2'-OH attacks the adjacent phosphodiester bond intramolecularly, cleaving the RNA backbone — which is why RNA is described as alkali-labile, while DNA, lacking this group, resists alkaline hydrolysis and is comparatively alkali-stable. RNA also differs from DNA in being generally shorter and single-stranded — though a single RNA strand routinely folds back on itself to form local intra-strand (secondary-structure) hydrogen bonds, exactly as seen in tRNA's cloverleaf and rRNA's stem-loops — and in using uracil in place of thymine as the pyrimidine that pairs with adenine.

## explicit_objective
State that RNA, not DNA, is alkali-labile because of its ribose 2'-OH group, and list this alongside RNA's other standard distinguishing properties versus DNA: shorter length, single-strandedness with intra-strand base pairing, and uracil instead of thymine.

## pitfalls
Assuming "single-stranded" means RNA cannot have any base pairing at all — a single RNA strand still folds back on itself to hydrogen-bond intra-strand (secondary structure), which is exactly how tRNA's cloverleaf and rRNA's functional shapes form; single-stranded refers to there being one covalent backbone, not the absence of hydrogen bonding.
