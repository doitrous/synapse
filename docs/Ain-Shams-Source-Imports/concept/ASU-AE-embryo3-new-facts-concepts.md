CON-DEV-ASU-AE-ADRENAL-ORIGIN

## label
The adrenal cortex is mesodermal; the adrenal medulla is from neural crest

## canonical_key
adrenal-gland.cortex-mesoderm-medulla-neural-crest

## definition
The adrenal (suprarenal) gland has two embryologically unrelated parts fused into one organ. The cortex arises from mesodermal cells of the posterior abdominal wall that condense on either side of the root of the dorsal mesentery. The medulla arises separately, from neural crest cells that migrate in and differentiate into chromaffin cells -- the same neural-crest lineage that gives sympathetic ganglia, melanocytes, Schwann cells and the sensory ganglia elsewhere in the body. Because the two parts come from different germ-layer lineages, a question that lists the adrenal cortex among ectodermal derivatives, or the adrenal medulla among mesodermal derivatives, is testing exactly this split.

## explicit_objective
State that the adrenal cortex is mesodermal and the adrenal medulla is neural-crest (ectodermal-lineage) in origin, and recognise questions that swap the two.

# Item

## id
CON-DEV-ASU-AE-TERATOGEN-PRINCIPLES

## label
Teratogens do the most damage in the first trimester, and not every drug given in pregnancy crosses the placenta

## canonical_key
teratogen.critical-period-first-trimester-and-placental-transfer

## definition
Teratogenic susceptibility follows a critical-period pattern. Exposure before implantation (roughly the first two weeks) tends to be all-or-none: the embryo is either lost or develops unaffected, because at that stage cells are still largely undifferentiated and totipotent-like. The embryonic period (weeks 3 to 8, the bulk of the first trimester, when the organ systems are being laid down) is the period of greatest structural vulnerability -- this is when a teratogen produces the most severe and varied congenital malformations, because different organs each have their own narrow window of peak sensitivity within it. Exposure during the later fetal period mostly causes growth restriction or minor functional/physiological deficits rather than major structural malformation, because the organs are already formed by then. Not every drug reaches the embryo or fetus in the first place: a drug must cross the placenta to have any teratogenic potential, and molecular size is one determinant of that -- heparin is the standard teaching example of a drug that, because of its large molecular size, does not cross the placenta and is therefore considered safe (non-teratogenic) in pregnancy, unlike its comparator warfarin, which does cross and is a recognised teratogen.

## explicit_objective
State that the first trimester (embryonic period) is the period of greatest teratogenic risk, and that a drug's inability to cross the placenta (for example heparin, by virtue of its size) makes it non-teratogenic regardless of that timing.

## id
CON-DEV-ASU-AE-POLYHYDRAMNIOS-GI

## label
Polyhydramnios classically points to a fetal GI/esophageal atresia

## canonical_key
polyhydramnios.gi-esophageal-atresia-cause

## definition
Polyhydramnios is an abnormally increased amniotic fluid volume. Because fetal swallowing of amniotic fluid is one of the main routes by which fluid is normally removed from the amniotic cavity (balancing fetal urine production, the main route by which it is added), any structural obstruction that prevents the fetus from swallowing and processing that fluid lets it accumulate. Esophageal atresia -- a blind-ending esophagus -- is the classic example: the fetus cannot swallow at all, so fluid builds up. Other upper gastrointestinal obstructions (for example duodenal atresia) produce the same effect. This is the functional mirror image of oligohydramnios, which classically points to reduced fetal urine output (for example from renal agenesis) rather than reduced fetal swallowing.

## explicit_objective
Link polyhydramnios to a fetus that cannot swallow amniotic fluid normally, and name esophageal/GI atresia as the classic structural cause.
