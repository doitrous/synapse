# Item

## label
The ventral posteromedial nucleus (VPM) of the thalamus receives the trigeminal lemniscus

## id
CON-NEU-AC256ED3CCDC2A

## canonical_key
ventralposteromedialnucleus.receives.trigeminal-lemniscus

## definition
The trigeminal lemniscus (the combined trigeminothalamic pathway) carries pain, temperature and touch information from the face and oral cavity, relayed through the principal sensory and spinal trigeminal nuclei, crosses the midline in the pons and medulla, and ascends to terminate in the ventral posteromedial (VPM) nucleus of the thalamus. VPM is the facial counterpart of the ventral posterolateral (VPL) nucleus, which receives the medial lemniscus and spinothalamic tract carrying the equivalent modalities from the body. From VPM, third-order neurons project through the posterior limb of the internal capsule to the face representation of the primary somatosensory cortex in the postcentral gyrus.

## explicit_objective
Identify the ventral posteromedial (VPM) nucleus of the thalamus as the destination of the trigeminal lemniscus.

## label
The Papez circuit mediates the prolongation and elaboration of emotional experience

## id
CON-NEU-74E50820464FCE

## canonical_key
papezcircuit.function.prolongs-elaborates-emotional-experience

## definition
The Papez circuit is a closed neural loop — hippocampus to fornix to mammillary bodies to the anterior thalamic nucleus via the mammillothalamic tract to the cingulate gyrus and back to the hippocampus via the cingulum — first proposed by James Papez as the anatomical substrate of emotion. Its principal physiological role is not to generate an emotional response from scratch (a task more dependent on the amygdala) but to sustain, elaborate and give lasting, consciously felt quality to an emotion once it has begun, binding it to memory. Lesions affecting circuit components, such as mammillary body damage in Korsakoff syndrome, disrupt this link between emotion and memory rather than abolishing emotional reactivity outright.

## explicit_objective
Attribute prolongation and conscious elaboration of an ongoing emotional experience to the Papez circuit.

The third pharyngeal pouch gives rise to the inferior parathyroid glands and the thymus

## id
CON-NEU-A0A833E5DEEF36

## canonical_key
thirdpharyngealpouch.derivatives.inferior-parathyroid-and-thymus

## definition
The third pharyngeal pouch has dorsal and ventral wings: the dorsal wing forms the inferior parathyroid glands, and the ventral wing forms the thymus. The third-pouch parathyroid primordium migrates further caudally than the fourth pouch's does, so the inferior parathyroid glands end up more variable in final position and are occasionally pulled down with the descending thymus into the superior mediastinum, the classic explanation for ectopic mediastinal parathyroid tissue. Sharing a pouch of origin also explains why thymic and inferior parathyroid tissue are occasionally found together in developmental anomalies such as DiGeorge syndrome.

## canonical_key

## definition
The metencephalon, the rostral part of the embryonic hindbrain, gives rise to both the pons and the cerebellum. The cerebellum develops specifically from the rhombic lips, paired dorsolateral thickenings of the metencephalon's alar plate, which fuse in the midline over the roof of the fourth ventricle. Neuroblasts migrating from the rhombic lip form the cerebellar cortex and deep cerebellar nuclei, and, via a transient external germinal (granular) layer, give rise to the numerous granule cells characteristic of the mature cerebellum.

## id
CON-NEU-3078C4E4D17C32

## canonical_key
facerecognition.lateralisation.representational-nondominant-hemisphere

## definition
Cerebral hemispheric specialisation assigns language, calculation and sequential/analytic tasks chiefly to the dominant (usually left) hemisphere, while the non-dominant hemisphere — often termed the 'representational' hemisphere — specialises in visuospatial and holistic processing tasks, including facial recognition, spatial orientation, and musical/emotional prosody. Damage to the non-dominant parietal and temporal-occipital association cortex characteristically produces prosopagnosia (inability to recognise familiar faces) and other visuospatial deficits, even though language remains intact. This lateralisation is not absolute but reflects a strong statistical bias, particularly well documented in right-handed individuals whose left hemisphere is dominant for language.

## label
Naming a seen object depends on a pathway from the visual cortex through Wernicke's area and Broca's area to the primary motor cortex

## id
CON-NEU-8CE1ADBC086629

## canonical_key
objectnaming.pathway.visualcortex-wernicke-broca-motorcortex

## definition
Naming an object that has just been seen requires the primary visual cortex to pass the visual percept, via visual association areas and the angular gyrus, to Wernicke's area in the posterior superior temporal gyrus, where the visual image is matched to its corresponding word. That word-formulation output travels via the arcuate fasciculus to Broca's area in the inferior frontal gyrus, which programs the sequence of articulatory movements, and finally to the primary motor cortex, which executes the movements of the lips, tongue and larynx via the corticobulbar tract. A lesion at any point along this chain produces a different, localisable deficit — from visual object agnosia to Wernicke's (receptive) or Broca's (expressive) aphasia to dysarthria.

## explicit_objective
Sequence the pathway from seeing an object to naming it aloud: visual cortex, Wernicke's area, Broca's area, primary motor cortex.

## label
Flumazenil is the specific antidote for midazolam (benzodiazepine) overdose

## id
CON-NEU-56820521AB4DA8

## canonical_key
midazolam.antidote.flumazenil

## definition
Midazolam, like other benzodiazepines, acts as a positive allosteric modulator at the GABA-A receptor, increasing the frequency of chloride channel opening in response to GABA. Flumazenil is a competitive antagonist at the benzodiazepine binding site of the GABA-A receptor: it displaces midazolam and other benzodiazepines from the receptor without itself activating it, rapidly reversing sedation, respiratory depression and anaesthetic effects. Because flumazenil's half-life is shorter than that of most benzodiazepines, re-sedation can occur once it wears off, so patients require monitoring after reversal, and flumazenil can precipitate seizures in benzodiazepine-dependent patients.

## explicit_objective
Identify flumazenil as the receptor-competitive antidote that reverses midazolam and other benzodiazepine effects.

## definition
The first pharyngeal (mandibular) arch gives rise to eight striated muscles: the four muscles of mastication (temporalis, masseter, medial pterygoid, lateral pterygoid) and four further muscles — tensor tympani, tensor veli palatini, mylohyoid, and the anterior belly of digastric. Because muscles derived from a pharyngeal arch retain the nerve supply of that arch regardless of where they migrate to in the adult, all eight of these muscles are supplied by the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve (CN V3), the first arch's nerve. This shared nerve supply is a reliable way to recall the full, otherwise unrelated-seeming, list of first-arch muscle derivatives.

## definition
Varenicline is a partial agonist at the alpha4beta2 subtype of the neuronal nicotinic acetylcholine receptor, the principal receptor subtype mediating nicotine's rewarding, dopamine-releasing effect in the mesolimbic pathway. As a partial agonist, varenicline provides enough receptor stimulation to relieve nicotine craving and withdrawal symptoms, while its competitive occupancy of the receptor blocks nicotine from binding and producing its full rewarding effect if the patient smokes, blunting the reinforcement of relapse. Common adverse effects include nausea, vivid dreams, and a labelled risk of neuropsychiatric symptoms that warrants monitoring during a smoking-cessation course.

Disulfiram irreversibly inhibits aldehyde dehydrogenase, the enzyme that normally oxidises acetaldehyde (the first oxidation product of ethanol) to acetic acid. If a patient taking disulfiram drinks alcohol, acetaldehyde accumulates to toxic levels, producing an unpleasant disulfiram-ethanol reaction — flushing, throbbing headache, nausea, vomiting, palpitations and hypotension — that is intended to deter further drinking as part of alcohol use disorder management. Because this reaction can be severe or even dangerous in patients with cardiac disease, disulfiram requires patient selection, informed consent, and avoidance of alcohol in any form, including some medications and foods that contain it.

## label
Sodium bicarbonate is added to local anaesthetic solution to decrease its acidity and increase its activity

## id
CON-NEU-ADDDF80E33C8ED

## canonical_key
sodiumbicarbonate.additive.alkalinizes-local-anesthetic-increases-activity

## definition
Commercial local anaesthetic solutions are formulated at an acidic pH to keep the drug soluble and stable, but this leaves most of the anaesthetic molecules in their ionised (charged), poorly lipid-soluble form, which crosses the nerve sheath and axonal membrane slowly. Adding sodium bicarbonate raises the pH of the solution towards physiological, shifting more of the weak-base local anaesthetic into its unionised (uncharged), lipid-soluble form, which diffuses through the nerve membrane faster to reach its intracellular binding site on the voltage-gated sodium channel. The practical result of bicarbonate alkalinization is a faster onset of anaesthesia and less injection-site discomfort, without changing the eventual depth or duration of the block once equilibrium is reached.

Explain how adding sodium bicarbonate to local anaesthetic solution decreases acidity and increases the drug's activity by favouring its unionised, lipid-soluble form.

## label
Infected or repeatedly inflamed tissue is more acidic, which reduces local anaesthetic activity

## id
CON-NEU-8F689B62E470F4

## canonical_key
infectedtissue.acidity.reduces-local-anesthetic-activity

## definition
Infected or chronically inflamed tissue has a lower (more acidic) extracellular pH than healthy tissue, a consequence of local metabolic byproducts, hypoxia, and the inflammatory response. Because local anaesthetics are weak bases whose proportion of unionised, lipid-soluble drug depends on the surrounding pH relative to their pKa, this acidic extracellular environment shifts more of the drug into its ionised, poorly diffusible form, so less anaesthetic penetrates the nerve sheath to reach its site of action inside the axon. This is the pharmacological basis for the common clinical observation that local anaesthesia is less effective, or fails outright, when injected into an infected or abscessed area, sometimes requiring a regional nerve block placed outside the acidic field instead.

## definition
Imipramine, a tricyclic antidepressant, has a well-established non-psychiatric use in childhood nocturnal enuresis, historically one of the most common indications for prescribing it in paediatrics before behavioural and desmopressin-based approaches became first line. Its beneficial effect in enuresis is thought to combine its anticholinergic action (increasing bladder capacity, relaxing detrusor tone) with an effect on sleep architecture that reduces the depth of sleep during the hours the bladder would otherwise need to signal fullness. Because tricyclic antidepressants carry cardiotoxicity and overdose risk, particularly relevant in children, imipramine for enuresis is now generally reserved for cases refractory to first-line measures.

Duloxetine, a serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (SNRI), has a non-psychiatric application in stress urinary incontinence, exploiting its action on the sacral spinal cord's Onuf's nucleus, whose motor neurons innervate the urethral striated sphincter. # Item

## label
The anterior cerebral artery's cortical branches supply the medial hemispheric surface (except the occipital lobe), a strip of the superolateral surface, and part of the orbital surface

## id
CON-NEU-B1D7785BB3B753

## canonical_key
anteriorcerebralartery.corticalterritory.medial-lateral-strip-orbital

## definition
The anterior cerebral artery's cortical branches supply almost the entire medial surface of the cerebral hemisphere from the frontal to the parietal lobe, sparing only the occipital lobe, which is instead supplied by the posterior cerebral artery. These branches also wrap over the superomedial margin to supply a roughly one-inch-wide strip of the adjoining superolateral (convexity) surface, and supply part of the orbital (inferior frontal) surface via the medial orbital branches. This territory explains the classic clinical picture of an ACA infarct: contralateral leg-predominant weakness and sensory loss (from the medial surface's leg/foot motor and sensory homunculus), sparing the face and hand, which lie in the middle cerebral artery's lateral convexity territory instead.

## pitfalls
Assuming the ACA supplies the entire medial surface without exception — the occipital lobe's medial surface belongs to the posterior cerebral artery, a boundary that matters for localising infarcts and for understanding why ACA strokes spare vision.
