# Item

## label
Secondary hyperalgesia is increased pain sensitivity in normal (uninjured) skin surrounding an area of tissue damage

## id
CON-NEU-CC062FB023FF03

## canonical_key
hyperalgesia.secondary.normal-skin-surrounding-injury

## definition
Secondary hyperalgesia is an exaggerated pain response to a stimulus applied to normal, uninjured skin surrounding (but not within) an area of tissue damage. Unlike primary hyperalgesia, which results from direct peripheral sensitisation of nociceptors within the damaged tissue itself, secondary hyperalgesia arises from central sensitisation — increased excitability of dorsal horn neurons receiving convergent input from the surrounding, undamaged skin — so the surrounding skin becomes painfully sensitive even though its own nociceptors were never directly injured.

## explicit_objective
Attribute increased pain sensitivity in the normal skin surrounding an injury (not the injury itself) to secondary hyperalgesia, driven by central sensitisation.

## id

## canonical_key

## definition

Because the nociceptors themselves are sensitised, previously innocuous or mildly painful stimuli (such as the light pressure of clothing) are then perceived as markedly more painful.

# Item

## label
The cerebellum coordinates voluntary movement on the same (ipsilateral) side of the body as the lesion, unlike the cerebral cortex

## id
CON-NEU-5CC40C261365E4

## canonical_key
cerebellum.laterality.ipsilateral-limb-coordination

## definition
Each cerebellar hemisphere coordinates voluntary movement of the limbs on its own (ipsilateral) side of the body, because cerebellar efferent pathways cross twice — once leaving the deep cerebellar nuclei toward the contralateral red nucleus/thalamus, and again when the corticospinal tract they influence subsequently decussates in the medulla — restoring a net ipsilateral relationship between hemisphere and limb. This is the opposite laterality to the cerebral motor cortex, which controls the contralateral side after a single decussation, and it is why a unilateral cerebellar lesion produces incoordination (ataxia, dysmetria) on the same side as the lesion.

## explicit_objective
State that the cerebellum coordinates ipsilateral limb movement (net double decussation), in contrast to the cerebral cortex's single-decussation contralateral control.

## label
A lesion of the primary motor cortex, such as an infarction of the cerebral cortex supplying it, causes contralateral spastic hemiplegia

## id
CON-NEU-AE9ACDA40D8D0A

## canonical_key
primarymotorcortex.lesion.contralateral-spastic-hemiplegia-cortical-infarction

## definition
The primary motor cortex (precentral gyrus, Brodmann area 4) gives rise to corticospinal fibres that decussate at the medullary pyramids; a lesion of the cortex itself — for example, ischaemic infarction in the territory of the blood vessels supplying that part of the cortex — therefore produces an upper motor neuron syndrome of spastic paralysis (hemiplegia), exaggerated tendon reflexes, and an extensor plantar response on the contralateral side of the body. This is the same general upper motor neuron laterality rule that applies to a lesion anywhere along the corticospinal pathway above its medullary decussation, whether at the cortex itself or, as classically taught for the internal capsule, further downstream.

## explicit_objective
Attribute contralateral spastic hemiplegia to a lesion (e.g. vascular infarction) of the primary motor cortex, the same upper motor neuron laterality rule as an internal capsule lesion.

# Item

## label
A unilateral lesion of the fasciculus gracilis causes ipsilateral, not contralateral, loss of vibration sense below the lesion

## id
CON-NEU-5E9B60B8E4B73B

## canonical_key
dorsalcolumn.lesion.ipsilateral-vibration-loss-below-lesion

## definition
Because dorsal column fibres (fasciculus gracilis for the lower limb, fasciculus cuneatus for the upper limb) ascend on the same side of the spinal cord and do not decussate until they form internal arcuate fibres in the lower medulla, a unilateral lesion of the fasciculus gracilis — for example, from cord compression or a demyelinating plaque — causes loss of vibration and proprioceptive sense on the ipsilateral (same) side of the body, below the level of the lesion, not the contralateral side. A right fasciculus gracilis lesion, for instance, produces loss of vibration sense in the right leg (including the right tibia), the mirror image of the crossed pattern seen with spinothalamic (pain/temperature) tract lesions.

## explicit_objective
State that a unilateral dorsal column (fasciculus gracilis) lesion causes ipsilateral, not contralateral, loss of vibration sense below the lesion.

## label
The afferent limb of the flexor withdrawal reflex is carried by free nerve endings (nociceptors)

## id
CON-NEU-BD62A264C6B252

## canonical_key
withdrawalreflex.afferentlimb.free-nerve-endings-nociceptors

## definition
The flexor withdrawal reflex is a polysynaptic spinal reflex triggered by a noxious (painful) stimulus, such as touching a hot surface; its afferent (sensory) limb is carried by free nerve endings — the nociceptors that transduce noxious mechanical, thermal, or chemical stimuli — rather than by an encapsulated receptor. These afferents synapse on spinal interneurons that, in turn, excite flexor motor neurons of the stimulated limb (producing withdrawal) while reciprocally inhibiting its extensors, and cross the cord to drive the crossed extensor reflex in the opposite limb.

Identify free nerve endings (nociceptors) as the afferent receptor type for the flexor withdrawal reflex.

## label
Superficial reflexes such as the abdominal reflex are lost in an upper motor neuron lesion because they depend on an intact corticospinal tract

## id
CON-NEU-32727BD31AF93E

## canonical_key
superficialreflex.upperlmotorneuronlesion.corticospinal-tract-dependency

## definition
Superficial (cutaneous) reflexes, such as the abdominal reflex (contraction of the underlying abdominal muscles on stroking the overlying skin) and the cremasteric reflex, differ from deep tendon reflexes in that their reflex arc requires a functioning corticospinal tract input to the spinal segment, not just an intact peripheral nerve and spinal cord segment. Consequently, an upper motor neuron lesion that interrupts the corticospinal tract abolishes these superficial reflexes below the lesion, even as it simultaneously exaggerates the deep tendon reflexes (which do not share this corticospinal dependency) — loss of superficial reflexes together with hyperactive deep reflexes is a classic combined sign of an upper motor neuron lesion.

## explicit_objective
Attribute the loss of superficial (e.g. abdominal) reflexes in an upper motor neuron lesion to their dependency on an intact corticospinal tract, contrasting with the simultaneous exaggeration of deep tendon reflexes.

## definition
The Golgi tendon organ, situated in series with extrafusal muscle fibres at the musculotendinous junction, signals muscle tension via its Ib afferent fibres.
