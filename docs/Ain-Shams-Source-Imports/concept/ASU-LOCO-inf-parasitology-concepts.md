# Item

## label
Hard ticks (Ixodidae) are told from soft ticks (Argasidae) by a dorsal scutum, a visible anterior capitulum, and posterior festoons

## id
CON-INF-8ADA71C650D6CA

## canonical_key
tick.anatomy.scutum-capitulum-festoons

## definition
Ticks (order Ixodida) are classified into hard ticks (family Ixodidae) and soft ticks (family Argasidae) by gross anatomy visible without dissection. Hard ticks carry a chitinous dorsal shield, the scutum (small in the female, covering the whole dorsum in the male), a capitulum (the mouthparts-bearing "head") that projects visibly forward from the body, and a row of rectangular cuticular divisions at the posterior margin called festoons. Soft ticks lack a scutum entirely, have a capitulum tucked underneath the body and invisible from above, and have no festoons; their cuticle is instead leathery and wrinkled.

## explicit_objective
List the three features (scutum, capitulum visibility, festoons) that identify a hard tick, and state what a soft tick lacks in each.

Fleas are speciated by their combs -- a genal (cheek) comb and a pronotal comb, whose presence and tooth count differ by species

## id
CON-INF-1D4F12EFB7A01E

## canonical_key
flea.anatomy.genalcomb-pronotalcomb

## definition
Fleas (order Siphonaptera) are laterally flattened, wingless blood-feeding insects identified to genus/species partly by chitinous combs of stout spines called ctenidia. The genal comb sits along the lower margin of the head (genal = cheek); the pronotal comb sits along the posterior margin of the first thoracic segment (pronotum). Presence, absence and tooth number of each comb are used diagnostically -- for example the human flea (Pulex irritans) lacks both combs, while the cat and dog fleas (Ctenocephalides) carry both.

## id

## definition
Lice (order Anoplura, the sucking lice of medical importance) are wingless, dorsoventrally flattened ectoparasitic insects with piercing-sucking mouthparts retracted inside the head capsule between blood meals, distinguishing them on inspection from biting/chewing lice. ## id

The flies most often implicated are differentiated by larval and adult features taught together in practical identification: Sarcophaga (flesh flies) are larviparous (deposit live larvae, not eggs) and adults show three dark longitudinal thoracic stripes with a checkerboard abdominal pattern; Calliphora (blue-bottle blow flies) have a metallic blue-black body and lay eggs on wounds/necrotic tissue; Wohlfahrtia is an obligate-myiasis flesh fly whose larvae actively invade healthy tissue (not just wounds); Musca (house fly) is a non-biting, sponging-mouthpart fly that is a mechanical vector for enteric pathogens more than a myiasis agent itself, included in this identification set as the "non-myiasis" contrast case.

Adult Onchocerca volvulus worms are told apart by size and coiling -- the female is much longer and coiled in a subcutaneous nodule, the male is short, thin and free within it

## id
CON-INF-AF25784BB83DE8

## canonical_key
onchocerca.identification.male-vs-female

## definition
Onchocerca volvulus, the filarial worm causing onchocerciasis (river blindness), lives as coiled adult pairs inside fibrous subcutaneous nodules (onchocercomata). The adult female is markedly longer (up to about 50 cm) and thicker, and is found tightly coiled within the nodule; the adult male is much shorter (2 to 4 cm), thinner, and moves freely within the nodule rather than being fixed in a coil. The female is ovoviviparous, releasing motile microfilariae that migrate through skin and eye tissue and are responsible for the itching, skin changes and blindness of the disease, not the adults themselves.

## label
Adult Trichinella spiralis worms are tiny intestinal nematodes told apart by a blunt, forked posterior in the male versus a single ovary and anterior vulva in the ovoviviparous female

## id
CON-INF-330B28C8BFAB37

## canonical_key
trichinella.identification.male-vs-female

## definition
Trichinella spiralis is a small nematode whose adults live briefly in the small intestine after infected meat is eaten; the female (about 3 mm) is roughly twice the length of the male (about 1.5 mm). The male's posterior end carries two small copulatory appendages (a bluntly forked/lobed tail with no spicule, unusual among nematodes), while the female is ovoviviparous with a single tubular ovary and a vulva located near the anterior third of the body, through which she releases live first-stage larvae directly into the intestinal mucosa. Those larvae migrate via blood to skeletal muscle, where they encyst -- the stage responsible for the clinical disease.
