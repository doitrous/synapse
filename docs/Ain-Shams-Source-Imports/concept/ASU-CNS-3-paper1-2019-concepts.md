## label
Apomorphine, a dopamine agonist given by subcutaneous injection, is used as rescue therapy for sudden "off" akinesia episodes in Parkinson disease

## id
CON-NEU-749026E7CD8D47

## canonical_key
apomorphine.use.off-akinesia-rescue-parkinson

## definition
Apomorphine is a potent, non-selective dopamine (D1/D2) receptor agonist. Because oral levodopa's effect can wear off unpredictably in advanced Parkinson disease, producing sudden "off" periods of akinesia (inability to move), apomorphine is given by subcutaneous injection (or pump infusion) as rescue therapy: its rapid onset (minutes) restores mobility quickly until the next dose of oral medication takes effect. It is not used as first-line or maintenance therapy because of its short duration of action, need for parenteral administration, and prominent nausea (routinely co-prescribed with an antiemetic such as domperidone).

Buprenorphine is a partial agonist at the mu-opioid receptor

## id
CON-NEU-B07F58129358C2

## canonical_key
buprenorphine.pharmacology.partial-mu-agonist

## definition
Buprenorphine is a partial agonist at the mu-opioid receptor (and a kappa-receptor antagonist), unlike full agonists such as morphine or methadone. Because it only partially activates the mu receptor, it produces a "ceiling effect" on analgesia and respiratory depression, giving it a wider safety margin in overdose, and its high receptor affinity lets it displace full agonists, which can precipitate withdrawal if given too soon after another opioid. These properties make it useful both as an analgesic and, like methadone, as opioid substitution therapy for opioid use disorder.

## explicit_objective

## label
Mossy fibres, the major cerebellar afferents, synapse on granule cell dendrites in the granular layer of the cerebellar cortex, not the molecular layer

## id
CON-NEU-DB3C4B0B818935

## canonical_key
mossy-fibers.location.granular-layer-granule-cell-synapse

## definition
Mossy fibres are one of the two main classes of afferent input to the cerebellar cortex (the other being climbing fibres). They terminate in the innermost of the cerebellar cortex's three layers, the granular layer, where they synapse on the dendrites of granule cells to form characteristic glomeruli; granule cell axons then ascend to the molecular layer as parallel fibres, which synapse on Purkinje cell dendrites. Mossy fibres themselves are therefore not found in the molecular layer, and a mossy fibre's direct synaptic partner is the granule cell, not the Purkinje cell.

## explicit_objective

This makes it a key anatomical landmark linking the two fossae's neurovascular contents.

## label
Stimulation of the basal ganglia's indirect pathway disinhibits the subthalamic nucleus

## id
CON-NEU-B6287FC9787A1F

## canonical_key
basal-ganglia.indirect-pathway.subthalamic-nucleus-disinhibition

## definition
The basal ganglia's indirect pathway runs from the striatum to the external segment of the globus pallidus (GPe), which normally exerts tonic inhibitory (GABAergic) drive onto the subthalamic nucleus. When the indirect pathway is stimulated, striatal neurons inhibit the GPe; this inhibition of an inhibitory structure removes the GPe's restraint on the subthalamic nucleus, disinhibiting it, so subthalamic nucleus activity increases. The now more active subthalamic nucleus, in turn, excites the internal segment of the globus pallidus (GPi)/substantia nigra pars reticulata, increasing their inhibitory output to the thalamus and net-suppressing movement — the opposite overall effect to the direct pathway.

Thermal nociceptors are stimulated by skin temperatures above about 50 degC, and their brief stimulation by extreme cold can also evoke a paradoxical cold sensation

## id
CON-NEU-A7B29403AEDF27

## canonical_key
thermal-nociceptors.stimulus.above-50c-paradoxical-cold

## definition
Thermal (heat) nociceptors are free nerve endings that begin firing only once skin temperature exceeds roughly 50 degC, the threshold at which tissue damage risk begins, distinguishing them from the separate warm and cold thermoreceptors that signal ordinary, non-painful temperature. A related phenomenon is paradoxical cold sensation: intense, rapid activation of cold-sensitive afferents (or, in some accounts, transient co-activation of cold-type fibres by extreme cold/heat) can be perceived as a brief, sharp "cold" sensation, an exception to the otherwise orderly heat-versus-cold coding of the thermal system.

Otitis media is a cause of extracranial headache

## id
CON-NEU-0895C65227DB47

## canonical_key
otitis-media.cause.extracranial-headache

## definition
Extracranial headache is referred or locally generated pain arising from pain-sensitive structures outside the cranial cavity (scalp, sinuses, teeth, ears, cervical spine, temporomandibular joint, and so on), as opposed to intracranial causes (meningeal irritation, raised or lowered CSF pressure, vascular structures). Otitis media, inflammation/infection of the middle ear, is a classic example: the inflamed, pressurised middle ear generates pain that is perceived as headache, mediated by the sensory innervation of the ear (branches of the trigeminal, facial, glossopharyngeal and vagus nerves) rather than by any intracranial mechanism.

## label
The auriculotemporal nerve carries postganglionic parasympathetic secretomotor fibres from the otic ganglion to the parotid gland

## id
CON-NEU-BDA7D4142523F8

## canonical_key
auriculotemporal-nerve.function.postganglionic-parotid-secretomotor

## definition
Parasympathetic secretomotor supply to the parotid gland begins in the inferior salivatory nucleus and travels via the glossopharyngeal nerve, its tympanic branch, and the lesser petrosal nerve to synapse in the otic ganglion. From there, the auriculotemporal nerve, a branch of the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve, is the specific route by which these now-postganglionic parasympathetic fibres are distributed to the parotid gland; the auriculotemporal nerve is the classic "hitchhiking" pathway, carrying autonomic fibres it did not itself originate, alongside its own sensory fibres to the scalp and temporomandibular joint.
