## label
The inferior salivatory nucleus provides preganglionic parasympathetic supply to the parotid gland via the glossopharyngeal nerve

## id
CON-NEU-BE9B3000C07F58

## canonical_key
inferiorsalivatorynucleus.function.parotid-gland-supply

## definition
The inferior salivatory nucleus, a general visceral efferent (parasympathetic) brainstem nucleus in the medulla, gives rise to preganglionic parasympathetic fibres that travel with the glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX), then via its tympanic branch and the lesser petrosal nerve, to synapse in the otic ganglion; postganglionic fibres from the otic ganglion then join the auriculotemporal nerve to supply secretomotor innervation to the parotid gland. This pathway is classically contrasted with the superior salivatory nucleus, which instead supplies the submandibular, sublingual, lacrimal and nasal glands via the facial nerve. The inferior salivatory nucleus/glossopharyngeal/parotid pathway is a distinctive single-target parasympathetic route among the head's salivary glands.

Benztropine, an anticholinergic, is used to treat drug-induced (extrapyramidal) tremor

## id
CON-NEU-FDCC38EE8142A2

## canonical_key
benztropine.indication.extrapyramidal-tremor

## definition
Benztropine is a centrally acting anticholinergic (muscarinic antagonist) that restores the normal dopamine-acetylcholine balance in the basal ganglia when it is disrupted by dopamine-receptor-blocking drugs (chiefly typical antipsychotics), and is used to treat the resulting drug-induced extrapyramidal symptoms, including tremor, rigidity and acute dystonic reactions. Its mechanism works by reducing the relative cholinergic excess that occurs when dopaminergic tone is pharmacologically blocked, rather than by directly restoring dopamine itself. This is distinct from Parkinson disease's own idiopathic dopamine-deficiency tremor, for which benztropine may also give symptomatic relief through the same cholinergic-blocking mechanism, though dopaminergic agents remain first-line there.

## label
Astrocytes take up glucose from cerebral capillaries via the GLUT1 glucose transporter

## id
CON-NEU-F2CA1D324B6D86

## canonical_key
astrocyte.glucosetransport.glut1

## definition
Astrocytes, which envelop cerebral capillaries with their perivascular end-feet as part of the blood-brain barrier and the neurovascular unit, take up glucose from the capillary blood using the insulin-independent facilitative glucose transporter GLUT1, which is expressed at high density on both the capillary endothelium and the astrocyte end-foot membrane. This astrocytic glucose uptake supports the astrocyte-neuron lactate shuttle model, in which astrocytes metabolise glucose glycolytically to lactate and export it to neurons as an additional fuel source, particularly during high neuronal activity. GLUT1 is distinct from GLUT3, the transporter that predominates on neurons themselves for direct glucose uptake.

## label
The sympathetic outflow of the spinal cord extends caudally as far as the second lumbar (L2) segment

## id
CON-NEU-D62D80FC034511

## canonical_key
sympatheticoutflow.extent.t1-to-l2

## definition
The sympathetic (thoracolumbar) outflow arises from preganglionic neurons in the lateral horn of the spinal cord grey matter, extending from the first thoracic (T1) segment down to the second lumbar (L2) segment, in contrast to the parasympathetic (craniosacral) outflow, which arises separately from brainstem nuclei and the sacral (S2-S4) spinal segments. L2 marks the caudal limit of this thoracolumbar sympathetic column, below which no further preganglionic sympathetic cell bodies are found in the spinal cord. This T1-L2 extent is the anatomical basis for the paravertebral sympathetic chain's own longer cranial and caudal extensions beyond the cord segments that actually contribute outflow, since ascending and descending fibres within the chain redistribute the fixed T1-L2 output to targets at other levels.

## label
The cornea's sensory receptors are free nerve endings, making it exquisitely pain-sensitive but without specialised mechanoreceptors

## id
CON-NEU-4A1FD396E72DDC

## canonical_key
cornea.receptor.free-nerve-endings

## definition
The cornea is densely innervated by the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve, but its sensory endings consist almost exclusively of bare (free) nerve endings rather than the encapsulated, specialised mechanoreceptors (e.g. Meissner's or Pacinian corpuscles) found in the skin. Because free nerve endings are predominantly nociceptors, the cornea is one of the most pain-sensitive tissues in the body, and even minor mechanical, thermal or chemical stimuli are perceived chiefly as pain rather than as discriminative touch. This exclusively free-nerve-ending innervation is the physiological basis of the corneal (blink) reflex, in which even light corneal touch triggers a protective reflex blink via the trigeminal afferent and facial nerve efferent limbs.

## label
Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome results from thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency, classically in chronic alcoholics

## id
CON-NEU-DE2F08E8ED9580

## canonical_key
wernickekorsakoffsyndrome.cause.thiamine-deficiency

## definition
Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome results from thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency, most classically in chronic alcoholics because of a combination of poor dietary intake, impaired intestinal thiamine absorption and reduced hepatic thiamine storage capacity caused by chronic alcohol use. The acute phase, Wernicke encephalopathy, presents with the classic triad of confusion, ophthalmoplegia/nystagmus and ataxia, reflecting thiamine-dependent neuronal injury concentrated in the mammillary bodies, periaqueductal grey and medial thalamus; if untreated, it can progress to the chronic, often irreversible Korsakoff psychosis/amnestic syndrome, marked by severe anterograde amnesia and confabulation from mammillary body and medial thalamic damage. Because thiamine is a required cofactor for glucose metabolism, giving IV glucose to a thiamine-deficient patient without first replacing thiamine can precipitate or worsen Wernicke encephalopathy, which is why thiamine is given before or with glucose in suspected cases.

# Item

## label
The accessory olivary nuclei are not present at the level of the motor (pyramidal) decussation in the medulla

## id
CON-NEU-F9265FDB9FD8C6

## canonical_key
accessoryolivarynuclei.absent.motordecussation-level

## definition
The medulla oblongata is cross-sectioned at several teaching levels, and the level of the motor (pyramidal) decussation, the most caudal and rostral-to-the-spinal-cord level, is defined by the crossing corticospinal fibres and lacks the inferior olivary complex (the principal olivary nucleus and its accessory olivary nuclei), which only appears more rostrally, at the level of the sensory (medial lemniscus) decussation and above. This absence of the olivary nuclear complex is one of the standard identifying features used to recognise the pyramidal-decussation level on a medullary cross-section in neuroanatomy teaching, distinguishing it from the mid- and upper-medulla levels where the inferior olivary nuclear complex, including its accessory nuclei, is prominent.

## explicit_objective
State that the accessory olivary nuclei are absent at the level of the motor (pyramidal) decussation in the medulla.

## label
Lamina I of the spinal cord grey matter (the posteromarginal nucleus) is the most posterior (dorsal-most) Rexed lamina, chiefly relaying pain and temperature

## id
CON-NEU-601C4B778CF344

## canonical_key
rexedlaminai.identity.posteromarginal-nucleus

## definition
In the Rexed lamination scheme of the spinal cord dorsal horn grey matter, Lamina I, also called the posteromarginal nucleus (nucleus posteromarginalis), is the thin, most dorsal (posterior-most) layer capping the dorsal horn, and it receives predominantly nociceptive (pain) and thermal (temperature) primary afferent input, chiefly from A-delta and C fibres, before relaying it onward into the ascending spinothalamic pathway. Its dorsal-most position, immediately beneath the dorsolateral tract (of Lissauer) through which these same primary afferents first enter and ascend/descend a segment or two before synapsing, is a key identifying feature distinguishing it from the deeper laminae (e.g. lamina II, the substantia gelatinosa, which modulates pain transmission via interneurons rather than directly relaying it onward).

## explicit_objective
Identify lamina I (the posteromarginal nucleus) as the dorsal-most Rexed lamina, chiefly relaying pain and temperature afferents.
