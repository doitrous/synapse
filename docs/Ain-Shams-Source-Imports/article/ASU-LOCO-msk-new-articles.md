## summary
This ASU Year 1 Locomotor Physiology slice teaches McArdle's disease (glycogen storage disease type V) as a myophosphorylase deficiency that blocks muscle glycogenolysis, producing early exercise-induced cramp, stiffness and myoglobinuria while sparing liver glucose homeostasis.

## sections
### Definition
McArdle's disease (glycogen storage disease type V) is a deficiency of myophosphorylase (muscle glycogen phosphorylase), the enzyme that starts glycogenolysis in skeletal muscle by cleaving glucose-1-phosphate units from stored glycogen. Liver phosphorylase is a separate gene product and is unaffected, so blood glucose homeostasis stays normal.

### Mechanism
Exercising muscle normally draws on its own glycogen store for a fast burst of glucose-1-phosphate. Without myophosphorylase, that store cannot be tapped, so within the first few minutes of activity the patient develops cramp, stiffness and early fatigue. A brief rest lets circulating fatty acids and blood glucose substitute for muscle glycogen as fuel, allowing many patients to resume at a lower intensity — the classic "second wind" phenomenon. If exercise is pushed past the point the second wind can compensate for, muscle fibres break down (rhabdomyolysis), releasing myoglobin into the blood and urine (myoglobinuria).

### Key determinants
The determining factor is which phosphorylase gene is affected: the muscle isoform blocks only exercising skeletal muscle, while the liver isoform (unaffected here) still releases glucose from liver glycogen on demand. This is why McArdle's disease is exercise-triggered rather than fasting-triggered, unlike the hepatic glycogenoses.

### Clinical significance
Recognise McArdle's disease from early exercise-induced cramp/stiffness, a second-wind phenomenon, and myoglobinuria with normal fasting glucose. Distinguish it from a glycolytic block (phosphofructokinase deficiency, Tarui disease) or a hepatic glycogenosis, both of which present differently.

## hold_these
McArdle's disease = myophosphorylase (muscle glycogen phosphorylase) deficiency.
Symptoms are exercise-triggered: cramp, stiffness, myoglobinuria; "second wind" after brief rest.
Liver phosphorylase is unaffected -- no fasting hypoglycaemia.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing this with a glycolytic block (phosphofructokinase/Tarui disease) or a liver glycogenosis causing fasting hypoglycaemia.
Forgetting that blood glucose stays normal because the liver enzyme is a separate gene product.

## summary
This ASU Year 1 Locomotor Biochemistry slice teaches the extracellular, copper-dependent cross-linking step of collagen maturation (lysyl oxidase) and the inherited fibrillar-collagen disorders (Ehlers-Danlos syndrome) that follow when collagen synthesis or maturation fails.

## sections
### Definition
Lysyl oxidase is a copper-dependent extracellular enzyme that cross-links mature collagen fibrils. Ehlers-Danlos syndrome is a group of inherited disorders of fibrillar collagen (commonly type I, III or V), from mutations in the collagen genes themselves or in the enzymes that process them.

### Mechanism
After intracellular hydroxylation and glycosylation, procollagen is secreted and its terminal propeptides are cleaved to give tropocollagen, which self-assembles into fibrils. Lysyl oxidase then oxidatively deaminates specific lysine and hydroxylysine residues to aldehydes, which spontaneously condense with neighbouring residues to form covalent cross-links between adjacent tropocollagen molecules. These cross-links give mature collagen its tensile strength; without them collagen stays weak and soluble. In Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, a defect anywhere in this synthesis-to-cross-linking chain (for example a deficient procollagen peptidase in the dermatosparaxis type, or a deficient lysyl hydroxylase in the kyphoscoliotic type) produces structurally weak fibrillar collagen.

### Key determinants
Hydroxylation (proline/lysine hydroxylase) is intracellular and vitamin-C dependent; cross-linking (lysyl oxidase) is extracellular and copper dependent — two different steps, two different cofactors, two different diseases when either fails (scurvy versus a copper-dependent cross-linking defect). Which collagen type and which processing enzyme is affected determines the Ehlers-Danlos subtype and its severity; the vascular type (type IV, COL3A1) carries the highest risk of arterial and organ rupture.

### Clinical significance
The shared Ehlers-Danlos picture is hyperextensible, fragile skin that heals with thin "cigarette-paper" scars, hypermobile joints prone to dislocation, and fragile blood vessels. Recognise it as a fibrillar (not basement-membrane) collagen disease, and recognise lysyl oxidase/copper as the cross-linking step distinct from vitamin-C-dependent hydroxylation.

### Primary structure and the synthesis sequence (added, author6)
Each alpha-chain is about 1000 residues, built from a repeating Gly-X-Y motif (glycine every third position; X and Y often proline and hydroxyproline) -- glycine's small size is the only side chain that fits at the crowded triple-helix core, so a bulky substitution there (the classic osteogenesis imperfecta mutation) distorts the whole helix. Proline's ring structure also means this triple helix is not a classical alpha helix, which instead relies on intrachain hydrogen bonding roughly every 3.6 residues; collagen's stability comes mainly from interchain hydrogen bonds and, once mature, cross-links. The full synthesis-to-maturation sequence runs: cleavage of the preprocollagen signal peptide, hydroxylation of proline/lysine, glycosylation of some hydroxylysine, and disulfide-bond-driven chain alignment -- all intracellular, in the rough endoplasmic reticulum -- followed by secretion, then extracellular propeptide cleavage (preprochain -> prochain -> procollagen -> tropocollagen), fibril assembly, and finally lysyl-oxidase cross-linking to give the mature collagen fibril. Cysteine is essentially absent from mature triple-helical collagen (it survives only in the propeptides, which are cut off extracellularly). Mature fibrils are then packed differently by tissue: parallel bundles in tendon (and, by the same tensile-strength logic, teeth), a loose gel in the vitreous humor, and near-crystalline packing in the cornea.

### Osteogenesis imperfecta (added, author6)
Osteogenesis imperfecta is typically a dominant-negative missense point mutation substituting a bulky residue for one of the obligatory Gly-X-Y glycines in type I collagen (COL1A1/COL1A2), distorting the triple helix from that point on and producing fragile, easily-fractured bone.

## hold_these
Lysyl oxidase (copper-dependent) cross-links collagen extracellularly by oxidising lysine/hydroxylysine.
Hydroxylation (vitamin C, intracellular) and cross-linking (copper, extracellular) are two different steps with two different cofactors.
Ehlers-Danlos syndrome affects fibrillar collagen (I/III/V); vascular type IV (COL3A1) has the highest rupture risk.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing lysyl oxidase's copper dependence with vitamin C, the cofactor of the earlier hydroxylation step.
Naming a basement-membrane collagen defect as the Ehlers-Danlos mechanism -- that is Alport/Goodpasture territory.

## summary
This ASU Year 1 Locomotor Biochemistry slice teaches the two-step hepatic/renal activation of vitamin D to calcitriol and the three-hormone loop (parathyroid hormone, calcitriol, calcitonin) that keeps serum calcium constant.

## sections
### Definition
Vitamin D (cholecalciferol, from skin synthesis or diet) is biologically inactive until it is hydroxylated twice, and it then works together with parathyroid hormone and calcitonin to hold serum calcium within a narrow range.

### Mechanism
The liver adds a hydroxyl group at carbon 25 (25-hydroxylase) to give 25-hydroxyvitamin D, the main circulating storage form. The kidney's proximal tubule then adds a second hydroxyl at carbon 1 (1-alpha-hydroxylase) to give 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol), the active hormone. Calcitriol raises intestinal calcium and phosphate absorption and, together with parathyroid hormone, mobilises bone calcium. Falling serum calcium is sensed by the parathyroid glands' calcium-sensing receptor and triggers parathyroid hormone (PTH) release. PTH raises calcium by mobilising it from bone, increasing renal tubular calcium reabsorption, and stimulating renal 1-alpha-hydroxylase to make more calcitriol. Calcitonin, from thyroid parafollicular (C) cells, is released when calcium rises and opposes PTH by inhibiting osteoclastic bone resorption.

### Key determinants
Renal 1-alpha-hydroxylase activity — the rate-limiting activation step — is itself stimulated by PTH and by low serum phosphate, linking the two halves of the loop. Calcitonin's physiological role in humans is comparatively minor next to PTH and vitamin D, which do most of the day-to-day regulatory work.

### Clinical significance
Sequence the two vitamin D hydroxylation steps by organ and enzyme, identify calcitriol as the active hormone, and name the three calcium-regulating hormones with the direction each moves serum calcium — this is the basis for understanding renal osteodystrophy, vitamin D-resistant states, and hypo/hyperparathyroidism.

### Dosing when the kidney's activation step is lost (added, author6)
Chronic renal failure and 1-alpha-hydroxylase-deficient vitamin-D-dependent rickets type I both fail at the same step -- the kidney's own 1-alpha-hydroxylation. Because plain vitamin D3 and even 25-hydroxyvitamin D still require that missing step, both scenarios are treated by bypassing it: giving calcitriol itself or a preparation that is already 1-alpha-hydroxylated (e.g. 1-alpha-hydroxycholecalciferol / "1-hydroxy D3"), not plain D3 or 25-hydroxy D3.

### Sources and body distribution (added, author6)
Preformed vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) is found in dietary sources such as cod liver oil, fatty fish and egg yolk; skin does not contain preformed D3, only its inactive precursor 7-dehydrocholesterol, which UV light converts photochemically. Body calcium is heavily skewed toward the skeleton: roughly 99% is stored in bone, and only about 1% sits in soft tissue, extracellular fluid and plasma -- plasma calcium is therefore a small, tightly regulated fraction of the whole-body total, held constant by the PTH-calcitriol-calcitonin loop described above.

## hold_these
Vitamin D activation: liver adds 25-OH, kidney adds 1-alpha-OH -> calcitriol.
PTH and calcitriol raise serum calcium; calcitonin lowers it (minor role in humans).
Renal 1-alpha-hydroxylase is stimulated by PTH and by low phosphate.

## lose_the_mark
Reversing the organ order (kidney-then-liver) for vitamin D activation.
Treating calcitonin as the dominant regulator -- PTH and calcitriol do most of the physiological work in humans.

## summary
This ASU Year 1 Locomotor Biochemistry slice pairs two purine-pathway enzyme deficiencies with their diseases: HGPRT deficiency with Lesch-Nyhan syndrome, and adenosine deaminase (ADA) deficiency with severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID).

## sections
### Definition
Two purine-salvage/catabolism enzyme deficiencies are classically paired with their diseases in this curriculum: hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase (HGPRT) deficiency causes Lesch-Nyhan syndrome, and adenosine deaminase (ADA) deficiency causes a form of severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID).

### Mechanism
HGPRT salvages hypoxanthine and guanine back to their nucleotides. Its near-total deficiency forces purine synthesis down the wasteful de novo pathway, raising uric acid and producing the Lesch-Nyhan syndrome triad: hyperuricaemia/gout, choreoathetosis, and compulsive self-mutilation. ADA deficiency, unrelated to HGPRT, lets deoxyadenosine accumulate to toxic levels selectively in lymphocytes, destroying developing T and B cells and producing SCID.

### Key determinants
Inheritance pattern separates the two: Lesch-Nyhan syndrome (HGPRT) is X-linked, while the ADA form of SCID is autosomal recessive. Only the HGPRT block raises uric acid, because it is a salvage failure that pushes flux toward de novo synthesis and its uric acid end product; ADA deficiency's toxicity is lymphocyte-selective and produces an immunodeficiency, not a gout syndrome.

### Clinical significance
Pair HGPRT deficiency with Lesch-Nyhan syndrome and ADA deficiency with SCID, name the enzyme missing in each, and use the presence or absence of hyperuricaemia to tell the two apart on a vignette.

### The six enzyme-reaction pairings (added, author6)
Purine catabolism/salvage runs through six separately-testable enzyme-to-reaction conversions: HGPRT (hypoxanthine -> IMP), purine nucleoside phosphorylase/PNP (a nucleoside -> its free base + ribose-1-P, e.g. guanosine -> guanine), 5'-nucleotidase (a nucleotide -> the corresponding nucleoside), guanase/guanine deaminase (guanine -> xanthine), adenosine deaminase/ADA (adenosine -> inosine), and xanthine oxidase (xanthine -> uric acid, and hypoxanthine -> xanthine upstream of that). Allopurinol, a hypoxanthine analogue, competitively inhibits xanthine oxidase specifically, lowering uric acid production and treating gout; uric acid overproduction via this pathway is the direct cause of acute gouty arthritis, classically at the big toe.

### ADA versus PNP: which lymphocyte lineage fails (added, author6)
ADA deficiency lets deoxyadenosine accumulate, which is toxic to both developing T and B lymphocytes -- a combined (T and B low) immunodeficiency, i.e. a form of SCID. PNP deficiency instead lets deoxyguanosine accumulate, which is selectively toxic to T lymphocytes, sparing B cells -- a T-cell-only immunodeficiency with normal B-cell numbers. The two are distinguished on a vignette by whether B cells are low (ADA/SCID) or normal (PNP).

## hold_these
HGPRT deficiency -> Lesch-Nyhan syndrome (hyperuricaemia, choreoathetosis, self-mutilation), X-linked.
Adenosine deaminase (ADA) deficiency -> a form of SCID (lymphocyte-selective toxicity), autosomal recessive.
Only the HGPRT block raises uric acid.

## lose_the_mark
Swapping the two pairings (HGPRT with SCID, or ADA with Lesch-Nyhan).
Assuming both deficiencies raise uric acid.

## summary

## sections
### Definition
Creatine phosphate is skeletal muscle's fast energy buffer. Creatine is synthesised from arginine and glycine in two organs, and both it and creatine phosphate spontaneously break down to creatinine at a rate proportional to muscle mass.

### Mechanism
Arginine:glycine amidinotransferase (AGAT), in the kidney, transfers the amidino group of arginine to glycine, releasing ornithine and forming guanidinoacetate (GAA). GAA travels to the liver, where guanidinoacetate methyltransferase (GAMT) methylates it using S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) as the methyl donor, producing creatine. Creatine is then taken up by muscle and brain and phosphorylated by creatine kinase to creatine phosphate. Creatinine forms non-enzymatically, at a roughly constant rate proportional to muscle mass, from the spontaneous dehydration of creatine/creatine phosphate, and is cleared almost entirely by glomerular filtration. AGAT deficiency, a rare autosomal recessive cerebral creatine deficiency syndrome, blocks synthesis at the first, kidney step, so guanidinoacetate and downstream creatine are never made.

### Key determinants
Which step fails determines the disease: AGAT deficiency sits upstream of guanidinoacetate formation, so guanidinoacetate stays low or normal and muscle creatine/urinary creatinine are both low; GAMT deficiency blocks the second, liver step instead, letting neurotoxic guanidinoacetate accumulate. Muscle mass is the key determinant of baseline creatinine, which is why a low-muscle-mass patient can have a deceptively "normal" creatinine despite reduced glomerular filtration rate.

### Clinical significance
AGAT deficiency presents with global developmental delay, speech delay, hypotonia and sometimes seizures, and oral creatine supplementation bypasses the block and improves the clinical picture. Serum creatinine and creatinine clearance remain the everyday clinical tool for estimating glomerular filtration rate.

### Fuel selection alongside the phosphagen system (added, author6)
Creatine phosphate is muscle's fastest energy buffer, regenerating ATP within seconds via creatine kinase and providing the earliest-available energy system for a short sprint, before anaerobic glycolysis and then oxidative phosphorylation take over. Muscle's choice of fuel beyond this immediate buffer shifts with state: in the well-fed state muscle takes up glucose (to replenish glycogen) and amino acids (to synthesise protein); in early fasting, resting muscle burns fatty acids; under anaerobic conditions only glycogen/glucose can be used (fatty acid and ketone oxidation both require oxygen); and the aerobic oxidative system draws on glucose, fatty acids and ketone bodies together.

### Creatine kinase isoenzymes (added, author6)
Creatine kinase itself exists as tissue-specific isoenzymes built from M and B subunits: CK-MM (CK3) predominates in skeletal muscle, CK-MB (CK2) is relatively specific to cardiac muscle, and CK-BB (CK1) predominates in brain. A rise in plasma CK-MB is a classic marker of myocardial infarction, because cardiac injury releases the cardiac-specific isoenzyme into the circulation.

## hold_these
Creatine synthesis: AGAT (kidney, arginine+glycine -> guanidinoacetate) then GAMT (liver, SAM methylates to creatine).
Creatinine is a non-enzymatic breakdown product proportional to muscle mass, cleared by glomerular filtration.
AGAT deficiency: low creatine/creatinine, normal guanidinoacetate, developmental delay -- improves with creatine supplementation.

## lose_the_mark
Reversing the organs (liver-then-kidney) for creatine synthesis.
Confusing AGAT deficiency (guanidinoacetate normal/low, upstream block) with GAMT deficiency (guanidinoacetate elevated and neurotoxic, downstream block).
