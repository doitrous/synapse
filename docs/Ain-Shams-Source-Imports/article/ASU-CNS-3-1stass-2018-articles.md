## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: the tuber cinereum is part of the hypothalamus (median-eminence floor of the third ventricle, giving rise to the infundibulum), and is not one of the basal ganglia, which comprise the caudate nucleus, putamen, globus pallidus, subthalamic nucleus and substantia nigra.

## sections
### Definition
The tuber cinereum is part of the hypothalamus — the median-eminence region of the floor of the third ventricle, between the optic chiasm and the mammillary bodies, from which the infundibulum (pituitary stalk) descends — and is functionally and anatomically distinct from the basal ganglia.

### Mechanism
State that the tuber cinereum is a hypothalamic structure and is not one of the basal ganglia.

### Key determinants
The basal ganglia proper comprise the caudate nucleus and putamen (together the neostriatum), the globus pallidus (paleostriatum), the subthalamic nucleus and the substantia nigra; the tuber cinereum plays no part in this motor-control system.

### Clinical significance
This batch does not extend the source's plain fact into diagnosis, treatment or management claims beyond what the compilation itself states.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming every deep, grey-matter forebrain structure belongs to the basal ganglia — the tuber cinereum's hypothalamic location and endocrine function place it in a functionally separate system.

## hold_these
The tuber cinereum is a hypothalamic (median-eminence) structure, not one of the basal ganglia, which comprise the caudate, putamen, globus pallidus, subthalamic nucleus and substantia nigra.

## lose_the_mark
Naming the tuber cinereum among the basal ganglia because of its similarly deep, grey-matter location.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: a brain tumour causes headache through intracranial mechanisms — raised intracranial pressure, hydrocephalus and traction on pain-sensitive dura, vessels and venous sinuses — the mechanistic counterpart to extracranial headache; classically worse on waking and aggravated by coughing, straining or bending.

## sections
### Definition
A brain tumour classically causes headache through intracranial mechanisms — mass effect raising intracranial pressure, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid flow (causing hydrocephalus), and traction or distortion of pain-sensitive intracranial structures such as the dura mater, large blood vessels and venous sinuses — none of which is itself pain-sensitive at the level of brain tissue.

### Mechanism
State that a brain tumour causes headache through intracranial mechanisms — raised intracranial pressure, hydrocephalus and traction on pain-sensitive intracranial structures — as opposed to an extracranial cause.

### Key determinants
This intracranial route is the mechanistic counterpart to extracranial headache, which instead arises from pain-sensitive structures outside the cranial cavity such as the scalp, sinuses and cervical spine; a tumour-related headache is classically worse in the morning or on waking, and aggravated by coughing, straining or bending, reflecting further transient rises in intracranial pressure.

### Clinical significance
This batch does not extend the source's plain fact into diagnosis, treatment or management claims beyond what the compilation itself states.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming all headache is generated the same way regardless of cause — a brain tumour's headache arises from intracranial mass effect and traction on pain-sensitive dura/vessels, a distinct mechanism from an extracranial cause.

## hold_these
A brain tumour causes headache through intracranial mechanisms — raised intracranial pressure, hydrocephalus and traction on pain-sensitive dura/vessels — worse on waking and with coughing/straining, distinct from extracranial headache.

## lose_the_mark
Treating brain-tumour headache as mechanistically identical to an extracranial cause such as sinus or scalp disease.
