This ASU Year 3 CNS physiology slice covers: Kinetic (intention) tremor is a tremor that appears or worsens during a targeted, voluntary movement — classically demonstrated as past-pointing and increasing oscillation on the finger-nose-finger and heel-shin tests as the target is approached. Kinetic (intention) tremor is a tremor that appears or worsens during a targeted, voluntary movement — classically demonstrated as past-pointing and increasing oscillation on the finger-nose-finger and heel-shin tests as the target is approached. Attribute kinetic (intention) tremor, worsening as a target is approached, to a spinocerebellar lesion.

Confusing kinetic (intention) tremor, which worsens on approaching a target, with the resting (static) tremor of Parkinson disease, which is present at rest and improves with voluntary movement.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS physiology slice covers: Brown-Séquard syndrome follows hemisection (lateral half-section) of the spinal cord. Because the dorsal columns (fine touch, vibration, proprioception) ascend uncrossed until the medulla, hemisection causes ipsilateral loss of these modalities below the level of the lesion. ## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS physiology slice covers: Because the corticospinal tract, which originates chiefly from the primary motor cortex (Brodmann area 4), decussates at the pyramidal decussation in the lower medulla, a destructive lesion of area 4 on one side produces hemiplegia (or hemiparesis) of the contralateral (crossed) side of the body. Fine, discrete, skilled voluntary movements — most severely affecting the distal limb and hand — are lost first and most completely, because these movements depend most heavily on direct corticospinal (particularly corticomotoneuronal) control, unlike gross axial and proximal movements, which are partly preserved through other descending pathways.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS physiology slice covers: The gate control theory (Melzack and Wall) proposes that a 'gate' in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, formed by inhibitory interneurons in the substantia gelatinosa, regulates the passage of nociceptive signals toward higher centres. Large-diameter, fast-conducting A-beta touch/pressure afferents activate these inhibitory interneurons, closing the gate and suppressing transmission of slower, small-diameter A-delta/C-fibre pain signals arriving from the same or a nearby segment — which is why rubbing or massaging a painful area (activating A-beta afferents) provides real, physiologically grounded pain relief.
