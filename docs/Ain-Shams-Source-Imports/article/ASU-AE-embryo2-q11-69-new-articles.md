## summary
This ASU Year 1 Introduction to Anatomy and Embryology slice ("MCQs - Embryo 2.pdf", Q11-69 pass) covers nine facts about cleavage, tubal transport and fertilisation that `find-existing.mjs` found nowhere else in the corpus: the zona pellucida's role confining the cleaving zygote's size, the timing of tubal transport (morula reaching the uterine cavity by day 4, being the first stage to do so), the site of fertilisation (the ampulla, not the uterine cavity), the identity of the implanting stage (the blastocyst), the timing of sex determination, the direct results of fertilisation (and that implantation is not one of them), the layers the sperm penetrates (excluding the cumulus oophorus), and the site of the acrosome reaction (the fallopian tube). It is a local answer-key-grounded draft and still needs independent embryology evidence before publication.

## sections
### Definition
Cleavage is the series of mitotic divisions the zygote undergoes as it is transported along the uterine tube, producing progressively smaller blastomeres within a size-confining zona pellucida. Fertilisation itself takes place in the ampulla of the uterine tube and directly produces four outcomes: diploid restoration, completion of the second meiotic division, extrusion of the second polar body, and initiation of cleavage.
### Mechanism
Once fertilised in the ampulla, the zygote cleaves repeatedly while the intact zona pellucida keeps the whole conceptus at a constant outer size -- more, smaller blastomeres, not a larger embryo. The morula is the first stage to complete the tubal journey and reach the uterine cavity, arriving about 3-4 days (commonly cited as the 4th day) after fertilisation; only once it has further developed into a blastocyst, with a trophoblast layer, can it implant. To fertilise the oocyte at all, the sperm must first undergo the acrosome reaction in the fallopian tube, penetrating the corona radiata, zona pellucida and perivitelline space -- but not the cumulus oophorus, which the oocyte has already shed by the time it is in the tube. The chromosomal sex of the resulting embryo is fixed at this moment of fertilisation, by whichever sperm (X- or Y-bearing) achieves fusion.
### Key determinants
Keep these straight: (1) the zona pellucida confines the cleaving zygote's size, a separate function from its polyspermy block; (2) the morula, not an earlier stage, is first to reach the uterine lumen, about 3-4 days (commonly "day 4") after fertilisation; (3) fertilisation occurs in the ampulla of the tube, never in the uterine cavity itself; (4) it is the blastocyst, not the morula, that implants; (5) fertilisation directly restores diploidy, completes meiosis II, extrudes the second polar body and starts cleavage -- implantation is a separate, later event; (6) the cumulus oophorus is not among the layers the sperm penetrates; (7) the acrosome reaction happens in the fallopian tube, not in the male tract.
### Clinical significance
This article does not make prescribing or diagnostic-workup claims. Its study value is foundational: understanding that fertilisation happens in the tube (not the uterus) and that the sex chromosome is fixed at that moment underpins counselling around conception timing and the biology of tubal (ectopic) pregnancy.
### Common misconceptions
Do not confuse the zona pellucida's size-confining role during cleavage with its separate polyspermy-blocking role after fertilisation.

Do not name the zygote, 2-cell or 4-cell stage as reaching the uterine lumen -- only the morula has completed that journey.

Do not place fertilisation in the uterine cavity -- it occurs in the ampulla of the uterine tube.

Do not list implantation as a direct result of fertilisation -- it is a separate event roughly six days later.

Do not include the cumulus oophorus among the layers the sperm must penetrate to fertilise the oocyte.

## sections
### Definition
Chorionic villi mature in three named, sequential stages defined by their composition. Extraembryonic mesoderm -- the tissue whose invasion marks the primary-to-secondary villus transition -- is itself the primary (1ry) mesoderm of the conceptus, appearing before the intraembryonic (secondary) mesoderm that forms later during gastrulation.
### Mechanism
A primary villus is trophoblast alone (cytotrophoblast plus syncytiotrophoblast), with no mesenchymal core. Once extraembryonic mesoderm invades that trophoblastic core, the villus becomes a secondary villus (trophoblast plus primary mesoderm) -- not yet vascularised. Once fetal blood vessels develop within that mesodermal core, the villus becomes a tertiary (definitive) villus: trophoblast, primary mesoderm and small fetal capillaries together, and it is such tertiary villi, once anchored to the decidua basalis, that are called stem (anchoring) villi. The chorion as a whole -- cytotrophoblast, syncytiotrophoblast and extraembryonic mesoderm together -- is built from exactly this same extraembryonic mesoderm, which is the primary mesoderm; it also forms the connecting stalk, the extraembryonic coelom lining, and the covering around the yolk sac. It is not the secondary mesoderm -- that term belongs to the later, intraembryonic mesoderm formed within the embryonic disc during gastrulation.
### Key determinants
Keep two things straight: (1) mesoderm invasion makes a villus secondary; vascularisation (fetal capillary development) makes it tertiary -- two separate, sequential steps; (2) extraembryonic mesoderm is the primary (1ry) mesoderm, not the secondary mesoderm, regardless of how many "generations" of villi it has by then helped build.
### Clinical significance
This article does not make prescribing or diagnostic-workup claims. Its study value is foundational: understanding villus maturation underlies how placental structure is described in later obstetric and pathology teaching (e.g. villous immaturity, delayed villous maturation).
### Common misconceptions
Do not call the mesenchymal core of a secondary villus "2ry (secondary) mesoderm" -- it is the primary (extraembryonic) mesoderm; "secondary" describes the villus stage, not the mesoderm's own generation.

Do not confuse invasion-by-mesoderm (secondary villus) with vascularisation (tertiary villus) -- these are two separate, sequential events.

Do not call extraembryonic mesoderm "the secondary mesoderm" -- it is the primary mesoderm; the secondary (intraembryonic) mesoderm forms later, within the embryonic disc.

## sections
### Definition
The definitive placenta has a fetal part (chorion frondosum) and a maternal part (decidua basalis), which together enclose the intervillous space where maternal blood bathes the chorionic villi. The decidua as a whole is subdivided by its relation to the implanted blastocyst: decidua basalis deep to the embryonic pole, decidua capsularis over the abembryonic pole (faced by the regressed chorion leave), and decidua parietalis lining the rest of the uterine cavity.
### Mechanism
The placental barrier separating maternal from fetal blood is thickest early in pregnancy (syncytiotrophoblast, cytotrophoblast, primary mesoderm, fetal endothelium) and thins in the second half as the cytotrophoblast and primary mesoderm become discontinuous, leaving mainly syncytiotrophoblast directly against fetal capillary endothelium -- shortening the diffusion distance as fetal demands grow. The intervillous space, fed by maternal spiral arteries, is filled with maternal blood that bathes the villi directly, while fetal blood stays confined within the villous capillaries. The placenta is an active endocrine organ, secreting hCG, progesterone, oestrogen and human placental lactogen, but not the pituitary gonadotrophins LH or FSH. When implantation occurs abnormally low in the uterine cavity, it produces placenta praevia -- graded by how much of the internal os the placenta covers, with praevia marginalis covering it only partially -- as distinct from a true ectopic pregnancy, which implants entirely outside the uterine cavity (most often the uterine tube). Twinning abnormalities follow their own logic: conjoined twins, being an incomplete separation of a single zygote, are always monozygotic, and pygopagus specifically names sacral-region fusion.
### Key determinants
Keep these straight: (1) chorion frondosum (fetal) pairs with decidua basalis (maternal) to form the placenta; chorion leave pairs with decidua capsularis instead; (2) the placental barrier thins in the second half of pregnancy as cytotrophoblast and primary mesoderm regress; (3) intervillous space = maternal blood, never mixing with fetal blood in the villous capillaries; (4) the placenta secretes hCG and steroid hormones but not pituitary LH/FSH; (5) decidua basalis lies deep to the embryonic pole; (6) the lower uterine body is still intrauterine (praevia risk), not ectopic -- true ectopic sites are extrauterine; (7) praevia marginalis covers the os only partially; (8) conjoined twins are always monozygotic, and pygopagus means sacral fusion.
### Clinical significance
This article does not make prescribing or diagnostic-workup claims. Its study value is foundational: the placental-barrier thinning explains improved late-pregnancy exchange efficiency; the decidua/implantation-site distinctions underpin how placenta praevia and ectopic pregnancy are diagnosed and graded in clinical obstetrics; and the hormone list underlies pregnancy testing (hCG) and luteal support physiology.
### Common misconceptions
Do not pair chorion frondosum with decidua capsularis, or chorion leave with decidua basalis -- the correct pairings are frondosum/basalis (placenta) and leave/capsularis (regressed membranes).

Do not believe the placental barrier keeps a constant four-layer thickness throughout pregnancy -- it thins in the second half.

Do not believe fetal and maternal blood mix in the intervillous space.

Do not attribute LH or FSH secretion to the placenta -- these are pituitary hormones; the placenta's own analogue is hCG.

Do not count the lower part of the uterine body as an ectopic pregnancy site -- it is still intrauterine.

Do not confuse placenta praevia marginalis (partial os coverage) with complete coverage.

Do not call conjoined twins dizygotic -- they are always monozygotic.

## sections
### Definition
During the second week, the inner cell mass reorganises into the bilaminar embryonic disc (epiblast and hypoblast), the extraembryonic coelom opens up within the extraembryonic mesoderm, and the amniotic cavity appears on the epiblast side. The zona pellucida, present since the primary oocyte stage, must be shed ("hatching") before any of this implantation-linked development can proceed.
### Mechanism
The inner cell mass differentiates into two layers: the epiblast (facing the amniotic cavity) and the hypoblast (facing the blastocoele/yolk sac) -- the first two, and earliest, germ layers, distinct from the later ectoderm/mesoderm/endoderm produced by third-week gastrulation. Once extraembryonic mesoderm has formed, coalescing lacunae within it create the extraembryonic coelom (chorionic cavity), entirely lined by that same mesoderm, and its expansion narrows the connecting stalk toward the future umbilical cord. This local answer bank's own printed key dates amniotic cavity appearance to the first week after fertilisation, a timing point flagged here as source-specific rather than independently verified. None of this can happen with the zona pellucida still in place: it persists from the primary oocyte through the early blastocyst (helping to keep the conceptus a constant size and preventing premature tubal adhesion), but the blastocyst must "hatch" -- shedding the zona -- before its trophoblast can contact and attach to the endometrium; the zona is gone before implantation, not after it.
### Key determinants
Keep four things straight: (1) epiblast and hypoblast, not ectoderm and endoderm, are the first two germ layers, differentiating during the second week; (2) the extraembryonic coelom forms within, and is lined by, extraembryonic mesoderm; (3) this bank's own key places amniotic cavity development in the first week -- flagged as a source-specific answer, not a settled consensus fact; (4) the zona pellucida disappears (hatches) before implantation, not after.
### Clinical significance
This article does not make prescribing or diagnostic-workup claims. Its study value is foundational: understanding the bilaminar disc and hatching timeline underlies how early pregnancy dating and pre-implantation genetic testing (which requires an intact, then hatched, blastocyst) are taught.
### Common misconceptions
Do not call epiblast/hypoblast "ectoderm and endoderm" -- those terms apply only after third-week gastrulation.

Do not place the extraembryonic coelom between the cytotrophoblast and syncytiotrophoblast, or date its first appearance to a postnatal week.

Do not treat this bank's "first week" amniotic-cavity timing as universally agreed mainstream teaching without checking against an independent source.

Do not believe the zona pellucida is still present once the blastocyst has implanted -- hatching precedes implantation.
