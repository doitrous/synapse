## summary
Denosumab is a monoclonal antibody that binds RANKL, blocking osteoclast activation and reducing bone resorption.

## sections
### Definition
Denosumab is a human monoclonal antibody directed against RANKL (receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand).

### Mechanism
By binding RANKL, denosumab prevents it from activating RANK on osteoclast precursors, inhibiting osteoclast formation, function and survival and thereby reducing bone resorption.

### Key determinants
The antibody-RANKL interaction is the determinant of effect; denosumab has no direct action on osteoblasts or on bone mineralisation itself.

### Clinical significance
Denosumab is given by subcutaneous injection (typically every six months) for osteoporosis and to reduce skeletal-related events in bone metastases, offering an alternative mechanism to bisphosphonates for patients who cannot tolerate oral dosing or who have renal impairment limiting bisphosphonate use.

### Common misconceptions
Do not confuse denosumab's antibody-mediated RANKL blockade with bisphosphonates, which act by binding hydroxyapatite and inducing osteoclast apoptosis through a different, non-antibody mechanism.

## hold_these
Denosumab inhibits RANKL.
This blocks osteoclast activation and reduces bone resorption.

## lose_the_mark
Stating that denosumab works by binding hydroxyapatite (that is the bisphosphonate mechanism).

## summary
Beta blockers control the sympathetic symptoms of hyperthyroidism and are cardioprotective, without reducing thyroid hormone synthesis.

## sections
### Definition
Beta-adrenergic blockers, typically propranolol, are used as adjunct therapy in hyperthyroidism.

### Mechanism
They block peripheral beta-adrenergic receptors, blunting the sympathetic manifestations of excess thyroid hormone (tremor, tachycardia, palpitations, anxiety, heat intolerance) and reducing heart rate and myocardial oxygen demand.

### Key determinants
Cardioprotection follows from reduced heart rate and reduced arrhythmia risk during the hyperadrenergic thyrotoxic state; propranolol at high dose also modestly inhibits peripheral T4-to-T3 conversion.

### Clinical significance
Beta blockers are given for rapid symptom relief while definitive antithyroid therapy (thionamides, radioiodine, or surgery) takes effect; they are not curative and do not reduce hormone synthesis themselves.

### Common misconceptions
Do not assume beta blockers treat the underlying hyperthyroidism — they are purely symptomatic and cardioprotective adjuncts, not a substitute for thionamides.

## hold_these
Beta blockers block the sympathetic symptoms of hyperthyroidism.
They are cardioprotective but do not reduce thyroid hormone synthesis.

## lose_the_mark
Stating that beta blockers cure hyperthyroidism or replace thionamide therapy.

## summary
Lugol's iodine is given for 7-10 days before subtotal thyroidectomy to shrink and de-vascularise the hyperplastic thyroid gland.

## sections
### Definition
Lugol's iodine is a strong iodine solution given as short-term preoperative preparation before thyroid surgery.

### Mechanism
High-dose iodine transiently inhibits thyroid hormone release and reduces glandular size and vascularity ahead of surgery.

### Key determinants
The effect is time-limited: the antithyroid action of iodine is transient, so escape occurs with prolonged dosing, which is why the drug is given only as a short preoperative course, not as chronic therapy.

### Clinical significance
Reduced gland size and vascularity make surgery technically easier and reduce intraoperative blood loss during subtotal/near-total thyroidectomy.

### Common misconceptions
Do not use Lugol's iodine as long-term definitive treatment for hyperthyroidism instead of thionamides, radioiodine, or surgery.

## hold_these
Lugol's iodine is given for 7-10 days before subtotal thyroidectomy.
It reduces gland size and vascularity, easing surgery.

## summary
Methimazole is avoided in the first trimester in favour of PTU, then preferred from the second trimester onward — it is not contraindicated throughout pregnancy.

## sections
### Definition
Methimazole is a thionamide antithyroid drug whose pregnancy safety profile changes by trimester.

### Mechanism
Methimazole crosses the placenta and, in the first trimester, is associated with rare embryopathy (aplasia cutis, choanal/oesophageal atresia); PTU lacks this specific association and is preferred instead despite its own hepatotoxicity risk.

### Key determinants
From the second trimester onward, methimazole is generally preferred over PTU because PTU carries the greater hepatotoxicity risk over a full pregnancy course.

### Clinical significance
The correct clinical practice is a trimester-specific switch (PTU in the first trimester, methimazole from the second trimester), not avoidance of methimazole throughout pregnancy.

### Common misconceptions
Do not assume methimazole is banned throughout pregnancy, and do not continue first-trimester methimazole unmodified without considering a switch to PTU.

## hold_these
Methimazole is avoided in the first trimester (PTU preferred) but generally preferred from the second trimester onward.
It is not contraindicated throughout all of pregnancy.

## lose_the_mark
Stating that methimazole is simply "contraindicated in pregnancy" without the trimester-specific nuance.

## summary
Bromocriptine is an orally active ergot dopamine D2 agonist that suppresses prolactin secretion.

## sections
### Definition
Bromocriptine is an ergot-derived dopamine D2 receptor agonist, taken orally.

### Mechanism
By activating dopamine D2 receptors on pituitary lactotrophs, bromocriptine mimics hypothalamic dopamine's tonic inhibition of prolactin secretion.

### Key determinants
Oral bioavailability is what makes it practical for chronic outpatient use in hyperprolactinaemia, unlike injectable somatostatin analogues used for other pituitary conditions.

### Clinical significance
Bromocriptine is first-line for hyperprolactinaemia (including prolactinoma), reducing prolactin levels and often shrinking prolactinoma tumour size; it has also been used in Parkinson disease and to suppress lactation.

### Common misconceptions
Do not confuse bromocriptine (oral dopamine agonist, for hyperprolactinaemia) with somatostatin analogues such as octreotide (injectable, for acromegaly).

## hold_these
Bromocriptine is an orally active dopamine agonist.
It suppresses prolactin secretion and is first-line for hyperprolactinaemia.

## lose_the_mark

## summary
Conivaptan, a vasopressin V1a/V2 antagonist used for hyponatraemia, is administered by IV infusion only, unlike the oral vaptan tolvaptan.

## sections
### Definition
Conivaptan is a non-selective vasopressin receptor antagonist ("vaptan") used for euvolaemic and hypervolaemic hyponatraemia.

### Mechanism
It blocks V1a and V2 receptors, reducing water reabsorption in the renal collecting duct and correcting excess free-water retention.

### Key determinants
Its formulation restricts it to intravenous infusion, which limits use to short-term inpatient treatment, in contrast to tolvaptan, which is taken orally and used for both inpatient and longer-term management.

### Clinical significance
Route of administration determines the clinical setting in which each vaptan is used: conivaptan for acute inpatient IV correction, tolvaptan for oral (including longer-term) use.

### Common misconceptions
Do not assume all vaptans share the same route — conivaptan is IV-only, tolvaptan is oral.

## summary
Bisphosphonates are renally cleared and require dose adjustment or avoidance in significant renal impairment — they are not simply "safe" renally.

## sections
### Definition
Bisphosphonates are cleared largely unchanged by the kidney.

### Mechanism
In significant renal impairment, reduced clearance raises nephrotoxicity risk (including acute tubular necrosis with rapid IV dosing) and compounds hypocalcaemia risk from impaired renal calcium/phosphate handling.

### Key determinants
The degree of renal impairment (commonly a creatinine clearance threshold around 30-35 mL/min, drug-specific) determines whether dose adjustment or avoidance is required.

### Clinical significance
Renal function should be checked before starting or continuing bisphosphonate therapy; this is a separate precaution from the drug class's gastrointestinal contraindications.

### Common misconceptions
Do not assume bisphosphonates are universally renally safe — the statement "safely taken renally" is false without qualification.

## hold_these
Bisphosphonates are renally cleared and are not simply "safe" in renal impairment.
Significant renal impairment requires dose adjustment or avoidance.

## lose_the_mark

## summary
Active peptic ulcer disease and oesophageal disorders are contraindications to oral bisphosphonates because of direct mucosal irritation.

## sections
### Definition
Oral bisphosphonates are directly irritant to the upper gastrointestinal mucosa.

### Mechanism
Contact with the oesophageal and gastric mucosa can cause or worsen oesophagitis, ulceration and erosion, which is why active peptic ulcer disease and oesophageal disorders (stricture, achalasia, delayed emptying, or inability to remain upright) are contraindications.

### Key determinants

## summary
Glucocorticoids act largely by inhibiting NF-kB/AP-1-driven transcription of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1, IL-6 and TNF-alpha.

## sections
### Definition
Glucocorticoids exert broad anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects through intracellular glucocorticoid receptor signalling.

### Mechanism
They induce anti-inflammatory proteins (e.g., annexin-1/lipocortin-1, inhibiting phospholipase A2) and, centrally, inhibit the transcription factors NF-kB and AP-1, reducing transcription of proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1, IL-2, IL-6, TNF-alpha) and other inflammatory mediators.

### Key determinants
Transcriptional (genomic) suppression of cytokine genes is the dominant mechanism underlying the broad, multi-system anti-inflammatory effect of glucocorticoids.

### Clinical significance
This mechanism underlies corticosteroid use across autoimmune, allergic, and inflammatory conditions, and is distinct from their separate metabolic and mineralocorticoid effects.

### Common misconceptions
Do not reduce corticosteroid action to only "membrane stabilisation" — the principal anti-inflammatory mechanism is genomic, transcription-factor-mediated cytokine suppression.

## hold_these
Corticosteroids inhibit NF-kB/AP-1-driven cytokine gene transcription.
This reduces production of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-alpha.

## lose_the_mark
Describing corticosteroid action only in terms of membrane stabilisation without the transcriptional cytokine-suppression mechanism.

## summary
Dexamethasone, given antenatally before anticipated preterm delivery, accelerates fetal surfactant production and lung maturation.

## sections
### Definition
Dexamethasone (with betamethasone) is one of the two antenatal corticosteroids used before anticipated preterm delivery.

### Mechanism
It induces type II pneumocyte surfactant production and structural lung maturation, reducing the incidence and severity of neonatal respiratory distress syndrome.

### Key determinants
Placental handling is the key determinant of fetal effect: dexamethasone crosses the placenta relatively unmetabolised by placental 11-beta-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase, unlike cortisol, which is largely inactivated by that enzyme.

### Clinical significance
Timing before anticipated preterm delivery is essential for the surfactant-inducing benefit to be realised before birth.

### Common misconceptions
Do not confuse antenatal corticosteroid administration (fetal lung maturation) with tocolytic therapy, which only delays labour rather than maturing the fetal lungs.

## hold_these
Dexamethasone is given antenatally to accelerate fetal lung (surfactant) maturation before anticipated preterm birth.
It crosses the placenta relatively unmetabolised, unlike cortisol.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing antenatal corticosteroids with tocolytics, or choosing cortisol instead (which is largely inactivated by the placenta).

## summary
Insulin glargine's slow, relatively peakless release profile allows once-daily basal dosing.

## sections
### Definition
Insulin glargine is a recombinant long-acting insulin analogue used for basal insulin replacement.

### Mechanism
Amino acid substitution plus an acidic formulation that microprecipitates in subcutaneous tissue gives a slow, continuous release lasting approximately 20-24 hours with a relatively flat (peakless) plasma profile.

### Key determinants
The duration and flatness of the absorption profile are what allow once-daily dosing, distinguishing glargine from intermediate-acting insulins such as NPH, which have a more pronounced peak and typically require twice-daily dosing.

### Clinical significance
Once-daily basal dosing simplifies regimens and reduces the risk of nocturnal hypoglycaemia compared with peaked intermediate-acting insulins.

### Common misconceptions
Do not mix insulin glargine in the same syringe as short-acting insulin — its acidic formulation precludes mixing, unlike NPH.

## hold_these
Insulin glargine has a slow, relatively peakless absorption profile lasting about 20-24 hours.
This allows once-daily basal dosing.

## lose_the_mark
Mixing glargine with short-acting insulin in the same syringe, or dosing it twice daily like NPH.

## summary
Metformin lowers blood glucose mainly by activating hepatic AMPK, suppressing gluconeogenesis and decreasing hepatic glucose output.

## sections
### Definition
Metformin is a biguanide oral antidiabetic drug.

### Mechanism
It activates AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) in hepatocytes, suppressing gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis and thereby decreasing hepatic glucose production.

### Key determinants
This insulin-independent hepatic action is the dominant determinant of metformin's glucose-lowering effect, distinguishing it from insulin secretagogues.

### Clinical significance
Because it does not stimulate insulin secretion, metformin does not cause hypoglycaemia as monotherapy, and it also modestly increases peripheral glucose uptake and reduces intestinal glucose absorption as secondary actions.

### Common misconceptions
Do not attribute metformin's main effect to increased insulin secretion (that is the mechanism of sulfonylureas, not metformin).

## hold_these
Metformin's dominant mechanism is decreasing hepatic glucose production via AMPK-mediated gluconeogenesis suppression.
It does not stimulate insulin secretion.

## lose_the_mark
Stating that metformin works primarily by stimulating pancreatic insulin release.
