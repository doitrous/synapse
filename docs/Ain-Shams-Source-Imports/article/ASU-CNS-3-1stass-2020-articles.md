## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: Complete transection of the spinal cord severs every ascending and descending pathway bilaterally at that level, so below the lesion there is total loss of all sensory modalities (touch, pain, temperature, proprioception, vibration) together with bilateral paraplegia (or tetraplegia, if cervical) from loss of all descending motor control. This distinguishes complete transection from partial cord syndromes such as Brown-Séquard syndrome (hemisection, dissociated ipsilateral/contralateral losses) or anterior cord syndrome (bilateral pain/temperature loss with dorsal columns spared), which selectively spare specific tracts because only part of the cord cross-section is damaged.

## sections
### Definition
Complete transection of the spinal cord severs every ascending and descending pathway bilaterally at that level, so below the lesion there is total loss of all sensory modalities (touch, pain, temperature, proprioception, vibration) together with bilateral paraplegia (or tetraplegia, if cervical) from loss of all descending motor control. This distinguishes complete transection from partial cord syndromes such as Brown-Séquard syndrome (hemisection, dissociated ipsilateral/contralateral losses) or anterior cord syndrome (bilateral pain/temperature loss with dorsal columns spared), which selectively spare specific tracts because only part of the cord cross-section is damaged.

### Mechanism
State that complete spinal cord transection causes bilateral paraplegia with total (all-modality) sensory loss below the lesion, unlike partial cord syndromes.

### Key determinants
Confusing complete transection (total bilateral loss of every modality) with partial syndromes like Brown-Séquard (dissociated ipsilateral/contralateral losses) or anterior cord syndrome (dorsal columns spared) — only complete transection abolishes every tract.

### Clinical significance
Recognising the complete-transection pattern (no sparing of any modality) is the first step in localising and grading traumatic spinal cord injury.

### Common misconceptions
Confusing complete transection (total bilateral loss of every modality) with partial syndromes like Brown-Séquard (dissociated ipsilateral/contralateral losses) or anterior cord syndrome (dorsal columns spared) — only complete transection abolishes every tract.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: Each embryonic brain vesicle encloses part of the ventricular system: the paired lateral ventricles are the cavities of the telencephalon, the third ventricle is the cavity of the diencephalon, the cerebral aqueduct is the cavity of the mesencephalon, and the fourth ventricle is the cavity of the rhombencephalon (hindbrain, comprising the developing pons/cerebellum and medulla). ## sections
### Definition
Each embryonic brain vesicle encloses part of the ventricular system: the paired lateral ventricles are the cavities of the telencephalon, the third ventricle is the cavity of the diencephalon, the cerebral aqueduct is the cavity of the mesencephalon, and the fourth ventricle is the cavity of the rhombencephalon (hindbrain, comprising the developing pons/cerebellum and medulla). This embryological derivation is why the fourth ventricle lies between the pons/cerebellum and the medulla in the adult.

### Mechanism
State that the fourth ventricle develops as the cavity of the rhombencephalon (hindbrain).

### Key determinants
Confusing the fourth ventricle's origin (rhombencephalon/hindbrain) with the third ventricle's origin (diencephalon/forebrain) or the cerebral aqueduct's origin (mesencephalon/midbrain) — each major ventricular cavity maps to a different embryonic vesicle.

### Clinical significance
Knowing the vesicle-to-cavity mapping helps localise posterior fossa developmental anomalies (e.g. Dandy-Walker malformation) to the rhombencephalon-derived fourth ventricle.

### Common misconceptions
Confusing the fourth ventricle's origin (rhombencephalon/hindbrain) with the third ventricle's origin (diencephalon/forebrain) or the cerebral aqueduct's origin (mesencephalon/midbrain) — each major ventricular cavity maps to a different embryonic vesicle.

## summary

## sections
### Definition
Second-order fibres of the lateral spinothalamic tract decussate near their level of entry and ascend as the spinal lemniscus, which continues rostrally as the neospinothalamic tract — a fast, few-synapse pathway carrying sharp, well-localised (fast) pain directly to the ventral posterolateral thalamic nucleus and on to the somatosensory cortex. This is distinct from the paleospinothalamic pathway, a slower, multisynaptic route through the reticular formation and intralaminar thalamic nuclei that carries poorly localised, dull (slow) pain.

### Mechanism
Identify the neospinothalamic tract as the rostral continuation of the spinal lemniscus (lateral spinothalamic pathway), carrying fast pain.

### Key determinants
Confusing the neospinothalamic tract (fast, discriminative pain via the spinal lemniscus, few synapses, direct to VPL thalamus) with the paleospinothalamic tract (slow, poorly localised pain via the reticular formation and intralaminar thalamic nuclei, multisynaptic).

### Clinical significance
Distinguishing the fast (neospinothalamic) from the slow (paleospinothalamic) pain pathway explains the two-phase quality of acute pain (sharp then dull).

### Common misconceptions
Confusing the neospinothalamic tract (fast, discriminative pain via the spinal lemniscus, few synapses, direct to VPL thalamus) with the paleospinothalamic tract (slow, poorly localised pain via the reticular formation and intralaminar thalamic nuclei, multisynaptic).

### definition_of · CON-NEU-C04FD05E0EEF6C
Quote: The common facial vein is formed by the union of the (anterior) facial vein and the retromandibular vein's anterior division, in the upper part of the neck. It crosses superficial to the carotid sheath and drains directly into the internal jugular vein. This is distinct from other named tributaries (e.g. the occipital vein, which more commonly joins the internal jugular vein separately or via the posterior auricular vein), giving the internal jugular vein multiple, individually named venous inputs along its course.
