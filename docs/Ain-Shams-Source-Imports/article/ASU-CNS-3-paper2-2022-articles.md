## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: congenital (berry/saccular) aneurysms form at branch points of the circle of Willis, most often at the anterior communicating artery, the origin of the posterior communicating artery, or the middle cerebral artery bifurcation. Their rupture is the leading cause of spontaneous subarachnoid haemorrhage, classically a sudden "thunderclap" headache with blood in the CSF, distinct from Charcot-Bouchard microaneurysms which cause intracerebral haemorrhage.

## sections
### Definition
Congenital (berry/saccular) aneurysms form at branch points of the circle of Willis, most often at the anterior communicating artery, the origin of the posterior communicating artery from the internal carotid, or the middle cerebral artery bifurcation, where the vessel wall's muscular and elastic layers are congenitally deficient. Their rupture is the leading cause of spontaneous (non-traumatic) subarachnoid haemorrhage, classically presenting as a sudden, severe ("thunderclap") headache, sometimes precipitated by exertion, with blood found in the cerebrospinal fluid on lumbar puncture.

### Mechanism
State that rupture of a berry aneurysm at the circle of Willis is the classic cause of subarachnoid haemorrhage.

### Key determinants
This differs from Charcot-Bouchard microaneurysms, which form on small penetrating vessels deep within the brain parenchyma as a consequence of chronic hypertension and rupture to cause intracerebral, not subarachnoid, haemorrhage.

### Clinical significance
This batch does not extend the source's plain fact into diagnosis, treatment or management claims beyond what the compilation itself states.

### Common misconceptions
Confusing a congenital berry aneurysm (circle of Willis, subarachnoid haemorrhage) with a Charcot-Bouchard microaneurysm (small penetrating vessels, hypertension, intracerebral haemorrhage) — the two are different lesions on different vessels causing different bleeds.

## hold_these
Congenital (berry/saccular) aneurysms form at branch points of the circle of Willis, most often at the anterior communicating artery, the origin of the posterior communicating artery from the internal carotid, or the middle cerebral artery bifurcation. Their rupture is the leading cause of spontaneous subarachnoid haemorrhage, classically a sudden "thunderclap" headache with blood found in the CSF on lumbar puncture.

## lose_the_mark

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: decorticate posturing — bilateral upper-limb flexion with lower-limb extension — results from a lesion at or above the red nucleus that spares the rubrospinal tract, in contrast to decerebrate posturing (all four limbs extended), which reflects a more caudal, pontine-level lesion that also interrupts the rubrospinal tract.

## sections
### Definition
Decorticate (decortication) posturing — bilateral flexion and adduction of the upper limbs at the elbow and wrist with extension of the lower limbs — results from a severe lesion at or above the level of the red nucleus (e.g. in the cerebral hemispheres, internal capsule or upper midbrain) that interrupts the corticospinal and corticorubral pathways while sparing the rubrospinal tract, which continues to drive upper-limb flexor tone.

### Mechanism
State that decorticate posturing (upper-limb flexion, lower-limb extension) localises a lesion above the red nucleus, in contrast to the more caudal lesion of decerebrate posturing.

### Key determinants
Decerebrate (decerebration) posturing — extension of all four limbs — results from a lower, pontine-level lesion that additionally interrupts the rubrospinal tract itself, leaving only the extensor-promoting vestibulospinal and pontine reticulospinal tracts active; decerebrate posturing therefore indicates a more caudal, generally worse-prognosis lesion than decorticate posturing.

### Clinical significance
This batch does not extend the source's plain fact into diagnosis, treatment or management claims beyond what the compilation itself states.

### Common misconceptions
Confusing decorticate posturing (upper limb flexed, lesion above the red nucleus) with decerebrate posturing (all four limbs extended, lower pontine lesion) — the direction of the upper-limb response is the key distinguishing sign, and decerebrate posturing indicates a more caudal, worse-prognosis lesion.

## hold_these
Decorticate posturing (bilateral upper-limb flexion, lower-limb extension) results from a lesion at or above the red nucleus that spares the rubrospinal tract; decerebrate posturing (all four limbs extended) reflects a more caudal, pontine-level lesion that also interrupts the rubrospinal tract.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing decorticate posturing (upper limb flexed) with decerebrate posturing (all four limbs extended) — the direction of the upper-limb response is the key distinguishing sign.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: pilocytic astrocytoma, the most common paediatric brain tumour, characteristically arises in the cerebellum and shows a biphasic pattern of pilocytic bipolar cells and microcystic areas, with Rosenthal fibres as its histological hallmark; it is a slow-growing, WHO grade 1 tumour with an excellent prognosis after resection, unlike glioblastoma.

## sections
### Definition
Pilocytic astrocytoma (WHO grade 1) is the most common paediatric brain tumour and characteristically arises in the cerebellum, presenting with signs of raised intracranial pressure and cerebellar dysfunction (ataxia, dysmetria) in a child. Histologically it shows a biphasic pattern of compact, elongated ("hair-like", pilocytic) bipolar cells alternating with looser microcystic areas, and its hallmark feature is the Rosenthal fibre — an eosinophilic, corkscrew-shaped, degenerating astrocytic process.

### Mechanism
State that pilocytic astrocytoma is a childhood cerebellar tumour whose histological hallmark is the Rosenthal fibre.

### Key determinants
Unlike glioblastoma, pilocytic astrocytoma is a slow-growing, well-circumscribed tumour with an excellent prognosis after surgical resection.

### Clinical significance
This batch does not extend the source's plain fact into diagnosis, treatment or management claims beyond what the compilation itself states.

### Common misconceptions
Confusing pilocytic astrocytoma's Rosenthal fibres with medulloblastoma (also a childhood posterior-fossa tumour, but composed of sheets of primitive small round blue cells with no Rosenthal fibres) or with glioblastoma's necrosis and microvascular proliferation.

## hold_these
Pilocytic astrocytoma, the most common paediatric brain tumour, characteristically arises in the cerebellum, with Rosenthal fibres as its histological hallmark; it is a slow-growing, WHO grade 1 tumour with an excellent prognosis after resection.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing pilocytic astrocytoma's Rosenthal fibres with medulloblastoma (primitive small round blue cells, no Rosenthal fibres) or glioblastoma (necrosis and microvascular proliferation).

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: the stria terminalis is a thin, C-shaped fibre bundle carrying the amygdala's principal efferent output to the hypothalamus and septal area, distinct from the Papez circuit's hippocampus-fornix-mammillary body-cingulate loop, mediating the autonomic and neuroendocrine components of amygdala-generated emotional and fear responses.

## sections
### Definition
The stria terminalis is a thin, C-shaped fibre bundle that follows the curve of the lateral ventricle from the amygdala, in the temporal lobe, forward to the hypothalamus (chiefly its anterior and ventromedial nuclei) and the septal area, carrying the amygdala's principal efferent output to these targets.

### Mechanism
State that the stria terminalis connects the amygdala with the hypothalamus, as the amygdala's principal efferent pathway.

### Key determinants
It is the amygdala's own major projection pathway, distinct from the Papez circuit's hippocampus-fornix-mammillary body-cingulate loop, and mediates the autonomic and neuroendocrine (hypothalamic) components of emotional and fear responses generated in the amygdala.

### Clinical significance
This batch does not extend the source's plain fact into diagnosis, treatment or management claims beyond what the compilation itself states.

### Common misconceptions
Confusing the stria terminalis (amygdala's own efferent pathway to the hypothalamus and septal area) with the fornix (the Papez circuit's hippocampus-to-mammillary-body pathway) — two separate limbic white-matter tracts arising from two different limbic structures.

## hold_these
The stria terminalis is the amygdala's principal efferent pathway, connecting it with the hypothalamus and septal area, distinct from the Papez circuit's fornix.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing the stria terminalis (amygdala's efferent pathway) with the fornix (the Papez circuit's hippocampus-to-mammillary-body pathway).

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: Alzheimer disease is characterised histologically by two hallmark lesions, extracellular neuritic (senile) plaques (beta-amyloid core) and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles (hyperphosphorylated tau), accumulating in the hippocampus, entorhinal cortex and neocortex and driving progressive cortical atrophy and cognitive decline.

## sections
### Definition
Alzheimer disease, the most common cause of dementia, is characterised histologically by two hallmark lesions: extracellular neuritic (senile) plaques, composed of a core of beta-amyloid protein surrounded by degenerating neuronal processes, and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles, composed of paired helical filaments of hyperphosphorylated tau protein.

### Mechanism
State that neuritic plaques (beta-amyloid) and neurofibrillary tangles (hyperphosphorylated tau) are the histological hallmarks of Alzheimer disease.

### Key determinants
These lesions accumulate progressively in the hippocampus, entorhinal cortex and neocortex, driving progressive cortical atrophy and the disease's characteristic short-term memory loss and cognitive decline.

### Clinical significance
This batch does not extend the source's plain fact into diagnosis, treatment or management claims beyond what the compilation itself states.

### Common misconceptions
Confusing Alzheimer's extracellular beta-amyloid plaques and intracellular tau tangles with Huntington disease's caudate atrophy or Parkinson disease's alpha-synuclein Lewy bodies — each neurodegenerative disease has a distinct, non-interchangeable histological signature.

## hold_these
Alzheimer disease is characterised histologically by extracellular neuritic (senile) plaques (beta-amyloid) and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles (hyperphosphorylated tau), accumulating in the hippocampus, entorhinal cortex and neocortex.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing Alzheimer's plaques and tangles with Huntington disease's caudate atrophy or Parkinson disease's Lewy bodies.
