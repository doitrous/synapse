## summary
This ASU Year 1 Locomotor Parasitology practical slice teaches the identifying anatomy of the arthropod ectoparasites and vectors covered in the practical image-identification quiz: hard vs soft ticks, flea combs, louse mouthparts and species, myiasis-causing flies, and the blackfly vector of onchocerciasis.

## sections
### Definition
The practical set asks students to identify arrowed structures on arthropod ectoparasites and vectors relevant to skin and subcutaneous disease: hard and soft ticks, fleas, lice, myiasis-causing flies, and the blackfly vector of onchocerciasis.

### Mechanism
Hard ticks (Ixodidae) carry a dorsal scutum, a capitulum visible from above, and posterior festoons; soft ticks (Argasidae) lack all three. Fleas (Siphonaptera) are speciated partly by combs of stout spines (ctenidia): the genal comb on the lower head margin, and the pronotal comb on the posterior margin of the first thoracic segment. Lice are wingless, dorsoventrally flattened, with piercing-sucking mouthparts retracted into the head; Pediculus humanus exists as capitis (head louse) and corporis (body louse, the vector for epidemic typhus, relapsing fever and trench fever) ecotypes, while Phthirus pubis (crab louse) is shorter and broader with crab-like hind legs. Myiasis-causing flies are told apart by larval/adult features: Sarcophaga (larviparous, three thoracic stripes), Calliphora (metallic blue-black, lays eggs on wounds), Wohlfahrtia (invades healthy tissue), and Musca (a non-biting mechanical vector, the "non-myiasis" contrast case). Simulium (blackfly) transmits Onchocerca volvulus by depositing infective larvae during a bite.

### Key determinants
Lice are dorsoventrally flattened and cement nits to hair/clothing fibres; fleas are laterally flattened, jump on powerful hind legs, and scatter eggs loosely in the environment — this pairing is the commonest identification confusion in the set. Simulium breeds specifically in fast-flowing rivers, which is why onchocerciasis clusters along river valleys ("river blindness"), unlike the standing-water mosquito vectors of other filarial and arboviral disease.

### Clinical significance
Identify each organism from its diagnostic feature(s), differentiate lice from fleas by body-flattening plane and egg-laying habitat, and name Simulium as the specific river-breeding vector of Onchocerca volvulus rather than a mosquito.

## hold_these
Hard tick: scutum + visible capitulum + festoons. Soft tick: none of the three.
Flea: genal comb (head) + pronotal comb (thorax); presence/absence is diagnostic.
Lice are dorsoventrally flat and cement nits; fleas are laterally flat, jump, and scatter eggs.
Simulium (blackfly, river-breeding) transmits Onchocerca volvulus.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming a visible capitulum means a soft tick -- it is the hard-tick feature.
Confusing Simulium (Onchocerca) with mosquitoes (lymphatic filariasis/malaria vectors).

## summary
This ASU Year 1 Locomotor Parasitology practical slice teaches the identification of Onchocerca volvulus and Trichinella spiralis adult worms, the kinetoplast structure of trypanosomatid flagellates, and the clinical presentations of trichinosis, leishmaniasis and cutaneous larva migrans.

## sections
### Definition
The practical set also asks students to identify helminth and protozoan organisms and to recognise the skin/soft-tissue presentations they cause: Onchocerca volvulus, Trichinella spiralis, trypanosomatid flagellates, and the clinical pictures of trichinosis, leishmaniasis and cutaneous larva migrans.

### Mechanism
Adult Onchocerca volvulus pairs live coiled inside subcutaneous nodules: the female is much longer (up to about 50 cm) and is found tightly coiled, the male is short (2-4 cm) and moves freely within the nodule, and the female's released microfilariae — not the adults — cause the itching, skin change and blindness of the disease. Adult Trichinella spiralis females (about 3 mm) are roughly twice the male's length (about 1.5 mm); the male's posterior carries a bluntly forked tail with no spicule, while the ovoviviparous female has an anteriorly placed vulva releasing larvae directly into the intestinal mucosa. Migrating Trichinella larvae reaching highly vascular striated muscle and nail-bed capillaries produce periorbital oedema and splinter haemorrhages. Trypanosomatid flagellates (Leishmania, Trypanosoma) carry a kinetoplast — a mass of mitochondrial DNA at the base of the flagellum — alongside the nucleus. Phlebotomus (sandfly) injects the Leishmania promastigote form, which transforms to the amastigote form inside human macrophages; cutaneous leishmaniasis evolves from papule to painless nodule to a centrally ulcerating lesion. Animal hookworm larvae causing cutaneous larva migrans penetrate skin but lack the enzymes to cross the human dermal basement membrane, producing an itchy, serpiginous, slowly advancing track.

### Key determinants
Size and position within the nodule (coiled/long vs free/short) separate Onchocerca sexes; anterior vulva plus ovoviviparity (not egg-laying) separates the Trichinella female; the kinetoplast's constant position at the flagellar base, distinct from the nucleus, confirms a trypanosomatid on smear; and which parasite stage is present (promastigote injected by the vector vs amastigote seen in human tissue) is the determinant tested for Leishmania.

### Clinical significance
Distinguish male from female Onchocerca and Trichinella by size/position, identify a trypanosomatid flagellate by its kinetoplast, and match each clinical presentation (periorbital oedema/splinter haemorrhage, nodule-to-ulcer, serpiginous track) to its organism.

## hold_these
Onchocerca: female long and coiled, male short and free within the nodule; microfilariae (not adults) cause disease.
Trichinella: female ~3 mm ovoviviparous with anterior vulva; male ~1.5 mm with forked tail, no spicule.
Kinetoplast = mitochondrial DNA mass at the flagellar base, alongside the nucleus, in trypanosomatid flagellates.
Leishmania: sandfly injects promastigote; amastigote is the tissue form seen on smear.

## lose_the_mark
Attributing Onchocerca pathology to the adult worms rather than the released microfilariae.
Expecting Trichinella eggs on stool exam -- it releases larvae, not eggs.
Naming the promastigote as the tissue-smear form of Leishmania -- that is the amastigote.
