## sections
### Definition
Secondary hyperalgesia is an exaggerated pain response to a stimulus applied to normal, uninjured skin surrounding (but not within) an area of tissue damage. Unlike primary hyperalgesia, which results from direct peripheral sensitisation of nociceptors within the damaged tissue itself, secondary hyperalgesia arises from central sensitisation — increased excitability of dorsal horn neurons receiving convergent input from the surrounding, undamaged skin — so the surrounding skin becomes painfully sensitive even though its own nociceptors were never directly injured.

### Mechanism
Attribute increased pain sensitivity in the normal skin surrounding an injury (not the injury itself) to secondary hyperalgesia, driven by central sensitisation.

### Key determinants
Confusing secondary hyperalgesia (exaggerated pain in the normal skin surrounding an injury, from central sensitisation) with primary hyperalgesia (exaggerated pain within the injured tissue itself, from peripheral nociceptor sensitisation) — the two share a mechanism family but occur in different tissue and by a different route.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: Each cerebellar hemisphere coordinates voluntary movement of the limbs on its own (ipsilateral) side of the body, because cerebellar efferent pathways cross twice — once leaving the deep cerebellar nuclei toward the contralateral red nucleus/thalamus, and again when the corticospinal tract they influence subsequently decussates in the medulla — restoring a net ipsilateral relationship between hemisphere and limb. This is the opposite laterality to the cerebral motor cortex, which controls the contralateral side after a single decussation, and it is why a unilateral cerebellar lesion produces incoordination (ataxia, dysmetria) on the same side as the lesion.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: The primary motor cortex (precentral gyrus, Brodmann area 4) gives rise to corticospinal fibres that decussate at the medullary pyramids; a lesion of the cortex itself — for example, ischaemic infarction in the territory of the blood vessels supplying that part of the cortex — therefore produces an upper motor neuron syndrome of spastic paralysis (hemiplegia), exaggerated tendon reflexes, and an extensor plantar response on the contralateral side of the body. This is the same general upper motor neuron laterality rule that applies to a lesion anywhere along the corticospinal pathway above its medullary decussation, whether at the cortex itself or, as classically taught for the internal capsule, further downstream.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: Because dorsal column fibres (fasciculus gracilis for the lower limb, fasciculus cuneatus for the upper limb) ascend on the same side of the spinal cord and do not decussate until they form internal arcuate fibres in the lower medulla, a unilateral lesion of the fasciculus gracilis — for example, from cord compression or a demyelinating plaque — causes loss of vibration and proprioceptive sense on the ipsilateral (same) side of the body, below the level of the lesion, not the contralateral side. A right fasciculus gracilis lesion, for instance, produces loss of vibration sense in the right leg (including the right tibia), the mirror image of the crossed pattern seen with spinothalamic (pain/temperature) tract lesions.

## sections
### Definition
Because dorsal column fibres (fasciculus gracilis for the lower limb, fasciculus cuneatus for the upper limb) ascend on the same side of the spinal cord and do not decussate until they form internal arcuate fibres in the lower medulla, a unilateral lesion of the fasciculus gracilis — for example, from cord compression or a demyelinating plaque — causes loss of vibration and proprioceptive sense on the ipsilateral (same) side of the body, below the level of the lesion, not the contralateral side. A right fasciculus gracilis lesion, for instance, produces loss of vibration sense in the right leg (including the right tibia), the mirror image of the crossed pattern seen with spinothalamic (pain/temperature) tract lesions.

### Mechanism
State that a unilateral dorsal column (fasciculus gracilis) lesion causes ipsilateral, not contralateral, loss of vibration sense below the lesion.

### Key determinants
Assuming a spinal cord sensory lesion always produces contralateral loss, as it does for the spinothalamic tract — the dorsal columns are uncrossed until the medulla, so their lesions produce ipsilateral loss, the opposite pattern (this is also the basis of the ipsilateral half of Brown-Séquard syndrome).

## sections
### Definition
The Golgi tendon organ, situated in series with extrafusal muscle fibres at the musculotendinous junction, signals muscle tension via its Ib afferent fibres. These Ib afferents enter the spinal cord and synapse on an inhibitory interneuron, which in turn inhibits the alpha motor neurons supplying the same (homonymous) muscle — a disynaptic circuit, not a direct Ib-to-alpha-motor-neuron synapse — so that excessive tension in a muscle reflexively reduces its own contraction, a protective reflex termed autogenic (inverse myotatic) inhibition.

### Mechanism
Trace the Golgi tendon organ reflex arc: Ib afferent to an inhibitory interneuron to inhibition of the alpha motor neurons of the same muscle (autogenic inhibition), noting the circuit is disynaptic, not monosynaptic.

### Key determinants
Assuming the Golgi tendon organ's Ib afferents synapse directly on the alpha motor neuron, as the monosynaptic stretch reflex does — the tendon organ reflex is disynaptic, routed through an inhibitory interneuron, and it inhibits (not excites) the alpha motor neurons of the same muscle.
