## summary
Steroid and thyroid hormones are lipid-soluble, cross the plasma membrane, and bind intracellular receptors that act as transcription factors to alter gene expression.

## sections
### Definition
Lipophilic hormones (steroid hormones and thyroid hormone) are small, lipid-soluble molecules that diffuse across the plasma membrane and bind intracellular (cytoplasmic or nuclear) receptors. The hormone-receptor complex translocates to the nucleus (or is already there) and acts as a transcription factor, binding hormone-response elements on DNA to alter gene expression, producing effects over a timescale of hours rather than seconds.

### Mechanism
After diffusing across the plasma membrane, the hormone binds its cytoplasmic or nuclear receptor. The hormone-receptor complex undergoes a conformational change, dimerises, and binds hormone-response elements in DNA, recruiting coactivators to alter transcription of target genes.

### Key determinants
Lipid solubility is the determinant that allows direct membrane diffusion; receptor location (cytoplasmic vs already-nuclear) and the specific hormone-response element determine which genes are regulated.

### Clinical significance
This mechanism explains the delayed onset (hours) but prolonged duration of action of steroid and thyroid hormone therapy, and why genomic effects persist after the hormone is cleared, since new proteins must be synthesised and degraded.

### Common misconceptions
Do not assume all hormones act through cell-surface receptors and second messengers — lipophilic hormones are the exception that act through direct transcriptional regulation.

## hold_these
Lipophilic hormones diffuse across the membrane and bind intracellular receptors.
The hormone-receptor complex acts as a transcription factor to alter gene expression.

## lose_the_mark
Stating that steroid or thyroid hormones act through a cell-surface G-protein-coupled receptor.

## summary
Cholera toxin locks Gs active and pertussis toxin locks Gi inactive, both by ADP-ribosylation, so both toxins cause sustained adenylyl cyclase activity and elevated cAMP.

## sections
### Definition
Cholera toxin ADP-ribosylates the Gs-alpha subunit, preventing GTP hydrolysis and locking Gs in its active state, causing continuous, unregulated stimulation of adenylyl cyclase and persistently elevated intracellular cAMP. Pertussis toxin ADP-ribosylates Gi-alpha, preventing it from inhibiting adenylyl cyclase, which also raises cAMP.

### Mechanism
Cholera toxin's A subunit ADP-ribosylates a conserved arginine on Gs-alpha, abolishing its intrinsic GTPase activity so Gs remains GTP-bound and continuously stimulates adenylyl cyclase. Pertussis toxin ADP-ribosylates a cysteine on Gi-alpha, uncoupling it from its receptor so it can no longer inhibit adenylyl cyclase; the net effect of both is unopposed, elevated cAMP production.

### Key determinants
The determinant is which G-alpha subunit is modified (Gs vs Gi) and the resulting loss of normal on/off regulation of adenylyl cyclase.

### Clinical significance
In cholera, the resulting rise in enterocyte cAMP drives massive chloride and water secretion into the gut lumen via CFTR, causing the profuse watery diarrhoea characteristic of the disease.

### Common misconceptions
Do not assume both toxins act identically on the same G protein — they modify different G-alpha subunits (Gs vs Gi) but converge on the same downstream effect of raised cAMP.

## hold_these
Cholera toxin locks Gs-alpha active by ADP-ribosylation, continuously stimulating adenylyl cyclase.
Pertussis toxin ADP-ribosylates Gi-alpha, preventing it from inhibiting adenylyl cyclase.

## lose_the_mark
Stating that cholera toxin inhibits adenylyl cyclase (it activates it) or that pertussis toxin acts on Gs (it acts on Gi).

## summary
Growth hormone binds a cytokine-family receptor with no intrinsic kinase activity, which signals via associated JAK kinases that phosphorylate STAT transcription factors.

## sections
### Definition
Growth hormone binds a cytokine-receptor-family receptor that lacks intrinsic enzymatic activity but is constitutively associated with cytoplasmic Janus kinases (JAKs). Ligand-induced receptor dimerisation juxtaposes and activates the JAKs, which phosphorylate the receptor and recruit STAT (signal transducer and activator of transcription) proteins; phosphorylated STATs dimerise and translocate to the nucleus to regulate gene transcription.

### Mechanism
GH-induced receptor dimerisation brings two receptor-associated JAK2 molecules together, allowing trans-phosphorylation and activation. Activated JAK2 phosphorylates tyrosines on the receptor and on STAT5, which then dimerises, translocates to the nucleus and drives transcription of GH-responsive genes including IGF-1.

### Key determinants
The determinant is that the GH receptor itself has no catalytic activity — signalling depends entirely on the associated JAK kinase, distinguishing this from receptor tyrosine kinases like the insulin receptor which phosphorylate substrates directly.

### Clinical significance
This pathway underlies GH's stimulation of hepatic IGF-1 production; mutations disrupting JAK2-STAT5 signalling (e.g., GH receptor defects) cause GH insensitivity (Laron syndrome) despite normal or elevated GH levels.

### Common misconceptions
Do not confuse the JAK-STAT pathway (growth hormone, prolactin, many cytokines) with receptor tyrosine kinase signalling (insulin, IGF-1), which uses an intrinsically catalytic receptor rather than an associated kinase.

## hold_these
Growth hormone's receptor has no intrinsic kinase activity and signals via associated JAK kinases.
Activated JAK phosphorylates STAT, which dimerises and regulates transcription.

## lose_the_mark
Stating that growth hormone acts through a G-protein-coupled receptor or through intrinsic receptor tyrosine kinase activity.

## summary
The insulin receptor is an alpha2-beta2 tetramer whose beta subunits carry intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity, activated when insulin binds the extracellular alpha subunits.

## sections
### Definition
The insulin receptor is a disulfide-linked alpha2-beta2 tetramer: the two extracellular alpha subunits bind insulin, and the two transmembrane beta subunits each possess intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity in their cytoplasmic domain. Insulin binding to the alpha subunits induces a conformational change that activates the beta subunits' kinase, causing autophosphorylation and subsequent phosphorylation of substrates such as insulin receptor substrate (IRS) proteins.

### Mechanism
Insulin binding to the alpha subunits induces a conformational change transmitted across the membrane to the beta subunits, activating their intracellular tyrosine kinase domains. The beta subunits autophosphorylate each other in trans, then phosphorylate tyrosines on IRS-1/IRS-2, creating docking sites for downstream signalling proteins (PI3K, Grb2) that mediate insulin's metabolic and growth effects.

### Key determinants
The determinant of activation is insulin binding to the alpha subunits, which is transmitted structurally to switch on the beta subunit's intrinsic kinase — no separate associated kinase is required, unlike cytokine-receptor-family hormones.

### Clinical significance
Mutations in the insulin receptor kinase domain cause severe insulin resistance syndromes; this receptor tyrosine kinase mechanism is also the basis for insulin's ability to activate the PI3K-Akt pathway driving GLUT4 translocation and glycogen synthesis.

### Common misconceptions
Do not assume the insulin receptor needs an associated JAK kinase like growth hormone's receptor — the insulin receptor's kinase activity is intrinsic to the beta subunit itself.

## hold_these
The insulin receptor beta subunit has intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity.
Insulin binding to the alpha subunits activates this kinase, causing autophosphorylation and IRS phosphorylation.

## lose_the_mark
Stating that the insulin receptor's alpha subunit has kinase activity, or that the receptor requires an associated JAK kinase.

## summary
Glucagon activates its Gs-coupled receptor in the fixed sequence G protein → adenylyl cyclase → cAMP → protein kinase A → cellular effect.

## sections
### Definition
Glucagon binding to its Gs-protein-coupled receptor activates Gs-alpha, which stimulates adenylyl cyclase to convert ATP to cyclic AMP (cAMP). Rising cAMP binds the regulatory subunits of protein kinase A (PKA), releasing active catalytic subunits that phosphorylate downstream target enzymes (e.g., glycogen phosphorylase kinase, hormone-sensitive lipase) to produce the hormone's metabolic effects.

### Mechanism
Glucagon binding activates the receptor-coupled Gs-alpha subunit (GTP-bound), which stimulates adenylyl cyclase to convert ATP into cAMP. cAMP binds PKA's regulatory subunits, releasing catalytic subunits that phosphorylate glycogen phosphorylase kinase (promoting glycogenolysis) and inhibit glycogen synthase, raising blood glucose.

### Key determinants
Each step is strictly sequential and dependent on the one before it; the determinant of cascade amplitude is the amount of active Gs and the resulting cAMP concentration.

### Clinical significance
This cascade is the classic paradigm for Gs-coupled hormone signalling and underlies glucagon's rapid glycogenolytic response to hypoglycaemia, distinguishing it from insulin's opposing PI3K-based signalling.

### Common misconceptions
Do not place PKA activation before cAMP generation, or the G protein after adenylyl cyclase — the cascade proceeds strictly receptor → G protein → adenylyl cyclase → cAMP → PKA.

## hold_these
Glucagon's cascade proceeds: G protein activates adenylyl cyclase, which generates cAMP, which activates protein kinase A.

## lose_the_mark
Reversing any step of the sequence, such as placing protein kinase A activation before cAMP generation.

## summary
Nitric oxide diffuses across the membrane and binds soluble guanylyl cyclase in the cytoplasm, generating cGMP.

## sections
### Definition
Nitric oxide (NO) is a lipophilic gas that diffuses freely across the plasma membrane and binds the heme moiety of cytoplasmic (soluble) guanylyl cyclase, activating the enzyme to convert GTP to cyclic GMP (cGMP). Rising cGMP activates protein kinase G, which in vascular smooth muscle lowers intracellular calcium and causes relaxation (vasodilation).

### Mechanism
NO binds the heme prosthetic group of soluble guanylyl cyclase, inducing a conformational change that markedly increases the enzyme's catalytic conversion of GTP to cGMP. cGMP then activates protein kinase G, which phosphorylates targets that lower intracellular calcium and cause smooth muscle relaxation.

### Key determinants
The determinant of NO's rapid, receptor-independent membrane crossing is its small, uncharged, gaseous nature; the determinant of downstream effect is the local concentration of active soluble guanylyl cyclase and cGMP.

### Clinical significance
This pathway is the basis of nitrate/nitroprusside vasodilator therapy and of phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors (e.g., sildenafil), which prolong cGMP action by blocking its breakdown.

### Common misconceptions
Do not confuse NO's soluble (cytoplasmic) guanylyl cyclase receptor with ANF's membrane-bound (particulate) guanylyl cyclase receptor — both generate cGMP but via distinct receptor forms.

## hold_these
Nitric oxide diffuses across the membrane and activates cytoplasmic soluble guanylyl cyclase.
This raises cGMP, which activates protein kinase G.

## lose_the_mark
Stating that nitric oxide acts on a membrane-bound receptor or through cAMP rather than cGMP.

## summary
Phospholipase C, activated by Gq-coupled receptors, hydrolyses membrane PIP2 into the second messengers DAG and IP3.

## sections
### Definition
Phospholipase C, typically activated downstream of Gq-protein-coupled receptors, hydrolyses the membrane phospholipid PIP2 (phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate) into two second messengers: diacylglycerol (DAG), which stays in the membrane and activates protein kinase C, and inositol trisphosphate (IP3), which diffuses into the cytosol and opens IP3 receptors on the endoplasmic reticulum, releasing stored calcium.

### Mechanism
Receptor activation of Gq-alpha stimulates membrane-bound phospholipase C-beta, which hydrolyses PIP2 into DAG (retained in the membrane, activating protein kinase C) and IP3 (soluble, diffusing to the endoplasmic reticulum to open IP3-gated calcium channels and raise cytosolic calcium).

### Key determinants
The determinant is which G protein couples the receptor: Gq activates phospholipase C, whereas Gs/Gi act on adenylyl cyclase instead — the choice of G-alpha subtype dictates which second-messenger pathway is engaged.

### Clinical significance
This pathway mediates the action of hormones and neurotransmitters such as angiotensin II, alpha-1 adrenergic agonists, and TRH, and its dysregulation contributes to processes such as vascular smooth muscle contraction and platelet activation.

### Common misconceptions
Do not attribute DAG/IP3 production to adenylyl cyclase or phospholipase A2 — only phospholipase C generates this specific pair of second messengers from PIP2.

## hold_these
Phospholipase C hydrolyses PIP2 into DAG and IP3.
DAG activates protein kinase C; IP3 releases calcium from the endoplasmic reticulum.

## lose_the_mark
Stating that adenylyl cyclase or phospholipase A2 generates DAG and IP3.

## summary

## sections
### Definition
Heterotrimeric G proteins (Galpha-Gbeta-Ggamma) are activated when an agonist-bound receptor catalyses GDP-GTP exchange on G-alpha, causing dissociation from G-beta-gamma. G-alpha has intrinsic GTPase activity, hydrolysing its own bound GTP back to GDP after a defined interval, which terminates the active state and allows reassociation of the heterotrimer — this self-inactivation is the built-in timer of G-protein signalling.

### Mechanism

### Common misconceptions
Do not assume G proteins need an external enzyme to turn off — inactivation is intrinsic to the G-alpha subunit itself via its own GTPase activity.

## summary

## sections
### Definition
Leucine and lysine are the only two amino acids classified as purely (exclusively) ketogenic: their catabolism yields only acetyl-CoA and/or acetoacetyl-CoA, precursors of ketone bodies and fatty acids, with no pathway to pyruvate or any TCA-cycle intermediate, so unlike every other amino acid they cannot serve as a substrate for gluconeogenesis.

### Common misconceptions
Do not assume all amino acids can contribute to gluconeogenesis — leucine and lysine are the fixed exceptions that are exclusively ketogenic.

### Definition

## summary
Galactokinase phosphorylates galactose to galactose-1-phosphate as the first step of galactose metabolism; its deficiency causes a cataract-only form of galactosemia.

## sections
### Definition
Galactokinase catalyses the first committed step of galactose metabolism, phosphorylating galactose to galactose-1-phosphate using ATP. Deficiency of galactokinase causes galactokinase-deficiency galactosemia (galactosemia type 2): unmetabolised galactose is instead shunted through aldose reductase to galactitol, which accumulates in the lens and causes cataracts, but — unlike classic (GALT-deficiency) galactosemia — does not cause liver failure, renal Fanconi syndrome, or E. coli sepsis risk, because galactose-1-phosphate itself (the toxic metabolite in classic galactosemia) does not accumulate.

### Mechanism
Galactokinase uses ATP to phosphorylate dietary galactose (from lactose digestion) to galactose-1-phosphate. Galactose-1-phosphate uridyltransferase (GALT) then converts this, using UDP-glucose, to glucose-1-phosphate and UDP-galactose; UDP-galactose-4-epimerase interconverts UDP-galactose and UDP-glucose to regenerate the cycle and allow entry of galactose carbons into glycolysis.

### Key determinants
The determinant of clinical severity across the galactosemia subtypes is which pathway enzyme is missing: galactokinase deficiency spares galactose-1-phosphate accumulation (mild, cataract-only), whereas GALT deficiency allows toxic galactose-1-phosphate to accumulate (severe, multi-organ).

### Clinical significance
Galactokinase-deficiency galactosemia typically presents with early infantile cataracts (from lenticular galactitol, an osmotically active sugar alcohol formed via aldose reductase) but normal liver and kidney function, and is treated by galactose restriction.

### Common misconceptions
Do not equate galactokinase deficiency with classic (GALT-deficiency) galactosemia — only GALT deficiency causes the severe hepatic, renal and infective complications; galactokinase deficiency is limited chiefly to cataracts.

## summary
Unlike glycolysis's three irreversible committed steps, phosphoglycerate kinase's reaction is near-equilibrium and runs equally in reverse during gluconeogenesis.

## sections
### Definition
Phosphoglycerate kinase catalyses the near-equilibrium, freely reversible transfer of a phosphate group from 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate to ADP, forming 3-phosphoglycerate and ATP. Unlike hexokinase, phosphofructokinase-1, and pyruvate kinase — the three irreversible, regulated 'committed steps' of glycolysis — phosphoglycerate kinase's reaction runs equally well in reverse and is used unchanged in gluconeogenesis.

### Mechanism
Phosphoglycerate kinase transfers the high-energy phosphate of 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate directly to ADP (substrate-level phosphorylation), forming ATP and 3-phosphoglycerate; because the free-energy change of this reaction is small, the same enzyme and reaction run in the reverse direction, unchanged, when gluconeogenesis proceeds from 3-phosphoglycerate back to 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate.

### Key determinants
The determinant of reversibility is the magnitude of the standard free-energy change: near-zero for phosphoglycerate kinase (reversible) versus large and negative for hexokinase, PFK1, and pyruvate kinase (irreversible, requiring separate bypass enzymes in gluconeogenesis).

### Clinical significance
Because it is shared unchanged by both pathways, phosphoglycerate kinase deficiency (an X-linked disorder) impairs both glycolysis and gluconeogenesis, causing haemolytic anaemia and, in some patients, myopathy and CNS effects.

### Common misconceptions
Do not assume every glycolytic 'kinase' is irreversible — phosphoglycerate kinase is the classic exception, distinct from hexokinase, PFK1, and pyruvate kinase.

## summary

## sections
### Definition
During early fasting the brain relies on glucose, but as starvation progresses (roughly 2-3 weeks), rising hepatic ketogenesis (driven by low insulin/high glucagon and increased adipose lipolysis) raises blood ketone body (beta-hydroxybutyrate, acetoacetate) concentrations enough that the brain switches to using ketone bodies as its predominant fuel, sparing glucose and reducing the rate of muscle protein breakdown needed for gluconeogenesis.

### Mechanism
Prolonged low insulin/high glucagon signalling maximally activates adipose lipolysis, delivering free fatty acids to the liver, where beta-oxidation and ketogenesis convert them to beta-hydroxybutyrate and acetoacetate. ### Key determinants
The determinant of this adaptation's timescale is the gradual induction of ketone-body-utilising enzymes in brain tissue and the progressive rise in circulating ketone body concentration over the first weeks of starvation.

### Clinical significance
This adaptation is teleologically protective, since it markedly reduces the rate of muscle (protein) breakdown otherwise needed to supply gluconeogenic amino acids, prolonging survival during famine; it is distinct from the pathological, rapid, unregulated ketoacidosis of type 1 diabetes.

### Common misconceptions
Do not confuse adaptive starvation ketosis (brain fuel-sparing, gradual, moderate ketone levels) with diabetic ketoacidosis (rapid, severe, accompanied by hyperglycaemia and acidosis) — the biochemistry of ketogenesis is similar but the clinical context and severity differ sharply.

## summary

## sections
### Definition
Among the counter-regulatory hormones (glucagon, epinephrine, cortisol, growth hormone), glucagon is the first and primary hormone to respond to falling blood glucose, being secreted within minutes by pancreatic alpha cells and rapidly stimulating hepatic glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis; epinephrine acts as an important rapid backup, while cortisol and growth hormone contribute only if hypoglycaemia is more prolonged.

The determinant of the response hierarchy is the speed and directness of each hormone's action on hepatic glucose output: glucagon acts fastest and most directly, hence responds first.

### Clinical significance
Loss of the glucagon response to hypoglycaemia (common in long-standing type 1 diabetes) leaves patients dependent on the slower/less robust epinephrine response, increasing the risk of severe, prolonged hypoglycaemia and hypoglycaemia unawareness.

### Common misconceptions
Do not assume epinephrine is the first responder to hypoglycaemia in a healthy individual — glucagon normally responds first; epinephrine's prominence rises specifically when glucagon secretion is impaired.

## summary
When insulin is low, unrestrained lipolysis and disinhibited hepatic fatty acid oxidation together drive ketone body production.

## sections
### Definition
Low circulating insulin (as in fasting, or absolute deficiency in type 1 diabetes) removes insulin's normal restraint on hormone-sensitive lipase, permitting adipose lipolysis and free fatty acid release; low insulin also fails to suppress malonyl-CoA production, so hepatic carnitine palmitoyltransferase-1 (CPT1) is not inhibited, allowing fatty acyl-CoA into mitochondria for beta-oxidation, generating acetyl-CoA that, in excess of TCA-cycle capacity, is diverted to ketone body (acetoacetate, beta-hydroxybutyrate) synthesis.

### Mechanism
With low insulin, hormone-sensitive lipase in adipocytes is disinhibited, releasing free fatty acids into the circulation. ### Key determinants
The determinant of ketogenesis is the combination of substrate supply (lipolysis-derived fatty acids) and disinhibited CPT1 (low malonyl-CoA), both consequences of low insulin.

### Clinical significance
Absolute insulin deficiency in type 1 diabetes can drive ketogenesis to a pathological degree, producing diabetic ketoacidosis; adequate insulin (even low-dose) is normally sufficient to suppress this process, which is why basal insulin is never omitted even when a type 1 diabetic patient is not eating.

### Common misconceptions
Do not attribute ketogenesis to high insulin states — it is specifically the absence/deficiency of insulin's restraining signal that permits both the lipolysis and the hepatic fatty acid oxidation required for ketone body formation.

## sections
### Definition

## summary

## sections
### Definition
Galactokinase deficiency (galactosemia type 2) classically presents in infancy with cataracts (from galactitol accumulation in the lens via the aldose reductase pathway) and a urine reducing-substance test that is positive (galactose reduces Benedict's/Clinitest reagent) while a glucose-specific enzymatic dipstick is negative, with normal blood glucose — since galactose-1-phosphate does not accumulate in this subtype, hepatic and renal function remain normal, unlike classic (GALT-deficiency) galactosemia.

### Mechanism

The determinant of interpreting this vignette is recognising that a reducing-substance-positive/glucose-dipstick-negative discrepancy always implies a non-glucose reducing sugar, and that isolated cataracts without hepatic/renal disease localises the defect to galactokinase rather than GALT.

### Clinical significance
Early recognition and dietary galactose restriction can prevent or halt progression of the cataracts, which is why urine reducing-substance testing remains a useful bedside screening tool in infants with unexplained cataracts.

### Common misconceptions
Do not assume a negative glucose dipstick rules out a sugar-related disorder — it only rules out glucose specifically; a positive reducing-substance test with a negative glucose dipstick should prompt testing for other reducing sugars such as galactose.

## hold_these

## summary
Hepatic absence of succinyl-CoA:3-ketoacid CoA transferase (SCOT) means the liver synthesises ketone bodies for export but cannot itself oxidise them as fuel.

## sections
### Definition
The liver, despite being the principal site of ketone body (acetoacetate, beta-hydroxybutyrate) synthesis (ketogenesis), lacks succinyl-CoA:3-ketoacid CoA transferase (SCOT/thiophorase), the enzyme required to activate acetoacetate to acetoacetyl-CoA for entry into the TCA cycle (ketolysis). This physiological absence ensures the liver exports the ketone bodies it produces for use as fuel by extrahepatic tissues such as brain, cardiac and skeletal muscle, which express SCOT.

### Mechanism
Hepatic mitochondrial beta-oxidation generates excess acetyl-CoA during fasting, which HMG-CoA synthase and HMG-CoA lyase convert to acetoacetate (and, via beta-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase, to beta-hydroxybutyrate) for release into the blood. Because the liver lacks SCOT, it cannot convert circulating or locally-produced acetoacetate back to acetoacetyl-CoA for TCA-cycle oxidation; extrahepatic tissues (brain, heart, skeletal muscle), which do express SCOT, take up these ketone bodies and use SCOT followed by thiolase to regenerate two acetyl-CoA molecules for energy.

### Key determinants
The determinant of this hepatic ketone body 'export-only' physiology is the tissue-specific absence of a single enzyme, SCOT, that is otherwise required for the CoA-activation step of ketolysis.

### Clinical significance
This division of labour is why the liver can safely fuel other organs during prolonged fasting or starvation without itself competing for its own ketone body output, and why hereditary SCOT deficiency (in extrahepatic tissues) causes severe, recurrent ketoacidosis due to inability to utilise ketone bodies.

### Common misconceptions
Do not assume the liver's inability to use ketone bodies is pathological — it is the normal, physiological consequence of the liver lacking SCOT, which allows it to be a net ketone body exporter rather than consumer.

## hold_these

## lose_the_mark

## summary

## sections
### Definition

### Clinical significance

## summary

## sections
### Definition

### Mechanism

### Key determinants
