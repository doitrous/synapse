## summary
The ASU MBG cytogenetic techniques lecture introduces conventional karyotype as a systematic chromosome arrangement and then separates high-resolution karyotype by its earlier mitotic timing.

## sections
### Definition
A conventional karyotype is presented as a systematic arrangement of the chromosomes of an individual.
The lecture lists dividing cells in the body as possible sample material, including peripheral blood lymphocytes, bone marrow and amniotic fluid.

### Mechanism
The teaching point is not a management instruction; it is the vocabulary needed to read cytogenetic test names.
Conventional karyotype is the chromosome-arrangement baseline against which the lecture later compares FISH and microarray.

### Key determinants
High-resolution karyotype is taught as stopping division at prophase or prometaphase, when chromosomes are still relatively uncondensed.
That timing is the distinction preserved here; no extra resolution threshold is inferred beyond the source text.

### Clinical significance
This is a local-curriculum foundation slice. Its safe use is to explain what the named karyotype methods mean before choosing among cytogenetic tests.

### Common misconceptions
Do not define karyotype as an isolated chromosome image; the source defines it as a systematic arrangement of chromosomes.
Do not add unsupported numeric resolution cut-offs to high-resolution karyotype.

## published_summary

## published_sections

## hold_these
A karyotype is a systematic arrangement of an individual's chromosomes.
High-resolution karyotype uses earlier prophase or prometaphase chromosomes that are still relatively uncondensed.

## lose_the_mark
Calling any chromosome picture a karyotype without the systematic arrangement idea.
Adding resolution thresholds that are not present in the local lecture.

## summary
The same ASU lecture contrasts FISH with karyotyping and then introduces microarray as a genome-wide copy-number method with a balanced-translocation limitation.

## sections
### Definition
FISH and microarray are introduced in this lecture as cytogenetic diagnostic techniques compared with conventional karyotype.
The lecture frames them by what they detect and by whether the requested target must already be known.

### Mechanism
In the ASU comparison, FISH can detect chromosomal aneuploidy or rearrangements of any size, takes less time than karyotyping, and requires knowing what to look for.
This article keeps that local wording narrow and does not add probe design or laboratory protocol details.

### Key determinants
Microarray is taught as detecting small copy-number rearrangements across the genome without needing to know the locus, while being unable to detect balanced translocations.
The lecture names deletion and duplication as detectable outputs in this microarray section.

### Clinical significance
This is not a treatment rule. Its value is diagnostic literacy: students compare what a technique can detect with what the clinical question asks.

### Common misconceptions
Do not present FISH as a non-targeted screen; the lecture says the requester has to know what they are looking for.
Do not present microarray as detecting balanced translocations; the lecture states that it is unable to detect them.

## published_summary

## published_sections

## hold_these
FISH is faster than karyotyping in the ASU comparison, but it requires knowing what to look for.
Microarray can detect deletions and duplications as copy-number changes, but not balanced translocations.

## lose_the_mark
Treating FISH as untargeted.
Treating microarray as a balanced-translocation test.
