## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: Pellagra results from a deficiency of niacin (vitamin B3, nicotinic acid/nicotinamide), a precursor of the coenzymes NAD and NADP used throughout cellular energy metabolism. It classically presents with the triad of the "3 Ds": a photosensitive, symmetrical dermatitis in sun-exposed skin, diarrhoea from gastrointestinal mucosal atrophy, and dementia (or a broader neuropsychiatric picture of confusion/encephalopathy) from CNS neuronal energy failure; a fourth D, death, follows if the deficiency is not corrected. Niacin can also be synthesised endogenously from the amino acid tryptophan, so pellagra classically occurs both in isolated niacin deficiency and in conditions of tryptophan deficiency/diversion (e.g. Hartnup disease, carcinoid syndrome), and it is treated by niacin/nicotinamide replacement.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: The urea cycle is the liver's main route for disposing of nitrogen waste (ammonia) as urea; a defect in the urea cycle, or severe liver failure, causes ammonia to accumulate in the blood (hyperammonaemia). In the brain, astrocytes are the principal site of ammonia detoxification: the enzyme glutamine synthetase combines the excess ammonia with glutamate to form glutamine. When ammonia delivery is high and sustained, this drives excessive intra-astrocytic glutamine accumulation, which is osmotically active and draws water into the astrocyte, causing astrocyte swelling and cerebral oedema — the core mechanism underlying hyperammonaemic (hepatic) encephalopathy, with clinical features ranging from confusion to coma.

### Mechanism

### Key determinants
Assuming ammonia itself is the osmotically active toxin causing astrocyte swelling, rather than recognising that ammonia is first detoxified to glutamine by astrocytic glutamine synthetase, and it is the resulting glutamine accumulation that is osmotically active.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: Methadone is a synthetic, long-acting full mu-opioid receptor agonist. Compared with morphine's relatively short elimination half-life, methadone's much longer half-life (roughly 15-60 hours) produces stable plasma levels with once-daily oral dosing and smooths out the peaks and troughs in opioid receptor occupancy that drive withdrawal symptoms. This pharmacokinetic property, rather than any difference in receptor selectivity, is the basis for using methadone as maintenance (substitution) therapy in opioid use disorder: it satisfies mu-receptor demand steadily enough to suppress withdrawal and craving while avoiding the rapid on/off reinforcing effect of shorter-acting opioids such as morphine or heroin.

### Definition
Methadone is a synthetic, long-acting full mu-opioid receptor agonist. ### Mechanism
State that methadone's much longer half-life than morphine's produces fewer/milder withdrawal symptoms, the pharmacokinetic basis for its use in opioid substitution (maintenance) therapy.

### Key determinants
Confusing methadone (a long-acting full mu-agonist used for substitution/maintenance) with naltrexone (a pure antagonist used for relapse prevention after detoxification) or naloxone (a short-acting antagonist used for acute overdose reversal) — the three drugs have related but mechanistically and clinically distinct roles in opioid use disorder management.
