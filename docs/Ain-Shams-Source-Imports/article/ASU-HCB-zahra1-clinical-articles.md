## summary
Four clinically anchored organelle facts tested by the ASU HCB Dr. Ahmed Zahra cytology bank (Part C) that have no existing pending cytology-concept coverage: defective proteasome function is implicated in the neurodegeneration of Alzheimer's disease; peroxisomal disorders characteristically affect the liver and kidney, the organs richest in peroxisomes; immature hepatocyte smooth endoplasmic reticulum (bilirubin-conjugating enzyme) underlies physiological neonatal jaundice; and thyroid hormone is activated (released from thyroglobulin) by lysosomal proteolysis in the thyroid follicular cell.

## sections
### Definition
Defective proteasome function is implicated in the neurodegeneration of Alzheimer's disease.
Peroxisomal disorders characteristically affect the liver and kidney, the organs where peroxisomes are normally most concentrated.
Immature hepatocyte smooth endoplasmic reticulum underlies physiological neonatal jaundice.
Thyroid hormone is activated by lysosomal proteolysis of thyroglobulin in the thyroid follicular cell.

### Mechanism
The ubiquitin-proteasome system is the cell's main route for clearing misfolded and abnormal cytosolic protein; when this clearance fails, misfolded protein species (such as those implicated in Alzheimer's disease) accumulate in neurons rather than being degraded, contributing to the disease's neurodegeneration.
Peroxisomes carry out beta-oxidation and detoxification reactions at their heaviest workload in the liver and kidney, so an inherited defect in peroxisomal biogenesis (as in Zellweger-spectrum disorders) disproportionately damages those same organs, alongside neurological involvement from disrupted peroxisomal lipid metabolism in the brain.
Bilirubin, produced from haemoglobin breakdown, must be conjugated by UDP-glucuronyltransferase in the hepatocyte's smooth endoplasmic reticulum before it can be excreted in bile; this enzyme system is not yet fully active at birth, so unconjugated bilirubin transiently accumulates as physiological neonatal jaundice until the sER matures over the first days to weeks of life.
Thyroglobulin, synthesised and iodinated within the thyroid follicle's colloid, is taken back up into the follicular cell by endocytosis; only after the resulting endosome fuses with a lysosome, whose proteolytic enzymes cleave free T3 and T4 from the thyroglobulin backbone, are the active hormones released into the circulation.

### Key determinants
"Defective… Alzheimer's disease" identifies the proteasome, not mitochondria, peroxisomes or sER.
"Defective… liver and kidney disease" identifies peroxisomes, matching their normal tissue distribution.
"Immature… neonatal jaundice" identifies smooth endoplasmic reticulum (the bilirubin-conjugating enzyme), not peroxisomes, mitochondria or proteasomes.
"Activation of thyroid hormone" identifies lysosomal proteolysis of thyroglobulin, not mitochondria or peroxisomes.

### Clinical significance
These four organelle-disease pairings are genuine, examinable clinical correlations built on pure cell-biology mechanism: proteasome failure and Alzheimer's disease connect protein quality control to neurodegeneration; peroxisomal disorders connect organelle tissue distribution to the pattern of organ involvement in inherited metabolic disease; physiological neonatal jaundice connects hepatocyte organelle maturity to a near-universal newborn finding; and thyroid hormone activation connects lysosomal digestion to endocrine physiology.

### Common misconceptions
Attributing Alzheimer's disease to defective mitochondria or sER rather than the proteasome, simply because those organelles appear as distractors in the same question.
Attributing peroxisomal-disorder organ involvement to any organ other than liver and kidney, the tissues where peroxisomes are normally most concentrated.
Attributing neonatal jaundice to immature peroxisomes rather than immature hepatocyte smooth endoplasmic reticulum (the actual site of the deficient bilirubin-conjugating enzyme).
Assuming thyroid hormone is "activated" by mitochondria or peroxisomes rather than by lysosomal proteolysis of thyroglobulin.
