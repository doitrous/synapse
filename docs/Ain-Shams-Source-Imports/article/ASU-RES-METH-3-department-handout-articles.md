## summary
Hypothesis testing is the point in a study where a research idea is converted into a decision. The ASU handout frames that decision around two opposing statements: the null hypothesis, where nothing is happening, and the alternative hypothesis, where the research effect or difference is present.

## sections
### Definition
Hypothesis testing is the research decision process used when a sample is collected to judge whether a proposed statement about a population is plausible.
It does not begin by proving the research idea directly. It begins with a statement about the population, collects sample data, then asks whether the data are unlikely enough under the null hypothesis to reject that null position.

### Mechanism
The ASU handout describes four steps: state the hypotheses, collect and summarise data while calculating the test statistic, assess the evidence, then make a decision.
That order matters. If the hypotheses are not stated first, the p-value has nothing precise to test against.

### Null and alternative hypotheses
The null hypothesis is the no-difference or no-effect position that is tested, while the alternative hypothesis is the opposing research statement.
For example, a null hypothesis may say that two treatment groups have the same mean reduction in blood glucose, while the alternative says that the mean reduction is different.

### Key determinants
The key determinant is whether the sample result would be rare if the null hypothesis were true. That judgement belongs to the p-value and is covered in the linked article.

### Clinical significance
This is not a treatment rule. Its clinical value is that students learn to read research results as decisions under uncertainty rather than as proof that a study's first idea was true.

### Common misconceptions
The commonest error is to treat the alternative hypothesis as the statement being directly proved. In the ASU sequence, the null hypothesis is the statement tested, and the conclusion is reject or fail to reject the null hypothesis.
Another error is to treat "fail to reject" as "prove true". It only means the sample did not provide sufficient evidence against the null hypothesis.

## summary
After hypotheses are stated, the ASU handout uses the p-value to judge the evidence against the null hypothesis. A small p-value supports rejecting the null hypothesis. The decision can still be wrong, and the two named wrong decisions are type I and type II errors.

## sections
### Definition
In this ASU handout, the p-value is read as the probability of observing data like the sample data, or more extreme, if the null hypothesis is true; a very small value is evidence against the null hypothesis.
The handout also teaches the usual cut-off as 0.05 for this module. That threshold is recorded here as ASU curriculum wording, not as a new recommendation beyond the source.

### Mechanism
The p-value only makes sense after a null hypothesis has been stated. The test asks what probability would be attached to results like the sample result if that null hypothesis were true.
If the probability is very small, the sample result is treated as evidence against the null hypothesis. If the probability is not very small, the sample does not provide sufficient evidence against the null hypothesis.

### Decision errors
A type I error rejects a true null hypothesis, whereas a type II error fails to reject a false null hypothesis.
The handout names the probability of type I error as alpha and the probability of type II error as beta. It also describes power as the probability of rejecting a false null hypothesis.

### Key determinants
The determinants in this slice are the truth of the null hypothesis, the decision made from the sample, and the p-value threshold selected before the decision. The exercise handout reinforces the same distinction by asking students to recognise type I error, type II error, level of significance and p-value statements.

### Clinical significance
This is a research-interpretation skill, not a bedside action. It keeps a student from reading every statistically non-significant result as proof of no effect, or every statistically significant result as proof that the proposed explanation is clinically important.

### Common misconceptions
The p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true.
Type I and type II errors are not interchangeable: one rejects a true null hypothesis, and the other fails to reject a false null hypothesis.
Statistical significance is not the same thing as clinical importance.
