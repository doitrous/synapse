## summary
This Ain Shams Year 1 foundation article covers the opening vocabulary of enzyme lecture 1: enzymes as biological catalysts, the ribozyme exception, the six IUBMB reaction classes, and the distinction between coenzymes and metal-ion cofactors.
## sections
### Definition
Enzymes are biological catalysts that increase the rate of chemical reactions.

Enzymes are mostly protein in nature, with catalytic RNA or ribozymes as an exception.

### Mechanism
The lecture first identifies what enzymes are, then separates the protein enzyme from any non-protein part required for activity.

### Key determinants
The six major enzyme classes are oxidoreductases, transferases, hydrolases, lyases, isomerases and ligases.

Coenzymes are small organic molecules, while cofactors are metal ions.

### Clinical significance
The enzyme definition, class names, and cofactor/coenzyme vocabulary are the lecture foundation for the later active-site and specificity material.
## body

## hold_these
Enzymes are biological catalysts.
Most enzymes are proteins, but catalytic RNA or ribozymes are the stated exception.
The lecture’s six enzyme classes are oxidoreductases, transferases, hydrolases, lyases, isomerases and ligases.
Coenzymes are small organic molecules, while cofactors are metal ions.
## lose_the_mark
Calling every enzyme a protein and omitting ribozymes.
Mixing coenzyme and cofactor terminology after the lecture separates small organic coenzymes from metal-ion cofactors.
Treating enzyme class names as enzyme examples rather than reaction classes.

## summary
This Ain Shams Year 1 foundation article follows the same enzyme lecture into the active site: substrate binding, enzyme-substrate complex formation, induced fit, and the lecture’s statement that enzymes are specific to substrate and reaction.
## sections
### Definition
The active site is a special pocket or cleft containing amino acid side chains that participate in substrate binding and catalysis.

### Mechanism
An enzyme-catalysed reaction begins when the substrate binds the enzyme to form an enzyme-substrate complex.

Substrate binding is thought to cause a conformational change in the enzyme that allows catalysis.

### Key determinants
The active-site sequence depends on the lecture’s earlier enzyme foundation: enzymes are biological catalysts, mostly protein in nature, and the lecture separates coenzymes from metal-ion cofactors.

### Clinical significance
Enzymes are highly specific to their substrate and to the reaction.
## body

## hold_these
The active site is involved in both substrate binding and catalysis.
Substrate binding forms an enzyme-substrate complex.
Induced fit means substrate binding is thought to cause a conformational change that allows catalysis.
Enzyme specificity in this lecture is specificity to substrate and reaction.
## lose_the_mark
Treating the active site as only a binding site when the lecture also ties it to catalysis.
Separating specificity from the active-site sequence when the lecture teaches substrate binding, induced fit and specificity together.
Confusing the allosteric-site slide on native page 41 with the specificity slide on native page 43.
