## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: Apomorphine is a potent, non-selective dopamine (D1/D2) receptor agonist. Because oral levodopa's effect can wear off unpredictably in advanced Parkinson disease, producing sudden "off" periods of akinesia (inability to move), apomorphine is given by subcutaneous injection (or pump infusion) as rescue therapy: its rapid onset (minutes) restores mobility quickly until the next dose of oral medication takes effect. It is not used as first-line or maintenance therapy because of its short duration of action, need for parenteral administration, and prominent nausea (routinely co-prescribed with an antiemetic such as domperidone).

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: Buprenorphine is a partial agonist at the mu-opioid receptor (and a kappa-receptor antagonist), unlike full agonists such as morphine or methadone. Because it only partially activates the mu receptor, it produces a "ceiling effect" on analgesia and respiratory depression, giving it a wider safety margin in overdose, and its high receptor affinity lets it displace full agonists, which can precipitate withdrawal if given too soon after another opioid. These properties make it useful both as an analgesic and, like methadone, as opioid substitution therapy for opioid use disorder.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: Mossy fibres are one of the two main classes of afferent input to the cerebellar cortex (the other being climbing fibres). They terminate in the innermost of the cerebellar cortex's three layers, the granular layer, where they synapse on the dendrites of granule cells to form characteristic glomeruli; granule cell axons then ascend to the molecular layer as parallel fibres, which synapse on Purkinje cell dendrites. Mossy fibres themselves are therefore not found in the molecular layer, and a mossy fibre's direct synaptic partner is the granule cell, not the Purkinje cell.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: Parasympathetic secretomotor supply to the parotid gland begins in the inferior salivatory nucleus and travels via the glossopharyngeal nerve, its tympanic branch, and the lesser petrosal nerve to synapse in the otic ganglion. From there, the auriculotemporal nerve, a branch of the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve, is the specific route by which these now-postganglionic parasympathetic fibres are distributed to the parotid gland; the auriculotemporal nerve is the classic "hitchhiking" pathway, carrying autonomic fibres it did not itself originate, alongside its own sensory fibres to the scalp and temporomandibular joint.

## sections
### Definition
Parasympathetic secretomotor supply to the parotid gland begins in the inferior salivatory nucleus and travels via the glossopharyngeal nerve, its tympanic branch, and the lesser petrosal nerve to synapse in the otic ganglion. From there, the auriculotemporal nerve, a branch of the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve, is the specific route by which these now-postganglionic parasympathetic fibres are distributed to the parotid gland; the auriculotemporal nerve is the classic "hitchhiking" pathway, carrying autonomic fibres it did not itself originate, alongside its own sensory fibres to the scalp and temporomandibular joint.

### Mechanism
State that the auriculotemporal nerve carries postganglionic parasympathetic secretomotor fibres from the otic ganglion to the parotid gland.

### Key determinants
Confusing the auriculotemporal nerve's role (carrying already-postganglionic fibres from the otic ganglion onward to the parotid gland) with the lesser petrosal nerve's role (carrying preganglionic fibres to the otic ganglion) — the two nerves are sequential legs of the same reflex arc, not interchangeable.
