## summary
Three identity facts about amino acids that this ASU-IBM bank tests directly: what makes an amino acid water-soluble, why proteins absorb UV light at 280nm, and which residue in a protein has no dedicated codon of its own.

## sections
### Definition
An amino acid's shared backbone, not its side chain, is what this bank credits for water solubility; the side chain instead decides a different commonly tested property, a protein's UV signature.

### Mechanism
All standard amino acids are water-soluble because of their zwitterionic backbone. Their ionizable alpha-amino and alpha-carboxyl groups form a zwitterion at physiological pH, and this backbone-driven solubility holds regardless of whether the side chain itself is polar, charged, or non-polar.
Proteins absorb ultraviolet light at 280nm chiefly because of the aromatic ring of tryptophan. Of the twenty side chains, tryptophan's indole ring is the strongest chromophore at that wavelength, so a protein's 280nm absorbance is read as a proxy for its tryptophan content.
Not every residue found in a mature protein is written into the genetic code directly. Hydroxyproline is a non-standard amino acid formed by post-translational hydroxylation of proline residues already incorporated into a polypeptide; it has no dedicated codon of its own in the genetic code.

### Key determinants
Zwitterion at the alpha-carbon → water-soluble amino acid, any side chain.
Indole ring (tryptophan) → 280nm UV absorbance.
Post-translational hydroxylation of an already-coded residue (proline → hydroxyproline) → no dedicated codon.

### Clinical significance
Measuring a protein solution's absorbance at 280nm is a routine, low-cost way to estimate protein concentration in a lab — a direct application of the tryptophan fact above.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming solubility depends on the side chain being charged or polar — this bank's key credits the zwitterion/backbone explanation instead: all standard amino acids are water-soluble, including those with a non-polar side chain.
Naming tyrosine or phenylalanine for the 280nm signal — both absorb weakly in that range, but tryptophan's indole ring dominates it.
Treating proline as having no genetic code — proline is directly coded; it is the post-translationally hydroxylated derivative, hydroxyproline, that is not.

## summary
Folding a newly synthesised protein into its correct three-dimensional shape is not always spontaneous. This article covers the helper proteins (chaperones) that assist correct folding, and what goes wrong when folding fails at the population level, as in mad cow disease.

## sections
### Definition
A molecular chaperone is a protein that assists another polypeptide in reaching its native, correctly folded conformation, without itself becoming part of the final structure.

### Mechanism
A molecular chaperone is a helper protein that assists another polypeptide's folding into its native conformation, refolds misfolded protein, and does not itself become part of the final folded structure.
Folding of a newly synthesised protein is accelerated by protein disulfide isomerase (which corrects mispaired disulfide bonds), cis-trans (proline) isomerase, and chaperonins — a barrel-shaped chaperone family that encloses an unfolded chain to give it a protected environment to fold in.
Not every folding failure is corrected. In mad cow disease (bovine spongiform encephalopathy), the causative prion protein is affected by misfolding into an abnormal conformation, not by a defect in its primary, secondary or alpha-helical structure per se.

### Key determinants
Chaperonins, protein disulfide isomerase and cis-trans isomerase → accelerate correct folding.
A protein needing another protein's assistance to fold → the assisting protein is a chaperone.
Prion disease (mad cow disease) → caused by protein misfolding, not a primary-structure defect.

### Clinical significance
Misfolding diseases (prion diseases, and by the same broad principle, Alzheimer disease's beta-amyloid) illustrate why correct three-dimensional folding, not just correct sequence, is required for a protein to function safely.

### Common misconceptions
Confusing a chaperone with an enzyme that permanently alters its substrate, or with the ribosome that synthesises the chain — a chaperone assists folding and is released unchanged.
Attributing mad cow disease to a primary-sequence or fixed alpha-helix defect rather than to misfolding (an abnormal conformational change).

## summary
Two covalent bonds define a protein's backbone chemistry and its ability to cross-link: the peptide bond, whose geometry is fixed and planar, and the disulfide bond, whose formation and cleavage are a redox pair.

## sections
### Definition
The peptide bond joins one amino acid's carboxyl carbon to the next amino acid's amino nitrogen. A disulfide bond joins two cysteine side chains through their sulfur atoms.

### Mechanism
The peptide bond has partial double-bond character from resonance, which locks the amide C-N bond, its flanking atoms and the two alpha-carbons into a single rigid plane, almost always in the trans configuration. So the peptide bond is planar and does not rotate freely the way a typical single bond would.
A disulfide bond forms by a different chemistry entirely. A disulfide bond forms when two cysteine sulfhydryl (-SH) groups are oxidised together, releasing two hydrogens and two electrons and joining the sulfurs into a covalent S-S bond. This product is cystine, and cleaving the bond back apart is the reverse reaction, a reduction.

### Key determinants
Resonance in the C-N bond → peptide bond planarity and trans configuration.
Oxidation of two cysteine -SH groups → disulfide bond formation (reduction reverses it).

### Clinical significance
Disulfide bonds bring together residues that are far apart in the primary sequence (e.g. position 10 and position 134 of a chain) into a stable covalent bridge, contributing to tertiary structure stability — this is why reducing agents are used experimentally, and in hair-straightening chemistry, to break these specific cross-links.

### Common misconceptions
Treating the peptide bond as freely rotating, or assuming the cis form is common — resonance removes rotational freedom around the C-N bond, and steric clash makes trans strongly favoured over cis.
Calling disulfide bond formation "condensation" (that term is reserved for peptide bond formation, which releases water) — disulfide bond formation is specifically an oxidation of two thiols.

## summary
Two general chemistry facts about carbohydrates that this ASU-IBM bank tests before moving into individual sugars: the class's shared empirical formula, and the one carbohydrate that breaks the usual chirality rule.

## sections
### Definition
Carbohydrates as a class share the general empirical formula (CH2O)n.

### Mechanism
Most monosaccharides carry at least one asymmetric (chiral) carbon, which is why most sugars exist as D and L enantiomers. Dihydroxyacetone, the simplest ketose, is the one carbohydrate with no asymmetric (chiral) carbon. Its central carbon carries two identical -CH2OH groups, so it is optically inactive and has no D/L or enantiomeric forms.

### Key determinants
(CH2O)n → the shared empirical formula of carbohydrates.
Two identical -CH2OH groups on its central carbon → dihydroxyacetone is the one carbohydrate with no chiral carbon.

### Clinical significance
Naming the general formula and the achiral exception are both classification checkpoints the bank uses before testing specific sugars (glucose, fructose, galactose) later in the same section.

### Common misconceptions
Writing (CH2O)2n, (CHO)n or CnH2nO instead of the correct (CH2O)n.
Naming glyceraldehyde instead of dihydroxyacetone for the achiral exception — glyceraldehyde is the reference chiral aldotriose (one asymmetric carbon); dihydroxyacetone is its non-chiral ketotriose counterpart.

## summary
Two clinical correlations this ASU-IBM lipid bank draws from ordinary phospholipid chemistry: an autoimmune disease that targets acidic phospholipids, and a neonatal disease that follows from a specific phospholipid's deficiency in lung surfactant.

## sections
### Definition
Antiphospholipid syndrome is an autoimmune disease in which the body forms antibodies against acidic (negatively charged) phospholipids. Respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) is a disease of premature infants whose lungs cannot yet make enough functional surfactant.

### Mechanism
Antiphospholipid syndrome is an autoimmune disease in which antibodies form against acidic (negatively charged) phospholipids. The targeted class includes phosphatidylserine, phosphatidylinositol, phosphatidylglycerol and cardiolipin, predisposing to thrombosis and pregnancy complications.
Respiratory distress syndrome in premature infants occurs when lung surfactant lacks enough dipalmitoyl phosphatidylcholine (dipalmitoyl lecithin, DPPC). This is the major surface-tension-lowering phospholipid of lung surfactant; without enough of it, alveoli tend to collapse at the end of expiration. The lecithin/sphingomyelin ratio of amniotic fluid is used clinically to estimate fetal lung maturity for exactly this reason.

### Key determinants
Autoantibodies against acidic phospholipids → antiphospholipid syndrome.
Deficient dipalmitoyl lecithin (DPPC) in surfactant → neonatal respiratory distress syndrome.

### Clinical significance
Both diseases show how a single class of membrane lipid — phospholipids — can fail in very different ways: as an autoimmune target in adults, or as a developmental deficiency in premature newborns.

### Common misconceptions
Naming fatty acids, cholesterol or surfactant as a whole as the antiphospholipid syndrome target, rather than the acidic phospholipids specifically.
Naming a minor surfactant lipid (cardiolipin, phosphatidylglycerol, plasmalogen) as the deficient component in RDS, rather than dipalmitoyl lecithin (DPPC), the surfactant's main tension-lowering phospholipid.

## summary
Five structure-and-classification facts this ASU-IBM bank tests directly: palmitic acid's carbon-and-group anatomy, which lipids share a glycerol backbone and which does not, which lipid is not a genuine membrane constituent, what triacylglycerols and glycerophospholipids share in their fatty acids, and what a lipoprotein particle's main job is.

## sections
### Definition
A saturated fatty acid, a glycerol-based lipid, a membrane lipid, and a lipoprotein particle each have a defining structural or functional feature this bank tests by contrast with a close but different lipid.

### Mechanism
Palmitic acid is a straight-chain saturated fatty acid with 16 carbon atoms in total: one terminal methyl group, one terminal carboxyl group, and the fourteen methylene (CH2) groups that link them in between.
Most major lipid classes are built on a glycerol backbone — triacylglycerols, glycerophospholipids such as lecithin, and phosphatidic acid all contain glycerol. Cholesterol is the exception: it is a steroid built from four fused rings, not from glycerol.
Biological membranes are built from free cholesterol, phospholipids (such as cardiolipin, sphingomyelin and plasmalogen) and glycolipids (such as gangliosides). Cholesterol ester — cholesterol esterified with a fatty acid — is a storage and transport form of cholesterol found in cytoplasmic lipid droplets and in lipoprotein particles, not a structural membrane constituent.
Triacylglycerols and glycerophospholipids both carry fatty acids that may be saturated or unsaturated — neither lipid class is restricted to one type — even though they differ in glycerol substitution, charge and linkage chemistry otherwise.
Lipoproteins are particles of lipid bound to protein whose main function is to transport lipids — triacylglycerol, cholesterol, cholesteryl ester and phospholipid — through the aqueous environment of the blood, since free lipids are not water-soluble enough to circulate on their own.

### Key determinants
16 total carbons, 1 methyl + 1 carboxyl + 14 methylene groups → palmitic acid's structure.
No glycerol, four fused rings instead → cholesterol, the exception among the glycerol-based lipids.
Esterified to a fatty acid and repackaged for storage or transport → cholesterol ester, not a membrane constituent.
Fatty-acid saturation not restricted to one type → the feature triacylglycerol and glycerophospholipid share.
Lipid bound to protein for aqueous-phase transport → a lipoprotein particle.

### Clinical significance
Distinguishing free cholesterol — a membrane constituent and the precursor of steroid hormones, bile acids and vitamin D — from cholesterol ester, its storage and transport form, matters clinically: the LDL cholesterol a lipid profile reports and statin therapy targets is carried mostly as cholesteryl ester, while the lipoprotein particle itself is what that same blood test is measuring.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming every lipid shares one common backbone, or that a fatty-acid- or phosphorus-containing molecule is automatically a membrane constituent — cholesterol (no glycerol) and cholesterol ester (storage/transport form, not a membrane lipid) are this bank's two "except" answers.

## summary
Four facts about membrane structure and fatty-acid naming that this ASU-IBM bank tests directly: why phospholipids can form a membrane at all, which phospholipids and which membrane component sit on which leaflet, what the omega-n naming rule actually counts, and which fatty acid is the body's own omega-9.

## sections
### Definition
A phospholipid's amphipathic structure, the membrane's leaflet asymmetry, and the omega-numbering convention are three separate facts that together explain how the membrane is built and how its fatty acids are named.

### Mechanism
Phospholipids are well-suited to forming the cell membrane because they are amphipathic: each molecule has a hydrophilic (polar) head group and a hydrophobic (nonpolar) fatty-acid tail, which lets it spontaneously orient into a bilayer with heads facing the surrounding water and tails buried inside, away from it.
The plasma membrane's lipids are asymmetrically distributed between its two leaflets. Phosphatidylcholine and most of the membrane's cholesterol sit on the outer leaflet; phosphatidylserine and phosphatidylethanolamine are concentrated on the inner (cytosolic) leaflet. Membrane carbohydrate, part of the glycocalyx, is found only on the outer leaflet, never on the inner one.
Fatty acids are named "omega-n" by counting carbons from the methyl end of the chain (the end opposite the carboxyl group, also called the omega end): an omega-3 fatty acid has its first double bond starting at the third carbon from that methyl end, regardless of how many total double bonds or carbons the chain has.
Oleic acid, an 18-carbon monounsaturated fatty acid, is classified as an omega-9 fatty acid because its single double bond sits at the ninth carbon from the methyl end. Unlike omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids, omega-9 fatty acids are not essential — the body can synthesize oleic acid endogenously.

### Key determinants
Polar head + nonpolar tail in one molecule → an amphipathic phospholipid, able to self-assemble into a bilayer.
Phosphatidylcholine and cholesterol outward, phosphatidylserine and phosphatidylethanolamine inward → the membrane's resting phospholipid asymmetry.
Carbon position of the first double bond counted from the methyl end → the omega-n name of a fatty acid.
Ninth carbon from the methyl end, non-essential → omega-9 (oleic acid).

### Clinical significance
Loss of the membrane's resting phospholipid asymmetry — phosphatidylserine appearing on the outer leaflet instead of staying confined to the inner one — is the signal that marks an apoptotic cell for phagocytic clearance, and the omega-3/omega-6/omega-9 naming system underlies dietary advice about essential-fatty-acid intake and cardiovascular risk.

### Common misconceptions
Placing membrane carbohydrate on the inner (cytosolic) leaflet — it is an outer-leaflet-only, extracellular feature. Confusing the omega-n count with the total number of double bonds, or counting from the carboxyl end instead of the methyl end.
