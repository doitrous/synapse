## summary
Three facts tested by the ASU HCB Dr. Hegazy cell-biology MCQ bank ("Cell MCQ set2") that are not covered by any existing cytology concept: the distinction between the constitutive and regulated pathways of protein secretion, tubular mitochondrial cristae as an electron-microscopy marker of a steroid-secreting cell, and phase-contrast microscopy as the technique used to watch organelle movement in living, unstained cells.

## sections
### Definition
The constitutive pathway of protein secretion releases its product continuously, with no storage step and no external trigger; the regulated pathway packages its product into secretory granules that are stored and released only on a specific signal.
Tubular (as opposed to shelf-like or lamellar) mitochondrial cristae are an ultrastructural marker seen in cells that secrete steroid hormones, such as the cells of the adrenal cortex.
Phase-contrast microscopy is a light-microscopy technique that converts small differences in refractive index into visible contrast, letting the observer watch unstained, living cells and follow the movement of their organelles in real time.

### Mechanism
Procollagen for bone and connective-tissue formation is secreted constitutively: fibroblasts and osteoblasts export it steadily as it is made, rather than storing it in granules for a triggered release.
By contrast, a cell such as a pancreatic islet beta cell packages insulin into secretory granules and releases it only when a signal (a rise in blood glucose) arrives — the regulated pathway.
A steroid-secreting cell needs a very large area of inner mitochondrial membrane to carry the cholesterol side-chain cleavage and hydroxylation enzymes of steroidogenesis, and it achieves that by folding its cristae as tubules rather than as flat shelves; identifying tubular cristae plus abundant smooth endoplasmic reticulum on an electron micrograph points to a steroidogenic cell such as an adrenal cortical cell.
Phase-contrast microscopy needs no fixation or staining, so it is the technique of choice for time-lapse observation of organelle movement (for example mitochondrial trafficking) inside a living cultured cell.

### Key determinants
An "except/every-day-basal" secretion pattern with a named constitutive example (procollagen) points to the constitutive pathway; a "stored until triggered" pattern points to the regulated pathway.
Tubular cristae specifically flag a steroid-secreting cell; do not confuse this with the shelf-like cristae of a general high-ATP-demand cell such as cardiac or skeletal muscle.
"Living, unstained cell" plus "watching movement" points to phase-contrast microscopy, not to electron microscopy (which needs fixed, non-living tissue) or to light microscopy with a routine stain (which needs fixed, dead tissue).

### Clinical significance
This is not a treatment rule. Its study value is telling apart three commonly confused cell-biology facts that this MCQ bank tests as stand-alone questions: secretion-pathway classification, an EM tissue-identification marker, and a microscopy-technique fact.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming all secreted proteins are stored in granules before release — many, like procollagen, are exported constitutively as they are made.
Assuming any abundant-mitochondria cell has tubular cristae — tubular cristae specifically mark steroidogenic cells, not simply energy-hungry ones.
Confusing phase-contrast microscopy (living, unstained cells) with electron microscopy or routine light microscopy (both need fixed, dead tissue).

## hold_these

## lose_the_mark
Calling procollagen a regulated-pathway/stored secretory product.
Attaching tubular cristae to any high-energy cell rather than specifically to steroidogenic cells.
Naming light or electron microscopy instead of phase-contrast microscopy for watching live organelle movement.
