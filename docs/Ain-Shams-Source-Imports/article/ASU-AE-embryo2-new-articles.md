## summary
This ASU Year 1 Introduction to Anatomy and Embryology slice ("MCQs - Embryo 2.pdf", Part 2, first 10 questions) covers five facts about fertilisation and implantation that `find-existing.mjs` found nowhere else in the corpus: the cortical-granule block to polyspermy, the timing of implantation, the blastocoele's fate as the primary yolk sac, the chorionic vesicle wall's composition, and the identity of the cell the sperm actually fertilises (the secondary oocyte, not the mature ovum). It is a local answer-key-grounded draft and still needs independent embryology evidence before publication.

## sections
### Definition
Fertilisation is completed by the fusion of a sperm with a secondary oocyte, restoring the diploid chromosome number and triggering the resumption and completion of the oocyte's second meiotic division. Implantation is the process by which the blastocyst embeds in the endometrium; the chorion is the trophoblast-derived wall of the chorionic vesicle, once extraembryonic mesoderm has lined it.
### Mechanism
When the first sperm fuses with the oocyte membrane, cortical granule release alters the surrounding coat, blocking any further sperm from penetrating and fertilising the same oocyte -- taught here as the vitelline block, more widely known as the zona reaction. This first sperm fuses with the secondary oocyte, not yet a "mature ovum": that term only applies once the resulting calcium signal drives completion of the second meiotic division. Implantation itself is a process spanning about days 6-7 (attachment and early invasion) to about days 11-12 (full embedding, with the surface epithelium closed over the blastocyst). Meanwhile the blastocoele -- the blastocyst's fluid-filled cavity -- is converted by the hypoblast and its exocoelomic (Heuser's) membrane lining into the primary yolk sac, distinct from the amniotic cavity (which forms separately within the inner cell mass) and from the extraembryonic/intraembryonic coeloms (later, separate cavities within mesoderm). Once extraembryonic mesoderm forms and lines the trophoblast, the resulting chorionic vesicle wall is trophoblast plus this extraembryonic somatic mesoderm -- the same somatic layer that also lines the amnion, as distinct from the splanchnic layer covering the yolk sac.
### Key determinants
Keep four things straight: (1) the polyspermy block follows the first sperm's fusion with the oocyte, not before; (2) the sperm fertilises the secondary oocyte, and only the completed second meiotic division afterward makes it a mature ovum; (3) implantation begins around day 6-7 and completes around day 11-12; (4) the blastocoele becomes the primary yolk sac, while the chorionic wall is trophoblast plus somatic (not splanchnic) mesoderm.
### Clinical significance
This article does not make prescribing or diagnostic-workup claims. Its study value is foundational: failure of the polyspermy block produces a non-viable triploid conceptus, and the implantation timeline underpins how early pregnancy is dated and how implantation bleeding is distinguished from a true period.
### Common misconceptions
Do not describe the acrosome reaction or capacitation as the block to polyspermy -- both are steps the fertilising sperm itself undergoes, not the mechanism that excludes further sperm.

Do not call the cell the sperm fertilises a "mature ovum" -- it is a secondary oocyte until the second meiotic division completes as a result of fertilisation.

Do not confuse when implantation begins (day 6-7) with when it is completed (day 11-12).

Do not attribute the exocoelomic membrane or splanchnic mesoderm to the chorionic wall -- those belong to the yolk sac lining, not the chorion.

## summary
This ASU Year 1 Introduction to Anatomy and Embryology slice ("MCQs - Embryo 2.pdf", Part 2, first 10 questions) covers two facts about abnormal implantation sites that `find-existing.mjs` found nowhere else in the corpus: placenta praevia (lower-uterine-segment implantation) and the uterine tube as the commonest site of ectopic pregnancy. It is a local answer-key-grounded draft and still needs independent obstetrics evidence before publication.

## sections
### Definition
Placenta praevia is a placental site abnormality: the blastocyst implants in the lower uterine segment, so the placenta lies over or near the internal cervical os. Ectopic pregnancy is implantation anywhere outside the normal uterine cavity site.
### Mechanism
Because the fertilised oocyte normally spends its first several days travelling along the uterine tube before the resulting blastocyst reaches the uterine cavity, the uterine tube -- most often its ampullary part -- is by far the commonest site of ectopic implantation, well ahead of the rarer ovarian, abdominal/peritoneal and cervical sites. Placenta praevia, by contrast, is an intrauterine but abnormally low implantation: it is distinct from depth-of-invasion placental abnormalities (accreta, percreta) and from shape/number abnormalities (membranacea, succenturiata), all of which are named placental anomalies but describe different problems. Because a low-lying placenta sits in the path the fetus must pass through at delivery, placenta praevia is a recognised cause of antepartum haemorrhage and can obstruct vaginal delivery.
### Key determinants
Keep two things straight: (1) the uterine tube, not the ovary, peritoneum or cervix, is the commonest ectopic site; (2) placenta praevia names a site abnormality (lower uterine segment) and must not be confused with depth-of-invasion or shape/number placental abnormalities that share the "placenta ___" naming pattern.
### Clinical significance
This article does not make prescribing or diagnostic-workup claims. Its study value is foundational: tubal ectopic pregnancy risks rupture and severe haemorrhage as the growing conceptus outgrows the tube, and placenta praevia is a classic cause of painless antepartum haemorrhage that must be distinguished from placental abruption.
### Common misconceptions
Do not name the ovary, peritoneum or cervix as the commonest ectopic site -- all occur but are considerably rarer than tubal implantation.

Do not confuse placenta praevia with placenta accreta/percreta (depth of invasion) or placenta membranacea/succenturiata (shape/number) -- they share a naming pattern but describe different abnormalities.
