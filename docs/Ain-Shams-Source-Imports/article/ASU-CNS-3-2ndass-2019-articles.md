## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: The stria medullaris thalami is a slender fibre bundle running along the dorsomedial margin of the thalamus that carries afferents from the septal area (and other limbic forebrain structures) to the habenular nucleus, forming one of the principal inputs of the habenulo-limbic system. From the habenular nucleus, the habenulointerpeduncular tract (fasciculus retroflexus) continues on to the midbrain, linking the limbic forebrain to brainstem monoaminergic centres.

## sections
### Definition
The stria medullaris thalami is a slender fibre bundle running along the dorsomedial margin of the thalamus that carries afferents from the septal area (and other limbic forebrain structures) to the habenular nucleus, forming one of the principal inputs of the habenulo-limbic system. From the habenular nucleus, the habenulointerpeduncular tract (fasciculus retroflexus) continues on to the midbrain, linking the limbic forebrain to brainstem monoaminergic centres.

### Mechanism
State that the stria medullaris thalami carries fibres from the septal area to the habenular nucleus.

### Key determinants
Confusing the stria medullaris thalami (septal area to habenular nucleus) with the stria terminalis (amygdala to hypothalamus) — two distinct limbic white-matter tracts that both run along the thalamus/lateral ventricle.

### Clinical significance
The stria medullaris thalami is part of the habenulo-limbic circuitry implicated in reward and aversion processing.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: Cocaine is a local anaesthetic that blocks voltage-gated sodium channels like other agents in its class, but is unique among clinically used local anaesthetics in also blocking presynaptic reuptake of noradrenaline, producing local vasoconstriction as an intrinsic property of the drug itself. Other local anaesthetics (e.g. lidocaine, bupivacaine) instead tend to cause vasodilation and are typically combined with an added vasoconstrictor (e.g. adrenaline) to prolong their action and limit systemic absorption.

## sections
### Definition
Cocaine is a local anaesthetic that blocks voltage-gated sodium channels like other agents in its class, but is unique among clinically used local anaesthetics in also blocking presynaptic reuptake of noradrenaline, producing local vasoconstriction as an intrinsic property of the drug itself. Other local anaesthetics (e.g. lidocaine, bupivacaine) instead tend to cause vasodilation and are typically combined with an added vasoconstrictor (e.g. adrenaline) to prolong their action and limit systemic absorption.

### Mechanism
State that cocaine, unlike other local anaesthetics, produces intrinsic vasoconstriction rather than requiring an added vasoconstrictor.

### Key determinants
Confusing cocaine's intrinsic vasoconstrictor property (from blocking noradrenaline reuptake) with other local anaesthetics, which cause vasodilation and need an added vasoconstrictor such as adrenaline.

### Clinical significance
Cocaine's intrinsic vasoconstriction is exploited in nasal/ENT surgery (topical cocaine for anaesthesia with haemostasis) but also underlies its cardiovascular toxicity risk.

### Common misconceptions
Confusing cocaine's intrinsic vasoconstrictor property (from blocking noradrenaline reuptake) with other local anaesthetics, which cause vasodilation and need an added vasoconstrictor such as adrenaline.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: The posterior (lateral) hypothalamus is the principal hypothalamic centre for sympathetic outflow. Its stimulation produces a coordinated sympathetic response — tachycardia, vasoconstriction and shivering (heat-conserving/heat-generating responses) — but not sweating, which is a cholinergic sympathetic response mediated by a different hypothalamic region. This contrasts with the anterior hypothalamus, which is chiefly associated with parasympathetic outflow and heat-dissipating responses.

## sections
### Definition
The posterior (lateral) hypothalamus is the principal hypothalamic centre for sympathetic outflow. Its stimulation produces a coordinated sympathetic response — tachycardia, vasoconstriction and shivering (heat-conserving/heat-generating responses) — but not sweating, which is a cholinergic sympathetic response mediated by a different hypothalamic region. This contrasts with the anterior hypothalamus, which is chiefly associated with parasympathetic outflow and heat-dissipating responses.

### Mechanism
State that stimulation of the posterior hypothalamus produces sympathetically mediated tachycardia (with vasoconstriction and shivering, but not sweating).

### Key determinants
Confusing the posterior hypothalamus's sympathetic effects (tachycardia, vasoconstriction, shivering, NOT sweating) with the anterior hypothalamus's parasympathetic, heat-dissipating effects (including sweating).

### Clinical significance
The anterior/posterior hypothalamic division of parasympathetic/sympathetic thermoregulatory control explains autonomic disturbances seen in hypothalamic lesions.

### Common misconceptions
Confusing the posterior hypothalamus's sympathetic effects (tachycardia, vasoconstriction, shivering, NOT sweating) with the anterior hypothalamus's parasympathetic, heat-dissipating effects (including sweating).
