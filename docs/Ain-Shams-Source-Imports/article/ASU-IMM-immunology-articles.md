## summary
Innate immunity is the body's rapid, non-specific, repeatable first line of defence. It recognises microbes through germline-encoded pattern-recognition receptors binding shared pathogen molecular patterns, clears them chiefly by phagocytosis, and is reinforced by physical barriers and the competitive presence of normal flora — but it does not itself produce antibody.

## sections
### Definition
Innate immunity is the immune system's rapid, non-specific, repeatable first line of defence against infection. It responds equally and quickly on every exposure to a given organism, unlike adaptive immunity, which is slower on first contact and improves only with repeated exposure through clonal memory.

### Mechanism
Innate cells detect microbes using germline-encoded pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs) that bind pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) — molecular signatures shared by whole classes of microbes, such as bacterial lipopolysaccharide, microbial nucleic acids and teichoic acid. Host-made antimicrobial peptides such as defensins are effectors, not PAMPs, because they are made by the host rather than borne by the pathogen. Once a pathogen is flagged, phagocytosis proceeds through a defined sequence: PRR-PAMP recognition, ingestion into a phagosome, and intracellular killing, chiefly through the oxidative burst — recognition by "a specific receptor" for one antigen is the adaptive pattern, not how this innate sequence is framed. The normal microbiota reinforces these barriers mainly by competing with incoming pathogens for attachment sites and nutrients, denying them a foothold on epithelial surfaces.

### Key determinants
Innate immunity's components are physical/chemical barriers, phagocytosis and the inflammatory response; antibody production is not one of them, because it requires clonal B-lymphocyte activation, a hallmark of adaptive immunity. Among the innate cell types, the basophil is notably non-phagocytic (unlike neutrophils, dendritic cells and macrophages), and the mast cell's role in this course is effector mediator release rather than immune regulation — neutrophils, T-helper cells and dendritic cells are the ones credited with a regulatory role over the wider response.

### Clinical significance
Recognising which structure a diabetic patient's innate cells use to detect a bacterial infection (a PRR binding a PAMP, not MHC-I or an antibody-dependent complement receptor) is the everyday clinical translation of this mechanism: innate recognition does not wait for antibody or T-cell help, which is exactly why it is available within minutes of infection.

### Applied physiology
Two lymphoid-organ facts sit alongside innate barrier defence in this course's own framing. The bone marrow's role is haematopoiesis (including red-cell generation) and B-lymphocyte maturation; it does not mature CD4+ T cells, which complete maturation in the thymus instead. Among the lymphoid organs, the lymph node — not the spleen — is considered the lymph filter; the spleen's filtering role is for blood.

## summary
Inflammation is the reaction sequence tissue damage and pathogen entry set off. Pro-inflammatory cytokines carry out this response — recruiting leukocytes, inducing endothelial adhesion molecules, driving bone-marrow leukocytosis and hepatic acute-phase proteins — while a distinct set of cytokines (IL-2, IFN-gamma, IL-7) drive lymphocyte proliferation, macrophage activation and lymphoid development, and IL-10/TGF-beta act as the system's brakes.

## sections
### Definition
Inflammation is the sequence of vascular and cellular reactions triggered when tissue damage and the entry of pathogenic organisms activate resident and recruited defence mechanisms at the affected site. It is not the same event as opsonisation (a single recognition step) or clonal expansion (a lymphocyte proliferation event); those are components of, or consequences within, a wider immune response, not the definition of inflammation itself.

### Mechanism
Pro-inflammatory cytokines carry out inflammation's programme: they induce recruitment of leukocytes to the infection site, stimulate bone-marrow leukocytosis, and induce hepatic production of acute-phase proteins such as C-reactive protein and mannose-binding lectin, alongside systemic effects such as hypothalamus-driven fever. IL-1 is the prototype endogenous pyrogen among the interleukins tested here. A macrophage-derived cytokine (of the TNF/IL-1 family) switches on endothelial adhesion molecule expression, the step that lets circulating leukocytes stick to and then cross the vessel wall; the chemokine IL-8 together with the complement fragment C5a then chemoattracts neutrophils to the site, while IFN-gamma is not itself a neutrophil chemoattractant.

### Key determinants
A separate group of cytokines governs lymphocyte and macrophage behaviour rather than the vascular/acute-phase programme above: IL-2 is the principal driver of activated T-cell proliferation and clonal expansion; IFN-gamma, made by activated T cells and NK cells (not by every nucleated cell), activates macrophages to kill ingested intracellular pathogens and upregulates MHC class II expression on antigen-presenting cells; and IL-7 stimulates lymphoid cell development from transplanted haematopoietic stem cells after a bone-marrow graft. IL-12 is a macrophage product that promotes this Th1/NK axis. Against this stimulatory cluster, IL-10 and TGF-beta are the two classic inhibitory cytokines that dampen immune activation.

### Clinical significance
A patient's fever, rising white-cell count and rising CRP after infection are the direct clinical footprint of pro-inflammatory cytokine action described above; a haematology patient given a stem-cell graft after chemotherapy benefits specifically from IL-7 to re-establish lymphoid development, a different and more targeted intervention from simply controlling the acute inflammatory programme.

IL-2 drives T-cell proliferation; IFN-gamma activates macrophages and upregulates MHC class II; IL-7 drives lymphoid development from stem cells — three distinct, separately examinable cytokine roles.
IL-10 and TGF-beta are the inhibitory cytokines; IFN-gamma is stimulatory and does not belong with them.

Assuming IFN-gamma is made by all nucleated cells like a type-I interferon; it is a product of activated lymphocytes and NK cells.
Grouping IFN-gamma with IL-10/TGF-beta as "inhibitory" because exam options pair them; IFN-gamma is stimulatory.

## summary
Four innate cell types carry distinct, separately examinable roles: the eosinophil rises with allergy and parasitic infection and carries parasite-toxic granules; the basophil is a non-phagocytic mediator-release cell; the mast cell's histamine release is the mechanism of an acute (type I) allergic reaction; and the macrophage combines phagocytosis, antigen presentation and cytokine production in one cell.

## sections
### Definition
Eosinophils, basophils, mast cells and macrophages are innate leukocytes with distinct roles in host defence and hypersensitivity. Each is tested in this course by the specific function or mechanism that sets it apart from the others, not by a shared "granulocyte" identity.

### Mechanism
The eosinophil is a myeloid-lineage leukocyte that increases in number in allergic reactions and helminthic infections, carrying cytoplasmic granules loaded with parasite-toxic mediators such as major basic protein. The basophil, unlike the neutrophil, dendritic cell and macrophage, does not perform phagocytosis; its contribution is mediator release. When a patient develops an acute allergic reaction — facial swelling, wheeze and hypotension after allergen exposure — the immediate mechanism is mast-cell degranulation releasing histamine, the defining feature of immediate (type I) hypersensitivity, and this immediate phase is mechanistically distinct from and faster than the eosinophil-mediated later phase of allergic and parasitic responses.

### Key determinants
The macrophage is the most functionally versatile of the four: it performs phagocytosis of pathogens, presents processed antigen to T cells, and produces the cytokines (including IL-12) that shape the wider response — all three functions belong to the same cell, so an exam question asking which functions a macrophage performs is very often answered by "all of the above" rather than any single option.

### Clinical significance
A child presenting with acute facial swelling, wheeze and hypotension minutes after allergen exposure is having a mast-cell-histamine-mediated reaction, which is treated as an emergency precisely because it is immediate; a patient with a rising eosinophil count instead points toward a parasitic infection or a more indolent allergic process, a different clinical tempo and a different diagnostic direction.

## published_sections


## hold_these
Eosinophils rise in allergy and parasitic infection and carry parasite-toxic granules; this is the fact the "myeloid leukocyte, allergy, parasite-toxic granules" stem is testing.
The basophil does not phagocytose — of the granulocytes and phagocytes taught together, it is the one exception.
An acute allergic reaction (facial swelling, wheeze, hypotension) is mast-cell histamine release, not eosinophil degranulation, which is slower.
The macrophage genuinely does all three of phagocytosis, antigen presentation and cytokine production — it is not a choice between them.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing the basophil and eosinophil because both are allergy-associated granulocytes; only the eosinophil is credited here with a parasite-toxic granule role and phagocytosis is not performed by either.
Attributing an acute, minutes-scale allergic reaction to eosinophil degranulation rather than the much faster mast-cell histamine mechanism.

## summary
A large antigen is more immunogenic because it carries more epitopes; a hapten needs a carrier protein before it can raise an antibody response. MHC class I is expressed on nearly all nucleated cells (not erythrocytes, and only class I on a fibroblast), is encoded by HLA-A/B/C on chromosome 6, requires beta2-microglobulin for surface expression, and underlies resistance to viral infection. Superantigens bypass all of this specificity by cross-linking the TCR V-beta chain directly to MHC class II.

## sections
### Definition
An antigen is immunogenic in proportion to features that let lymphocyte receptors engage it; an epitope is the specific antigenic determinant, presented in an MHC groove, that a T-cell receptor actually binds. The Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) class I molecule is the surface protein through which almost every nucleated cell displays a sample of its internal, cytosolic proteins for immune surveillance.

### Mechanism
Large molecules are more immunogenic than small ones chiefly because they carry more epitopes for lymphocyte receptors to recognise, not because they fit a receptor better or persist as a depot. A hapten is a small chemical group that cannot by itself induce an antibody response; once covalently coupled to a larger carrier protein, the hapten-carrier conjugate becomes immunogenic. MHC class I molecules are expressed on essentially all nucleated cells (fibroblasts express class I only, without class II; mature, anucleate red blood cells express neither), are encoded by the highly polymorphic HLA-A, HLA-B and HLA-C loci on the short arm of chromosome 6, and require the non-polymorphic polypeptide beta2-microglobulin to reach the cell surface. Their central function is presenting cytosolic (including viral) peptide, which underlies resistance to viral infection.

### Key determinants
A superantigen is a bacterial protein or toxin that activates a large fraction of the T-cell repertoire, not the antigen-specific few, by directly cross-linking the variable-beta domain of the T-cell receptor to an MHC class II molecule outside the normal peptide-binding groove — bypassing conventional antigen specificity entirely, which is why it triggers a disproportionate cytokine burst.

### Clinical significance
Distinguishing an epitope (on the antigen) from a paratope (the antibody or TCR's own binding site) and knowing that a hapten needs a carrier protein both matter directly for understanding drug-induced hypersensitivity, where a small drug molecule (a hapten) becomes immunogenic only after binding host protein. Recognising the superantigen mechanism explains why toxic shock and similar toxin-mediated illnesses produce a cytokine storm out of proportion to the size of the triggering infection.

## hold_these

Treating a superantigen as simply a very potent conventional antigen rather than a non-specific TCR-Vbeta/MHC-II cross-linker.

T cells recognise antigen only when it is processed and displayed on an antigen-presenting cell's MHC molecule, never free in solution. CD8+ T cells are MHC-class-I-restricted and CD4+ (helper) T cells are the MHC-class-II-restricted population; full activation needs a B7-CD28 costimulatory second signal, which CTLA-4 can later switch off, and tumours exploit an analogous brake (PD-L1) to escape attack. CD4+ T cells differentiate into Th1 and Th2 subsets with distinct cytokine profiles, T lymphocytes continuously recirculate through blood and lymphoid organs, and a memory-driven secondary immune response is faster and stronger than the primary response that first generated it.

## sections
### Definition
T lymphocytes are the adaptive immune system's cell-mediated arm. Unlike B cells, they cannot recognise free or soluble antigen; they recognise antigen only once it is processed and presented on the surface of an antigen-presenting cell, held within an MHC molecule's groove.

### Mechanism
Dendritic cells are the antigen-presenting cell best suited to activating a naive T lymphocyte for the first time. CD8+ (cytotoxic) T cells are restricted by, and receive antigen presented by, MHC class I; CD4+ (helper) T cells are distinguished from cytotoxic T cells by their CD4 marker (not the pan-T-cell marker CD3, which both subsets share) and are MHC-class-II-restricted. Full T-cell activation needs a second, costimulatory signal delivered by B7 on the antigen-presenting cell engaging CD28 on the T cell; later in the response, CTLA-4 on the T cell competes with CD28 for the same B7 molecule and delivers an inhibitory signal instead, down-regulating cell-mediated immunity. Tumour cells exploit an analogous brake by upregulating PD-L1, which engages the inhibitory PD-1 receptor on T cells and lets the tumour escape attack — raising MHC-I, B7 or the tumour's own antigen expression would instead make it more visible, not less.

### Key determinants
CD4+ T cells differentiate into Th1 cells, which secrete IFN-gamma and support macrophage-mediated immunity, and Th2 cells, which make IL-4, IL-5 and IL-13 and facilitate humoral/allergic-type responses; the two subsets characteristically cross-regulate (antagonise) rather than positively reinforce each other. T lymphocytes are also defined by continuous recirculation through the blood and secondary lymphoid organs (not by ingesting pathogens directly, and their education happens in the thymus, not the bone marrow). Antigen cross-reaction can trigger autoimmune disease through molecular mimicry, when a microbial antigen shares antigenic determinants with a human self-antigen closely enough that the anti-microbial response also attacks host tissue. A later, memory-driven (secondary) immune response is faster and stronger than the primary response, with a markedly shorter lag period, because it is carried out by memory rather than naive cells. B lymphocytes are not themselves a component of cell-mediated immunity; that arm is carried by T lymphocytes, lymphokines, NK cells and macrophages.

### Clinical significance
The CD28/B7 costimulation checkpoint and its CTLA-4 brake are the biological basis of checkpoint immunotherapy: blocking CTLA-4 or PD-1/PD-L1 releases the brake a tumour has exploited and lets a patient's own T cells attack it. Recognising molecular mimicry as an autoimmune trigger explains why some infections (for example streptococcal) are followed weeks later by an autoimmune complication, a clinically important delayed pattern distinct from a direct infective process.

## summary
Humoral immunity is carried out by B lymphocytes and the antibodies they secrete, which are the immune system's most effective tool against extracellular pathogens. This article covers how B cells are activated and matured, how antibody structure divides antigen-binding (Fab, variable region, idiotype) from effector function (Fc, isotype), how isotype switching and the individual isotypes' distinct roles work, and how passive antibody protects a newborn.

## sections
### Definition
Humoral immunity is the antibody-mediated arm of adaptive immunity, carried out by B lymphocytes and their secreted antibodies (immunoglobulins). B lymphocytes and their antibodies are the immune components most effective at destroying extracellular pathogens, since antibody can bind a free organism or its toxin directly in blood or tissue fluid and then recruit opsonisation, complement activation and neutralisation — pro-inflammatory cytokines, T-helper cells and type I interferon support this response but are not themselves the effector molecule.

### Mechanism
B-cell maturation happens in the bone marrow, where developing B cells acquire IgM and IgD as their B-cell receptor (BCR) and undergo negative selection to delete strongly self-reactive clones — but, unlike T-cell education in the thymus, B-cell maturation does not ensure self-MHC restriction, because the BCR recognises native antigen directly rather than a peptide in an MHC groove; this is also why the BCR, unlike the T-cell receptor, can recognise carbohydrate (CHO) antigen without processing. Antigen engagement of surface IgM/IgD is transduced into the cell by Ig-alpha and Ig-beta, a dedicated signalling heterodimer associated with the BCR (directly analogous to CD3 on the T-cell receptor) — not by a heavy-chain constant region or by CD59, a complement-regulatory protein unrelated to BCR signalling. What happens next depends on the antigen: a thymus-independent antigen (typically a repetitive polysaccharide epitope) cross-links the BCR and drives differentiation into plasma cells only, with no memory B-cell formation and no T-cell help; a thymus-dependent antigen instead engages cognate T-helper cells, whose CD40 ligand (CD40L) engages CD40 on the B cell — the specific molecular trigger for isotype (class-switch) recombination — yielding memory B cells and multiple antibody isotypes rather than a single one.

### Key determinants
An antibody's antigen-binding site is formed by the variable region of both the heavy and the light chain together, and the hypervariable region within that variable domain confers the antibody's specificity — not any constant region or the Fc portion. Class switching lets a B cell change isotype, Fc region and effector function while keeping the same antigen-binding specificity, so changing the antibody's idiotype (its unique variable-region binding identity) is specifically NOT a result of class switching; correspondingly, the Fab arm (built from the variable regions) carries the idiotype, while biological effector functions such as complement fixation are mediated by the Fc region, not the Fab portion. Among the isotypes, IgM and IgG carry the binding site for complement component C1q and can trigger the classical pathway, while IgD, IgA and IgE do not; IgM in particular is the single most efficient isotype at activating complement, which is its most important protective function in an extracellular bacterial infection, ahead of opsonisation or mucosal immunity. IgE's own correctly paired function is parasite defence together with binding to mast cells — not mucosal transport (a secretory-IgA role), complement fixation (an IgM/IgG role), being the first antibody secreted (also IgM's role, not IgA's), or being present only in minute serum amounts (that description is wrong for IgG, the most abundant serum isotype). Fusing an antibody-secreting plasma cell with an immortal myeloma (tumour) cell creates the fusion product used to manufacture monoclonal antibody, printed in this course's own bank as "Hyperdoma" rather than the standard term "hybridoma" — kept as printed rather than silently corrected.

### Clinical significance
Because only IgG crosses the placenta, and IgA is transferred through breast milk (including colostrum), a six-month-old breastfed baby carries both maternally derived IgG and milk-derived IgA, not a single isotype alone; IgA is in fact the most abundant antibody in colostrum specifically, ahead of IgM, IgG or IgE. Recognising IgM in the body of a newborn is a clinically important marker of intrauterine (congenital) infection, precisely because maternal IgM does not cross the placenta the way IgG does, so any fetal IgM must have been produced by the fetus itself in response to an in-utero infection — distinguishing it from the passively acquired IgG that is expected regardless of infection status.

### Applied physiology
The two antigen classes differ in more than which cells they engage: a thymus-dependent antigen's response, needing the CD40-CD40L signal, is processed by an antigen-presenting cell (such as a macrophage), induces isotype switching and generates memory B cells, whereas the thymus-independent pathway produces only a single antibody isotype from plasma cells and forms no memory — the exam's own distractors deliberately test this precise T-dependent-versus-T-independent contrast from multiple angles.

## summary
Cell-mediated immunity depends on thymic education (positive selection for self-MHC restriction, negative selection to delete self-reactive clones), a TCR-CD3 complex that recognises antigen and transduces the signal, and effector mechanisms that differ by cell type: cytotoxic T lymphocytes kill MHC-I-restricted targets with perforin and granzymes or the Fas/FasL pathway, while NK cells kill MHC-I-negative ("missing self") targets by the same granule pathway without MHC restriction. Antigen reaches these cells through two separate processing routes — exogenous/phagocytosed antigen onto MHC class II, and endogenous/cytosolic antigen onto MHC class I — and the whole response is bounded by costimulation, checkpoints, adhesion and CD4+CD25+ regulatory T cells that keep it from turning on self tissue.

## sections
### Definition
Cell-mediated immunity (CMI) is the T-lymphocyte, NK-cell and macrophage arm of adaptive immunity, distinct from the antibody-mediated humoral arm. Its cells are educated in the thymus before ever meeting antigen: developing T cells first undergo positive selection in the thymic cortex, in which only cells whose receptor can recognise self MHC are rescued (establishing self-MHC restriction), and then negative selection in the corticomedullary/medullary region, in which cells that bind self-peptide/self-MHC too strongly are deleted by apoptosis (central self-tolerance). The T cells that ultimately leave the thymus as naive — mature but antigen-inexperienced — cells are those that recognise foreign antigen only in the context of self MHC.

### Mechanism
Antigen recognition itself is carried out by the alpha-beta T-cell receptor (TCR), but the TCR cannot signal alone: it is intimately, non-covalently associated with the CD3 complex on the T-cell membrane, and CD3 transduces the recognition event into the cell. Full activation additionally needs B7 (APC) engaging CD28 (T cell) as a costimulatory second signal, and ICAM-1 (APC) engaging LFA-1 (T cell) as an adhesion molecule pair that stabilises this APC-T-cell contact; activation then proceeds in a fixed order — activation, then proliferation (driven by IL-2), then differentiation — and cytotoxic T-cell clonal expansion specifically requires antigen stimulation, costimulation and IL-2 from Th1 cells together, not any one alone. If antigen binds the TCR without the accompanying costimulatory signal (B7 fails to engage CD28), the cell becomes anergic (functionally unresponsive) rather than activated — a peripheral tolerance mechanism distinct from thymic negative selection.

Two separate pathways bring antigen to this recognition machinery. Phagocytosed, exogenous antigen (extracellular microbes and other material taken up into an endosome/phagosome) is processed and loaded onto MHC class II for presentation to CD4+ T-helper cells; this pathway does not operate inside virally infected or tumour cells. Newly synthesised, endogenous (intracytosolic) antigen — such as a virus replicating inside an infected cell — is instead degraded by the proteasome and loaded onto MHC class I for presentation to CD8+ cytotoxic T cells (CTLs); MHC class II association is false for this route. A superantigen bypasses ordinary antigen-specific recognition altogether, cross-linking the TCR V-beta chain directly to MHC class II outside the normal peptide groove and so activating large numbers of T cells irrespective of their antigen specificity — the basis of the toxic-shock-type presentation some bacterial toxins produce.

### Key determinants
Once activated, CD8+ CTLs kill their MHC-I-restricted targets chiefly by releasing perforin (which polymerises into a membrane pore) and granzymes (serine proteases that enter through that pore and trigger apoptosis) from cytoplasmic granules, and secondarily through the Fas/FasL pathway, in which Fas ligand on the CTL engages Fas (CD95) on the target to trigger apoptosis without granzyme involvement; lysozyme, a bactericidal enzyme in phagocyte lysosomes, plays no part in either apoptotic route. NK cells share the granule-exocytosis killing mechanism and also kill by apoptosis, but are not MHC-restricted the way CTLs are: an NK cell instead integrates opposing signals from killer-activating receptors and killer-inhibitory receptors, the latter normally engaged by self MHC class I to restrain killing. When a cell loses MHC class I expression — as many virally infected and tumour cells do specifically to escape CTL recognition — that inhibitory signal is removed (the "missing self" state) and the NK cell is released to kill it, the functional opposite of the CTL requirement for MHC-I-presented antigen.

CD4+ T cells are not a single functional pool: besides conventional Th1/Th2 helper subsets, CD4 is also carried by CD4+CD25+ regulatory T cells (Tregs), which are themselves antigen specific (not bystander-acting in an antigen-non-specific way) and enforce peripheral self-tolerance by inhibiting self-reactive clones through the inhibitory cytokines IL-10 and TGF-beta.

### Clinical significance
Distinguishing NK "missing-self" killing from CTL "presented-antigen" killing explains why viruses that succeed at completely down-regulating MHC class I to dodge CTLs remain vulnerable to NK surveillance instead — a classic exam vignette pairs MHC-I loss on virally infected cells with NK cells as the answer, not CTLs. Superantigen-driven, antigen-nonspecific T-cell activation (as in staphylococcal toxic shock) explains why illness onset is so rapid and severe: an enormous polyclonal fraction of the T-cell pool is engaged at once, rather than the small antigen-specific clone that ordinary infection would recruit.
