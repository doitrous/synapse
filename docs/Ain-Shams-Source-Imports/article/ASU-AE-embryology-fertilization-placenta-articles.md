## summary
This ASU Year 1 Introduction to Anatomy and Embryology slice (Embryology, "Embryo 2" fertilization-mechanics MCQ bank) covers where the acrosomal reaction actually happens and which layers around the oocyte a sperm genuinely has to cross to fertilize it. It is a local answer-key-grounded draft and still needs independent embryology evidence before publication.

## sections
### Definition
The acrosomal reaction is the sperm's exocytosis of acrosomal enzymes (acrosin, hyaluronidase) that lets it digest a path through the oocyte's investment layers. It takes place in the female genital tract, at the site of fertilization, never inside the male tract.

### Mechanism
Sequence matters here: capacitation happens first, in the female genital tract, stripping the glycoprotein coat and seminal-plasma proteins from the sperm surface; only a capacitated sperm can then undergo the acrosomal reaction on contact with the corona radiata/zona pellucida. Once triggered, the acrosomal reaction lets the sperm cross, in order, the cumulus oophorus, the corona radiata (a layer of follicle cells), and the zona pellucida (the glycoprotein coat carrying the sperm-binding receptors), before it fuses with the oolemma -- the oocyte's own cell (vitelline) membrane. The perivitelline space, the fluid-filled gap between the oolemma and the zona pellucida into which the polar bodies are extruded, is not one of these barriers: the sperm only crosses it after the zona is already breached, on the final short stretch to the oolemma.

### Key determinants
Keep two things straight: (1) where the acrosomal reaction happens (the female tract, at fertilization -- never the epididymis, vas deferens or seminiferous tubules), and (2) which of the five commonly-listed structures around the oocyte is a true barrier the sperm penetrates (cumulus oophorus, corona radiata, zona pellucida, oolemma/vitelline membrane) versus which is a space it merely crosses afterward (the perivitelline space).

### Clinical significance
This article does not make fertility-treatment or diagnostic-workup claims. Its study value is foundational: assisted-reproduction concepts such as intracytoplasmic sperm injection are taught later, once the student already separates the true investment barriers from the perivitelline space they overlie.

### Common misconceptions
Do not place the acrosomal reaction in the male reproductive tract -- it requires prior capacitation, which itself only occurs in the female genital tract, so the reaction cannot happen any earlier than that.

Do not list the perivitelline space as a barrier a sperm "penetrates" alongside the corona radiata, zona pellucida and vitelline membrane -- it is a space, not a barrier, and the sperm only enters it once the real barrier (the zona) is already crossed.

## summary
This ASU Year 1 Introduction to Anatomy and Embryology slice (Embryology, "Embryo 2" MCQ bank) covers why conjoined/fused twins are always monozygotic and names the sacral-region conjoined-twin term the ASU paper tests (pygopagus). It is a local answer-key-grounded draft and still needs independent embryology evidence before publication.

## sections
### Definition
Conjoined (fused) twins are monozygotic twins whose single embryonic disc failed to split completely after about day 13, leaving them joined at a named body region -- pygopagus twins, for example, are fused at the sacrococcygeal/buttock region.

### Mechanism
Monozygotic twinning comes from one fertilized ovum splitting at some point after fertilization; the later the split, the more incomplete it can be, and a split late enough (after the primitive streak/embryonic disc has formed) leaves the two embryos joined rather than fully separate -- this is a conjoined-twin pair, always monozygotic by definition, because it started from a single zygote. Dizygotic twins, by contrast, come from two separate ova fertilized by two separate sperm and implanted as two independent conceptuses; there is no single disc to split incompletely, so dizygotic twins can never be conjoined.

### Key determinants
Hold onto one clean rule: conjoined/conjoint = monozygotic, always, no exceptions, because fusion requires a single disc that split late and incompletely; two independently implanted dizygotic conceptuses have no shared disc to fuse.

### Clinical significance
This article does not make surgical-separation or prognosis claims about conjoined twins. Its study value is foundational: recognizing that any conjoined pair is necessarily monozygotic is a prerequisite for later obstetric and genetic teaching on twin pregnancy classification.

### Common misconceptions
Do not call conjoined/conjoint twins dizygotic -- the ASU paper specifically tests this as the false statement in a "choose the INCORRECT statement" question, precisely because it is a common wrong instinct to treat "conjoined" as just another twin subtype independent of zygosity.
