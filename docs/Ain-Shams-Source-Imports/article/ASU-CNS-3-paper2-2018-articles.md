## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: The abducens (CN VI) nucleus lies in the caudal pons, immediately beneath the facial colliculus in the floor of the fourth ventricle, with the looping fibres of the facial nerve (internal genu) wrapped around it. Because of this superficial, floor-facing position, an expanding mass in the fourth ventricle (such as an ependymoma or a pontine glioma bulging into the ventricle) compresses the abducens nucleus early, producing an ipsilateral lateral rectus palsy sometimes accompanied by ipsilateral facial weakness from the adjacent facial genu. This makes abducens palsy a classically taught localising sign of fourth-ventricle floor pathology.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: Wernicke's area, in the posterior part of the superior temporal gyrus of the dominant (usually left) hemisphere, is classically described as the general interpretative (gnostic) area where incoming auditory and visual language signals are analysed for meaning and where coherent thoughts are formulated. These formulated thoughts are then relayed, chiefly via the arcuate fasciculus, to Broca's area in the inferior frontal gyrus, which converts them into the motor programme for articulate speech. A lesion of Wernicke's area therefore produces fluent but meaningless (receptive/Wernicke) aphasia, because the patient can still produce speech sounds but has lost the capacity to formulate or comprehend coherent language content.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: The great cerebral vein (of Galen) is a short, unpaired venous trunk formed beneath the splenium of the corpus callosum by the union of the two internal cerebral veins (which themselves drain the deep structures of the cerebral hemispheres), together with the paired basal veins of Rosenthal. It then curves backward and joins the inferior sagittal sinus to form the straight sinus. As a deep venous confluence, its thrombosis or malformation (e.g. vein of Galen aneurysmal malformation) has serious clinical consequences distinct from superficial cortical venous pathology.

## summary

Individual axonal action potentials are too brief, too asynchronous, and too poorly aligned in orientation to summate into a detectable scalp signal, so the EEG specifically reflects this spontaneous graded synaptic activity rather than spike (action potential) activity. ## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: Cerebrospinal fluid, produced chiefly by the choroid plexus, is reabsorbed back into the systemic venous circulation through arachnoid villi (and their larger clusters, the arachnoid granulations), finger-like projections of the arachnoid mater that protrude into the venous sinuses, principally the superior sagittal sinus. These villi act as one-way pressure-sensitive valves, allowing bulk flow of CSF into venous blood when CSF pressure exceeds venous sinus pressure, but preventing back-flow of blood into the subarachnoid space. ## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: Tetanospasmin, the neurotoxin of Clostridium tetani, travels retrogradely along motor axons and transsynaptically into inhibitory interneurons (including Renshaw cells) in the spinal cord and brainstem, where it cleaves synaptobrevin (VAMP), a protein required for synaptic vesicle docking and fusion, blocking the release of the inhibitory neurotransmitters glycine and GABA. Loss of this normal inhibitory restraint on motor neurons produces uncontrolled, simultaneous contraction of agonist and antagonist muscles, manifesting clinically as the sustained muscle spasms (trismus/lockjaw, opisthotonus, generalised rigidity) characteristic of tetanus. This mechanism mirrors botulinum toxin's cleavage of similar vesicle-fusion proteins, but botulinum acts at the neuromuscular junction on excitatory (acetylcholine-releasing) synapses rather than on inhibitory CNS synapses, producing flaccid rather than spastic paralysis.

## summary

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: Bridging (cortical) veins drain the surface of the cerebral cortex and cross the subdural space to empty into the dural venous sinuses (chiefly the superior sagittal sinus); because they traverse this space largely unsupported, they are vulnerable to tearing from shearing forces during head trauma, particularly with rapid deceleration, and their rupture is the classic mechanism of subdural haemorrhage. This contrasts with epidural (extradural) haemorrhage, most often caused by tearing of the middle meningeal artery after a temporal bone fracture, giving the two haemorrhage types distinct vessels, spaces and clinical time-courses (subdural bleeding often slower and more insidious, given its lower-pressure venous source). ## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: Clozapine, an atypical antipsychotic reserved for treatment-resistant schizophrenia because of its superior efficacy, carries a well-known risk of agranulocytosis (severe neutropenia), a potentially life-threatening adverse effect that mandates regular absolute neutrophil count monitoring throughout treatment, with mandatory dose interruption or discontinuation if counts fall below defined thresholds. This risk is a defining feature that distinguishes clozapine's safety profile from other atypical antipsychotics, and is the reason clozapine is not used as a first-line agent despite its strong efficacy in resistant cases. The exact mechanism is thought to involve toxic or immune-mediated injury to bone marrow granulocyte precursors.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: The hypoglossal nerve (CN XII) provides motor innervation to nearly all the intrinsic and extrinsic muscles of the tongue (genioglossus, hyoglossus, styloglossus and the intrinsic tongue muscles), the single exception being palatoglossus, which is instead supplied by the vagus nerve (via the pharyngeal plexus, carrying fibres from the cranial accessory) because it develops from pharyngeal arch mesenchyme rather than occipital myotomes. A unilateral hypoglossal nerve lesion produces ipsilateral tongue weakness and wasting, with the protruded tongue deviating toward the side of the lesion because of the unopposed action of the healthy-side genioglossus. This near-total but not absolute rule (excepting palatoglossus) is a classic anatomy exam nuance.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: The chorda tympani, a branch of the facial nerve (CN VII) carrying preganglionic parasympathetic fibres from the superior salivatory nucleus, joins the lingual nerve (a branch of the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve) and travels with it to reach the submandibular ganglion, where it synapses; postganglionic fibres from this ganglion then supply secretomotor innervation to the submandibular and sublingual salivary glands. The chorda tympani also carries special sensory (taste) fibres from the anterior two-thirds of the tongue, making it a mixed nerve with both a distinct secretomotor and a distinct gustatory function along the same short course through the middle ear. Its close relationship to the lingual nerve, despite arising from a different cranial nerve, is a classic anatomical pairing tested in this region.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: The anterior nucleus of the thalamus receives a major direct input from the mammillary bodies of the hypothalamus via the mammillothalamic tract (the tract of Vicq d'Azyr), a specific white-matter connection that forms one link of the Papez circuit (hippocampus to fornix to mammillary bodies to mammillothalamic tract to anterior thalamic nucleus to cingulate gyrus). ## hold_these
The anterior nucleus of the thalamus receives a major direct input from the mammillary bodies of the hypothalamus via the mammillothalamic tract (the tract of Vicq d'Azyr), a specific white-matter connection that forms one link of the Papez circuit (hippocampus to fornix to mammillary bodies to mammillothalamic tract to anterior thalamic nucleus to cingulate gyrus). The anterior nucleus then projects onward to the cingulate gyrus via the anterior thalamic radiation, continuing the circuit's role in memory consolidation and emotional processing. This mammillothalamic connection is clinically relevant because lesions here (e.g. in Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome, which damages the mammillary bodies) disrupt this specific relay and contribute to the resulting anterograde amnesia.
