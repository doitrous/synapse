## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: The inferior salivatory nucleus, a general visceral efferent (parasympathetic) brainstem nucleus in the medulla, gives rise to preganglionic parasympathetic fibres that travel with the glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX), then via its tympanic branch and the lesser petrosal nerve, to synapse in the otic ganglion; postganglionic fibres from the otic ganglion then join the auriculotemporal nerve to supply secretomotor innervation to the parotid gland. This pathway is classically contrasted with the superior salivatory nucleus, which instead supplies the submandibular, sublingual, lacrimal and nasal glands via the facial nerve. The inferior salivatory nucleus/glossopharyngeal/parotid pathway is a distinctive single-target parasympathetic route among the head's salivary glands.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: Benztropine is a centrally acting anticholinergic (muscarinic antagonist) that restores the normal dopamine-acetylcholine balance in the basal ganglia when it is disrupted by dopamine-receptor-blocking drugs (chiefly typical antipsychotics), and is used to treat the resulting drug-induced extrapyramidal symptoms, including tremor, rigidity and acute dystonic reactions. Its mechanism works by reducing the relative cholinergic excess that occurs when dopaminergic tone is pharmacologically blocked, rather than by directly restoring dopamine itself. This is distinct from Parkinson disease's own idiopathic dopamine-deficiency tremor, for which benztropine may also give symptomatic relief through the same cholinergic-blocking mechanism, though dopaminergic agents remain first-line there.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: Astrocytes, which envelop cerebral capillaries with their perivascular end-feet as part of the blood-brain barrier and the neurovascular unit, take up glucose from the capillary blood using the insulin-independent facilitative glucose transporter GLUT1, which is expressed at high density on both the capillary endothelium and the astrocyte end-foot membrane. This astrocytic glucose uptake supports the astrocyte-neuron lactate shuttle model, in which astrocytes metabolise glucose glycolytically to lactate and export it to neurons as an additional fuel source, particularly during high neuronal activity. ## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: The sympathetic (thoracolumbar) outflow arises from preganglionic neurons in the lateral horn of the spinal cord grey matter, extending from the first thoracic (T1) segment down to the second lumbar (L2) segment, in contrast to the parasympathetic (craniosacral) outflow, which arises separately from brainstem nuclei and the sacral (S2-S4) spinal segments. L2 marks the caudal limit of this thoracolumbar sympathetic column, below which no further preganglionic sympathetic cell bodies are found in the spinal cord. This T1-L2 extent is the anatomical basis for the paravertebral sympathetic chain's own longer cranial and caudal extensions beyond the cord segments that actually contribute outflow, since ascending and descending fibres within the chain redistribute the fixed T1-L2 output to targets at other levels.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: The cornea is densely innervated by the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve, but its sensory endings consist almost exclusively of bare (free) nerve endings rather than the encapsulated, specialised mechanoreceptors (e.g. Meissner's or Pacinian corpuscles) found in the skin. Because free nerve endings are predominantly nociceptors, the cornea is one of the most pain-sensitive tissues in the body, and even minor mechanical, thermal or chemical stimuli are perceived chiefly as pain rather than as discriminative touch. This exclusively free-nerve-ending innervation is the physiological basis of the corneal (blink) reflex, in which even light corneal touch triggers a protective reflex blink via the trigeminal afferent and facial nerve efferent limbs.

In the Rexed lamination scheme of the spinal cord dorsal horn grey matter, Lamina I, also called the posteromarginal nucleus (nucleus posteromarginalis), is the thin, most dorsal (posterior-most) layer capping the dorsal horn, and it receives predominantly nociceptive (pain) and thermal (temperature) primary afferent input, chiefly from A-delta and C fibres, before relaying it onward into the ascending spinothalamic pathway. Its dorsal-most position, immediately beneath the dorsolateral tract (of Lissauer) through which these same primary afferents first enter and ascend/descend a segment or two before synapsing, is a key identifying feature distinguishing it from the deeper laminae (e.g. lamina II, the substantia gelatinosa, which modulates pain transmission via interneurons rather than directly relaying it onward).
