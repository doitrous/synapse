## sections
### Definition
The adrenal (suprarenal) gland has two embryologically unrelated parts fused into one organ: the cortex, from mesoderm, and the medulla, from neural crest cells (the same cell population that gives sympathetic ganglia elsewhere). A teratogen is any agent -- drug, infection or physical factor -- capable of disrupting normal embryonic or fetal development. Polyhydramnios is an abnormally increased amniotic fluid volume.

### Mechanism
The adrenal cortex arises from mesodermal cells of the posterior abdominal wall that condense on either side of the root of the dorsal mesentery; the adrenal medulla arises separately, from neural crest cells that migrate in and differentiate into chromaffin cells, so a question that asks "which of these is mesodermal/ectodermal/neural crest" about the adrenal gland is really asking the student to keep the cortex and the medulla apart. Teratogenic susceptibility follows a critical period: the embryonic period (roughly the first trimester, when organs are being laid down) is when a teratogen does the most damage, while exposure before implantation tends to be all-or-none (the embryo survives unaffected or is lost) and later fetal-period exposure mostly causes growth or functional deficits rather than structural malformation. Not every drug crosses the placenta or disrupts development -- heparin is the standard teaching example of a safe anticoagulant in pregnancy because its large molecular size prevents placental transfer, unlike warfarin, which does cross and is teratogenic. Polyhydramnios most often reflects a fetus that cannot swallow and process amniotic fluid normally -- esophageal atresia is the classic structural cause (the fetus cannot swallow at all), and other upper-GI obstructions produce the same effect, the functional mirror of oligohydramnios from reduced fetal urine output.

### Key determinants
Keep three distinctions straight: (1) adrenal cortex = mesoderm, adrenal medulla = neural crest -- never both the same layer; (2) the first trimester (embryonic period) is the highest-risk teratogen window, and a drug that does not cross the placenta (heparin) cannot be teratogenic regardless of timing; (3) polyhydramnios points toward the fetus swallowing too little (GI/esophageal obstruction), the mirror image of oligohydramnios pointing toward the fetus urinating too little (renal/urinary obstruction).

### Clinical significance
This article does not make prescribing or diagnostic-workup claims. Its study value is foundational: it is the standard first-year framework for reading a hydramnios finding as a clue to a specific fetal anomaly, for keeping the "safe in pregnancy" reasoning (does the drug cross the placenta?) distinct from a drug's other properties, and for not conflating the adrenal gland's two embryologically distinct parts.

### Common misconceptions
Do not treat the adrenal gland as a single-origin organ -- cortex and medulla come from different germ-layer lineages and are frequently tested against each other as "which one is the exception."

Do not assume every drug given during pregnancy is teratogenic by default, or that safety is unrelated to placental transfer -- heparin is the standard example of a large molecule that simply cannot cross.

Do not swap oligohydramnios and polyhydramnios causes -- reduced fetal urine output (renal agenesis) causes too little fluid; reduced fetal swallowing (esophageal/GI atresia) causes too much.

## hold_these
Adrenal cortex is mesodermal; adrenal medulla is from neural crest (ectodermal lineage).
Teratogenic damage is greatest during the first trimester (the embryonic period, organogenesis).
Heparin does not cross the placenta and is the standard non-teratogenic-anticoagulant example.
Polyhydramnios classically points to a fetal GI/esophageal atresia (the fetus cannot swallow amniotic fluid).

## lose_the_mark
Calling the adrenal medulla mesodermal, or the cortex neural-crest-derived.
Assuming heparin is teratogenic because most drugs are treated with caution in pregnancy.
Confusing polyhydramnios (too much fluid, GI/esophageal cause) with oligohydramnios (too little fluid, renal cause).
