## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS anatomy slice covers: The spinal part of the accessory nerve (cranial nerve XI) arises from the upper five or six cervical spinal cord segments, ascends through the foramen magnum, and then exits the skull through the jugular foramen to supply sternocleidomastoid and trapezius as their sole motor nerve. Damage to the spinal accessory nerve therefore produces weakness or paralysis of sternocleidomastoid, seen clinically as difficulty turning the head to the opposite side.

## sections
### Definition
The spinal part of the accessory nerve (cranial nerve XI) arises from the upper five or six cervical spinal cord segments, ascends through the foramen magnum, and then exits the skull through the jugular foramen to supply sternocleidomastoid and trapezius as their sole motor nerve. Damage to the spinal accessory nerve therefore produces weakness or paralysis of sternocleidomastoid, seen clinically as difficulty turning the head to the opposite side.

### Mechanism

Attributing sternomastoid weakness to the cranial root of the accessory nerve (which actually joins the vagus to supply pharyngeal and laryngeal muscles) rather than the spinal root.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS anatomy slice covers: The superior ophthalmic vein communicates anteriorly with the facial vein (via the angular vein) and drains posteriorly into the cavernous sinus. Because facial veins in the 'danger area' of the face are valveless, infection there (for example from a boil or squeezed pimple near the nose or upper lip) can travel retrogradely along the superior ophthalmic vein into the cavernous sinus and cause septic cavernous sinus thrombosis.

## sections

## sections

## sections
### Definition
The medial longitudinal fasciculus is a brainstem tract that coordinates eye movements with head/neck posture, carrying fibres from the vestibular nuclei, and receiving contributions from tracts such as the tectospinal and (variably taught) interstitiospinal and vestibulospinal systems in its caudal continuation. The corticospinal tract, the principal pathway for voluntary skilled movement, runs separately through the pyramids and is not one of the tracts contributing to the MLF.
