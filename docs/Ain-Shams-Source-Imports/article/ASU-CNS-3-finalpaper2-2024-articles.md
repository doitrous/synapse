## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: The trigeminal lemniscus (the combined trigeminothalamic pathway) carries pain, temperature and touch information from the face and oral cavity, relayed through the principal sensory and spinal trigeminal nuclei, crosses the midline in the pons and medulla, and ascends to terminate in the ventral posteromedial (VPM) nucleus of the thalamus. VPM is the facial counterpart of the ventral posterolateral (VPL) nucleus, which receives the medial lemniscus and spinothalamic tract carrying the equivalent modalities from the body. From VPM, third-order neurons project through the posterior limb of the internal capsule to the face representation of the primary somatosensory cortex in the postcentral gyrus.

### Mechanism

Confusing VPM (face sensation via the trigeminal lemniscus) with VPL (body sensation via the medial lemniscus and spinothalamic tract) — the two nuclei sit side by side in the thalamus and are functionally parallel, differing only in the body region relayed.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: Cerebral hemispheric specialisation assigns language, calculation and sequential/analytic tasks chiefly to the dominant (usually left) hemisphere, while the non-dominant hemisphere — often termed the 'representational' hemisphere — specialises in visuospatial and holistic processing tasks, including facial recognition, spatial orientation, and musical/emotional prosody. Damage to the non-dominant parietal and temporal-occipital association cortex characteristically produces prosopagnosia (inability to recognise familiar faces) and other visuospatial deficits, even though language remains intact. This lateralisation is not absolute but reflects a strong statistical bias, particularly well documented in right-handed individuals whose left hemisphere is dominant for language.

## sections
### Definition
Cerebral hemispheric specialisation assigns language, calculation and sequential/analytic tasks chiefly to the dominant (usually left) hemisphere, while the non-dominant hemisphere — often termed the 'representational' hemisphere — specialises in visuospatial and holistic processing tasks, including facial recognition, spatial orientation, and musical/emotional prosody. Damage to the non-dominant parietal and temporal-occipital association cortex characteristically produces prosopagnosia (inability to recognise familiar faces) and other visuospatial deficits, even though language remains intact. This lateralisation is not absolute but reflects a strong statistical bias, particularly well documented in right-handed individuals whose left hemisphere is dominant for language.

Attribute facial recognition to the representational (non-dominant) cerebral hemisphere.

### Key determinants
Assuming the non-dominant hemisphere is functionally silent because it lacks language — it carries its own specialised repertoire (facial recognition, spatial processing, musical/emotional prosody), and its damage produces distinct deficits such as prosopagnosia rather than aphasia.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: Astrocytes are the neuroglial cell type whose cytoskeleton is characteristically built on glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), an intermediate filament specific to astrocytes and used histologically and immunohistochemically to identify them and tumours derived from them (e.g. astrocytomas). Astrocytic end-feet envelop the capillary endothelium of the central nervous system, and this astrocyte-endothelium relationship, together with the endothelial tight junctions themselves, is a key structural and functional component of the blood-brain barrier, which restricts and regulates the passage of substances between blood and brain tissue. Astrocytes also help maintain the extracellular ionic environment, provide metabolic support to neurons, and contribute to repair (gliosis) after CNS injury.

### definition_of · CON-NEU-ADDDF80E33C8ED
Quote: Commercial local anaesthetic solutions are formulated at an acidic pH to keep the drug soluble and stable, but this leaves most of the anaesthetic molecules in their ionised (charged), poorly lipid-soluble form, which crosses the nerve sheath and axonal membrane slowly. Adding sodium bicarbonate raises the pH of the solution towards physiological, shifting more of the weak-base local anaesthetic into its unionised (uncharged), lipid-soluble form, which diffuses through the nerve membrane faster to reach its intracellular binding site on the voltage-gated sodium channel. The practical result of bicarbonate alkalinization is a faster onset of anaesthesia and less injection-site discomfort, without changing the eventual depth or duration of the block once equilibrium is reached.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: Infected or chronically inflamed tissue has a lower (more acidic) extracellular pH than healthy tissue, a consequence of local metabolic byproducts, hypoxia, and the inflammatory response. Because local anaesthetics are weak bases whose proportion of unionised, lipid-soluble drug depends on the surrounding pH relative to their pKa, this acidic extracellular environment shifts more of the drug into its ionised, poorly diffusible form, so less anaesthetic penetrates the nerve sheath to reach its site of action inside the axon. This is the pharmacological basis for the common clinical observation that local anaesthesia is less effective, or fails outright, when injected into an infected or abscessed area, sometimes requiring a regional nerve block placed outside the acidic field instead.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: Imipramine, a tricyclic antidepressant, has a well-established non-psychiatric use in childhood nocturnal enuresis, historically one of the most common indications for prescribing it in paediatrics before behavioural and desmopressin-based approaches became first line. Its beneficial effect in enuresis is thought to combine its anticholinergic action (increasing bladder capacity, relaxing detrusor tone) with an effect on sleep architecture that reduces the depth of sleep during the hours the bladder would otherwise need to signal fullness. ## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: Valproate, a broad-spectrum antiepileptic and mood-stabilising drug, carries a well-documented set of adverse effects that disproportionately affect females of reproductive age: it is strongly teratogenic (associated with neural tube defects and a broader fetal valproate syndrome, among the highest-risk anticonvulsants in pregnancy) and is also associated with a polycystic-ovary-like endocrine syndrome, producing menstrual irregularities, hyperandrogenism-related hirsutism, and weight gain. Because of these risks, regulatory bodies and prescribing guidelines specifically restrict or require additional safeguards (pregnancy prevention programmes, specialist review) when valproate is prescribed to females of childbearing potential, favouring alternative antiepileptic or mood-stabilising agents where seizure or mood control allows.

Atypical (second-generation) antipsychotics combine lower or more transient D2 antagonism with serotonin 5-HT2A antagonism, a combination that produces fewer extrapyramidal side effects at therapeutic doses and, through this same serotonin-dopamine interaction, more meaningful efficacy against the negative symptoms of schizophrenia (flat affect, avolition, social withdrawal) than typical agents achieve. These two advantages, together with a generally lower risk of tardive dyskinesia, are the principal reasons atypical antipsychotics are now preferred as first-line therapy in most guidelines, despite their own distinct risk of metabolic side effects.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming atypical antipsychotics are free of significant adverse effects because they cause fewer extrapyramidal symptoms — their advantage is specifically in the EPS and negative-symptom domains, while they carry their own distinct liability for metabolic side effects (weight gain, dyslipidaemia, new-onset diabetes).

## summary
