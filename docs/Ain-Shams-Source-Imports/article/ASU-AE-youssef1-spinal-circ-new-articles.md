## summary
This ASU Year 1 Introduction to Anatomy and Embryology slice (the "youssef1" MCQ bank, "MCQs - Bg Mcq dr.youssef.pdf", pp.18-19) covers six facts about spinal nerve structure and the autonomic outflow that `find-existing.mjs` found nowhere else in the corpus: the dorsal-root/ventral-root sensory-motor split, the ventral-root pathway of preganglionic sympathetic fibres, the craniosacral origin of the parasympathetic outflow, the ventral-rami origin of nerve plexuses and the definition of a ganglion, the paired CNS/PNS terminology (nucleus/ganglion, tract/nerve, synapse), and the subarachnoid location of cerebrospinal fluid. It is a local answer-key-grounded draft and still needs independent anatomy evidence before publication.

## sections
### Definition
A spinal nerve forms where a dorsal (sensory) root and a ventral (motor) root unite as they leave the spinal cord. Beyond that union, the nerve splits again into a dorsal ramus and a ventral ramus, both of which are mixed (motor and sensory). The autonomic nervous system leaves the CNS at two separate levels: the sympathetic division via the thoracolumbar spinal cord, and the parasympathetic division via cranial nerve nuclei and the sacral spinal cord (craniosacral).

### Mechanism
The dorsal root carries only afferent, sensory fibres, with its cell bodies in the dorsal root ganglion; the ventral root carries only efferent, motor fibres from the ventral grey horn. Once united into the spinal nerve trunk, and again after it splits into dorsal and ventral rami, this pure sensory/motor separation is lost. Preganglionic sympathetic fibres, arising from the lateral horn of the thoracolumbar cord, leave via the ventral root alongside the somatic motor fibres before branching off through the white ramus communicans to the sympathetic chain. The parasympathetic outflow instead arises from cranial nerve nuclei (III, VII, IX, X) and spinal segments S2-S4. The great somatic plexuses (cervical, brachial, lumbar, sacral) are formed by the ventral rami only, never the dorsal rami, which instead supply the back segmentally. Structurally, a group of nerve cell bodies is a nucleus inside the CNS and a ganglion outside it; a bundle of nerve fibres is a tract inside the CNS and a nerve outside it; a synapse is the site of contact between two neurons wherever it lies. Cerebrospinal fluid fills the subarachnoid space, between the arachnoid mater and the pia mater -- not the subdural space (between dura and arachnoid) or the epidural space.

### Key determinants
Keep four pairings straight: (1) dorsal root = sensory, ventral root = motor, but the nerve trunk and rami beyond the union are mixed; (2) sympathetic = thoracolumbar outflow, parasympathetic = craniosacral outflow; (3) plexuses form from ventral rami, never dorsal rami; (4) nucleus/tract are CNS terms, ganglion/nerve are the matching PNS terms, and CSF lives specifically in the subarachnoid space.

### Clinical significance
This article does not make prescribing or diagnostic-workup claims. Its study value is foundational: a dorsal root or dorsal root ganglion lesion produces a segmental sensory deficit with motor sparing, exactly because the roots are functionally pure at that level, and locating CSF correctly in the subarachnoid space is what makes a lumbar puncture technique make sense.

### Common misconceptions
Do not treat the spinal nerve trunk, or its dorsal/ventral rami, as still functionally "pure" sensory or motor -- that separation exists only at the root level, proximal to their union.

Do not swap the autonomic outflow labels -- sympathetic is thoracolumbar, parasympathetic is craniosacral, not the reverse.

Do not attribute plexus formation to the dorsal rami, or describe the ventral rami as purely motor.

Do not place CSF in the subdural or epidural space -- it fills the subarachnoid space, between arachnoid and pia.

## sections
### Definition
An end artery supplies a territory with no adequate alternative blood supply if it is blocked; end arteries are either anatomical (no anastomosis at all) or functional (anastomoses present but usually insufficient). A portal circulation runs from one capillary bed, through a portal vein, to a second capillary bed, rather than straight from capillaries to veins. A sinusoid is a wide, irregular, leaky vascular channel intermediate between a capillary and a small vein. Lymphatic vessels return excess interstitial fluid to the venous system, beginning as blind-ended lymph capillaries in the tissues.

### Mechanism
An anatomical end artery, such as the central retinal artery, has no anastomosis with neighbouring arteries at all. A functional end artery, such as the coronary or mesenteric arteries, has small collateral anastomoses that are ordinarily too narrow to compensate for a sudden block, though they may enlarge to compensate a gradual one. The tricuspid valve, the right atrioventricular valve, closes during ventricular systole to stop blood regurgitating from the right ventricle back into the right atrium. Pulmonary circulation carries deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle, through the pulmonary trunk, to the lungs, the reverse of the systemic circulation's left-ventricle-to-aorta path. Sinusoids are most numerous in the liver, where they are the organ's principal exchange vessels. A portal circulation, typified by the hepatic portal system, begins in the intestinal capillary bed, passes through the portal vein, and ends in the liver sinusoids -- a second capillary-like bed -- before draining via the hepatic veins. Lymphatic capillaries are absent from avascular tissues (cartilage, cornea, epidermis, nails), from the central nervous system, and from bone marrow; where they are obstructed, as in the upper limb, the affected part becomes oedematous rather than ischaemic. Arteriovenous anastomoses are direct arteriole-to-venule connections, numerous in the skin of the nose, nail beds and lips, that help control body temperature; communication between arteries and veins can also occur via ordinary capillaries, cavernous tissue, or sinusoids. Among the three principal vessel classes, veins are richest in valves, capillaries are the exchange vessels present in virtually every tissue, and arteries (especially the larger elastic arteries) are richest in elastic fibres. By convention an artery gives off branches while a vein receives tributaries; venae comitantes are the small paired veins that accompany a small or medium artery, not a big vein. Lymph capillaries characteristically start blindly in the tissues -- their one universally true property, since they are not present in every tissue and are not permeable to small particles only. Lymph vessels share several features (numerous valves, they accompany veins, they are interrupted by lymph nodes, they drain into the venous system), but not all of them end in the thoracic duct: the right upper limb, the right side of the head and neck, and the right thorax drain instead via the separate right lymphatic duct.

### Key determinants
Keep six pairings straight: (1) anatomical end artery = no anastomosis at all; functional end artery = anastomoses present but usually insufficient for a sudden block; (2) pulmonary circulation runs right-ventricle-to-lungs, the systemic circulation runs left-ventricle-to-body; (3) a portal circulation ends in a second capillary bed, not directly in a vein; (4) lymphatic obstruction causes oedema, not ischaemia; (5) veins have valves and tributaries, arteries have elastic fibres and branches, capillaries are the universal exchange vessel, and venae comitantes pair with small/medium arteries, not big veins; (6) lymph capillaries start blindly, and not all lymph drains via the thoracic duct -- the right lymphatic duct is a separate route.

### Clinical significance
This article does not make prescribing or diagnostic-workup claims. Its study value is foundational: recognising an anatomical end artery (such as the central retinal artery) explains why its sudden occlusion causes irreversible infarction with no rescue route, understanding lymphatic obstruction explains post-surgical or filarial lymphoedema, and knowing the right lymphatic duct's separate drainage territory matters for tracing metastatic spread from the right side of the body.

### Common misconceptions
Do not assume any artery with a named collateral anastomosis can always compensate for a sudden block -- a functional end artery's collaterals are usually insufficient for that.

Do not confuse the tricuspid valve's atrioventricular role with a semilunar valve's role, or attribute lymphatic obstruction to an arterial cause such as ischaemia.

Do not describe the portal circulation as ending directly in a vein, or assume a tissue must be avascular to lack lymphatics.

Do not swap "branches" and "tributaries" between arteries and veins, or pair venae comitantes with a big vein rather than a small or medium artery.

Do not treat "present in all tissues" or "permeable to small particles only" as universally true of lymph capillaries, and do not assume every lymph vessel drains via the thoracic duct alone.
