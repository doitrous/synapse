## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: adjunctive dexamethasone, given with or just before the first dose of antibiotics for bacterial meningitis, blunts the inflammatory response to bacterial lysis and reduces neurological complications such as hearing loss; the benefit depends on early, first-dose timing.

## sections
### Definition
Adjunctive dexamethasone, given intravenously with or just before the first dose of antibiotic therapy for suspected or confirmed bacterial meningitis, reduces the inflammatory response generated as bacterial cell walls lyse under antibiotic action, thereby lowering the risk of neurological complications such as sensorineural hearing loss.

### Mechanism
State that dexamethasone is given with or immediately before the first dose of antibiotics in bacterial meningitis, to reduce neurological complications such as hearing loss.

### Key determinants
Its benefit depends on early, first-dose timing — dexamethasone started after antibiotics have already been running for some time no longer meaningfully blunts this early lytic inflammatory surge, so it must be given immediately, not as an afterthought once treatment is under way.

### Clinical significance
This batch does not extend the source's plain fact into diagnosis, treatment or management claims beyond what the compilation itself states.

### Common misconceptions
Giving dexamethasone only after antibiotics have already been running, rather than with or immediately before the first dose.

## hold_these
Adjunctive dexamethasone, given with or just before the first dose of antibiotics for bacterial meningitis, reduces neurological complications such as hearing loss; the benefit depends on early, first-dose timing.

## lose_the_mark
Giving dexamethasone only after antibiotics have already been running — the timing is what determines the benefit, not merely the drug choice.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: quetiapine, an atypical antipsychotic with weak, rapidly-dissociating D2 receptor blockade, is preferred for treating psychosis in Parkinson disease because it controls psychotic symptoms without markedly worsening parkinsonian motor symptoms, unlike most other antipsychotics.

## sections
### Definition
Psychosis (hallucinations, delusions) can complicate Parkinson disease, both from the disease itself and as an adverse effect of dopaminergic therapy, but most antipsychotics are potent D2 receptor antagonists that would worsen the patient's parkinsonism.

### Mechanism
State that quetiapine is preferred over other antipsychotics for treating psychosis in Parkinson disease because of its low risk of worsening parkinsonian motor symptoms.

### Key determinants
Quetiapine, an atypical (second-generation) antipsychotic with comparatively weak and rapidly-dissociating D2 receptor blockade, controls psychotic symptoms with a substantially lower risk of exacerbating extrapyramidal motor symptoms than typical antipsychotics or most other atypical agents.

### Clinical significance
This batch does not extend the source's plain fact into diagnosis, treatment or management claims beyond what the compilation itself states.

### Common misconceptions
Prescribing a typical or strongly D2-blocking atypical antipsychotic for psychosis in a Parkinson disease patient — this would relieve psychosis at the cost of markedly worsening rigidity and bradykinesia.

## hold_these
Quetiapine is preferred for psychosis in Parkinson disease because its weak, rapidly-dissociating D2 blockade avoids markedly worsening parkinsonian motor symptoms, unlike most other antipsychotics.

## lose_the_mark
Prescribing a strongly D2-blocking antipsychotic in a Parkinson disease patient, worsening rigidity and bradykinesia.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: habituation is a non-associative form of learning in which the response to a repeated, non-threatening stimulus progressively decreases, distinct from classical/operant conditioning and from its opposite, sensitisation.

## sections
### Definition
Habituation is a simple, non-associative form of learning in which the strength of a behavioural response to a repeated, unchanging stimulus progressively decreases as the nervous system learns that the stimulus is neither novel nor threatening and can safely be ignored.

### Mechanism
State that habituation is a non-associative form of learning in which the response to a repeated, non-threatening stimulus progressively decreases.

### Key determinants
It requires no association between two stimuli or between a stimulus and a reinforcer (distinguishing it from classical and operant conditioning), and its opposite, sensitisation, is the enhancement of a response after exposure to a strong or noxious stimulus.

### Clinical significance
This batch does not extend the source's plain fact into diagnosis, treatment or management claims beyond what the compilation itself states.

### Common misconceptions
Confusing this physiological sense of "habituation" (non-associative learning) with the unrelated pharmacological sense of the same word (psychic drug dependence).

## hold_these
Habituation is a non-associative form of learning in which the response to a repeated, non-threatening stimulus progressively decreases; its opposite is sensitisation.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing "habituation" the learning phenomenon with "habituation" the pharmacology term for psychic drug dependence.

## summary
This ASU Year 3 CNS slice covers: the posterior communicating artery links the internal carotid artery with the posterior cerebral artery, completing the posterior circle of Willis and allowing collateral flow between the carotid and vertebrobasilar systems; its origin from the internal carotid is also a classic berry-aneurysm site that can compress the oculomotor nerve.

## sections
### Definition
The posterior communicating artery is a short anastomotic vessel that runs backward from the internal carotid artery to join the posterior cerebral artery on each side, forming part of the posterior half of the circle of Willis and linking the anterior (carotid) and posterior (vertebrobasilar) cerebral circulations.

### Mechanism
State that the posterior communicating artery connects the internal carotid artery with the posterior cerebral artery.

### Key determinants
This anastomosis allows collateral blood flow between the carotid and vertebrobasilar systems if one is compromised, and its origin from the internal carotid artery is also one of the classic sites at which a congenital berry aneurysm forms, which can compress the adjacent oculomotor nerve and cause a painful third nerve palsy with pupillary involvement.

### Clinical significance
This batch does not extend the source's plain fact into diagnosis, treatment or management claims beyond what the compilation itself states.

### Common misconceptions
Confusing the posterior communicating artery's anastomotic role (internal carotid to posterior cerebral) with the anterior communicating artery, which connects the two anterior cerebral arteries across the midline.

## hold_these
The posterior communicating artery connects the internal carotid artery with the posterior cerebral artery, completing the posterior circle of Willis; its origin is a classic berry-aneurysm site that can compress the oculomotor nerve.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing the posterior communicating artery with the anterior communicating artery — they link different pairs of vessels.

## sections
### Definition
Sumatriptan, a selective serotonin 5-HT1B/1D receptor agonist ("triptan"), is used for the acute (abortive) treatment of moderate-to-severe migraine attacks once a headache has already started, working by causing cranial vasoconstriction and inhibiting the release of vasoactive neuropeptides from trigeminal nerve terminals.

### Mechanism
State that sumatriptan is used for the acute, abortive treatment of a moderate-to-severe migraine attack, distinct from prophylactic migraine therapy.

### Key determinants
This acute, attack-terminating role is distinct from prophylactic agents such as propranolol and amitriptyline, which are taken regularly to reduce the frequency of future attacks rather than to treat one already under way.

### Clinical significance
This batch does not extend the source's plain fact into diagnosis, treatment or management claims beyond what the compilation itself states.

### Common misconceptions
Confusing sumatriptan's acute, abortive role with the prophylactic role of propranolol/amitriptyline, taken regularly to prevent future attacks.

## hold_these

## lose_the_mark

## summary

## sections
### Definition
Interferon beta-1, given by injection, is a first-line disease-modifying therapy for relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis, reducing the frequency of relapses and slowing the accumulation of new demyelinating lesions through its immunomodulatory effects.

### Mechanism
State that interferon beta-1 is a first-line disease-modifying therapy for multiple sclerosis, distinct from second-line agents such as natalizumab.

### Key determinants
This first-line position contrasts with natalizumab, a monoclonal antibody reserved for patients whose disease remains active despite first-line therapy, or for more aggressive disease, reflecting natalizumab's greater efficacy but also its greater risk (including progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy).

### Clinical significance
This batch does not extend the source's plain fact into diagnosis, treatment or management claims beyond what the compilation itself states.

### Common misconceptions
Confusing interferon beta-1's first-line position with natalizumab's second-line/escalation role — the two occupy different steps of the treatment ladder.

## hold_these
Interferon beta-1 is a first-line disease-modifying therapy for multiple sclerosis; natalizumab is reserved for breakthrough or more aggressive disease.

## lose_the_mark
Treating natalizumab as interchangeable with first-line interferon beta-1, rather than a second-line escalation with added PML risk.
