## summary
This ASU Blood physiology practical slice defines bleeding time through platelet plug formation. The current evidence is a local Ain Shams lecture deck, so the article is drafted for curriculum alignment and still needs independent clinical verification before publication.

## sections
### Purpose and indications
The lecture presents bleeding time as one test used in the assessment of haemostatic function.

Use this article to connect bleeding time with platelet plug formation. It does not turn the lecture into a clinical protocol or add indications absent from the source.

### Principle
Bleeding time is the time required for bleeding from an injured vessel to stop by platelet plug formation.

### How it is performed
The lecture describes pricking a cleaned finger or ear lobule, removing blood drops every 15 seconds with filter paper without pressing or squeezing, and calculating the time from the number of blood spots.

### Normal findings
The local lecture gives a normal bleeding time of 2 to 5 minutes.

### Abnormal findings and interpretation
The lecture links prolonged bleeding time to vessel wall defects and platelet defects.

Drug and disease examples named in the lecture are not expanded here because interpretation needs independent clinical review.

### Limitations and pitfalls
Keep bleeding time with platelet plug formation rather than the clotting pathways screened by PT and aPTT.

Do not treat this local-curriculum source as independent clinical verification.

## hold_these
Bleeding time belongs with platelet plug formation.

## lose_the_mark
Placing bleeding time under the PT or aPTT clotting pathway would contradict this lecture.

## summary
This ASU Blood physiology practical slice separates prothrombin time from activated partial thromboplastin time by the clotting pathway each test assesses. The current evidence is a local Ain Shams lecture deck, so the article is drafted for curriculum alignment and still needs independent clinical verification before publication.

## sections
### Purpose and indications
The lecture presents PT and aPTT as coagulation tests used in the assessment of haemostatic function.

Use this article to keep the pathway screened by each test distinct. It does not turn the lecture into a clinical protocol or add indications absent from the source.

### Principle
Prothrombin time tests the integrity of the extrinsic clotting pathway, including factors V, VII and X, prothrombin, and fibrinogen.

Activated partial thromboplastin time tests the integrity of the intrinsic clotting pathway, including factors XII, XI, VIII, IX, X and V, prothrombin, and fibrinogen.

### How it is performed
The lecture names PT and aPTT as coagulation tests but does not give their laboratory procedure. That procedure is therefore not authored here.

### Normal findings
The local lecture does not supply a normal PT or aPTT range, so this draft leaves those ranges out.

### Abnormal findings and interpretation
The lecture links prolonged PT or aPTT to deficiencies in the pathway factors each test assesses.

Drug and disease examples named in the lecture are not expanded here because interpretation needs independent clinical review.

### Limitations and pitfalls
PT is the extrinsic-pathway screen in this lecture, while aPTT is the intrinsic-pathway screen.

Do not add normal ranges absent from the authorised source or treat this local-curriculum source as independent clinical verification.

## hold_these
PT is the extrinsic-pathway screen in this lecture.
aPTT is the intrinsic-pathway screen in this lecture.

## lose_the_mark
Swapping PT and aPTT reverses the pathway each test is meant to screen.
Adding normal PT or aPTT values from memory would go beyond this source.
