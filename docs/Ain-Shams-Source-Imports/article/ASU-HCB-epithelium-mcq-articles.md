## summary
The ASU HCB Dr. Hegazy epithelium MCQ bank tests epithelium as the one basic tissue with apical–basal polarity, general features that mark it out from connective, muscular and nervous tissue (avascularity, minimal intercellular substance, continuous turnover), the growth-abnormality vocabulary (dysplasia, metaplasia, neoplasia), and a set of named epithelial types tied to specific body locations: stratified squamous (keratinized in skin, non-keratinized in the esophagus and mouth), endothelium (blood vessels), mesothelium (pleura), pseudostratified columnar (trachea), simple columnar ciliated with goblet cells (Fallopian tube), transitional/urothelium (distention-adapted, e.g. bladder), and neuroepithelium (taste buds).

## sections
### Definition
Epithelium is one of the four basic tissues, and the only one that shows polarity — a structural and functional difference between its free apical surface and its attached basal surface.
It is built of cells with minimal intercellular substance, sitting on a basement membrane, and it is avascular: no blood vessels penetrate the epithelium itself, so it is nourished by diffusion from the underlying connective tissue. Epithelium also undergoes continuous cell turnover.

### Mechanism
In a stratified epithelium, only the basal cell layer actually rests on and attaches to the basement membrane, commonly via hemidesmosomes; the cells of the surface layer are the ones that reach the free apical surface and, in stratified squamous epithelium, are the flattened squamous cells. This basal-attachment rule is why "all cells rest on the basement membrane" and "all cells reach the apical surface" are both false statements about a stratified epithelium — only the basal layer does the former, only the surface layer does the latter.
Growth abnormalities of epithelium form a specific vocabulary: metaplasia is a reversible change of one epithelial type into another; dysplasia is disordered growth that falls short of tumour formation; neoplasia is the abnormal increase in the growth of cells that defines tumour formation.

### Key determinants
Different body sites are lined by epithelial types matched to their local mechanical and functional demands: stratified squamous keratinized epithelium withstands abrasion and dehydration in the skin and the palm of the hand; stratified squamous non-keratinized epithelium withstands abrasion in a wet environment, as in the esophagus and the mouth cavity; simple squamous endothelium lines blood vessels for a thin diffusion barrier; simple squamous mesothelium lines serous cavities such as the pleura; pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium (with goblet cells) lines the trachea for mucociliary clearance; simple columnar ciliated epithelium with goblet cells lines the Fallopian tube to move the ovum; transitional epithelium (urothelium) folds and stretches to withstand distention, as in the urinary bladder; and neuroepithelium, a sensory epithelium linked to nerve endings, forms the taste buds.

### Clinical significance
This is not a treatment rule. Its study value is recognising that a change in the epithelial type lining an organ (metaplasia) is a real, examinable process — for example, chronic irritation of a normally non-keratinized surface can drive metaplastic change — and that neoplasia is defined, not just implied, as abnormal cell-growth increase, a distinction tested directly in this bank.

### Common misconceptions
A common error is assuming epithelium is vascularised because it lies close to a richly vascular connective tissue layer; the vessels stay in the connective tissue.
Another is assuming every cell of a stratified epithelium touches the basement membrane or reaches the apical surface — only the basal and surface layers respectively do so.
A third is mismatching epithelial type to location: assigning a pseudostratified pattern to the Fallopian tube (it is simple columnar ciliated with goblet cells, not pseudostratified) or a stratified pattern to a site that needs distention resistance (transitional epithelium, not stratified squamous, withstands distention).

## summary
The same ASU HCB Dr. Hegazy MCQ bank tests three exocrine secretion mechanisms by their named example glands — merocrine secretion by exocytosis with the cell kept intact, holocrine secretion by whole-cell disintegration in the sebaceous gland, and apocrine secretion of the mammary gland's lipid component by pinching off apical cytoplasm — together with the light-microscopic appearance that distinguishes a mucous acinar cell from a serous one.

## sections
### Definition
Exocrine glands release their secretory product to a body surface or a duct system by one of three mechanisms, distinguished by what happens to the secreting cell: merocrine (cell intact), apocrine (apical cytoplasm lost with the product), and holocrine (whole cell disintegrates and becomes the secretion).

### Mechanism
In merocrine secretion, secretory material leaves the cell packaged in membrane-bound vesicles that fuse with the plasma membrane and release their contents by exocytosis; the cell and its membrane remain intact afterward. Most exocrine glands, including salivary and endocrine glands, secrete this way.
In holocrine secretion, the entire cell fills with secretory product, then disintegrates completely, and the cell debris itself becomes part of the secretion; the sebaceous gland is the classic example.
In apocrine secretion, only the apical portion of the cytoplasm — surrounding the secretory product — pinches off and is released, while the rest of the cell survives and regenerates that part; the mammary gland uses this route specifically for its lipid-droplet component (its protein component is merocrine).

### Key determinants
By light microscopy, a mucous-secreting acinar cell is recognised by vacuolated, pale basophilic cytoplasm and a flattened, basally displaced nucleus, reflecting mucous-filled distended cytoplasm; a serous-secreting acinar cell instead shows deeply basophilic, RER-rich cytoplasm with a rounded, more central nucleus and a prominent nucleolus, reflecting active protein synthesis for its zymogen-type secretion.

### Clinical significance
This is not a treatment rule. Its study value is that identifying the secretion mechanism by the named gland (sebaceous = holocrine, mammary lipid = apocrine, most others = merocrine) is a repeatedly tested fact pattern, and the light-microscopic mucous-versus-serous distinction is the same skill used later when interpreting real gland histology slides.

### Common misconceptions
A common error is assuming all exocrine secretion is exocytotic (merocrine); the sebaceous and mammary-lipid examples are deliberately not merocrine and are the ones the bank singles out.
Another is swapping the mucous and serous acinar-cell descriptions — assigning mucous cells the acidophilic, rounded-nucleus appearance that instead belongs to serous cells.
