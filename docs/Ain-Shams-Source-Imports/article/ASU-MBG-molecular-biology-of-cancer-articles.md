## summary
Cancer arises when the genes that normally control cell division stop working correctly, and this article works through the two gene classes involved — proto-oncogenes, whose gain-of-function mutation into an oncogene drives proliferation, and tumor suppressor genes, whose loss of function removes a brake on it — plus the two worked tumor suppressors this chapter tests in most depth, Rb (which restrains passage through the G1 restriction point by sequestering E2F) and p53 (the guardian of the genome, which halts the cycle or triggers apoptosis on DNA damage). It closes with the mechanisms that activate a proto-oncogene, the retrovirus-specific version of one of them, and the four hallmark features that together define a cancer cell.

## sections
### Definition
A proto-oncogene's normal product promotes cell division — a growth factor, its receptor, a signal-transduction protein, or a cell-cycle driver such as a cyclin. A tumor suppressor gene's normal product restrains division or repairs/eliminates damaged cells; RB and p53 are the two worked examples in this chapter, and CDK inhibitors (p15, p16, p21) are tumor suppressor genes too, not oncogenes, while cyclins drive the cycle and so are not tumor suppressors. Both gene classes function, in their normal form, in every healthy cell — a proto-oncogene is not switched on only once a cell becomes malignant. Recognised oncogenes named in this chapter's own key include Cerb-B2, c-Myc and Bcl-2.

### Mechanism
The retinoblastoma protein (Rb), in its hypophosphorylated (active) state, binds the E2F transcription factor and prevents it from driving the cell into S phase. A mitogenic signal triggers cyclin-CDK complexes to phosphorylate Rb; phosphorylated (inactivated) Rb releases E2F, and the cell passes the late-G1 restriction point committed to complete the cycle — so it is Rb's loss of function, not its activity, that permits uncontrolled proliferation. p53 acts at the same G1-to-S transition on a different arm: in a normal cell it is kept inactive by Mdm2 binding; DNA damage disrupts this interaction and stabilises p53, which halts the cycle at the G1-S checkpoint through p21, stimulates DNA-repair enzyme synthesis, and — if the damage is irreparable — commits the cell to apoptosis through the pro-apoptotic protein Bax. p53 halts the G1-S transition; it does not cause it, and it activates pro-apoptotic, never anti-apoptotic, gene expression.

A proto-oncogene is converted to an oncogene by point mutation, gene amplification, chromosomal translocation (the Philadelphia chromosome's BCR-ABL fusion is the worked example), or insertion mutagenesis — never by deletion, which removes a proto-oncogene's function rather than activating it, in direct contrast to a tumor suppressor gene, where deletion of the remaining functional copy is exactly what contributes to cancer. Retroviruses cause insertion mutagenesis by a specific route: the virus's RNA genome is first copied into DNA by its own reverse transcriptase, and if the resulting provirus integrates next to a host proto-oncogene, the strong viral promoter it carries drives that gene's overexpression.

### Key determinants
A cancer cell is defined by the combination of four features together, not any one alone: uncontrolled, autonomous cell division; invasion of neighbouring tissue; metastasis to distant sites; and resistance to apoptosis. Recognising that proto-oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes are functionally opposite — one gains function to drive proliferation, the other loses function to remove a brake on it — is the single distinction this whole topic turns on, and every mechanism, example and exception in this article follows from keeping that distinction straight.

### Clinical significance
The Philadelphia chromosome (a 9;22 reciprocal translocation producing the BCR-ABL fusion oncogene, diagnostic of chronic myeloid leukaemia) is the standard worked example connecting chromosomal translocation to oncogene activation. HER2/Cerb-B2 amplification in breast cancer is the standard worked example of gene amplification as an activating mechanism, and it is also a therapeutic target. Rb and p53 loss of function underlie retinoblastoma and the Li-Fraumeni family of cancers respectively, and are the two tumor suppressor genes a clinical question is most likely to name directly.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming proto-oncogenes are expressed only in cancer cells, when they are normal genes expressed in every healthy cell — only their mutated, oncogene form is pathological. Assuming Rb or a proto-oncogene's product "drives" the cell cycle in the same active sense that a growth factor does, when Rb specifically restrains it and its loss, not its action, is what causes disease. Assuming p53 causes the G1-S transition rather than halting it, or that it activates anti-apoptotic genes. Applying the tumor-suppressor-gene rule (deletion causes cancer) to a proto-oncogene, where deletion instead silences a growth-promoting function and cannot activate it.

## summary
Apoptosis is executed by a cascade of caspases with two separate entry points that converge on one shared exit: caspase 9 initiates the intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway once the apoptosome assembles, caspase 8 initiates the extrinsic (death-receptor) pathway once a receptor adaptor protein activates it, and both hand off to caspase 3, the effector caspase that actually executes the cell's death. This article works through exactly that wiring — which caspase belongs to which pathway, what the apoptosome does and does not contain, and what directly switches procaspase-8 on — since the exam tests each junction of this cascade as a separate, precise fact.

## sections
### Definition
A caspase is a cysteine protease — it carries cysteine, not aspartate, in its active site, and this class of enzyme carries out the controlled, catalytic dismantling of a cell during apoptosis. Caspases divide functionally into initiators, which start the cascade in response to a specific upstream signal, and effectors (executioners), which carry out the actual destruction once activated by an initiator.

### Mechanism
The intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway begins when cytochrome c is released from mitochondria into the cytosol; cytochrome c, Apaf-1 (apoptosis activation factor), procaspase-9 and ATP/dATP assemble into the apoptosome, a wheel-shaped complex that activates caspase 9 — the intrinsic pathway's initiator caspase. Procaspase-8 is not a component of the apoptosome; it belongs to the separate extrinsic pathway. The extrinsic (death-receptor) pathway begins when a death ligand binds its receptor (FAS or a TNF-family receptor); this recruits a receptor-bound adaptor protein, which directly activates procaspase-8 — not cytochrome c, not Apaf-1 — making caspase 8 the extrinsic pathway's initiator caspase. Caspase 8 is specific to the extrinsic pathway and is not involved in the intrinsic pathway. Both initiator caspases converge on caspase 3, the shared effector caspase that both pathways ultimately activate, and it is caspase 3 — not caspase 8 or 9 alone — that actually executes the death of the cell.

### Key determinants
The pathway a question describes (a mitochondrial/cytochrome-c cue, versus a receptor/ligand cue) tells you which initiator caspase (9 versus 8) is being asked about; the executioner is always caspase 3 regardless of which pathway triggered it. The apoptosome's four named components (cytochrome c, Apaf-1, procaspase-9, ATP/dATP) are a closed list — procaspase-8 is the standard distractor precisely because it sounds like it should belong, but it is the extrinsic pathway's own initiator, assembled by a different, receptor-driven mechanism.

### Clinical significance
Distinguishing intrinsic from extrinsic apoptotic triggers matters clinically because chemotherapy and radiation typically act through the intrinsic (DNA-damage, mitochondrial) pathway, while cytotoxic-T-cell and Fas-ligand-mediated killing act through the extrinsic pathway — the same caspase-3 execution step is the shared endpoint either way.

### Common misconceptions
Swapping caspase 9 and caspase 8 between the two pathways. Naming caspase 9 or 8 as the executioner instead of caspase 3. Including procaspase-8 as an apoptosome component, or naming cytochrome c/Apaf-1 rather than an adaptor protein as procaspase-8's direct activator. Describing caspases as aspartate proteases rather than cysteine proteases.
