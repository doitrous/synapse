## sections
### Definition
The ureter crosses the obturator nerve and vessels as it runs along the lateral pelvic wall.

### Mechanism
As the ureter descends into the pelvis it runs on the medial aspect of the internal iliac vessels and crosses anterior to the obturator nerve and vessels near the lateral pelvic wall, at the point where these structures enter the obturator canal. This crossing is the classic surgical landmark used to identify the ureter during pelvic surgery, since it must be traced across this point to avoid inadvertent ligation.

### Key determinants
Confusing the obturator crossing (lateral pelvic wall) with the uterine artery crossing ('water under the bridge'), which is a separate, more medial and more distal relation in females.

## sections
### Definition
The right kidney's posterior relations include the 12th rib but not the 11th rib, unlike the left kidney.

### Mechanism
The right kidney sits slightly lower than the left because of the liver above it, so posteriorly it is related to only the 12th rib, not the 11th rib; the left kidney, which sits higher, is related to both the 11th and 12th ribs posteriorly. This asymmetry is a classic exam point distinguishing the two kidneys' posterior relations.

### Key determinants
Assuming the right and left kidneys share identical rib relations; the right kidney sits lower (pushed down by the liver) and so is related only to the 12th rib posteriorly, not the 11th.

## sections
### Definition
Conivaptan is contraindicated in hypovolemic hyponatremia.

### Mechanism
Conivaptan is a non-selective vasopressin V1A/V2-receptor antagonist ('vaptan') used to treat euvolemic or hypervolemic hyponatremia, including SIADH; because it promotes free-water excretion (aquaresis) without retaining sodium, it is contraindicated in hypovolemic hyponatremia, where it would worsen intravascular volume depletion. Vaptans are reserved for states of water excess, not volume depletion.

### Key determinants
Assuming a vasopressin-receptor antagonist ('vaptan') is safe in any hyponatremia; vaptans cause aquaresis and are contraindicated specifically in hypovolemic hyponatremia, where they would worsen volume depletion.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming a vasopressin-receptor antagonist ('vaptan') is safe in any hyponatremia; vaptans cause aquaresis and are contraindicated specifically in hypovolemic hyponatremia, where they would worsen volume depletion.

## sections
### Definition
Glycine is classified as a purely glucogenic amino acid.

### Mechanism
Amino acids are classified by metabolic fate into pure glucogenic, pure ketogenic, or mixed groups; glycine, like most amino acids, is degraded to intermediates (serine, then pyruvate) that can feed gluconeogenesis, making it purely glucogenic. Only leucine and lysine are purely ketogenic, and only four amino acids (phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan, isoleucine) are mixed.

### Key determinants
Assuming any amino acid not explicitly named ketogenic must be mixed; only leucine and lysine are purely ketogenic and only four (phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan, isoleucine) are mixed — every other amino acid, including glycine, is purely glucogenic.

### Clinical significance

## sections
### Definition
Carnitine deficiency causes muscle weakness and fasting hypoglycemia.

### Mechanism
Carnitine shuttles long-chain fatty acyl groups across the inner mitochondrial membrane for beta-oxidation; carnitine deficiency impairs fatty-acid oxidation, so muscle cannot use fat for energy and the liver cannot generate ketone bodies and gluconeogenic support as efficiently during fasting, producing proximal muscle weakness together with fasting (hypoketotic) hypoglycemia. Skeletal and cardiac muscle are especially affected because they rely heavily on fatty-acid oxidation.

### Key determinants
Attributing fasting hypoglycemia with muscle weakness to a glycogen storage disease rather than a fat-oxidation defect; carnitine deficiency impairs beta-oxidation specifically, producing hypoketotic hypoglycemia plus muscle weakness rather than hepatomegaly with lactic acidosis.

### Definition
21-alpha-hydroxylase deficiency causes precocious puberty via adrenal androgen excess.

### Mechanism
21-alpha-hydroxylase deficiency (the most common cause of congenital adrenal hyperplasia) blocks synthesis of cortisol and aldosterone from their shared precursor, 17-hydroxyprogesterone; the resulting precursor buildup is shunted into the androgen synthesis pathway, causing excess adrenal androgen production. In a genetic female this virilizes the external genitalia at birth, and in either sex the chronic androgen excess drives precocious puberty — a distinct clinical facet of the same enzyme defect from the salt-wasting, hyponatremic crisis caused by the accompanying aldosterone deficiency.

### Key determinants
Assuming 21-alpha-hydroxylase deficiency only causes salt-wasting; the same enzyme block also shunts precursors into the androgen pathway, so excess androgens separately drive precocious puberty and, in genetic females, virilized genitalia.

### Definition
Acidosis increases cellular potassium efflux and decreases renal potassium secretion.

### Mechanism
In acidosis, excess extracellular hydrogen ions are buffered partly by movement into cells in exchange for intracellular potassium moving out, so cellular potassium efflux increases; at the same time, acidosis reduces basolateral Na+/K+-ATPase activity in the distal nephron's principal cells and lowers luminal potassium secretion, so renal potassium secretion decreases. Both effects combine to predispose to hyperkalemia in acidotic states.

### Key determinants
Assuming acidosis and its two potassium effects (increased cellular efflux, decreased renal secretion) act in opposite directions; both changes push toward hyperkalemia together.

### Definition
Lupus nephritis shows mesangial immune complex deposits on light microscopy.

### Mechanism
Lupus nephritis is an immune-complex-mediated glomerulonephritis in which circulating immune complexes (often containing anti-dsDNA antibodies) deposit in the mesangium, and — depending on the class of lupus nephritis — also in the subendothelial and/or subepithelial regions of the glomerular basement membrane. Mesangial deposits are a defining light-microscopic and immunofluorescence finding across the spectrum of lupus nephritis classes, often producing a 'full-house' immunofluorescence pattern.

### Key determinants
Confusing the granular mesangial/subendothelial immune-complex deposition pattern of lupus nephritis with the linear pattern of anti-GBM disease or the amyloid deposits of renal amyloidosis.
