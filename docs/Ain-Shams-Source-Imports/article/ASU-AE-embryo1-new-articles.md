## summary
This ASU Year 1 Introduction to Anatomy and Embryology slice ("MCQs - Embryo 1.pdf", Part 1) covers seven facts about spermatogenesis, spermiogenesis and semen terminology that `find-existing.mjs` found nowhere else in the corpus: necrospermia and azoospermia terminology, sperm viability after ejaculation, the primary spermatocyte as the largest and last-diploid spermatogenic cell, the growth-and-transformation phases that involve no division, the 60-64 day spermatogenic timeline, the spermatid's inability to divide further, and the structural transformations of spermiogenesis (Golgi to acrosome, mitochondria to middle piece, centriole to axial filament, cytoplasm mostly shed). It is a local answer-key-grounded draft and still needs independent andrology evidence before publication.

## sections
### Definition
Spermatogenesis is the production of sperm from spermatogonia, run in four phases: proliferation (mitotic multiplication), growth (enlargement into the primary spermatocyte), maturation (the two meiotic divisions), and transformation/spermiogenesis (structural remodelling of the spermatid). Semen abnormalities have specific names by parameter: azoospermia (no sperm), oligospermia (low count), necrospermia (dead sperm), teratospermia (abnormal forms), and hypospermia (low volume).
### Mechanism
The primary spermatocyte, formed by growth of a spermatogonium, is both the largest cell in the series and the last diploid one, since the first meiotic division that follows halves the chromosome number. Growth and transformation are the two phases with no cell division: growth is pure enlargement, and transformation (spermiogenesis) is a remodelling of an already-formed haploid spermatid. The whole sequence from spermatogonium to mature sperm takes about 60-64 days. During spermiogenesis, Golgi-derived proacrosomal granules form the acrosomal cap over the condensing nucleus; mitochondria migrate to form the middle-piece sheath; the centrioles form the tail's axial filament; and most cytoplasm is shed as a residual body, so the sperm head is built from the nucleus and acrosome, not from retained cytoplasm. Because the spermatid does not divide again, it is the only cell in the series (alongside its non-dividing precursors' downstream fate) that cannot increase male germ cell numbers. Once ejaculated, sperm remain viable and capable of fertilising an ovum for about 2 days.
### Key determinants
Keep five things straight: (1) azoospermia = absent sperm, necrospermia = dead sperm present, oligospermia = low count -- distinct parameters; (2) the primary spermatocyte is both the largest and the last diploid cell; (3) growth and transformation are the only division-free phases; (4) spermiogenesis is Golgi-to-acrosome, mitochondria-to-middle-piece, centriole-to-tail, with cytoplasm mostly shed, not retained in the head; (5) the full spermatogenic timeline is about 60-64 days, separate from the roughly 2-day post-ejaculation sperm viability window.
### Clinical significance
This article does not make prescribing or diagnostic-workup claims. Its study value is foundational: distinguishing azoospermia from necrospermia and oligospermia on a semen analysis report guides very different fertility work-ups, and the spermatogenic timeline explains why a treatment or exposure affecting sperm production takes roughly two months to show its full effect on a semen analysis.
### Common misconceptions
Do not confuse necrospermia (dead sperm present, normal count possible) with azoospermia (no sperm at all) or oligospermia (low count).

Do not assume the mature sperm, as the pathway's end-product, is the largest cell -- the primary spermatocyte is largest, and cell size falls steadily thereafter.

Do not attribute the sperm tail's axial filament to the Golgi apparatus, or the acrosome to the centriole or mitochondria -- each organelle has one specific fate in spermiogenesis.

Do not treat spermiogenesis as a division that increases germ cell numbers -- the spermatid remodels but does not divide.

## summary
This ASU Year 1 Introduction to Anatomy and Embryology slice ("MCQs - Embryo 1.pdf", Part 1) covers five facts about oogenesis, meiotic timing and chromosome formulas that `find-existing.mjs` found nowhere else in the corpus: that ovulation releases a secondary oocyte rather than a mature ovum, that the primary spermatocyte and primary oocyte are the last diploid cells in their germ lines, the timing of the oocyte's two meiotic divisions relative to ovulation and fertilisation, the prophase-I arrest of the primary oocyte from fetal life to puberty, and the somatic (44+2) chromosome formula. It is a local answer-key-grounded draft and still needs independent embryology evidence before publication.

## sections
### Definition
Oogenesis is the formation of the female gamete, beginning in fetal life when oogonia become primary oocytes that enter, and then arrest in, the first meiotic prophase. A normal human somatic cell is diploid with 46 chromosomes (44 autosomes plus 2 sex chromosomes, same or different); a haploid gamete carries 22 autosomes plus one sex chromosome.
### Mechanism
The primary oocyte remains arrested in prophase of the first meiotic division from before birth until puberty; cycle by cycle thereafter, a dominant follicle's oocyte resumes and completes that division only just before ovulation, becoming the secondary oocyte that is actually released -- not a "mature ovum", which only exists once the second meiotic division also completes. The secondary oocyte immediately arrests again, this time at metaphase of the second meiotic division, and stays arrested there throughout ovulation and its short fertile lifespan; only the calcium signal from sperm-oocyte fusion at fertilisation completes this second division. The primary spermatocyte and primary oocyte are, symmetrically, the last diploid cells in the male and female germ lines respectively, since each precedes the first meiotic division that halves the chromosome number in its own line.
### Key determinants
Keep three timings straight: (1) the first meiotic division of the oocyte completes just before ovulation; (2) the second completes only at fertilisation; (3) the primary oocyte's prophase-I arrest itself runs from fetal life to puberty, a separate and far longer arrest than the metaphase-II one. Separately, remember the diploid baseline (44 autosomes + 2 sex chromosomes) that every haploid gamete formula (22 autosomes + 1 sex chromosome) is derived from by halving.
### Clinical significance
This article does not make prescribing or diagnostic-workup claims. Its study value is foundational: the decades-long prophase-I arrest of the primary oocyte is a leading explanation for the rising rate of meiotic non-disjunction (and so aneuploidies such as trisomy 21) with advancing maternal age.
### Common misconceptions
Do not call the cell released at ovulation a "mature ovum" -- it is a secondary oocyte, still arrested at metaphase of the second meiotic division.

Do not assume every pre-gamete germ cell is haploid -- the primary spermatocyte and primary oocyte are both still diploid.

Do not swap the two meiotic arrest points: prophase I lasts from fetal life to puberty; metaphase II lasts from ovulation to fertilisation.

Do not describe the diploid somatic formula as "46 autosomes plus 2 sex chromosomes" -- the 46 total already includes the 2 sex chromosomes, leaving 44 autosomes.

## summary
This ASU Year 1 Introduction to Anatomy and Embryology slice ("MCQs - Embryo 1.pdf", Part 1) covers five facts about the ovarian and menstrual cycles that `find-existing.mjs` found nowhere else in the corpus: the corpus luteum's divergent fate and progesterone-source role in menstruation versus pregnancy, the accompaniments of ovulation (pain, and a post-ovulatory rise, not fall, in basal temperature), the follicular phase's control by pituitary FSH, the mechanism of combined contraceptive pills, and the secretory-phase change in the endometrial spiral arteries. It is a local answer-key-grounded draft and still needs independent gynaecology evidence before publication.

## sections
### Definition
The ovarian cycle has a follicular phase (FSH-driven follicle growth), ovulation (the LH-surge-triggered release of a secondary oocyte), and a luteal phase (progesterone-dominant, run by the corpus luteum). The matching endometrial (menstrual) cycle has a proliferative phase, a secretory (progestational) phase, and menstruation.
### Mechanism
Pituitary FSH drives follicular growth in the follicular phase; rising estrogen from the dominant follicle then triggers the LH surge that causes ovulation. Some women feel ovulation as a brief pain (mittelschmerz), from follicular stretch and rupture, and basal body temperature rises (not falls) afterward, from progesterone's thermogenic effect. If fertilisation does not occur, the resulting corpus luteum of menstruation has a fixed lifespan of about 12-14 days before it regresses, and its falling progesterone triggers menstrual shedding; the secretory-phase endometrium it had been supporting shows coiled glands and markedly tortuous spiral arteries right up until that regression. If implantation does occur, hCG from the syncytiotrophoblast rescues the corpus luteum, which then persists as the corpus luteum of pregnancy for about 3-4 months, after which the placenta itself takes over as the dominant progesterone source for the remainder of pregnancy -- so the corpus luteum and the placenta are, between them, the two sequential sources of pregnancy progesterone. Combined contraceptive pills exploit the follicular-phase dependency on FSH: their exogenous estrogen and progestogen suppress pituitary FSH/LH by negative feedback, so follicles fail to mature and ovulation never occurs.
### Key determinants
Keep four things straight: (1) FSH controls the follicular phase, not LH or progesterone; (2) ovulation may cause pain and always raises (not lowers) basal temperature afterward; (3) the corpus luteum of menstruation regresses at about 12-14 days, while the corpus luteum of pregnancy is rescued by hCG and persists to about the 4th month, with the placenta then taking over; (4) contraceptive pills work upstream, by suppressing follicle maturation, not by acting on sperm or the fertilised ovum directly.
### Clinical significance
This article does not make prescribing or diagnostic-workup claims. Its study value is foundational: the near-fixed 12-14 day luteal-phase length (rather than the more variable follicular phase) is the basis for backward-dating ovulation from a known period date, and the luteo-placental progesterone handover around the 4th month explains why surgical removal of the corpus luteum can cause miscarriage in early pregnancy but is usually safe later.
### Common misconceptions
Do not attribute follicular-phase control to LH, progesterone, or the corpus luteum -- FSH is the driving hormone before ovulation.

Do not assume basal body temperature falls at ovulation -- it rises afterward, under progesterone's influence.

Do not swap the 12-14 day (corpus luteum of menstruation) and 3-4 month / 4th month (corpus luteum of pregnancy) figures.

Do not describe contraceptive pills as spermicidal or embryocidal -- their primary action is suppressing follicle maturation upstream of ovulation.
