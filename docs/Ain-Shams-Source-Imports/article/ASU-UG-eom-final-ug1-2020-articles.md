## sections
### Definition
Correcting metabolic acidosis helps treat hyperkalemia by shifting potassium into cells.

### Mechanism
Acidosis drives potassium out of cells as hydrogen ions move intracellularly and are buffered partly by an exchange for intracellular potassium; correcting the acidosis, for example with sodium bicarbonate, reverses this shift and drives potassium back into cells, lowering the serum potassium concentration. This is one of the standard rapid, shift-based measures used in hyperkalemia management, alongside insulin-plus-glucose and beta-2 agonists, all of which act by moving potassium intracellularly rather than removing it from the body. Correcting acidosis is particularly useful when a patient with hyperkalemia is also acidotic, since it treats both derangements together.

### Key determinants
Confusing correction of acidosis (which shifts K+ into cells and helps hyperkalemia) with inducing alkalosis using a potassium-sparing diuretic, which would worsen rather than treat hyperkalemia.

## sections
### Definition
Disodium hydrogen phosphate is the main buffer of distal tubular titratable acidity.

### Mechanism
Titratable acid refers to filtered urinary buffer, chiefly disodium hydrogen phosphate (Na2HPO4), that is converted to its acid form (NaH2PO4) by combining with hydrogen ions secreted in the distal tubule; the amount of base needed to titrate urine back to plasma pH defines titratable acidity. Phosphate buffers effectively in this role because its pKa (about 6.8) lies close to the pH range of tubular fluid as urine becomes progressively more acidic along the nephron. Titratable acid excretion, together with ammonium excretion, makes up the kidney's total net acid excretion.

### Key determinants
Confusing titratable acid excretion (phosphate-buffered) with ammonium excretion (ammonia-buffered) — both contribute to net acid excretion but via different urinary buffers.

### Common misconceptions
Confusing titratable acid excretion (phosphate-buffered) with ammonium excretion (ammonia-buffered) — both contribute to net acid excretion but via different urinary buffers.

## sections
### Definition
Anti-DNase B is more specific than ASO for confirming a preceding streptococcal skin infection.

### Mechanism
Anti-DNase B (anti-deoxyribonuclease B) titers rise reliably after both streptococcal pharyngitis and streptococcal skin infections, whereas the antistreptolysin O (ASO) response is characteristically blunted after skin infections because skin lipids inhibit streptolysin O; anti-DNase B is therefore the more specific and sensitive serologic marker to confirm a preceding streptococcal infection when the antecedent infection was cutaneous (impetigo), as in post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis following skin infection. This distinction is a classic microbiology and nephrology teaching point used to choose the right serologic test depending on the site of the antecedent infection. Combining anti-DNase B with ASO increases overall sensitivity for confirming recent streptococcal infection regardless of site.

### Key determinants
Assuming ASO titer is always the right confirmatory test for streptococcal infection, when its response is characteristically blunted after skin (as opposed to throat) infection, making anti-DNase B the more reliable marker in that setting.

### Definition
The second part of the duodenum is an anterior relation at the hilum of the right kidney.

### Mechanism
The second (descending) part of the duodenum lies directly anterior to the hilum and medial border of the right kidney, an important relation in retroperitoneal surgery and in explaining how right renal pathology, such as a perinephric abscess, can occasionally involve or be confused with duodenal pathology. This anterior relation is specific to the right kidney because of its position relative to the duodenal loop and the head of the pancreas. The corresponding left kidney relation differs, since the tail of the pancreas and spleen lie anterior to the left kidney rather than the duodenum.

### Key determinants
Applying the left kidney's anterior relations (tail of pancreas, spleen, stomach) to the right kidney, whose anterior hilar relation is instead the second part of the duodenum.

### Definition
Chronic bacterial prostatitis is treated with a prolonged antibiotic course of about 6 weeks.

### Mechanism
Chronic bacterial prostatitis requires a substantially longer antibiotic course than simple cystitis or acute prostatitis, typically on the order of 4 to 6 weeks (commonly cited as approximately 6 weeks), using an agent such as a fluoroquinolone that achieves adequate penetration into prostatic tissue. This prolonged course is needed because relatively poor antibiotic penetration into the prostate and the tendency for bacteria to persist in prostatic ducts or calculi make short courses prone to relapse. Fluoroquinolones such as ciprofloxacin are preferred because their lipophilicity gives them better prostatic tissue penetration than agents like beta-lactams.

### Key determinants
Applying the short antibiotic courses used for uncomplicated cystitis to chronic bacterial prostatitis, which instead requires several weeks of therapy to achieve adequate prostatic tissue levels.

## sections
### Definition
L-carnitine transports long-chain fatty acyl groups across the inner mitochondrial membrane for beta-oxidation.

### Mechanism
L-carnitine's principal physiological role is intracellular lipid transport: it shuttles long-chain fatty acyl-CoA groups from the cytosol across the otherwise-impermeable inner mitochondrial membrane, via the carnitine acyltransferase system (CPT-I and CPT-II), so that they can undergo beta-oxidation in the mitochondrial matrix. Without this carnitine-mediated transport step, cells cannot access long-chain fatty acids as an energy substrate, which is why carnitine deficiency produces muscle weakness and fasting hypoglycemia when fatty-acid oxidation is impaired. This transport function is distinct from, and does not itself involve, transporting glucose, calcium, or glycogen-related substrates.

### Key determinants
Confusing carnitine's intracellular lipid-transport role with unrelated transport processes such as glucose uptake, calcium binding, or glycogen synthesis regulation.

### Definition
Tyrosine becomes an essential amino acid in phenylketonuria.

### Mechanism

### Definition
Apical pinocytic vesicles are a characteristic ultrastructural feature of proximal convoluted tubule cells.

### Mechanism
Proximal convoluted tubule cells are characterized by a well-developed apical brush border of microvilli together with numerous apical pinocytic (endocytic) vesicles, which mediate receptor-mediated endocytosis of small filtered proteins and other macromolecules that escape the glomerular filtration barrier; these vesicles fuse with lysosomes for intracellular degradation, reclaiming filtered protein that would otherwise be lost in urine. Along with abundant basal mitochondria supporting active transport and extensive basolateral membrane infoldings, apical pinocytic vesicles are a defining ultrastructural feature that distinguishes PCT cells on electron microscopy. This ultrastructure directly supports the PCT's role as the nephron segment responsible for bulk reabsorption of filtered solutes and reclamation of filtered protein.

### Definition
Decreased renal production of calcitriol causes renal osteodystrophy with recurrent fractures.

### Mechanism
The kidney performs the final hydroxylation step, via renal 1-alpha-hydroxylase, that converts 25-hydroxyvitamin D to the active hormone 1,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol (calcitriol); in chronic renal dysfunction this activation step declines, producing calcitriol deficiency, reduced intestinal calcium absorption, secondary hyperparathyroidism, and a mineral bone disease (renal osteodystrophy) that predisposes to recurrent, sometimes minimal-trauma, fractures. This mechanism, distinct from primary nutritional vitamin D deficiency, is why renal osteodystrophy is a recognized skeletal complication of chronic kidney disease in older patients. Recognizing renal dysfunction as the driver, rather than isolated dietary deficiency, determines the correct management, which targets both the renal disease and the resulting secondary hyperparathyroidism.

Attributing recurrent fractures in a patient with renal dysfunction to isolated dietary vitamin D or B12 deficiency rather than to the kidney's own failure to activate vitamin D via 1-alpha-hydroxylation.

### Definition
Erythropoietin deficiency causes the anemia of chronic renal disease.

### Mechanism
Erythropoietin, secreted mainly by peritubular interstitial fibroblast-like cells in the renal cortex in response to hypoxia, stimulates erythroid progenitor proliferation and differentiation in the bone marrow; in chronic kidney disease, progressive loss of functioning renal parenchyma reduces erythropoietin production, producing a normocytic, normochromic anemia (the anemia of chronic kidney disease) that is a recognized and clinically important complication. The mechanism is distinct from anemias caused by iron, B12, or folate deficiency, though those can coexist and should also be excluded or corrected.

### Definition
The median umbilical ligament, a urachal remnant, attaches to the apex of the urinary bladder.

### Mechanism
The median umbilical ligament is the fibrous remnant of the fetal urachus (allantoic duct), running in the midline from the apex of the urinary bladder up to the umbilicus, and it raises the median umbilical fold on the internal aspect of the anterior abdominal wall. This single, midline ligament is a key anatomical landmark distinguishing it from the paired medial umbilical ligaments (remnants of the umbilical arteries), which do not attach to the bladder apex the same way. Persistence of a patent urachus along this ligament's course can produce a urachal fistula, cyst, or sinus, clinically relevant complications of incomplete urachal obliteration.

### Key determinants
Confusing the single, midline median umbilical ligament (urachal remnant, attaches to the bladder apex) with the paired medial umbilical ligaments (obliterated umbilical arteries) or the lateral umbilical folds (inferior epigastric vessels).
