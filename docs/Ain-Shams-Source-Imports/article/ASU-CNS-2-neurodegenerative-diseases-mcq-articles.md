## summary
How CNS neurodegenerative disease is classified: by the functional network of neurons lost (not anatomical proximity), by clinical presentation, causative protein and genetics (never by treatment response), and how cortical versus basal ganglia involvement predicts a cognitive versus a movement-disorder presentation.

## sections
### Definition
Neurodegenerative diseases of the central nervous system selectively destroy neuron populations linked by function or connectivity rather than by physical location, which is why disease can span anatomically separated regions that share a circuit. They are classified along several complementary axes at once — clinical presentation (dementia versus movement disorder), the abnormal protein that accumulates (tau, alpha-synuclein, beta-amyloid, huntingtin), and known genetic abnormalities — never by response to pharmacological treatment, which is a management fact rather than a taxonomic one. A common thread across the group is accumulation of abnormal, misfolded proteins as the shared pathological mechanism, even though clinical pictures differ widely. Anatomically, cortical involvement produces the cognitive triad of memory, language and insight loss without primary weakness, while basal ganglia involvement instead produces movement disorders.

### Definition — per-concept facts
Neurodegenerative diseases selectively destroy populations of neurons that are linked by the functional circuit or neurotransmitter system they belong to, not by where they sit anatomically. This is why a single disease (for example Parkinson disease or Alzheimer disease) can affect widely separated brain regions that share a functional network, while sparing immediately adjacent neurons of a different functional class.

The standard classification schemes for neurodegenerative disease group conditions by their clinical syndrome (e.g. dementia versus movement disorder), by the abnormal protein that accumulates (e.g. tau, alpha-synuclein, beta-amyloid) and by known genetic abnormalities. Response to drug treatment is not a recognised classification axis, because most neurodegenerative diseases lack a treatment that alters their course, and the ones that do respond partially to therapy are still classified by pathology and clinical features.

When neurodegeneration selectively targets cerebral cortical neurons — as in Alzheimer disease and the frontotemporal dementias — the clinical result is a cortical dementia syndrome: progressive loss of memory, language function, insight and executive planning. This contrasts with subcortical (basal ganglia or cerebellar) neurodegeneration, which produces movement disorders and ataxia rather than a primary cognitive syndrome.

Most CNS neurodegenerative diseases share a common underlying mechanism: misfolded or abnormally processed proteins accumulate within or around neurons, aggregate, and are toxic to the cells that contain or surround them. Examples include beta-amyloid and tau in Alzheimer disease, alpha-synuclein in Parkinson disease and Lewy body dementia, and huntingtin in Huntington disease — a shared 'proteinopathy' mechanism despite very different clinical pictures.

No single classification scheme fully captures neurodegenerative disease; clinicians and pathologists use clinical presentation (movement disorder versus dementia), the accumulating protein (tauopathy, synucleinopathy, amyloidopathy), and underlying genetics as complementary, simultaneously valid classification axes, not mutually exclusive alternatives.

Neurodegeneration confined to the basal ganglia circuitry — the caudate/putamen, globus pallidus and substantia nigra — produces the movement disorder syndromes (parkinsonism and the hyperkinetic disorders), because these nuclei regulate voluntary motor output rather than cognition or memory.

Cortical neurodegenerative disease presents with the cognitive triad of memory loss, language impairment and loss of insight, reflecting the association-cortex functions those neurons subserve. Muscle weakness is not part of this picture, because voluntary motor strength depends on the corticospinal tract, basal ganglia and lower motor neuron/motor unit, not on association cortex integrity.

### Mechanism
Selective vulnerability follows the functional/synaptic network a neuron belongs to, and abnormal protein aggregation (a shared 'proteinopathy' mechanism) is toxic to the neurons that harbour or surround it, driving progressive cell loss along that network.

### Key determinants
The site of neurodegeneration determines the syndrome: cerebral cortex neurons losing function produces cognitive decline (memory, language, insight, planning) without weakness; basal ganglia neuron loss produces a movement disorder instead.

### Clinical significance
Correctly classifying a neurodegenerative presentation (cognitive versus movement-disorder, and by likely protein/genetic cause) guides prognosis counselling and the specific work-up (genetic testing, CSF/imaging biomarkers) even where disease-modifying treatment remains limited.

### Common misconceptions
Response to drug treatment is not a classification criterion, and cortical disease does not cause primary muscle weakness — weakness implicates the motor system (corticospinal tract, basal ganglia or motor neuron), not association cortex.

## summary
The basal ganglia/cerebellar/motor-neuron movement disorders sorted by their defining category: Huntington disease (hyperkinetic, striatal), Parkinson disease and striatonigral degeneration (akinetic-rigid, nigral/striatal), spinocerebellar ataxia (cerebellar) and ALS (motor neuron degeneration, upper and lower).

## sections
### Definition
Movement disorders arising from basal ganglia neurodegeneration split into two opposite categories: hyperkinetic disease, in which the prototype is Huntington disease (striatal degeneration causing chorea), and akinetic-rigid (hypokinetic) disease, whose prototype is Parkinson disease (substantia nigra dopaminergic neuron loss causing bradykinesia, rigidity and tremor). Striatonigral degeneration, the parkinsonian variant of multiple system atrophy, also falls in the akinetic-rigid category, combining striatal and nigral pathology and typically responding poorly to levodopa, unlike idiopathic Parkinson disease. Separate from the basal ganglia group, spinocerebellar ataxias degenerate cerebellar Purkinje and related neurons, producing ataxia rather than a basal-ganglia movement disorder, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis degenerates both upper and lower motor neurons, producing combined spastic-flaccid weakness with sparing of sensation and cognition in most cases.

### Definition — per-concept facts
Huntington disease is an autosomal dominant CAG-repeat expansion disorder that causes degeneration of the striatum (caudate and putamen), producing chorea — the prototypical hyperkinetic movement disorder — together with cognitive decline and psychiatric disturbance. It is classified opposite Parkinson disease on the movement-disorder spectrum: hyperkinetic (excess movement) rather than akinetic-rigid (reduced movement).

Parkinson disease results from loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta, producing the akinetic-rigid syndrome: bradykinesia, rigidity, resting tremor and postural instability. It is the prototype of the akinetic-rigid (hypokinetic) movement disorder category, opposite the hyperkinetic disorders such as Huntington disease.

Striatonigral degeneration, the parkinsonian-predominant subtype of multiple system atrophy (MSA-P), causes combined striatal and nigral neurodegeneration and presents with an akinetic-rigid parkinsonian syndrome that is typically levodopa-poorly-responsive, distinguishing it clinically from idiopathic Parkinson disease despite the shared akinetic-rigid category.

The spinocerebellar ataxias are a group of inherited (typically autosomal dominant, often trinucleotide-repeat) disorders causing progressive degeneration of cerebellar Purkinje cells and associated cerebellar/spinocerebellar tract neurons, producing gait and limb ataxia, dysarthria and nystagmus.

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive neurodegenerative disease that destroys both upper motor neurons (corticospinal tract) and lower motor neurons (anterior horn cells/brainstem motor nuclei), producing a combination of spasticity and flaccid weakness, fasciculations and muscle wasting, while sparing sensory, cognitive and oculomotor function in most cases.

### Mechanism
Striatal neuron loss disinhibits movement (hyperkinesia/chorea); nigral dopaminergic loss reduces basal ganglia output drive to the cortex (akinesia/rigidity); cerebellar Purkinje cell loss disrupts coordination (ataxia); and combined upper/lower motor neuron loss in ALS removes both corticospinal drive and the final common motor pathway.

### Key determinants
Levodopa responsiveness helps separate idiopathic Parkinson disease (good response) from atypical parkinsonism such as striatonigral degeneration (poor response) despite both being akinetic-rigid.

### Clinical significance
Correctly sorting a patient into hyperkinetic, akinetic-rigid, ataxic or motor-neuron categories directs the right investigation (genetic panel for Huntington/spinocerebellar ataxia, electromyography for ALS, dopaminergic imaging for atypical parkinsonism) and prognostic counselling.

### Common misconceptions
Do not assume all akinetic-rigid disease is idiopathic Parkinson disease — atypical parkinsonisms such as striatonigral degeneration share the akinetic-rigid category but differ in treatment response and prognosis; and ALS specifically spares sensory neurons, unlike peripheral neuropathies that mimic it.

The basal ganglia/cerebellar/motor-neuron movement disorders sorted by their defining category: Huntington disease (hyperkinetic, striatal), Parkinson disease and striatonigral degeneration (akinetic-rigid, nigral/striatal), spinocerebellar ataxia (cerebellar) and ALS (motor neuron degeneration, upper and lower).

## summary
The amyloid cascade: how APP is processed (amyloidogenic BACE/gamma-secretase pathway versus protective alpha-secretase pathway), how beta-amyloid accumulation triggers downstream tau hyperphosphorylation and tangle formation, the genetics (chromosome 21/APP for familial disease, ApoE4 for sporadic risk), and why Alzheimer disease is the leading cause of dementia.

## sections
### Definition
The amyloid cascade hypothesis holds that progressive accumulation of beta-amyloid (Abeta), generated from amyloid precursor protein (APP), is the crucial initiating event in Alzheimer disease. APP can be cleaved by two competing pathways: the amyloidogenic pathway (beta-secretase/BACE then gamma-secretase) releases intact, toxic Abeta, while the non-amyloidogenic pathway (alpha-secretase) cleaves within the Abeta sequence itself and precludes pathogenic peptide formation. Familial, early-onset Alzheimer disease arises from mutations in APP (chromosome 21), presenilin-1 (chromosome 14) or presenilin-2 (chromosome 1) — the chromosome 21/APP link also explaining the near-universal amyloid pathology of Down syndrome. For the far more common sporadic, late-onset form, the ApoE4 allele is the major genetic risk factor, raising risk roughly four-fold per allele by impairing Abeta clearance. Once deposited, Abeta is directly neurotoxic, impairing neurotransmission and triggering downstream hyperphosphorylation of tau, which then mislocalises from the axon into the cell body and dendrites and aggregates into neurofibrillary tangles. This amyloid-then-tau cascade is why Alzheimer disease is the leading cause of dementia in the elderly.

### Definition — per-concept facts
The amyloid cascade hypothesis holds that progressive cerebral accumulation of beta-amyloid (Abeta), generated by amyloidogenic cleavage of amyloid precursor protein (APP), is the initiating event in Alzheimer disease. Abeta accumulation triggers downstream tau hyperphosphorylation, synaptic dysfunction, neuroinflammation and ultimately neuronal death, making it the pathogenic trigger rather than a bystander finding.

Amyloid precursor protein (APP) can be processed by two competing pathways. The amyloidogenic (pathogenic) pathway uses beta-secretase (BACE) followed by gamma-secretase to release intact, aggregation-prone Abeta. The non-amyloidogenic pathway instead uses alpha-secretase, which cleaves APP within the Abeta domain itself, precluding formation of the full, pathogenic Abeta peptide and instead yielding non-toxic soluble fragments.

Familial (early-onset) Alzheimer disease is linked to mutations in three genes: APP on chromosome 21, presenilin-1 (PSEN1) on chromosome 14, and presenilin-2 (PSEN2) on chromosome 1. Of these, the APP gene on chromosome 21 directly explains the markedly increased Alzheimer disease risk and near-universal amyloid pathology seen in Down syndrome (trisomy 21), which effectively triples APP gene dosage.

Soluble Abeta oligomers and insoluble Abeta plaques disrupt synaptic function, impair neurotransmitter release and receptor signalling, promote oxidative stress and neuroinflammation, and ultimately drive neuronal death — the opposite of any protective or regenerative effect on neurons.

Downstream of Abeta accumulation, intracellular signalling changes (including kinase activation) drive abnormal hyperphosphorylation of the microtubule-associated protein tau. This tau hyperphosphorylation is the second major pathological event of the amyloid cascade, linking extracellular Abeta plaques to the intracellular neurofibrillary tangle pathology of Alzheimer disease.

Normal tau is an axonal, microtubule-stabilising protein. Once hyperphosphorylated, tau detaches from microtubules (destabilising them), mislocalises from the axon into the somatodendritic compartment, and self-aggregates into paired helical filaments that accumulate as neurofibrillary tangles — an intracellular inclusion distinct from the extracellular amyloid plaque.

Apolipoprotein E (ApoE) has three common alleles (E2, E3, E4). ApoE4 is the strongest common genetic risk factor for late-onset, sporadic Alzheimer disease, impairing Abeta clearance from the brain, whereas ApoE2 is relatively protective and ApoE3 is the population-average-risk allele.

ApoE4 confers risk in a gene-dose-dependent fashion: each copy of the E4 allele raises Alzheimer disease risk by roughly four-fold relative to the common ApoE3/E3 genotype, so a homozygote (E4/E4) carries substantially higher lifetime risk than a heterozygote (E4/E3).

Alzheimer disease accounts for the majority of dementia cases in older adults, more than vascular dementia, dementia with Lewy bodies or frontotemporal dementia combined, making it the leading single cause of cognitive decline in this age group worldwide.

### Mechanism
Abeta accumulation (from amyloidogenic APP cleavage) precedes and drives tau hyperphosphorylation and mislocalisation; ApoE4 amplifies the cascade by impairing Abeta clearance rather than by altering its production.

### Key determinants
Genetic testing distinguishes deterministic familial mutations (APP/PSEN1/PSEN2, autosomal dominant, early-onset) from the risk-modifying ApoE4 allele (not deterministic, late-onset, dose-dependent risk).

### Clinical significance
The amyloid cascade is the rationale behind anti-amyloid disease-modifying therapies, and ApoE4 genotyping informs individualised risk counselling and eligibility discussions for such therapies.

### Common misconceptions
Do not treat alpha-secretase as amyloidogenic — it is protective; and do not confuse ApoE4 (a risk-modifying allele for common sporadic disease) with the APP/PSEN1/PSEN2 mutations that deterministically cause rare familial disease.

## hold_these
The amyloid cascade: how APP is processed (amyloidogenic BACE/gamma-secretase pathway versus protective alpha-secretase pathway), how beta-amyloid accumulation triggers downstream tau hyperphosphorylation and tangle formation, the genetics (chromosome 21/APP for familial disease, ApoE4 for sporadic risk), and why Alzheimer disease is the leading cause of dementia.

## summary
What Alzheimer disease actually looks like on the bench: fronto-temporo-parietal cortical atrophy sparing the cerebellum, the two defining microscopic lesions (extracellular amyloid-core neuritic plaques and intracellular tau-based neurofibrillary tangles, the latter shown by silver stain), reactive gliosis accompanying neuronal loss, absence of demyelination, and coexisting cerebral amyloid angiopathy.

## sections
### Definition
Grossly, Alzheimer disease produces reduced brain weight with cortical atrophy concentrated in the frontal, temporal and parietal association cortices and widened sulci, while the cerebellum is relatively spared. Microscopically, the two defining lesions are the extracellular neuritic (senile) plaque — a central beta-amyloid core surrounded by dystrophic neurites — and the intracellular neurofibrillary tangle, composed of hyperphosphorylated tau that has mislocalised from the axon (where its normal role is microtubule binding) into the cell body; tangles are classically demonstrated with silver impregnation stains. Neuronal loss throughout the affected cortex is accompanied by reactive gliosis, the generic astrocytic response to CNS injury; white matter demyelination is not a defining feature, distinguishing Alzheimer disease pathologically from the demyelinating disease group. Cerebral amyloid angiopathy — beta-amyloid deposition in the walls of cerebral vessels rather than the parenchyma — very commonly coexists with Alzheimer disease because the two share the same amyloid pathogenesis, and it raises the risk of lobar intracerebral haemorrhage.

### Definition — per-concept facts
In its normal, non-phosphorylated state, tau is an axonal microtubule-associated protein that binds and stabilises microtubules, supporting axonal transport and cytoskeletal integrity. This normal binding function is lost once tau becomes hyperphosphorylated in Alzheimer disease.

Alzheimer disease produces a characteristic gross pattern of cerebral atrophy — reduced brain weight, widened cortical sulci and narrowed gyri predominantly in the frontal, temporal and parietal association cortices, with corresponding ventricular enlargement (hydrocephalus ex vacuo). The cerebellum is relatively spared grossly, unlike the cerebrum.

Neuritic plaques are extracellular, roughly spherical lesions in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus, consisting of a dense central core of aggregated beta-amyloid surrounded by dystrophic (swollen, degenerating) neurites and activated microglia/reactive astrocytes — one of the two defining microscopic lesions of Alzheimer disease, alongside neurofibrillary tangles.

Neurofibrillary tangles are intracellular, paired-helical-filament aggregates of hyperphosphorylated tau protein that accumulate in the neuronal cell body and proximal dendrites, disrupting the cytoskeleton and axonal transport, and persisting as extracellular 'ghost tangles' after the neuron dies.

Neurofibrillary tangles are highlighted using silver impregnation stains (e.g. Bielschowsky or Bodian silver stain), which bind the paired helical filaments of aggregated tau and render tangles clearly visible by light microscopy; modern practice supplements this with tau-specific immunohistochemistry.

Cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) is beta-amyloid deposition within the media and adventitia of small-to-medium cerebral cortical and leptomeningeal arteries, weakening the vessel wall. It commonly coexists with Alzheimer disease (sharing the amyloid pathogenesis) and is an important cause of lobar (as opposed to deep, hypertensive) intracerebral haemorrhage in older adults.

The core microscopic findings of Alzheimer disease are neuronal loss, reactive gliosis and extracellular neuritic plaques (together with neurofibrillary tangles). White matter demyelination is not a defining Alzheimer feature — it is instead the hallmark of a separate disease category, the demyelinating diseases such as multiple sclerosis.

When neurons die in Alzheimer disease, astrocytes proliferate and hypertrophy in the affected region — reactive astrogliosis — as the standard CNS reparative/reactive response to neuronal injury, alongside microglial activation. Gliosis is a generic CNS injury response, not specific to Alzheimer disease, but it is a consistent accompanying finding wherever neuronal loss occurs.

The gross cortical atrophy of Alzheimer disease follows a characteristic distribution: frontal, temporal (especially medial temporal/hippocampal) and parietal association cortices are disproportionately affected, while the primary sensorimotor and occipital (visual) cortices are relatively spared until late disease.

Because Alzheimer disease and cerebral amyloid angiopathy share the same beta-amyloid pathogenesis, the two conditions very frequently coexist: a substantial majority of Alzheimer disease brains show at least some degree of amyloid deposition in cortical/leptomeningeal vessel walls in addition to the parenchymal plaques and tangles.

### Mechanism
Neuritic plaques form from extracellular Abeta aggregation; neurofibrillary tangles form from intracellular hyperphosphorylated tau; both processes proceed in parallel and neuronal death from either (or both) triggers secondary reactive gliosis.

### Key determinants
The regional distribution of atrophy (fronto-temporo-parietal, cerebellum-sparing) and the plaque/tangle histology together confirm an Alzheimer diagnosis at autopsy, distinguishing it from vascular, Lewy body or frontotemporal pathology.

### Clinical significance
Recognising cerebral amyloid angiopathy as a common Alzheimer disease comorbidity matters clinically because it raises lobar haemorrhage risk, a consideration in anticoagulation decisions and in interpreting microhaemorrhages on MRI in Alzheimer patients.

### Common misconceptions
Do not confuse the extracellular, amyloid-core neuritic plaque with the intracellular, tau-based neurofibrillary tangle — they are the two distinct hallmark lesions; and do not attribute cerebellar atrophy or white matter demyelination to typical Alzheimer disease.

## summary
Two treatable dementia mimics that must be excluded before accepting a degenerative diagnosis: vitamin (B12) deficiency and hypothyroidism, both routinely screened for in a standard dementia work-up.

## sections
### Definition
A minority of patients presenting with a dementia syndrome have a reversible underlying cause rather than an irreversible neurodegenerative disease, and identifying these patients matters because treatment can improve or resolve their cognitive decline. Vitamin B12 (and less often thiamine or folate) deficiency is one recognised reversible cause, corrected with vitamin replacement. Hypothyroidism is the classic reversible endocrine cause, screened for with a serum TSH and corrected with thyroid hormone replacement.

### Definition — per-concept facts
Vitamin B12 deficiency (and less commonly thiamine or folate deficiency) can produce a dementia syndrome that improves or resolves with replacement therapy. Because it is treatable, vitamin deficiency is one of the standard causes screened for in any dementia work-up, alongside other reversible causes such as hypothyroidism, normal-pressure hydrocephalus and depression ('pseudodementia').

Hypothyroidism can cause a cognitive-slowing/dementia-like syndrome ('myxoedema madness' in severe cases) that improves with thyroid hormone replacement, making thyroid function testing (TSH) a standard, low-cost component of the reversible-dementia screening work-up.

### Mechanism
B12 deficiency impairs myelin synthesis and neuronal metabolism; hypothyroidism globally slows cerebral metabolic rate — both mechanisms are biochemical/metabolic rather than the protein-aggregation mechanism of neurodegenerative dementia, which is why they are reversible with correction.

### Key determinants
A standard reversible-dementia screen (B12 level, TSH, and typically also structural imaging for normal-pressure hydrocephalus or a mass lesion, plus mood assessment for depression/pseudodementia) is performed before a degenerative diagnosis such as Alzheimer disease is accepted.

### Clinical significance
Missing a reversible cause denies the patient an effective, low-risk treatment — this is why screening for vitamin deficiency and hypothyroidism is a mandatory, not optional, step in every new dementia work-up.

### Common misconceptions
Do not assume every cognitive decline in an older adult is degenerative and untreatable — vitamin deficiency and hypothyroidism are common, inexpensive to screen for, and genuinely reversible, unlike Huntington disease, Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease or frontotemporal dementia.

## summary
The shared biology of demyelinating disease: myelin (white matter) is the target, not the neuron body; the myelinoclastic (acquired, myelin-was-normal) versus leukodystrophic (inherited, myelin-never-normal) distinction; the classic ocular/coordination/weakness clinical picture (not seizures); and the two named CNS entities beyond MS — ADEM (autoimmune) and PML (viral, JC virus).

## sections
### Definition
Demyelinating diseases target the myelin sheath — the oligodendrocyte-derived insulation of CNS axons that makes up white matter — rather than the neuronal cell body, and myelin damage impairs or blocks axonal conduction, producing sensory, visual, coordination and motor symptoms rather than seizures, which instead arise from cortical grey matter pathology. The group splits into myelinoclastic disease, in which previously normal myelin is destroyed by an acquired toxic, chemical or (most often) autoimmune insult, and leukodystrophy, an inherited disorder in which myelin was never normally formed. Multiple sclerosis is the prototypical CNS myelinoclastic disease; acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM) is a related, typically monophasic, post-infectious autoimmune CNS demyelinating disease; and progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML) is a distinct, virally caused (JC virus) demyelinating disease of immunocompromised patients, in which the virus directly lyses oligodendrocytes rather than triggering autoimmune attack.

### Definition — per-concept facts
Demyelinating diseases are defined by injury targeted at the myelin sheath — the lipid-rich insulating wrap around axons produced by oligodendrocytes (CNS) or Schwann cells (PNS) — rather than at the neuronal cell body or axon itself, distinguishing them from primary neurodegenerative or axonal diseases.

Myelin normally allows fast, saltatory conduction of action potentials by insulating the axon between nodes of Ranvier. When myelin is damaged, conduction slows, becomes unreliable, or fails (conduction block), producing the sensory, motor and visual symptoms characteristic of demyelinating disease.

Myelinoclastic (acquired) demyelinating diseases — multiple sclerosis being the prototype — destroy myelin that formed normally in the first place, via an acquired toxic, metabolic or (most often) autoimmune insult occurring after normal myelination was already complete.

Leukodystrophies are inherited, typically enzymatic/metabolic disorders in which myelin is abnormally formed or maintained from the outset (myelin was never normal), whereas myelinoclastic diseases such as multiple sclerosis destroy myelin that had developed and functioned normally before the disease process began. This distinction underlies different imaging patterns, ages of onset and management approaches.

The classic clinical repertoire of CNS demyelinating disease includes optic/ocular disturbances (optic neuritis, internuclear ophthalmoplegia), impaired coordination (cerebellar/proprioceptive pathway involvement), and limb weakness (corticospinal tract involvement) from white matter lesions. Seizures are uncommon and not a standard teaching feature, since demyelinating lesions are predominantly white matter rather than the cortical grey matter that generates seizure activity.

Cerebral white matter is composed predominantly of myelinated axons travelling between cortical regions and subcortical structures; its pale colour on gross examination comes directly from the lipid-rich myelin sheaths. This is why demyelinating disease characteristically produces white matter lesions, visible as plaques on imaging and at autopsy.

Acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM) is a monophasic, typically post-infectious or post-vaccination autoimmune demyelinating disease of the CNS, causing widespread white matter inflammation and demyelination on brain and spinal cord MRI — distinct from the PNS demyelinating diseases (Guillain-Barre syndrome, CIDP) and from peripheral neuropathies such as Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease.

Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy is caused by reactivation of JC virus (a polyomavirus) in immunocompromised patients, which lytically infects and destroys oligodendrocytes, producing multifocal demyelinating lesions of the CNS white matter — a viral, not autoimmune or toxic, demyelinating mechanism.

### Mechanism
Autoimmune myelinoclastic disease (MS, ADEM) destroys myelin via immune-mediated attack on an originally normal sheath; PML destroys myelin via direct lytic viral infection of the oligodendrocyte that makes it — two different mechanisms converging on the same white-matter target.

### Key determinants
MRI white matter lesion pattern, tempo (monophasic ADEM versus relapsing MS versus progressive PML in an immunocompromised host) and immune status together distinguish these CNS demyelinating entities from one another and from the PNS demyelinating diseases (Guillain-Barre syndrome, CIDP).

### Clinical significance
PML is a feared complication of potent immunosuppression (e.g. natalizumab for MS, advanced HIV) precisely because it is a distinct, virally driven demyelinating process requiring immune reconstitution rather than more immunosuppression.

### Common misconceptions
Do not group PNS demyelinating diseases (Guillain-Barre syndrome, CIDP, Charcot-Marie-Tooth) with the CNS entities here; do not call myelinoclastic disease congenital (that describes leukodystrophy); and do not list seizures as a classic demyelinating-disease feature.

## hold_these
The shared biology of demyelinating disease: myelin (white matter) is the target, not the neuron body; the myelinoclastic (acquired, myelin-was-normal) versus leukodystrophic (inherited, myelin-never-normal) distinction; the classic ocular/coordination/weakness clinical picture (not seizures); and the two named CNS entities beyond MS — ADEM (autoimmune) and PML (viral, JC virus).

## lose_the_mark
Do not group PNS demyelinating diseases (Guillain-Barre syndrome, CIDP, Charcot-Marie-Tooth) with the CNS entities here; do not call myelinoclastic disease congenital (that describes leukodystrophy); and do not list seizures as a classic demyelinating-disease feature.

## summary
Who gets MS and how it behaves over time: prevalence (~1/1000 in the US/Europe), female predominance (~2:1), typical young-adult onset, the relapsing-remitting course with relapses that slow over time, the autoimmune loss-of-tolerance mechanism, and the genetic evidence (15-fold first-degree-relative risk, ~25% monozygotic twin concordance) for a multifactorial, not single-gene, cause.

## sections
### Definition
Multiple sclerosis affects roughly 1 in 1000 people in high-prevalence regions such as the United States and Europe, with women affected about twice as often as men, and onset typically in young adulthood — onset in childhood or after age 50 is comparatively uncommon. Most patients follow a relapsing-remitting course: discrete attacks with partial or complete recovery, rather than steady progression from onset; relapse frequency itself tends to decline with increasing disease duration, even as many patients transition toward a secondary progressive phase. Mechanistically, MS is an autoimmune disease driven by loss of tolerance to myelin self-antigens. Its genetic contribution is demonstrated by a roughly 15-fold increased risk in first-degree relatives of an affected person and an approximately 25% concordance rate in monozygotic twins — high enough to prove real genetic susceptibility, but far short of 100%, meaning environmental triggers are also required.

### Definition — per-concept facts
Multiple sclerosis affects roughly 1 in 1000 people in high-prevalence regions such as the United States and Europe, reflecting a latitude-linked prevalence gradient (higher farther from the equator) attributed to a combination of genetic susceptibility and environmental factors such as vitamin D and past Epstein-Barr virus infection.

Multiple sclerosis shows a clear female predominance, with women affected roughly twice as frequently as men — a sex disparity attributed to hormonal and immune-system differences, and one that has been increasing in relative magnitude over recent decades in epidemiological studies.

Multiple sclerosis characteristically begins between the late teens and about age 40-50, with onset in childhood or after age 50 being comparatively uncommon. Late-onset MS, when it occurs, tends to have a more progressive course from the outset compared with the relapsing-remitting pattern typical of young-adult-onset disease.

The majority of multiple sclerosis patients present with relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS): discrete attacks of new or worsening neurological symptoms (relapses), followed by partial or complete recovery (remission), rather than steady unremitting progression from onset — which instead characterises the less common primary progressive subtype.

Although individual relapses can be unpredictable, population-level natural-history data show that relapse frequency tends to decline as MS duration increases, even as many patients transition toward a more steadily progressive phase (secondary progressive MS) with accumulating disability despite fewer distinct relapses.

Multiple sclerosis pathogenesis begins with a breakdown of self-tolerance in which autoreactive T cells (and subsequently B cells and antibodies) target myelin antigens such as myelin basic protein and myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein, driving the inflammatory demyelination that defines the disease.

Multiple sclerosis shows significant familial clustering: the risk to a first-degree relative (parent, sibling or child) of an affected individual is roughly 15 times the general-population risk, reflecting a strong genetic contribution (chiefly HLA-linked) layered on top of environmental triggers.

Monozygotic (identical) twin studies show an MS concordance rate of roughly 25% — far higher than the population risk and higher than dizygotic twin or sibling concordance, confirming a substantial genetic contribution, but well short of 100%, which proves environmental factors are also required to trigger disease even in a genetically identical co-twin.

### Mechanism
Environmental factors interacting with genetic susceptibility (chiefly HLA-linked, see the companion pathology article) are needed to actually trigger autoimmune myelin attack, which is exactly what the well-below-100% monozygotic twin concordance demonstrates.

### Key determinants
Female sex, young-adult age, high-latitude residence and a first-degree relative with MS are all recognised risk-stratifying factors used in counselling and diagnostic threshold-setting.

### Clinical significance
Understanding the typical relapsing-remitting course (and its tendency to evolve toward secondary progression) underpins disease-modifying therapy timing and the counselling given at diagnosis about long-term trajectory.

### Common misconceptions
A falling relapse rate does not mean the disease is resolving — many patients are simultaneously entering a progressive phase; and twin concordance well below 100% is evidence for a multifactorial cause, not evidence against a genetic contribution.

## summary
MS at the bench: the HLA-DR/CD4+ T cell immunogenetic axis, the gross appearance of a plaque (well-circumscribed, glassy, grey-tan), its periventricular predilection, and the microscopic features that separate an active lesion (myelin-laden macrophages, perivascular lymphocyte cuffing) from a chronic one, with axons relatively — not completely — preserved throughout.

## sections
### Definition
Multiple sclerosis susceptibility is most strongly linked to the HLA-DR haplotype, a class II MHC molecule that presents myelin peptide antigens to CD4+ T cells, which play the central pathogenic role in driving the autoimmune attack. Grossly, an MS plaque is a well-circumscribed, slightly depressed, glassy-appearing, grey-tan lesion, reflecting focal myelin loss; plaques characteristically arise in periventricular white matter as well as the optic nerves, brainstem, cerebellum and spinal cord. Microscopically, an active plaque shows ongoing myelin breakdown with abundant macrophages containing myelin debris, together with lymphocytes and macrophages characteristically arranged as perivascular cuffs around small veins — the route by which they crossed the blood-brain barrier. Throughout this process, axons within the plaque are relatively preserved, though axonal number may be reduced, particularly with chronic or repeated demyelination.

### Definition — per-concept facts
The strongest and most consistently replicated genetic association in multiple sclerosis is with HLA-DR (specifically HLA-DRB1*15:01 in most populations studied), a class II MHC molecule involved in presenting myelin peptide antigens to CD4+ T cells, mechanistically linking this genetic risk factor to the disease's autoimmune pathogenesis.

Autoreactive CD4+ (helper) T cells, activated against myelin antigens and presented via HLA-DR, are considered central drivers of MS pathogenesis, orchestrating the inflammatory cascade that recruits macrophages, activates microglia and ultimately triggers demyelination — though CD8+ T cells and B cells also contribute to lesion pathology.

On gross pathological examination, MS plaques are sharply demarcated (well-circumscribed) lesions, often slightly sunken/depressed relative to surrounding white matter, with a translucent, glassy, grey-tan appearance that reflects the loss of the normal white, lipid-rich myelin in that area.

MS plaques show a characteristic predilection for periventricular white matter (the region immediately surrounding the lateral ventricles), as well as the optic nerves, brainstem, cerebellum and spinal cord — a distribution exploited diagnostically on MRI (periventricular ovoid lesions, 'Dawson fingers').

In an actively demyelinating MS lesion, macrophages (and activated microglia) infiltrate the plaque and phagocytose breakdown products of degraded myelin, so that macrophages laden with myelin debris are the histological hallmark of lesion activity, distinguishing active from chronic, inactive (burnt-out, gliotic) plaques.

Active MS lesions show inflammatory cells (lymphocytes and macrophages) characteristically arranged as sleeve-like aggregates surrounding small veins and venules — perivascular cuffing — reflecting the route by which these cells cross the blood-brain barrier to enter the CNS parenchyma and drive demyelination.

Although MS is primarily a demyelinating (myelin-directed) disease, axons within a plaque are relatively — not completely — preserved; some axonal loss and transection does occur, particularly with chronic/repeated demyelination, and contributes to permanent disability, but the axon is not the primary initial target the way myelin is.

### Mechanism
HLA-DR-restricted antigen presentation activates CD4+ T cells against myelin antigens; activated lymphocytes and macrophages cross the blood-brain barrier at venules (producing the perivascular cuff), then phagocytose myelin within the parenchyma (producing the myelin-laden macrophage population that marks lesion activity).

### Key determinants
The presence of myelin-laden macrophages and perivascular cuffing histologically distinguishes an active, currently demyelinating plaque from a chronic, burnt-out (gliotic, hypocellular) plaque.

### Clinical significance
Relative axonal preservation within plaques is the pathological basis for remyelination and clinical remission after a relapse, while incomplete remyelination and cumulative axonal loss over repeated relapses underlie the progressive disability seen later in the disease course.

### Common misconceptions
Do not describe an active MS plaque as showing normal myelin with no inflammation, and do not assume MS plaque axons are either completely destroyed or fully regenerated — both overstate the true, partial and lesion-stage-dependent picture.
