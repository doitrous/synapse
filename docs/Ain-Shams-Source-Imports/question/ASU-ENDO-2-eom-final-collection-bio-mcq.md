## title
Mechanism of lipophilic (steroid/thyroid) hormone action

## question
What is true about lipophilic hormones (steroid/thyroid hormones)?

## subject
endo

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
They affect transcription after binding to an intracellular receptor

## explanation_a
Correct. Lipophilic hormones (steroids, thyroid hormone) diffuse across the plasma membrane and bind intracellular (cytoplasmic or nuclear) receptors. The hormone-receptor complex acts as a transcription factor, altering gene expression over hours.

## answer_b
They bind a cell-surface receptor and activate a G protein

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Binding a cell-surface receptor and activating a G protein is the mechanism of hydrophilic peptide/amine hormones, not lipophilic steroid/thyroid hormones.

## answer_c
They act within seconds through a second-messenger cascade

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Lipophilic hormones act slowly (hours) via transcriptional changes, not within seconds; rapid second-messenger signalling is characteristic of membrane-receptor hormones.

## answer_d
They require a membrane transporter and cannot cross the plasma membrane

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Being lipid-soluble, these hormones diffuse directly across the plasma membrane and do not require a membrane transporter.

## title
Mechanism of cholera and pertussis toxin

## question
How does cholera toxin/pertussis toxin exert its effect on signal transduction?

## subject
endo

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
By continuous activation of adenylyl cyclase

## explanation_a
Correct. Cholera toxin ADP-ribosylates the Gs alpha subunit, locking it in its GTP-bound (active) state so it cannot hydrolyse GTP, giving continuous, unregulated activation of adenylyl cyclase and persistently elevated cAMP (the mechanism behind cholera's massive secretory diarrhoea). Pertussis toxin similarly ADP-ribosylates Gi, preventing it from inhibiting adenylyl cyclase, which also raises cAMP.

## answer_b
By irreversibly inhibiting phospholipase C

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Neither toxin acts on phospholipase C; their target is the alpha subunit of the Gs/Gi heterotrimeric G proteins that regulate adenylyl cyclase.

## answer_c
By blocking GTPase activity of Gq only

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The toxins act by ADP-ribosylating G-alpha subunits (Gs for cholera, Gi for pertussis), not by directly blocking Gq's GTPase activity.

## answer_d
By directly phosphorylating protein kinase A

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The toxins act upstream, at the level of the G protein controlling adenylyl cyclase, not by directly phosphorylating protein kinase A.

## title
Insulin receptor beta subunit has intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity

## question
What's true regarding insulin receptors?

## subject
endo

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The beta subunit has intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity

## explanation_a
Correct. The insulin receptor is a preformed tetramer of two extracellular alpha subunits and two transmembrane beta subunits linked by disulfide bonds. The intracellular portion of each beta subunit has intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity, which autophosphorylates and then phosphorylates substrates such as IRS-1 upon insulin binding to the alpha subunits.

## answer_b
The alpha subunit has intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The alpha subunits are entirely extracellular and bind insulin; they have no kinase activity — the kinase domain resides in the beta subunits.

## answer_c
The receptor is a single-pass monomer with no kinase activity

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The insulin receptor is a disulfide-linked alpha2-beta2 tetramer, not a single-pass monomer, and it does have intrinsic (beta-subunit) tyrosine kinase activity.

## answer_d
Ligand binding is required only for receptor synthesis, not for activation

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Insulin binding to the extracellular alpha subunits is required to trigger the conformational change that activates the beta subunit's kinase activity — binding is the activating event, not merely a synthesis requirement.

## title
Sequence of glucagon's Gs-cAMP-PKA cascade

## question
Choose the correct order of glucagon's intracellular signalling cascade.

## subject
endo

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
G protein → adenylyl cyclase → cAMP → protein kinase A → effect

## explanation_a
Correct. Glucagon binds its Gs-protein-coupled receptor, activating Gs, which stimulates membrane-bound adenylyl cyclase to convert ATP to cAMP. CAMP then activates protein kinase A (PKA), which phosphorylates target enzymes to produce the cellular effect (e.g., glycogenolysis).

## answer_b
Adenylyl cyclase → G protein → protein kinase A → cAMP → effect

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This reverses the order — the G protein must be activated by the receptor first, and it activates adenylyl cyclase, not the other way around; PKA is activated by cAMP, not before it.

## answer_c
cAMP → G protein → adenylyl cyclase → protein kinase A → effect

## explanation_c
Incorrect. cAMP is a downstream product of adenylyl cyclase activity, not an upstream activator of the G protein.

## answer_d
G protein → protein kinase A → adenylyl cyclase → cAMP → effect

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Protein kinase A is activated by cAMP; it cannot act before adenylyl cyclase has generated cAMP.

Phospholipase A2

Incorrect. Phospholipase A2 releases arachidonic acid from membrane phospholipids (the first step of eicosanoid synthesis), not DAG and IP3.

## answer_d
Guanylyl cyclase

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Guanylyl cyclase generates cGMP from GTP; it plays no role in producing DAG or IP3.

G proteins have intrinsic GTPase activity

## question
What is true regarding heterotrimeric G-proteins?

## subject
endo

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
They have intrinsic GTPase activity

## explanation_a
Correct. The G-alpha subunit has intrinsic GTPase activity: it hydrolyses its own bound GTP to GDP. This switches the G protein off and terminates signalling — this self-limiting property is essential for normal, time-limited hormone signalling (and is the property that cholera/pertussis toxins disable).

## answer_b
They are permanently active once bound to GTP, with no mechanism for inactivation

## explanation_b
Incorrect. G proteins are not permanently active; their intrinsic GTPase activity provides a built-in 'off switch' by hydrolysing GTP to GDP, re-associating G-alpha with G-beta-gamma to end signalling.

## answer_c
They directly phosphorylate their downstream target enzymes

## explanation_c
Incorrect. G proteins do not have kinase activity and do not phosphorylate anything; they act by modulating effector enzymes (adenylyl cyclase, phospholipase C) which then generate second messengers.

## answer_d
They act only on nuclear receptors

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Heterotrimeric G proteins couple cell-surface receptors to membrane effector enzymes; they do not act on nuclear receptors, which are activated by lipophilic hormones independently of G proteins.

## title
Galactokinase deficiency: first step of galactose metabolism

## question
What is the first step in galactose metabolism, and which enzyme is deficient in this form of galactosemia?

## subject
endo

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Galactokinase, which phosphorylates galactose to galactose-1-phosphate

## explanation_a
Correct. The first step of galactose metabolism is phosphorylation of galactose to galactose-1-phosphate by galactokinase. Deficiency of this enzyme causes galactokinase-deficiency galactosemia (galactosemia type 2), characterised chiefly by cataracts from galactitol accumulation in the lens, but without the severe hepatic/renal disease of classic (GALT-deficiency) galactosemia.

## answer_b
Galactose-1-phosphate uridyltransferase (GALT)

## explanation_b
Incorrect. GALT catalyses the second step (converting galactose-1-phosphate to glucose-1-phosphate); its deficiency causes classic galactosemia (type 1), a distinct and more severe disease, not the first step of the pathway.

## answer_c
UDP-galactose-4-epimerase

## explanation_c
Incorrect. UDP-galactose-4-epimerase acts later in the pathway, interconverting UDP-galactose and UDP-glucose; it is not the first enzyme and its deficiency causes a rarer, distinct form of galactosemia (type 3).

## answer_d
Aldose reductase

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Aldose reductase converts galactose to galactitol via the alternative (minor) polyol pathway when galactokinase's normal pathway is overwhelmed or blocked; it is not the first, primary enzyme of galactose metabolism.

## title
Phosphoglycerate kinase is a reversible kinase of glycolysis

## question
Which of the following is a reversible kinase (freely reversible glycolytic step)?

## subject
endo

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Phosphoglycerate kinase

## explanation_a
Correct. Phosphoglycerate kinase catalyses a near-equilibrium, freely reversible reaction (1,3-bisphosphoglycerate + ADP to 3-phosphoglycerate + ATP), and runs equally in the reverse direction during gluconeogenesis. It is one of the few glycolytic 'kinase' steps that is not committed/irreversible.

## answer_b
Hexokinase

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Hexokinase catalyses a large negative free-energy change (irreversible) step and is one of the three key regulated, irreversible enzymes of glycolysis, bypassed by glucose-6-phosphatase in gluconeogenesis.

## answer_c
Phosphofructokinase-1

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Phosphofructokinase-1 catalyses the committed, rate-limiting, irreversible step of glycolysis, bypassed by fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase in gluconeogenesis.

## answer_d
Pyruvate kinase

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Pyruvate kinase catalyses a highly exergonic, irreversible step of glycolysis, bypassed in gluconeogenesis by the pyruvate carboxylase/PEPCK shunt.

## title
Ketone bodies fuel the brain after prolonged starvation

## question
What is the brain's main source of energy after about 3 weeks of starvation?

## subject
endo

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Ketone bodies

## explanation_a
Correct. After roughly 2-3 weeks of prolonged starvation, hepatic ketogenesis rises sufficiently that ketone bodies (beta-hydroxybutyrate and acetoacetate) become the brain's major fuel. This spares glucose. It also reduces the need for muscle protein breakdown to supply gluconeogenic precursors.

## answer_b
Free fatty acids directly

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Free fatty acids cannot cross the blood-brain barrier in significant amounts and cannot be used directly by neurons; the liver must first convert them to ketone bodies, which the brain can use.

## answer_c
Muscle-derived glucose

## explanation_c
Incorrect. While muscle-derived amino acids support hepatic gluconeogenesis, that route is reduced (not the main energy source) precisely because rising ketone body use spares glucose and protein in prolonged starvation.

## answer_d
Dietary glucose

## explanation_d
Incorrect. By definition, starvation means no dietary intake, so dietary glucose cannot be an energy source after weeks without food.

## title
Glucagon/epinephrine/corticosteroid decrease peripheral glucose uptake

## question
What is a shared action of glucagon, epinephrine, and corticosteroids on peripheral tissue?

## subject
endo

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Decrease glucose uptake by peripheral tissues

## explanation_a
Correct. Glucagon, epinephrine and corticosteroids are all counter-regulatory (insulin-antagonistic) hormones. Among their shared, glucose-raising actions is decreasing glucose uptake by peripheral (especially muscle and fat) tissues, which — together with increased hepatic glucose output — raises blood glucose.

## answer_b
Increase GLUT4 translocation in muscle and fat

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Increased GLUT4 translocation is an insulin effect, which increases (not decreases) peripheral glucose uptake — the opposite of what these counter-regulatory hormones do.

## answer_c
Increase glycogen synthase activity in liver

## explanation_c
Incorrect. These hormones increase (not decrease) hepatic glycogen breakdown and inhibit glycogen synthase, opposing insulin's stimulation of glycogen synthesis.

## answer_d
Increase peripheral insulin sensitivity

## explanation_d
Incorrect. These hormones decrease, not increase, peripheral insulin sensitivity (they are diabetogenic/insulin-antagonistic), which is part of why they raise blood glucose.

## question
A female patient presents with hypoglycaemia. Which hormone responds first?

## subject
endo

A

## answer_a
Glucagon

## explanation_a

Cortisol

Epinephrine

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Epinephrine is an important, rapid second-line responder (particularly when glucagon secretion is impaired, e.g. long-standing type 1 diabetes) but glucagon is normally the first and primary defence against hypoglycaemia.

## title
Ketogenesis occurs with low insulin levels

## question
Which of the following occurs in states of low insulin levels?

## subject
endo

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Ketogenesis

## explanation_a
Correct. Low insulin (with relatively high glucagon) permits unrestrained adipose lipolysis, delivering free fatty acids to the liver. Low insulin also fails to inhibit hepatic carnitine palmitoyltransferase-1 (CPT1), so fatty acids enter mitochondria for beta-oxidation and ketogenesis, producing acetoacetate and beta-hydroxybutyrate.

## answer_b
Increased glycogen synthesis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Glycogen synthesis is stimulated by insulin (via activation of glycogen synthase); low insulin instead favours glycogen breakdown, not synthesis.

## answer_c
Increased lipogenesis (fatty acid synthesis)

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Lipogenesis (fatty acid synthesis) is an insulin-stimulated, fed-state process (via activation of acetyl-CoA carboxylase); low insulin instead favours lipolysis, the opposite process.

## answer_d
Increased GLUT4 translocation in muscle

## explanation_d
Incorrect. GLUT4 translocation to the muscle/fat cell membrane is stimulated by insulin; low insulin reduces, not increases, peripheral glucose uptake via this transporter.

Which cofactor is required for the pyruvate carboxylase reaction?

## subject
endo

## status

Claude

Biotin

## explanation_a
Correct. Pyruvate carboxylase is a biotin-dependent carboxylase. Biotin, covalently attached to the enzyme, carries an activated CO2 group as carboxybiotin. Thiamine (vitamin B1)

Pyridoxal phosphate (vitamin B6)

Incorrect. Pyridoxal phosphate is the cofactor for transaminases (e.g., ALT, AST) and other amino-acid-metabolising enzymes, not for pyruvate carboxylase.

## answer_d
Cobalamin (vitamin B12)

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Cobalamin (B12) is a cofactor for methylmalonyl-CoA mutase and methionine synthase, not for pyruvate carboxylase.

## title
Glucagon inhibits PFK2 to favour gluconeogenesis

## question
Glucagon's action favours gluconeogenesis by inhibiting which enzyme?

## subject
endo

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Phosphofructokinase-2 (PFK2)

## explanation_a
Correct. Glucagon, via PKA-mediated phosphorylation of the bifunctional PFK2/FBPase2 enzyme, inhibits its PFK2 (kinase) activity, lowering fructose-2,6-bisphosphate. Since fructose-2,6-bisphosphate normally activates glycolytic PFK1 and inhibits gluconeogenic FBPase1, this fall relieves inhibition of gluconeogenesis and removes glycolytic drive, favouring net gluconeogenic flux.

## answer_b
Fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Glucagon does not inhibit fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase — on the contrary, by lowering fructose-2,6-bisphosphate (its allosteric inhibitor), glucagon action effectively disinhibits/favours this gluconeogenic enzyme.

## answer_c
Glucose-6-phosphatase

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Glucagon does not inhibit glucose-6-phosphatase; it increases the expression/activity supporting the final step of gluconeogenesis (and glycogenolysis) that releases free glucose.

## answer_d
Pyruvate carboxylase

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Glucagon does not inhibit pyruvate carboxylase; hepatic gluconeogenic flux through this enzyme is supported, not opposed, in the glucagon-dominant (fasting) state.

Galactokinase deficiency presenting with infantile cataracts and a false-negative glucose dipstick

## question

Claude

A

Galactokinase

Fructokinase

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Fructokinase deficiency (essential fructosuria) is a benign condition causing fructose (also a reducing sugar) in urine, but it does not cause cataracts, distinguishing it from this vignette.

## title
SCOT deficiency: the liver cannot use ketone bodies

## question
Which enzyme, absent from the liver, prevents the liver from performing ketolysis (using ketone bodies as fuel)?

## subject
endo

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Succinyl-CoA:3-ketoacid CoA transferase (SCOT/thiophorase)

## explanation_a
Correct. Succinyl-CoA:3-ketoacid CoA transferase (SCOT, also called thiophorase) catalyses the key activating step of ketolysis — transferring CoA from succinyl-CoA to acetoacetate to form acetoacetyl-CoA — but the liver lacks this enzyme. Consequently, the liver can produce ketone bodies but cannot itself use them as fuel, ensuring ketones are exported for use by extrahepatic tissues (brain, muscle, heart).

## answer_b
HMG-CoA synthase

## explanation_b
Incorrect. HMG-CoA synthase is present in the liver and is required for ketone body synthesis (the rate-limiting, mitochondrial ketogenic enzyme); its absence is not what prevents hepatic ketolysis.

## answer_c
HMG-CoA lyase

## explanation_c
Incorrect. HMG-CoA lyase is also present in the liver and is required for ketogenesis (cleaving HMG-CoA to acetoacetate); it is not the enzyme whose hepatic absence explains the liver's inability to use ketones.

## answer_d
Beta-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Beta-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase (interconverting beta-hydroxybutyrate and acetoacetate) is present in the liver; it is SCOT, not this enzyme, that the liver specifically lacks, blocking ketolysis.

## title
Well-fed state: increased adipose TAG synthesis and hepatic amino acid degradation

## question
Which of the following occurs in the well-fed state?

## subject
endo

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Increased triacylglycerol (TAG) synthesis in adipose tissue and increased degradation of amino acids in the liver

## explanation_a
Correct. In the well-fed (postprandial, high-insulin) state, adipose tissue takes up glucose and fatty acids to increase triacylglycerol synthesis, storing fat. At the same time, the liver faces a dietary amino acid surplus beyond its immediate protein-synthesis needs. It increases amino acid degradation by transamination/deamination, using some carbon skeletons for energy or fat synthesis and disposing of nitrogen via the urea cycle.

## answer_b
Increased lipolysis in adipose tissue

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Lipolysis is suppressed, not increased, in the well-fed state, since high insulin inhibits hormone-sensitive lipase; lipolysis is a fasting-state process.

## answer_c
Increased hepatic ketogenesis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Ketogenesis is suppressed in the well-fed state (high insulin inhibits lipolysis and disinhibits malonyl-CoA-mediated CPT1 blockade); ketogenesis is characteristic of fasting/low-insulin states.

## answer_d
Increased muscle proteolysis to supply gluconeogenic substrate

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Muscle proteolysis to supply gluconeogenic amino acids is a fasting-state response to low insulin; in the well-fed state, insulin promotes net protein synthesis rather than breakdown in muscle.

## title
Glycogenesis is insulin's primary action in the well-fed state

## question
In an insulin-stimulated, well-fed state, what is the primary action of insulin?

## subject
endo

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Glycogenesis

## explanation_a
Correct. In the well-fed state, insulin's primary action is to promote glycogenesis. It does this by activating glycogen synthase, through inhibition of glycogen synthase kinase and activation of protein phosphatase-1. The net effect is to store the postprandial glucose surplus as glycogen in liver and muscle.

## answer_b
Glycogenolysis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Glycogenolysis (glycogen breakdown) is inhibited, not promoted, by insulin; it is stimulated instead by glucagon and epinephrine during fasting or stress.

## answer_c
Gluconeogenesis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Gluconeogenesis is inhibited by insulin (which suppresses expression of gluconeogenic enzymes such as PEPCK and glucose-6-phosphatase); it is a fasting-state, glucagon-driven process, the opposite of the fed-state action of insulin.

## answer_d
Ketogenesis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Ketogenesis is suppressed by insulin (via inhibition of lipolysis and disinhibition of malonyl-CoA-mediated CPT1 blockade); it is a fasting/low-insulin-state process.
