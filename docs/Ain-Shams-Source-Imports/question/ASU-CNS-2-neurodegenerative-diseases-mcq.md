## title
Which of the following best describes the characteristic feature of neurodegenerative diseases of the CNS?

## question
Which of the following best describes the characteristic feature of neurodegenerative diseases of the CNS?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Inflammation of neurons based on their physical location in the brain

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Neurodegenerative disease does not select neurons by anatomical location or produce an inflammatory reaction as its defining feature.

## answer_b
Cellular degeneration of subsets of neurons related by function rather than physical location

## explanation_b
Correct. Neurodegenerative diseases selectively destroy neuron populations linked by shared function or connectivity, which is why a single disease can affect widely separated regions belonging to one functional network while sparing neighbouring neurons of a different class. This functional, not anatomical, selectivity underlies disease-specific patterns such as Braak staging in Alzheimer disease and the basal-ganglia-restricted loss seen in Parkinson disease. It distinguishes neurodegeneration from processes like infarction or abscess, which spread by contiguous anatomy instead.

## answer_c
Random destruction of all neuronal cell types equally

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Neurodegeneration is selective, not a random or equal destruction of every neuronal cell type.

## answer_d
Primary involvement of glial cells with secondary neuronal damage

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Glial involvement is typically secondary/reactive (gliosis); the primary process targets neurons, not glia.

## explanation_d
Incorrect (this IS used). Known genetic abnormalities (e.g. APP/PSEN mutations, CAG-repeat expansions) are a recognised classification axis.

Incorrect. Neurodegenerative disorders are not, as a class, viral in origin — that mechanism instead characterises certain CNS infections.

## explanation_b
Correct. Most CNS neurodegenerative diseases share a common mechanism: misfolded or abnormally processed proteins accumulate within or around neurons and become toxic. Examples include beta-amyloid and tau in Alzheimer disease, alpha-synuclein in Parkinson disease and Lewy body dementia, and huntingtin in Huntington disease. Incorrect. Autoimmune myelin destruction characterises demyelinating disease (e.g. multiple sclerosis), a distinct disease category from neurodegeneration.

## answer_d
Ischemic neuronal injury

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Ischaemic injury is a vascular mechanism, not the shared mechanism of the neurodegenerative disease group.

## answer_b
Evidence of ongoing myelin breakdown with abundant macrophages containing myelin debris

## explanation_b
Correct. In an actively demyelinating MS lesion, macrophages and activated microglia infiltrate the plaque and phagocytose breakdown products of degraded myelin. Neuronal cell bodies only

Incorrect. Neuronal cell bodies alone do not describe the inflammatory, myelin-breakdown picture of an active plaque.

Normal myelin with no inflammation

Incorrect. Normal myelin with no inflammation is the opposite of an active lesion — it would describe unaffected white matter.

Mostly as perivascular cuffs

Correct. Active MS lesions show inflammatory cells characteristically arranged as sleeve-like aggregates surrounding small veins and venules, known as perivascular cuffing. Exclusively at the lesion periphery

Incorrect. Inflammatory cells are not confined exclusively to the periphery; they cuff vessels distributed through the lesion.

What is the state of axons within MS plaques?

## question
What is the state of axons within MS plaques?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Completely destroyed

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Complete destruction overstates axonal involvement, which is secondary to the primary myelin-directed process.

## answer_b
Relatively preserved but may be reduced in number

## explanation_b
Correct. Although MS is primarily a demyelinating disease, axons within a plaque are relatively — not completely — preserved. Some axonal loss and transection does occur, particularly with chronic or repeated demyelination, and this contributes to permanent disability. The axon is not, however, the primary initial target of the disease process the way myelin is.

## answer_c
Proliferating rapidly

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Axons do not proliferate in MS plaques; neurons and axons do not typically regenerate by proliferation in the adult CNS.

## answer_d
Remyelinated completely

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Remyelination is often incomplete, which is why plaques persist and disability can accumulate — 'completely' remyelinated overstates the typical outcome.
