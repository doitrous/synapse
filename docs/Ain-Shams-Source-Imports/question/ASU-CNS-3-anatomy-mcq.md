## title
Which descending tract mediates reflex head turning toward a visual stimulus?

## question
Which descending tract mediates reflex head turning toward a visual stimulus?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Rubrospinal tract

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The rubrospinal tract, from the red nucleus, facilitates flexor tone in the upper limb; it is not the tract responsible for reflex head/eye turning toward a visual stimulus.

## answer_b
Tectospinal tract

## explanation_b
Correct. The tectospinal tract arises from the superior colliculus, decussates in the midbrain at the level of origin (dorsal tegmental decussation), and descends to cervical cord levels to turn the head and neck reflexly toward a sudden visual stimulus. This crossing point, right at the level of origin, distinguishes it from tracts that decussate lower in the brainstem or spinal cord.

## answer_c
Vestibulospinal tract

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The vestibulospinal tracts adjust axial and limb extensor tone for balance in response to vestibular (head-position) input, not visual input.

## answer_d
Corticospinal tract

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The corticospinal tract carries voluntary skilled movement commands from the motor cortex; it does not mediate the involuntary head-turning reflex to a visual stimulus.

Paralysis of sternocleidomastoid points to a lesion of which nerve?

## explanation_a

Facial nerve

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The facial nerve supplies the muscles of facial expression and, via a small branch, posterior digastric and stylohyoid — not sternocleidomastoid.

Spinal part of the accessory nerve

## question
Infection from the face reaches the cavernous sinus most directly through which vein?

## subject

## status

## vignette

## correct_answer

## answer_a
External jugular vein

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The external jugular vein drains the scalp and superficial face to the subclavian vein; it has no direct communication with the cavernous sinus.

## answer_b
Superior ophthalmic vein

## explanation_b
Correct. The superior ophthalmic vein connects the valveless facial vein anteriorly to the cavernous sinus posteriorly, so infection in the 'danger area' of the face (nose, upper lip) can spread retrogradely through it to cause cavernous sinus thrombosis. Pterygoid venous plexus alone

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The pterygoid venous plexus does communicate with the cavernous sinus via emissary veins, but the superior ophthalmic vein is the classic, direct route tested for facial-infection spread.

Maxillary division

Buccal nerve

Rubrospinal tract

## question
In the neonate, the spinal cord (conus medullaris) ends at approximately which vertebral level?

## subject
neuro

## status

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
L1

## explanation_a
Incorrect. L1 (more precisely L1/L2) is where the conus medullaris ends in the adult, after the vertebral column has outgrown the spinal cord during childhood — not in the neonate.

L3

## explanation_b
Correct. In the neonate the vertebral column has not yet outgrown the spinal cord as much as it will in the adult, so the conus medullaris ends lower, at approximately the L3 vertebral level, and ascends relatively to L1/L2 by adulthood. S2

## explanation_c
Incorrect. S2 is the vertebral level at which the dural (and arachnoid) sac ends, well below the conus medullaris itself, at any age.

## answer_d
T12

## explanation_d
Incorrect. T12 is above both the adult and neonatal conus medullaris levels; the cord does not end this high even in the adult.

## question
In an upper motor neuron (central) facial nerve lesion, weakness affects orbicularis oris (lower face) on the contralateral side because:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The lower face receives only contralateral cortical innervation, while the upper face receives bilateral input

## explanation_a
Correct. The lower-face-supplying part of the facial motor nucleus receives corticobulbar input from the contralateral cortex only, so a UMN lesion produces contralateral lower-face weakness; the upper-face-supplying part receives bilateral cortical input and is spared. This is the clinical basis for asking a patient to raise their eyebrows: forehead sparing points to a central, not peripheral, facial nerve lesion.

## answer_b
The upper face receives only contralateral cortical innervation

## explanation_b
Incorrect. It is the lower face, not the upper face, that receives only contralateral cortical input; the upper face's bilateral input is exactly why it is spared in a UMN lesion.

## answer_c
Both upper and lower face receive purely ipsilateral cortical innervation

## explanation_c
Incorrect. If both parts received only ipsilateral input, a UMN lesion would not weaken the contralateral face at all, contrary to the classic clinical picture.

## answer_d
The facial nerve nucleus is entirely destroyed in a UMN lesion

## explanation_d
Incorrect. In a UMN lesion the facial nerve nucleus itself is intact; the lesion is in the descending corticobulbar pathway above the nucleus, which is why the bilaterally-innervated upper face is spared.

The deep petrosal nerve, joining the greater petrosal nerve at the pterygopalatine ganglion, carries which type of fibres?

## question
The deep petrosal nerve, joining the greater petrosal nerve at the pterygopalatine ganglion, carries which type of fibres?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Postganglionic sympathetic fibres that pass through without synapsing

## explanation_a
Correct. The deep petrosal nerve carries postganglionic sympathetic fibres (from the internal carotid plexus, ultimately from the superior cervical ganglion) that join the greater petrosal nerve as the nerve of the pterygoid canal and pass through the pterygopalatine ganglion without synapsing. Correctly separating the two petrosal nerves matters clinically, since only the greater petrosal nerve's parasympathetic fibres are lost in a proximal facial nerve lesion.

## answer_b
Preganglionic parasympathetic fibres that synapse in the ganglion

## explanation_b
Incorrect. It is the greater petrosal nerve, not the deep petrosal nerve, that carries the preganglionic parasympathetic fibres which do synapse in the pterygopalatine ganglion.

## answer_c
Special sensory taste fibres

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Taste fibres from the palate travel with the greater petrosal nerve toward the facial nerve's geniculate ganglion, not with the deep petrosal nerve.

## answer_d
Postganglionic parasympathetic fibres leaving the ganglion

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Postganglionic parasympathetic fibres leave the ganglion via its own branches (e.g. palatine, nasopalatine, zygomatic nerves), not via the deep petrosal nerve, which only contributes the sympathetic component passing through.

Internal jugular vein

Subclavian vein

## explanation_b
Correct. The external jugular vein, running superficially within the roof of the posterior triangle, pierces the investing deep cervical fascia to drain into the subclavian vein. Brachiocephalic vein directly

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The subclavian vein, not the brachiocephalic vein directly, is the external jugular vein's point of termination; the brachiocephalic vein is formed further downstream by the union of the subclavian and internal jugular veins.

## answer_d
Cephalic vein

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The cephalic vein is an upper-limb superficial vein unrelated to the posterior triangle of the neck.
