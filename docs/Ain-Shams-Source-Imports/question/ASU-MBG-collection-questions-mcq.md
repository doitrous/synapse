## title
Post-translational phosphorylation adds a phosphate group to a serine hydroxyl

## question
In post-translational phosphorylation of proteins:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Add phosphate on OH of serine.

## explanation_a
Correct. Protein kinases covalently attach a phosphate group to the hydroxyl (-OH) side chain of specific serine (as well as threonine and tyrosine) residues, forming a phosphoester bond. This reversible covalent modification is one of the cell's most common post-translational control mechanisms, changing the target protein's charge and conformation.

## answer_b
Always activate the protein

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Phosphorylation is not uniformly activating — whether it activates or inactivates a given protein depends entirely on which protein and which residue is phosphorylated; some enzymes are switched on by phosphorylation while others are switched off by it.

## answer_c
Always deactivate the protein

## explanation_c
Incorrect, for the same reason as "always activate" — phosphorylation's effect is protein-specific, not uniformly inactivating. Some enzymes are inactivated by phosphorylation, but others are activated by it.

## answer_d
Catalyzed by phosphatase.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A phosphatase catalyses the reverse reaction — removing a phosphate group (dephosphorylation) — not adding one. Phosphorylation itself is catalysed by a protein kinase.

## answer_e
Reversed by kinase.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Phosphorylation is reversed by a phosphatase, not a kinase; a kinase is the enzyme that adds the phosphate group in the first place, so it cannot also be the enzyme that reverses its own reaction.

## title
The Ras proto-oncogene is activated to an oncogene by point mutation

## question
In Ras gene, it is activated by which type of mutation

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Gene duplication

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Gene duplication is not the mechanism that activates RAS specifically; RAS is the textbook example of oncogene activation by a point mutation, not by an increase in gene copy number.

## answer_b
Insertion mutagenesis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Insertion mutagenesis (such as retroviral promoter insertion near a proto-oncogene) is a real oncogene-activation mechanism, but it is not the one associated with RAS, which is activated by a point mutation within the gene itself.

## answer_c
Translocation

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Chromosomal translocation activates oncogenes such as MYC (via immunoglobulin-gene translocation) or BCR-ABL (the Philadelphia chromosome), not RAS, which is activated by a point mutation.

## answer_d
Point mutation

## explanation_d
Correct. RAS is the classic example of a proto-oncogene converted into an active oncogene by a single point mutation — most often at codon 12, 13 or 61 of the RAS gene — which locks the RAS protein in its GTP-bound, constitutively active state and drives continuous downstream growth signalling even without an upstream growth-factor signal.

## answer_e
Frame shift mutation

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A frameshift mutation would disrupt the RAS reading frame and most often destroy the protein's function entirely, rather than producing the specific, subtly hyperactive GTPase-locked protein that a point mutation at codon 12, 13 or 61 produces.

Correct, as printed in the source key. Incorrect. XY denotes a male sex-chromosome constitution, not female; this option correctly reads "deletion" from the minus sign but assigns the wrong sex to the notation.

## answer_d
Female with 46 chromosomes with insertion of extra DNA in Chromosome 5.

## explanation_d
Incorrect on two counts: XY denotes male, not female, and the minus sign denotes a deletion, not an insertion of extra DNA.

## title
Reading the direction of transcription from a labelled DNA duplex

## question
Given the double-stranded DNA segment 5'-ATGCAGT-3' (top strand) paired with 3'-TACGTCA-5' (bottom strand), with an arrow marking the direction of transcription running from the top strand's 3' end towards its 5' end (right to left): if the top strand is read as the template in that direction, the base sequence of the resulting mRNA would be:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
5'-ACUGCAU-3'

## explanation_a
Correct, as printed in the source key. RNA polymerase reads the template strand 3' to 5' while synthesising mRNA 5' to 3'. Reading the top strand (5'-ATGCAGT-3') from its 3' end towards its 5' end gives, in order, T-G-A-C-G-T-A; transcribing this into RNA (complementary base, U replacing T) gives A-C-U-G-C-A-U, i.e. 5'-ACUGCAU-3'.

## answer_b
5'-ACTGCAT-3'

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This string uses T instead of U, which is a DNA base composition, not an RNA transcript — mRNA is synthesised with uracil in place of thymine, so a correctly transcribed answer cannot contain T.

## answer_c
5'-AUGCAGU-3'

## explanation_c
Incorrect as the answer to this specific keyed reading. This sequence would result from using the bottom strand (3'-TACGTCA-5') as the template instead of the top strand; the source's own printed key treats the top strand as the template read in the arrow's direction, giving 5'-ACUGCAU-3' instead.

## answer_d
5'-ATGCAGT-3'

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This is simply the top DNA strand's own sequence copied unchanged, including thymine — it is neither transcribed (U for T) nor read in the reverse direction required by a 3'-to-5' template reading, so it cannot be the mRNA product.

## answer_e
5'-UACGUCA-3'

## explanation_e
Incorrect. This would result from transcribing the top strand read 5' to 3' (its own printed left-to-right order) rather than 3' to 5' as the template-reading rule and the arrow's direction both require.

## title
A UAC-to-UAA change at a tyrosine codon is a nonsense mutation

## question
Which of the following best describes the mutation that results in a change of a codon of tyrosine from UAC into UAA on the mRNA transcript of gene X?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Silent

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A silent mutation changes the codon to a different synonymous codon for the same amino acid, with no change to the protein sequence — UAC (tyrosine) becoming UAA (a stop codon) changes the amino acid identity entirely (in fact truncates the protein), so it is not silent.

## answer_b
Missense

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A missense mutation changes a codon into a codon for a different amino acid, without creating a stop signal — UAA is not a sense codon for any amino acid, it is one of the three stop codons, so this substitution goes further than a simple amino-acid swap.

## answer_c
Frame-shift

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A frameshift results from an insertion or deletion that shifts the reading frame downstream of the change; this is a single base substitution (C to A at the third position) that does not add or remove any nucleotides, so the reading frame is unaffected.

## answer_d
Nonsense

## explanation_d
Correct. UAC (tyrosine) becoming UAA converts a sense codon into one of the three stop codons (UAA, UAG, UGA). A base substitution that creates a premature stop codon is by definition a nonsense mutation, and it truncates the protein at that point in translation.

## answer_e
Splice site

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A splice-site mutation disrupts the sequence at an intron-exon boundary that the spliceosome recognises, altering how the pre-mRNA is spliced; this question describes a single coding-codon substitution within the already-spliced mRNA transcript, not a change at a splice junction.

## title
Independent 1-in-2 recurrence risk per pregnancy for an affected achondroplasia father

## question
Which of the following is correct regarding the children of a male with achondroplasia and an unaffected wife?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
The probability that their second child will be affected if their first child is affected is 1 in 4

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Achondroplasia is autosomal dominant; an affected (heterozygous) father crossed with an unaffected mother gives each pregnancy an independent 1-in-2 (50%) risk, not 1 in 4 — 1 in 4 would be the recurrence risk pattern for an autosomal recessive cross between two carriers, not this autosomal dominant one.

## answer_b
If their first child is not affected then their second child will be affected

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Each pregnancy is an independent event with its own 1-in-2 risk; the outcome of the first pregnancy has no bearing on the second — an unaffected first child does not guarantee, or even shift the odds toward, an affected second child.

## answer_c
The probability that their third child will be affected if their first two children are affected is 1 in 2

## explanation_c
Correct. Because the father is heterozygous for this autosomal dominant condition, each individual pregnancy independently carries a 1-in-2 (50%) risk of inheriting the mutant allele, regardless of how many previous children were or were not affected. Gametes are not "remembered" from one pregnancy to the next, so the third child's risk stays at 1 in 2 even though the first two children happened to be affected.

## answer_d
If their first child is affected then their second child will not be affected

## explanation_d
Incorrect, for the same reason as option B — pregnancies are independent events. An affected first child does not protect, or in any way lower the risk for, the second child, whose own risk remains 1 in 2.

## answer_e
The probability that each of their children will be affected is 1 in 4

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The per-pregnancy risk for this autosomal dominant, heterozygous-father-by-unaffected-mother cross is 1 in 2, not 1 in 4.

## title
Penetrance is the proportion of a genotype's carriers who show any phenotype

## question
The proportion of individuals with a particular genotype that express the same phenotype is referred to as which of the following?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
New mutation

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A new (de novo) mutation explains why an unaffected couple can have an affected child; it is not a measure of what proportion of a genotype's carriers show a phenotype.

## answer_b
Variable expressivity

## explanation_b
Incorrect as the answer to this specific definition. Variable expressivity measures how severe the phenotype is among individuals who are already affected — a different, though related, measure from the proportion of carriers who are affected at all, which is penetrance.

## answer_c
Penetrance

## explanation_c
Correct. Penetrance is defined exactly as the proportion of individuals carrying a particular genotype who show any detectable phenotype at all. Complete penetrance means every carrier is affected to some degree; incomplete penetrance means some carriers show no phenotype whatsoever, which is why a dominant condition can appear to skip a generation in a pedigree.

## answer_d
Anticipation

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Anticipation describes earlier onset and greater severity of a disease in successive generations (classically in trinucleotide-repeat-expansion disorders), not the proportion of carriers who show any phenotype.

## answer_e
Genomic imprinting

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Genomic imprinting describes parent-of-origin-specific silencing of one gene copy, an entirely different mechanism from the population-level proportion-of-carriers-affected measure that penetrance describes.

Chromosome

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A bacterial chromosome is the cell's principal genetic material, not an extrachromosomal element separate from it — a plasmid is defined precisely by being additional to, and independent of, the chromosome.

Male-to-male transmission is a characteristic of autosomal dominant inheritance

## question
Which of the following is a characteristic of autosomal dominant inheritance?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
There is male to male transmission

## explanation_a
Correct. Autosomal dominant inheritance runs through the autosomes, not the sex chromosomes, so an affected father can transmit the mutant allele directly to a son. Male-to-male transmission is possible in autosomal dominant inheritance and is one of the clearest pedigree features distinguishing it from every X-linked pattern, which can never show it.

## answer_b
Cystic fibrosis is a common example of this mode of inheritance

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Cystic fibrosis is autosomal recessive, not autosomal dominant — it needs two mutant CFTR copies to cause disease, unlike a dominant condition, which needs only one.

## answer_c
If one parent is affected, each child has a 25% percent risk to inherit the abnormal gene from the parent.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. For an autosomal dominant condition with one affected (heterozygous) parent and one unaffected parent, each child's risk is 50% (1 in 2), not 25% — 25% is the recurrence risk pattern for an autosomal recessive cross between two carriers, a different pattern entirely.

## answer_d
Both alleles of the gene must be mutated to produce a disease phenotype.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This describes autosomal recessive inheritance, where two mutant copies are needed. Autosomal dominant inheritance needs only one mutant copy to produce the phenotype.

## answer_e
Only males manifest the phenotype

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Autosomal dominant inheritance affects males and females equally, since the responsible gene is on an autosome, not the X chromosome — "only males manifest" instead describes X-linked recessive inheritance.

Prader-Willi syndrome as an example of genomic imprinting

## question
Prader Willi syndrome is a common example of which of the following?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Trinucleotide repeat expansion

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Trinucleotide repeat expansion is the mechanism behind disorders such as Huntington's disease, not Prader-Willi syndrome, which follows a genomic-imprinting mechanism instead.

## answer_b
Genomic imprinting

## explanation_b
Correct. Prader-Willi syndrome is the standard teaching example, alongside its reciprocal disorder Angelman syndrome, of genomic imprinting — only the paternally-inherited copy of the chromosome 15q11-13 region is normally active (the maternal copy is imprinted/silenced), so losing that active paternal contribution, most often through a deletion of the paternal chromosome 15, produces Prader-Willi syndrome.

Mitochondrial inheritance

Incorrect. Mitochondrial inheritance passes exclusively through the maternal line via mitochondrial DNA; Prader-Willi syndrome instead results from a chromosome 15 imprinting/deletion mechanism on nuclear DNA.

Multifactorial modes of inheritance

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Multifactorial inheritance describes diseases from combined genetic and environmental contributions; Prader-Willi syndrome instead follows a single, well-defined chromosome 15 imprinting mechanism.

## answer_e
Anticipation

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Genetic anticipation (earlier onset and greater severity in successive generations) is a feature of trinucleotide-repeat-expansion disorders such as Huntington's disease, not of Prader-Willi syndrome.

## title
A 50% chance the first child of a heterozygous-affected-by-normal couple is unaffected

## question
An extra finger is rare but is due to a dominant gene. When one parent is normal and the other parent has an extra finger and is heterozygous for the trait, what is the probability that the first child of those couples will be normal?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
75%

## explanation_a
Incorrect. For a heterozygous-affected (Aa) by unaffected (aa) autosomal dominant cross, exactly half of the offspring genotypes (Aa) are affected and half (aa) are unaffected — 75% does not match either half of this 1:1 ratio.

## answer_b
100%

## explanation_b
Incorrect. 100% would mean every child is guaranteed to be normal, but the affected parent is heterozygous (Aa) and can pass either the mutant (A) or normal (a) allele to a given child with equal probability, so some children will be affected.

## answer_c
25%

## explanation_c
Incorrect. 25% is the recurrence-risk figure for an autosomal recessive cross between two heterozygous carriers, not for this autosomal dominant, heterozygous-affected-by-unaffected cross, whose ratio is 1:1, not 3:1 or 1:4.

## answer_d
0%

## explanation_d
Incorrect. 0% would mean every child is guaranteed to be affected, but the affected parent's unaffected allele (a) is transmitted to half of the children, who would then be unaffected (aa), so the chance of a normal child is not zero.

## answer_e
50%

## explanation_e
Correct. The affected parent is heterozygous (Aa) for this autosomal dominant trait, and the unaffected parent is homozygous normal (aa). A standard Aa x aa cross produces offspring in a 1 Aa (affected) : 1 aa (normal) ratio, so each child, including the first, has an independent 50% chance of being normal.
