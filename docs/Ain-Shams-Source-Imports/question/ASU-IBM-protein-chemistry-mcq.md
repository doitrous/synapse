## title
All amino acids have an enantiomer except

## question
All amino acids have an enantiomer except

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Valine

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Valine has a single asymmetric (chiral) alpha-carbon bearing four different groups (amino, carboxyl, hydrogen, and an isopropyl side chain), so it exists as a pair of non-superimposable mirror images — D- and L-valine. Having an enantiomer is the rule among standard amino acids, not the exception, so valine does not answer this "except" question.

## answer_b
Aspartate

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Aspartate's alpha-carbon is bonded to four different groups (amino, carboxyl, hydrogen, and a carboxymethyl side chain), giving it a chiral centre and therefore D- and L-enantiomers, just like valine. This option tests whether a student assumes an acidic side chain changes the chirality rule; it does not.

## answer_c
Tryptophan

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Despite its bulky indole side chain, tryptophan's alpha-carbon still carries four distinct substituents and is chiral, so it too has D- and L-forms. A large or complex side chain does not exempt an amino acid from having an enantiomer — only the identity of two of the four substituents being the same does that, which is the glycine case.

## answer_d
Glycine

## explanation_d
Correct. Glycine's side chain is a single hydrogen atom, so its alpha-carbon is bonded to two identical hydrogens alongside the amino and carboxyl groups — only three distinct groups, not four. With no four-way asymmetry there is no chiral centre, so glycine has no enantiomer and is optically inactive: it is the one exception among the twenty standard amino acids, and also the shortest of them.

## title
Regarding the solubility of amino acids

## question
Regarding the solubility of amino acids

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
All are soluble

## explanation_a
Correct. Every standard amino acid's alpha-amino and alpha-carboxyl groups ionise at physiological pH into a zwitterion — a dipolar ion carrying both a positive and a negative charge on the same molecule. This zwitterion interacts favourably with water regardless of what the side chain is, so all twenty amino acids are water-soluble; the backbone, not the side chain, is what this question credits for solubility.

## answer_b
Only amino acids with charged groups are soluble

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This restricts solubility to amino acids like lysine, aspartate or arginine, whose side chains carry an extra charge — but the bank's key rejects this restriction. The zwitterion at the alpha-carbon itself is already present on every amino acid, so solubility does not depend on an additional charged side chain.

## answer_c
Only with charged or polar groups are soluble

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This is a wider net than option B (it also allows polar-but-uncharged side chains like serine's), but it still excludes the non-polar amino acids (glycine, alanine, valine, leucine, phenylalanine), which this bank's key does not exclude. All amino acids are read as soluble because of their shared zwitterionic backbone, independent of the side chain's polarity.

## answer_d
Amino acids with non-polar groups need a carrier

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This describes something closer to how a hydrophobic drug or a fat-soluble vitamin might need a transport protein in blood — it is not how free amino acids behave in solution. Amino acids with non-polar side chains still dissolve in water directly, without a carrier, because of their zwitterionic alpha-amino/alpha-carboxyl groups.

Proteins absorb light at wavelength 280nm, due to presence of which amino acid

## question
Proteins absorb light at wavelength 280nm, due to presence of which amino acid

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Lysine

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Lysine's side chain is an aliphatic chain ending in a primary amino group, with no ring structure to absorb ultraviolet light at 280nm. Basic amino acids like lysine contribute essentially nothing to a protein's UV absorbance spectrum at this wavelength.

## answer_b
Tryptophan

## explanation_b
Correct. Tryptophan's side chain carries an indole ring, the strongest UV chromophore among the twenty standard amino acids, with an absorbance maximum close to 280nm. Because most proteins contain at least one tryptophan, measuring absorbance at 280nm (A280) is a standard, low-cost laboratory method for estimating protein concentration in solution — this single fact is the reason the wavelength 280nm is chosen for that assay.

## answer_c
Glycine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Glycine's side chain is a single hydrogen atom with no aromatic ring at all, so it has no meaningful UV absorbance. It is in fact notable for the opposite reason in this bank — being the one amino acid with no enantiomer — not for any UV property.

## answer_d
Basic amino acids

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The basic amino acids (lysine, arginine, histidine) carry aliphatic or imidazole side chains without the extended aromatic ring system needed for strong UV absorbance at 280nm. Histidine's imidazole ring absorbs weakly at much shorter wavelengths, not at 280nm.

## title
An amino acid which does not have a specific genetic code is

## question
An amino acid which does not have a specific genetic code is

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Leucine

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Leucine is one of the twenty standard amino acids and is directly specified by six degenerate codons (UUA, UUG, CUU, CUC, CUA, CUG). It is built into a polypeptide during translation exactly as coded, with no further modification needed to exist as leucine.

## answer_b
Hydroxyproline

## explanation_b
Correct. Hydroxyproline has no codon of its own. It is produced only after translation, when specific proline residues already incorporated into a growing collagen chain are hydroxylated by prolyl hydroxylase (a vitamin-C-dependent enzyme). Because the genetic code specifies proline, not hydroxyproline, hydroxyproline is the amino acid without a dedicated codon.

## answer_c
Hydroxyl phenylalanine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This distractor plays on the same "hydroxylated post-translationally" pattern as the correct answer, but hydroxylated phenylalanine is not a recognised standard collagen residue the way hydroxyproline and hydroxylysine are; phenylalanine itself is directly coded by four codons (UUU, UUC — actually UUU/UUC code phenylalanine as the standard residue).

## answer_d
Proline

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Proline itself is directly coded by four codons (CCU, CCC, CCA, CCG) and is incorporated into the growing polypeptide chain during normal translation, just like any other standard amino acid. It is only proline's post-translational derivative, hydroxyproline, that lacks a codon — not proline itself.

## title
Denaturation of protein occurs with all of the following except

## question
Denaturation of protein occurs with all of the following except

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Temperature

## explanation_a
Incorrect as the "except" answer — heat is one of the most common denaturing agents, because it supplies enough thermal energy to break the weak non-covalent bonds (hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, electrostatic bonds) holding the higher orders of protein structure together, unfolding the protein while leaving its peptide-bonded primary sequence intact.

## answer_b
Acids

## explanation_b
Incorrect as the "except" answer — a strong acid disrupts the ionic (electrostatic) bonds between oppositely charged side chains by protonating acidic and basic groups, altering the pattern of charges that helps hold tertiary and quaternary structure together, and so denatures the protein.

## answer_c
Alkali

## explanation_c
Incorrect as the "except" answer — a strong alkali has the mirror-image effect of an acid, deprotonating groups and again destroying the electrostatic bonds that stabilise the protein's folded shape, so alkalis are denaturing agents too.

## answer_d
Biological agents as enzyme

## explanation_d
Correct. An enzyme does not denature a protein by unfolding it non-specifically; instead, a protease enzyme hydrolyses specific peptide bonds, breaking the protein down into smaller fragments or free amino acids — a different process (proteolysis) from denaturation. Because enzymatic digestion is not classed as a denaturing agent in this list, it is the exception.

## answer_e
Solvents

## explanation_e
Incorrect as the "except" answer — an organic solvent (such as alcohol or acetone) can disrupt the hydrophobic interactions that drive tertiary folding by competing for those non-polar contacts, unfolding the protein and denaturing it, which is exactly why alcohol is used as a disinfectant against microbial proteins.

## title
The most important covalent bond that stabilizes tertiary structure is

## question
The most important covalent bond that stabilizes tertiary structure is

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Peptide bond

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The peptide bond is a covalent bond, but it holds the primary structure together (the linear sequence of residues), not tertiary structure. Tertiary structure is about how that already-linked chain folds into a 3D shape, which peptide bonds do not control.

## answer_b
Disulfide bond

## explanation_b
Correct. A disulfide bond is the one truly covalent bond among tertiary structure's stabilising forces — a strong S-S bond formed between the side chains of two cysteine residues that may be far apart in the linear sequence. Because it is a genuine covalent bond rather than a weak non-covalent interaction, it survives conditions (such as mild heat) that would break hydrogen bonds or hydrophobic interactions, making it the most important covalent contributor to tertiary structure.

## answer_c
Hydrophobic structure

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Hydrophobic interactions between non-polar side chains are a major stabilising force in tertiary structure, but they are non-covalent (driven by the exclusion of water, not by shared electron pairs), so they are not the covalent bond this question asks for.

## answer_d
Hydrogen bond

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Hydrogen bonds also help stabilise tertiary structure (for example between hydroxyl, amide or carboxylic side-chain groups), but a hydrogen bond is a weak electrostatic attraction, not a covalent bond, and is far more easily disrupted than a disulfide bond.

## title
Which of the following amino acids disrupt alpha helix?

## question
Which of the following amino acids disrupt alpha helix?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Aspartate

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Aspartate's side chain is a simple carboxymethyl group that can still form the intra-chain hydrogen bonds between backbone NH and C=O groups that hold the alpha helix together, and it does not force a kink in the backbone. It can appear within alpha-helical regions without disrupting them.

## answer_b
Proline

## explanation_b
Correct. Proline is an imino acid whose side chain cyclises back onto its own backbone nitrogen, which both removes the NH group needed to hydrogen-bond as part of the helix and locks the backbone's rotation (phi angle) into a value incompatible with the regular alpha-helical geometry. Wherever proline appears in a sequence, it introduces a kink or break in the alpha helix — this is why it is called a "helix breaker." The same ring-structure logic explains why proline also disrupts the beta-pleated sheet, making it one of the few residues that destabilises both major types of secondary structure.

## answer_c
Isoleucine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Isoleucine's branched aliphatic side chain is compatible with the alpha helix and, in fact, aliphatic non-polar residues like isoleucine are common within helical segments because their side chains project outward without interfering with the backbone hydrogen bonding.

## answer_d
Tyrosine

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Tyrosine's aromatic side chain also projects outward from the helix without disrupting the backbone hydrogen-bonding pattern; aromatic residues can and do occur within alpha helices.

## answer_e
Any amino acids with hydrophobic chains

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Hydrophobic side chains are, if anything, well tolerated within alpha helices (many transmembrane helices are built almost entirely of them); hydrophobicity itself does not disrupt the helix. Disruption comes specifically from proline's ring structure and from residues that favour ionic-bond formation in a way that competes with the helix's own hydrogen bonding, not from hydrophobicity generally.

## question

## subject

## status

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Non-covalent

## explanation_a
Incorrect. "Non-covalent" names the very category of interaction that denaturation destroys — hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, hydrophobic interactions — so it cannot describe what is left holding the chain together once those forces are gone. Denaturation is defined by the loss of exactly this category of bond, not its survival. Naming the broken category as the answer inverts what the process does.

## answer_b
Hydrogen bonds

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Hydrogen bonds are one of the specific non-covalent forces that stabilise alpha helices, beta sheets and parts of tertiary folding, and they are among the first interactions disrupted when heat, acid, alkali or solvent denatures a protein. If hydrogen bonds still held the chain together, the protein would not have lost its shape. Their loss is part of what denaturation means, not what remains after it.

## answer_c
Ionic bond

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Ionic (electrostatic) bonds between oppositely charged side chains are disrupted by pH extremes and by many other denaturing conditions, exactly like hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions. A denatured chain has lost this attraction along with the fold it helped maintain. It therefore cannot be the bond still holding the unfolded chain together.

## answer_d
Hydrophobic interaction

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Hydrophobic interactions between nonpolar side chains collapse once a protein unfolds, because unfolding exposes the buried nonpolar core to solvent and removes the packing that produced the interaction in the first place. This is one of the principal forces lost during denaturation, not one that persists. A denatured chain cannot be held together by an interaction that depended on the very fold it has just lost.

## answer_e
Peptide bonds

## explanation_e
Correct. Denaturation ruptures only the non-covalent interactions and disulfide bonds that build secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure, while leaving every covalent peptide bond of the primary sequence completely intact. That is exactly why a denatured protein keeps its original amino acid sequence even though it has lost its native three-dimensional shape. The peptide-bonded backbone is the one connection denaturation cannot touch, which is what still holds the unfolded chain together as a single continuous molecule.

## question
At pH above isoelectric point, amino acids show

## subject

## status

## vignette

## correct_answer

## answer_a
Net -ve charge

## explanation_a
Correct. Raising the pH above an amino acid's isoelectric point means the solution is more alkaline than the pH at which the molecule is neutral overall, which deprotonates the carboxyl and/or amino groups further and leaves the amino acid carrying a net negative charge. This is the mirror image of what happens below the isoelectric point, where the amino acid instead carries a net positive charge. A net-negative amino acid in an electric field migrates toward the anode (positive electrode), the opposite direction from its behaviour below the isoelectric point.

## answer_b
Net +ve charge

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A net positive charge is what an amino acid carries at a pH below its isoelectric point, where the solution is more acidic and the amino group stays protonated while the carboxyl group is less ionised. Above the isoelectric point the charge balance shifts the other way, toward net negative.

## answer_c
Migrate toward cathode

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Migration toward the cathode (negative electrode) is what a net positively charged amino acid does, which happens below the isoelectric point, not above it. Above the isoelectric point the net charge is negative, so migration is toward the anode instead.

## answer_d
Maximum precipitability

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Maximum precipitability occurs exactly at the isoelectric point itself, where the amino acid carries zero net charge and the electrostatic repulsion that normally keeps molecules in solution is at its weakest. Above the isoelectric point the molecule again carries a net charge, which increases its solubility rather than its precipitability.

The most important non-covalent bond that drives protein folding

## question
The most important non-covalent bond that will drive proteins folding.

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Hydrogen bond

## explanation_a
Incorrect as "most important." Hydrogen bonds do contribute to tertiary folding, but they are only one of several noncovalent forces involved, and they are not the dominant driving force that first collapses an unfolded chain into a compact globular shape.

## answer_b
Van der Waals forces

## explanation_b
Incorrect as "most important." Van der Waals forces are real but comparatively weak interactions that help fine-tune the tight packing of already-folded side chains; they supplement folding rather than initiating or dominating it.

## answer_c
Hydrophobic interaction

## explanation_c
Correct. The hydrophobic interaction — the tendency of nonpolar side chains to cluster away from surrounding water and bury themselves in the protein's interior — is considered the principal driving force of protein folding, because it is what first collapses an unfolded chain into a compact globular shape before the finer noncovalent contacts (hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, Van der Waals forces) lock the fold into place. This is why nonpolar residues like phenylalanine and isoleucine are typically found buried in a soluble protein's interior. Without this hydrophobic collapse, the other, weaker noncovalent forces would have no compact structure to stabilise.

## answer_d
Electrostatic interaction

## explanation_d
Incorrect as "most important." Electrostatic (ionic) bonds between oppositely charged side chains contribute to tertiary structure but are fewer in number and weaker in overall effect than the hydrophobic collapse that dominates the folding process.

## question
The most important non-covalent bond in secondary structure is

## subject

## vignette

## correct_answer

## answer_a
Hydrogen bond

## explanation_a
Correct. Secondary structure — the alpha helix and the beta-pleated sheet — is held together almost entirely by hydrogen bonds between the backbone NH and C=O groups of different peptide bonds, repeating regularly along or across the chain. This is what makes hydrogen bonding the dominant noncovalent force at this level of structure, distinct from tertiary folding, which additionally relies on hydrophobic and ionic interactions. Disrupting these backbone hydrogen bonds is exactly what unravels an alpha helix or beta sheet.

## answer_b
Van der Waals forces

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Van der Waals forces are weak, short-range interactions that play a minor supporting role in the tighter packing of tertiary structure; they are not the dominant force organising the regular, repeating hydrogen-bonded patterns of secondary structure.

## answer_c
Hydrophobic interaction

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The hydrophobic interaction is the dominant force in tertiary folding, where it buries nonpolar side chains in a protein's interior, but secondary structure is defined by backbone hydrogen bonding, not by side-chain burial.

## answer_d
Electrostatic interaction

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Electrostatic (ionic) bonds between charged side chains contribute to tertiary and quaternary structure, but the regular, repeating pattern that defines an alpha helix or beta sheet is built from backbone hydrogen bonds, not side-chain charge interactions.
