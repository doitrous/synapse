## title
Which drug is used as rescue therapy for sudden off-akinesia in Parkinson disease?

## question
A patient with advanced Parkinson disease develops a sudden, disabling "off" episode of akinesia despite regular oral levodopa. Which drug, given by subcutaneous injection, is used as rapid rescue therapy?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Apomorphine

## explanation_a
Correct. Apomorphine is a potent dopamine agonist given by subcutaneous injection; its rapid onset of action within minutes restores mobility during a sudden off-akinesia episode until the next dose of oral medication takes effect, making it the standard rescue therapy for this scenario. This rapid parenteral rescue role is a well-established exam point distinguishing apomorphine from the slower-onset oral antiparkinsonian agents used for maintenance therapy.

## answer_b
Amantadine

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Amantadine is an oral antiparkinsonian agent used for maintenance therapy or to reduce levodopa-induced dyskinesia, not as a rapid parenteral rescue drug for acute off-episodes.

## answer_c
Benztropine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Benztropine is an oral anticholinergic used mainly for drug-induced extrapyramidal tremor, not for rescuing an off-akinesia episode.

## answer_d
Selegiline

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Selegiline is an oral monoamine oxidase-B inhibitor used for maintenance therapy in early Parkinson disease, with a slow onset unsuitable for acute rescue.

## title
Buprenorphine acts at the mu-opioid receptor as which type of ligand?

## question
Buprenorphine is used both as an analgesic and in opioid substitution therapy. Which best describes its action at the mu-opioid receptor?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Partial agonist

## explanation_a
Correct. Buprenorphine is a partial agonist at the mu-opioid receptor, meaning it only partially activates the receptor even at high doses, producing a ceiling effect on analgesia and respiratory depression and giving it a wider safety margin than full agonists. This partial-agonist ceiling effect is the pharmacological basis for buprenorphine's use in office-based opioid substitution therapy, where its safety margin in overdose is clinically valuable.

## answer_b
Full agonist

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Morphine and methadone are full mu-agonists; buprenorphine's partial agonism, with its ceiling effect, is what distinguishes it from these drugs.

## answer_c
Competitive antagonist

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Naloxone and naltrexone are competitive mu-receptor antagonists; buprenorphine still activates the receptor, distinguishing it from a pure antagonist.

## answer_d
Inverse agonist

## explanation_d
Incorrect. An inverse agonist would reduce receptor activity below its baseline (constitutive) level; buprenorphine instead activates the receptor, just incompletely.

## question
A patient started on amantadine for Parkinson disease develops a mottled, lace-like reddish-blue skin discoloration of the legs. Which adverse effect is this?

A

## answer_a
Livedo reticularis

## explanation_a
Correct. Livedo reticularis, a mottled lace-like reddish-blue skin discoloration usually of the lower limbs, is a characteristic and distinctive adverse effect of amantadine, thought to relate to local catecholamine release affecting cutaneous vessels, and is generally reversible on stopping the drug. Orofacial dyskinesia

Postural hypotension

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Postural hypotension is more classically associated with levodopa or dopamine agonists, not the distinctive dermatological effect of amantadine.

## answer_d
Peripheral neuropathy

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Peripheral neuropathy is not amantadine's characteristic adverse effect; its distinctive finding is the dermatological livedo reticularis.

## title
In which layer of the cerebellar cortex do mossy fibres synapse on granule cells?

## question
Mossy fibres are the major afferent input to the cerebellar cortex. In which of the cerebellar cortex's three layers do they synapse on granule cell dendrites?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The granular layer

## explanation_a
Correct. Mossy fibres terminate in the innermost layer of the cerebellar cortex, the granular layer, where they synapse on the dendrites of granule cells to form characteristic glomeruli; they are not found in the molecular layer. This granular-layer synapse is the first stage of cerebellar cortical processing, upstream of the parallel-fibre input mossy fibres indirectly provide to Purkinje cells.

## answer_b
The molecular layer

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The molecular layer is where granule cell axons (as parallel fibres) synapse on Purkinje cell dendrites, along with climbing fibre synapses on Purkinje cells — mossy fibres themselves do not reach this layer.

## answer_c
The Purkinje cell layer

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The Purkinje cell layer is a single-cell-thick layer of Purkinje cell bodies between the molecular and granular layers; mossy fibres synapse on granule cells in the granular layer, not directly on Purkinje cell bodies.

## answer_d
The white matter

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The cerebellar white matter lies deep to the three-layered cortex and contains axons, not the mossy fibre-granule cell synapse itself.

## title
Stimulation of the basal ganglia's indirect pathway has what effect on the subthalamic nucleus?

## question
Stimulation of the striatal neurons of the basal ganglia's indirect pathway has what net effect on the subthalamic nucleus?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Disinhibition (increased activity)

## explanation_a
Correct. Indirect-pathway striatal neurons inhibit the external globus pallidus (GPe), which normally tonically inhibits the subthalamic nucleus; inhibiting that inhibitory GPe drive disinhibits the subthalamic nucleus, increasing its activity. This disinhibition, in turn, increases excitatory drive from the subthalamic nucleus onto the internal globus pallidus, net-suppressing movement via the indirect pathway.

## answer_b
Direct excitation via glutamate

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The striatum does not project directly (via glutamate) to the subthalamic nucleus in the indirect pathway; its effect is indirect, via first inhibiting the GPe.

## answer_c
No change in activity

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The subthalamic nucleus's activity does change — it increases, because the indirect pathway removes the GPe's inhibitory restraint on it.

## answer_d
Direct inhibition via GABA

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The striatum's GABAergic projection in this pathway targets the GPe, not the subthalamic nucleus directly, and the net effect on the subthalamic nucleus is disinhibition (increased, not decreased, activity).

## title
Which condition is a recognised cause of extracranial headache?

## question
Which of the following is classified as an extracranial (rather than intracranial) cause of headache?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Otitis media

## explanation_a
Correct. Otitis media, inflammation of the middle ear, is a classic extracranial cause of headache: pain from the inflamed, pressurised middle ear is perceived as headache through the ear's sensory innervation, without any intracranial mechanism. Recognising extracranial causes such as this is important clinically, since they are treated by addressing the local structure rather than by intracranial headache management.

## answer_b
Meningeal irritation

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Meningeal irritation is an intracranial cause of headache, arising from pain-sensitive structures inside the skull, not an extracranial one.

## answer_c
Decreased CSF pressure

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Decreased CSF pressure (as in a post-lumbar-puncture headache) is an intracranial cause of headache, related to traction on intracranial pain-sensitive structures, not an extracranial cause.

## answer_d
Cerebral vasodilation

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Cerebral vasodilation (as in a migraine attack) is an intracranial vascular cause of headache, not an extracranial one.
