## title
Correcting acidosis helps treat hyperkalemia

## question
Which of the following interventions helps correct hyperkalemia by promoting a shift of potassium into cells, in addition to its own effect on acid–base status?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Correction of metabolic acidosis (e.g., with sodium bicarbonate)

## explanation_a
Correct. Acidosis drives potassium out of cells as hydrogen ions move intracellularly and are buffered partly by an exchange for intracellular potassium; correcting the acidosis, for example with sodium bicarbonate, reverses this shift and drives potassium back into cells, lowering the serum potassium concentration. This is one of the standard rapid, shift-based measures used in hyperkalemia management, alongside insulin-plus-glucose and beta-2 agonists, all of which act by moving potassium intracellularly rather than removing it from the body. Correcting acidosis is particularly useful when a patient with hyperkalemia is also acidotic, since it treats both derangements together.

## answer_b
Induction of metabolic alkalosis with a loop diuretic alone

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A loop diuretic used alone lowers potassium mainly by increasing distal renal potassium secretion and elimination, a different mechanism from acid-base correction, and is not the intervention described here.

## answer_c
Administration of a potassium-sparing diuretic

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Potassium-sparing diuretics reduce renal potassium excretion and would worsen, not treat, hyperkalemia.

## answer_d
Correction of respiratory alkalosis with sedation

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Correcting a respiratory alkalosis would, if anything, tend to raise serum potassium rather than lower it, since alkalosis itself lowers serum potassium and reversing it removes that protective shift; this option does not describe a recognized treatment for hyperkalemia.

## title
Disodium phosphate buffers distal tubular titratable acidity

## question
Which of the following buffers is responsible for most of the titratable acid excreted by the distal convoluted tubule?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Disodium hydrogen phosphate (Na2HPO4)

## explanation_a
Correct. Titratable acid refers to filtered urinary buffer, chiefly disodium hydrogen phosphate (Na2HPO4), that is converted to its acid form (NaH2PO4) by combining with hydrogen ions secreted in the distal tubule; the amount of base needed to titrate urine back to plasma pH defines titratable acidity. Phosphate buffers effectively in this role because its pKa (about 6.8) lies close to the pH range of tubular fluid as urine becomes progressively more acidic along the nephron. Titratable acid excretion, together with ammonium excretion, makes up the kidney's total net acid excretion.

## answer_b
Bicarbonate (HCO3-)

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Bicarbonate is almost entirely reabsorbed, chiefly in the proximal tubule, and does not appear in significant amounts in the final urine to act as a distal titratable acid buffer.

## answer_c
Ammonia (NH3)

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Ammonia buffers secreted hydrogen ions as ammonium (NH4+), but ammonium excretion is accounted for separately from titratable acidity, which specifically refers to filtered buffers such as phosphate.

## answer_d
Plasma albumin

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Albumin is not filtered by the normal glomerulus in significant amounts and does not function as a urinary buffer for titratable acid excretion.

## title
Anti-DNase B is more specific after streptococcal skin infection

## question
In a patient with suspected acute post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis following a preceding streptococcal skin infection (impetigo) rather than pharyngitis, which serologic test is more specific for confirming recent streptococcal infection?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Anti-DNase B titer

## explanation_a
Correct. Anti-DNase B (anti-deoxyribonuclease B) titers rise reliably after both streptococcal pharyngitis and streptococcal skin infections, whereas the antistreptolysin O (ASO) response is characteristically blunted after skin infections because skin lipids inhibit streptolysin O; anti-DNase B is therefore the more specific and sensitive serologic marker to confirm a preceding streptococcal infection when the antecedent infection was cutaneous (impetigo), as in post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis following skin infection. This distinction is a classic microbiology and nephrology teaching point used to choose the right serologic test depending on the site of the antecedent infection. Combining anti-DNase B with ASO increases overall sensitivity for confirming recent streptococcal infection regardless of site.

## answer_b
Antistreptolysin O (ASO) titer

## explanation_b
Incorrect. ASO titers rise reliably after streptococcal pharyngitis but are characteristically blunted after streptococcal skin infections, making ASO a less reliable marker than anti-DNase B when the antecedent infection was cutaneous.

## answer_c
C3 complement level

## explanation_c
Incorrect. C3 complement is typically low in acute post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis and supports the diagnosis of an immune-complex-mediated process, but it is a marker of disease activity, not a specific serologic test for confirming antecedent streptococcal infection.

## answer_d
Antinuclear antibody (ANA) titer

## explanation_d
Incorrect. ANA is used to screen for lupus and other autoimmune connective tissue diseases, not to confirm a preceding streptococcal infection.

Second part of duodenum is anterior at the right kidney hilum

## question
Which of the following structures lies anterior to the hilum of the right kidney?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Second part of the duodenum

## explanation_a
Correct. The second (descending) part of the duodenum lies directly anterior to the hilum and medial border of the right kidney, an important relation in retroperitoneal surgery and in explaining how right renal pathology, such as a perinephric abscess, can occasionally involve or be confused with duodenal pathology. Tail of the pancreas

## answer_c
Spleen

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The spleen is an anterior-lateral relation of the left kidney via the splenorenal ligament, not a relation of the right kidney.

## answer_d
Stomach

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The stomach is an anterior relation of the left kidney's upper pole, not a relation of the right kidney.

## title
Chronic bacterial prostatitis needs about 6 weeks of antibiotics

## question
Regarding treatment of chronic bacterial prostatitis, which of the following is true?

## subject
androl

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
It typically requires a prolonged antibiotic course of approximately 6 weeks

## explanation_a
Correct. Chronic bacterial prostatitis requires a substantially longer antibiotic course than simple cystitis or acute prostatitis, typically on the order of 4 to 6 weeks (commonly cited as approximately 6 weeks), using an agent such as a fluoroquinolone that achieves adequate penetration into prostatic tissue. This prolonged course is needed because relatively poor antibiotic penetration into the prostate and the tendency for bacteria to persist in prostatic ducts or calculi make short courses prone to relapse. Fluoroquinolones such as ciprofloxacin are preferred because their lipophilicity gives them better prostatic tissue penetration than agents like beta-lactams.

## answer_b
It is treated with a single 3-day course of antibiotics, as for uncomplicated cystitis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A brief 3-day course is appropriate for uncomplicated cystitis, not chronic bacterial prostatitis, which requires weeks of therapy to achieve adequate prostatic tissue levels and prevent relapse.

## answer_c
Antibiotics are ineffective because no antimicrobial can penetrate the prostate

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Lipophilic antibiotics, particularly fluoroquinolones, do penetrate prostatic tissue adequately and are the mainstay of treatment; the prostate is not universally impermeable to antimicrobials.

## answer_d
It is managed with antifungal therapy rather than antibiotics

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Chronic bacterial prostatitis is a bacterial infection and is treated with antibiotics, not antifungal agents.

## title
L-carnitine performs intracellular fatty-acid transport

## question
Which of the following best describes the physiological role of L-carnitine?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
It transports long-chain fatty acyl groups across the inner mitochondrial membrane for beta-oxidation

## explanation_a
Correct. L-carnitine's principal physiological role is intracellular lipid transport: it shuttles long-chain fatty acyl-CoA groups from the cytosol across the otherwise-impermeable inner mitochondrial membrane, via the carnitine acyltransferase system (CPT-I and CPT-II), so that they can undergo beta-oxidation in the mitochondrial matrix. Without this carnitine-mediated transport step, cells cannot access long-chain fatty acids as an energy substrate, which is why carnitine deficiency produces muscle weakness and fasting hypoglycemia when fatty-acid oxidation is impaired. This transport function is distinct from, and does not itself involve, transporting glucose, calcium, or glycogen-related substrates.

## answer_b
It transports glucose across the plasma membrane into skeletal muscle

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Glucose transport into skeletal muscle is mediated by glucose transporters (e.g., GLUT4), not by carnitine, whose role is specifically fatty acid transport.

## answer_c
It serves as the primary intracellular calcium-binding protein in cardiac muscle

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The principal intracellular calcium-binding protein in cardiac muscle is troponin C, not carnitine.

## answer_d
It is the rate-limiting cofactor for glycogen synthase

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Glycogen synthase activity is regulated by its phosphorylation state and allosteric effectors such as glucose-6-phosphate, not by carnitine, which has no role in glycogen metabolism.

## title
Tyrosine becomes an essential amino acid in PKU

## question
In a patient with phenylketonuria (PKU), which amino acid becomes essential (i.e., must be obtained from the diet) because of the underlying enzyme defect?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Tyrosine

## explanation_a
Correct. PKU results from deficiency of phenylalanine hydroxylase (or, less commonly, its cofactor tetrahydrobiopterin), the enzyme that normally converts phenylalanine to tyrosine; because this conversion pathway is blocked, tyrosine can no longer be synthesized endogenously from phenylalanine in PKU patients and becomes a conditionally essential amino acid that must be supplied in the diet. This is why dietary management of PKU restricts phenylalanine intake while ensuring adequate tyrosine intake, since tyrosine is also the precursor for catecholamines, melanin and thyroid hormone. Untreated PKU therefore risks deficiency of tyrosine-dependent products in addition to the toxic accumulation of phenylalanine itself.

## answer_b
Alanine

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Alanine is a nonessential amino acid synthesized by transamination of pyruvate and is unrelated to the phenylalanine hydroxylase pathway affected in PKU.

## answer_c
Glutamate

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Glutamate is a nonessential amino acid central to nitrogen metabolism and is not rendered essential by the enzyme defect in PKU.

## answer_d
Aspartate

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Aspartate is a nonessential amino acid synthesized by transamination of oxaloacetate and is not rendered essential by the enzyme defect in PKU.

## title
Apical pinocytic vesicles are characteristic of the PCT

## question
Which of the following ultrastructural features is characteristic of proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) epithelial cells?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Numerous apical pinocytic (endocytic) vesicles

## explanation_a
Correct. Proximal convoluted tubule cells are characterized by a well-developed apical brush border of microvilli together with numerous apical pinocytic (endocytic) vesicles, which mediate receptor-mediated endocytosis of small filtered proteins and other macromolecules that escape the glomerular filtration barrier; these vesicles fuse with lysosomes for intracellular degradation, reclaiming filtered protein that would otherwise be lost in urine. Along with abundant basal mitochondria supporting active transport and extensive basolateral membrane infoldings, apical pinocytic vesicles are a defining ultrastructural feature that distinguishes PCT cells on electron microscopy. This ultrastructure directly supports the PCT's role as the nephron segment responsible for bulk reabsorption of filtered solutes and reclamation of filtered protein.

## answer_b
A single, non-motile apical cilium in every cell, as the defining feature

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A single non-motile primary cilium is described generally in several tubular epithelial cell types (relevant to polycystic kidney disease biology), but it is not the defining feature distinguishing PCT cells; PCT cells are specifically defined by their brush border and pinocytic vesicles.

## answer_c
Extensive apical microvilli forming true stereocilia

## explanation_c
Incorrect. True stereocilia (long, non-motile microvillus-like projections) are a feature of the epididymal epithelium, not the renal proximal tubule, whose apical projections are conventional brush-border microvilli.

## answer_d
A stratified, multi-layered epithelium

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The proximal convoluted tubule is lined by a simple cuboidal epithelium, not a stratified, multi-layered epithelium.

## title
Renal osteodystrophy follows decreased renal calcitriol production

## question
An elderly patient with chronic renal dysfunction presents with recurrent bone fractures. Which of the following best explains this presentation?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Decreased renal production of 1,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol (calcitriol)

## explanation_a
Correct. The kidney performs the final hydroxylation step, via renal 1-alpha-hydroxylase, that converts 25-hydroxyvitamin D to the active hormone 1,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol (calcitriol); in chronic renal dysfunction this activation step declines, producing calcitriol deficiency, reduced intestinal calcium absorption, secondary hyperparathyroidism, and a mineral bone disease (renal osteodystrophy) that predisposes to recurrent, sometimes minimal-trauma, fractures. This mechanism, distinct from primary nutritional vitamin D deficiency, is why renal osteodystrophy is a recognized skeletal complication of chronic kidney disease in older patients. Recognizing renal dysfunction as the driver, rather than isolated dietary deficiency, determines the correct management, which targets both the renal disease and the resulting secondary hyperparathyroidism.

## answer_b
Excess renal production of 1,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Chronic renal dysfunction reduces, rather than increases, active vitamin D production, because it impairs the kidney's 1-alpha-hydroxylase activity.

## answer_c
Isolated vitamin B12 deficiency

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Isolated vitamin B12 deficiency causes megaloblastic anemia and neurological symptoms, not the bone fragility syndrome linked to renal dysfunction described here.

## answer_d
Increased renal erythropoietin production

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Chronic renal dysfunction reduces, rather than increases, erythropoietin production, and reduced erythropoietin does not explain a bone-fracture presentation in any case.

## title
Erythropoietin deficiency causes the anemia of renal disease

## question
A patient with chronic kidney disease develops anemia. Deficiency of which renally produced hormone best explains this finding?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Erythropoietin

## explanation_a
Correct. Erythropoietin, secreted mainly by peritubular interstitial fibroblast-like cells in the renal cortex in response to hypoxia, stimulates erythroid progenitor proliferation and differentiation in the bone marrow; in chronic kidney disease, progressive loss of functioning renal parenchyma reduces erythropoietin production, producing a normocytic, normochromic anemia (the anemia of chronic kidney disease) that is a recognized and clinically important complication. This anemia is corrected in practice by administering recombinant erythropoiesis-stimulating agents to replace the deficient hormone. The mechanism is distinct from anemias caused by iron, B12, or folate deficiency, though those can coexist and should also be excluded or corrected.

## answer_b
Renin

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Renin deficiency affects blood pressure and sodium/volume regulation via the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, not red cell production, so it does not explain the anemia of chronic kidney disease.

## answer_c
Aldosterone

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Aldosterone regulates sodium and potassium handling, not erythropoiesis, so its deficiency does not explain anemia.

## answer_d
Calcitonin

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Calcitonin, from thyroid parafollicular cells rather than the kidney, regulates calcium homeostasis, not erythropoiesis.

## title
Median umbilical ligament attaches to the bladder apex

## question
Which of the following structures is attached to the apex of the urinary bladder?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Median umbilical ligament

## explanation_a
Correct. The median umbilical ligament is the fibrous remnant of the fetal urachus (allantoic duct), running in the midline from the apex of the urinary bladder up to the umbilicus, and it raises the median umbilical fold on the internal aspect of the anterior abdominal wall. This single, midline ligament is a key anatomical landmark distinguishing it from the paired medial umbilical ligaments (remnants of the umbilical arteries), which do not attach to the bladder apex the same way. Persistence of a patent urachus along this ligament's course can produce a urachal fistula, cyst, or sinus, clinically relevant complications of incomplete urachal obliteration.

## answer_b
Medial umbilical ligament

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The medial umbilical ligaments are paired fibrous remnants of the fetal umbilical arteries running from the internal iliac arteries toward the umbilicus; they raise the medial umbilical folds and do not attach specifically to the bladder apex the way the single median umbilical ligament does.

## answer_c
Lateral umbilical fold (containing the inferior epigastric vessels)

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The lateral umbilical folds overlie the inferior epigastric vessels, not an obliterated fetal structure, and are unrelated to the apex of the bladder.

## answer_d
Round ligament of the uterus

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The round ligament of the uterus runs from the uterus through the inguinal canal to the labium majus; it is unrelated to the apex of the urinary bladder.
