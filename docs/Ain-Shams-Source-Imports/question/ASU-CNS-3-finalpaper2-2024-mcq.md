## title
Which thalamic nucleus receives the trigeminal lemniscus?

## question
The trigeminal lemniscus (trigeminothalamic pathway), carrying pain, temperature and touch from the face, terminates in which thalamic nucleus?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The ventral posteromedial (VPM) nucleus

## explanation_a
Correct. The trigeminal lemniscus carries pain, temperature and touch from the face and oral cavity, relayed through the principal sensory and spinal trigeminal nuclei, and terminates in the ventral posteromedial (VPM) nucleus of the thalamus. VPM is the facial counterpart of VPL, which instead receives body sensation via the medial lemniscus and spinothalamic tract. From VPM, third-order neurons project through the internal capsule to the face area of the postcentral gyrus.

## answer_b
The ventral posterolateral (VPL) nucleus

## explanation_b
Incorrect. VPL receives the medial lemniscus and spinothalamic tract, carrying sensation from the body, not the face.

## answer_c
The medial geniculate body

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The medial geniculate body is the thalamic auditory relay, not a somatosensory nucleus.

## answer_d
The pulvinar

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The pulvinar is a large posterior thalamic nucleus involved in visual attention and association, not primary trigeminal sensory relay.

## title
What is the physiological function of the Papez circuit?

## question
What is the principal physiological function of the Papez circuit (hippocampus-fornix-mammillary bodies-anterior thalamic nucleus-cingulate gyrus)?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Prolonging and consciously elaborating an ongoing emotional experience

## explanation_a
Correct. The Papez circuit is a closed limbic loop whose principal role is to sustain, elaborate and give lasting, consciously felt quality to an emotional state once it has begun, linking it to memory. Lesions of circuit components, such as mammillary body damage in Korsakoff syndrome, disrupt this link between emotion and memory rather than abolishing emotional reactivity outright. This distinguishes the circuit from structures that initiate the emotional response itself.

## answer_b
Initiating the primary fear response to a threatening stimulus

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Initiating fear responses is primarily an amygdala function, a complementary but distinct limbic structure.

## answer_c
Generating the rhythm of slow-wave sleep

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Slow-wave sleep rhythm generation involves thalamocortical and brainstem circuits, not the Papez circuit.

## answer_d
Coordinating voluntary limb movement

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Voluntary limb movement is coordinated by motor cortex, basal ganglia and cerebellar circuits, unrelated to the Papez circuit's limbic role.

## subject

## answer_d
The midbrain tectum

Incorrect. The midbrain tectum develops from the mesencephalon, not the metencephalon's rhombic lip.

## title
Recognition of faces is a function of which cerebral hemisphere?

## question
Recognition of familiar faces is chiefly a function of which cerebral hemisphere?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The representational (non-dominant) hemisphere

## explanation_a
Correct. While the dominant hemisphere specialises in language, calculation and sequential/analytic tasks, the non-dominant, 'representational' hemisphere specialises in visuospatial and holistic processing, including facial recognition, spatial orientation and musical/emotional prosody. Damage to non-dominant parietal and temporo-occipital association cortex characteristically produces prosopagnosia, the inability to recognise familiar faces, with language remaining intact. This lateralisation is a strong statistical bias, well documented in right-handed individuals whose left hemisphere is dominant for language.

## answer_b
The dominant (language) hemisphere

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The dominant hemisphere specialises in language and sequential/analytic processing; its damage produces aphasia, not the selective loss of facial recognition (prosopagnosia).

## answer_c
Both hemispheres equally, with no lateralisation

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Facial recognition shows clear hemispheric lateralisation to the non-dominant side rather than being distributed equally.

## answer_d
The cerebellar hemisphere ipsilateral to the face being viewed

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The cerebellum contributes to motor coordination and some cognitive functions, but facial recognition is a cerebral hemispheric (association cortex) function, not a cerebellar one.

## title
What pathway underlies naming a seen object?

## question
Naming an object that has just been seen depends on a pathway that runs, in order, through which structures?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Visual cortex, Wernicke's area, Broca's area, primary motor cortex

## explanation_a
Correct. The primary visual cortex passes the visual percept, via association areas and the angular gyrus, to Wernicke's area, where the image is matched to its corresponding word. That output travels via the arcuate fasciculus to Broca's area, which programs the sequence of articulatory movements, and finally to the primary motor cortex, which executes lip, tongue and laryngeal movements via the corticobulbar tract. A lesion anywhere along this chain produces a distinct, localisable deficit, from visual object agnosia to Wernicke's or Broca's aphasia to dysarthria.

## answer_b
Visual cortex, Broca's area, Wernicke's area, primary motor cortex

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This reverses Wernicke's and Broca's areas; word-formulation/comprehension (Wernicke's) must precede motor speech programming (Broca's), connected by the arcuate fasciculus.

## answer_c
Primary motor cortex, Broca's area, Wernicke's area, visual cortex

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This reverses the entire pathway; the sequence begins with sensory input (visual cortex) and ends with motor output (primary motor cortex), not the other way round.

## answer_d
Visual cortex, primary motor cortex, Wernicke's area, Broca's area

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This places motor execution before the language areas that formulate the word to be spoken, which would produce movement without linguistic content.

## title
What is the antidote for midazolam overdose?

## question
Which drug is the specific pharmacological antidote for midazolam (benzodiazepine) overdose?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Flumazenil

## explanation_a
Correct. Flumazenil is a competitive antagonist at the benzodiazepine binding site of the GABA-A receptor: it displaces midazolam and other benzodiazepines without activating the receptor itself, rapidly reversing sedation and respiratory depression. Because flumazenil's half-life is shorter than most benzodiazepines', re-sedation can occur once it wears off, so patients require monitoring after reversal. Flumazenil can also precipitate seizures in benzodiazepine-dependent patients, so it must be used cautiously.

## answer_b
Naloxone

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Naloxone is the antidote for opioid overdose, acting at mu-opioid receptors, not the benzodiazepine (GABA-A) receptor.

## answer_c
Physostigmine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Physostigmine is used for anticholinergic toxicity (acetylcholinesterase inhibition), unrelated to benzodiazepine reversal.

## answer_d
N-acetylcysteine

## explanation_d
Incorrect. N-acetylcysteine is the antidote for paracetamol (acetaminophen) overdose, not benzodiazepine overdose.

6 weeks

## title
Why is sodium bicarbonate added to local anaesthetic solution?

## question
Adding sodium bicarbonate to a local anaesthetic solution increases the drug's activity chiefly through which mechanism?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
It raises the solution's pH, increasing the unionised (lipid-soluble) fraction of the weak-base anaesthetic

## explanation_a
Correct. Commercial local anaesthetic solutions are formulated at an acidic pH for stability, leaving most of the drug in its ionised, poorly lipid-soluble form. Adding sodium bicarbonate raises the pH towards physiological, shifting more of the weak-base anaesthetic into its unionised, lipid-soluble form, which diffuses through the nerve sheath and axonal membrane faster to reach its intracellular binding site on the voltage-gated sodium channel. The practical result is a faster onset of anaesthesia and less injection discomfort, without changing the eventual depth or duration of the block.

## answer_b
It directly blocks the voltage-gated sodium channel at the axonal membrane

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The local anaesthetic itself, once it reaches its intracellular binding site, blocks the sodium channel; bicarbonate's role is to speed the drug's access to that site by altering ionisation, not to block the channel directly.

## answer_c
It constricts local blood vessels, prolonging the drug's residence time

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Bicarbonate does not have vasoconstrictive properties; vasoconstriction (e.g. from added adrenaline/epinephrine) is a separate additive used to prolong anaesthetic action by slowing vascular clearance.

## answer_d
It chelates calcium ions, reducing membrane threshold for depolarisation

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Calcium chelation is not the mechanism of bicarbonate's effect on local anaesthetics; its action is via pH-dependent shifting of drug ionisation.

## title
Why does local anaesthesia often fail in infected tissue?

## question
Local anaesthetic injection is often less effective in infected or repeatedly inflamed tissue chiefly because of which mechanism?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The increased tissue acidity shifts more of the weak-base anaesthetic into its ionised, poorly diffusible form

## explanation_a
Correct. Infected or chronically inflamed tissue has a lower (more acidic) extracellular pH than healthy tissue, from local metabolic byproducts, hypoxia and the inflammatory response. Because local anaesthetics are weak bases whose proportion of unionised, lipid-soluble drug depends on pH relative to their pKa, this acidic environment shifts more drug into its ionised, poorly diffusible form, so less anaesthetic penetrates the nerve sheath to reach its intra-axonal site of action. This is the pharmacological basis for local anaesthesia commonly failing when injected into an infected or abscessed area.

## answer_b
Bacterial enzymes directly degrade the anaesthetic molecule

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Bacterial enzymatic degradation of the anaesthetic molecule is not the established mechanism taught for this clinical failure; the ionisation-shift mechanism from tissue acidity is.

## answer_c
Inflammatory oedema physically prevents the needle from reaching the nerve

## explanation_c
Incorrect. While inflammatory swelling can complicate injection technique, the pharmacologically primary reason taught for reduced efficacy is the pH-driven ionisation shift, not simple mechanical obstruction.

## answer_d
Fever inactivates the anaesthetic through a temperature-dependent conformational change

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Fever-related temperature change is not the mechanism responsible for reduced local anaesthetic efficacy in infected tissue; the acidic extracellular pH is.

## title
How is lithium toxicity managed?

## question
Management of lithium toxicity includes stopping lithium, increasing fluid intake, and diuresis while avoiding which class of diuretic?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Thiazide diuretics

## explanation_a
Correct. Management of lithium toxicity begins with immediately stopping lithium and increasing fluid intake to promote renal clearance, since lithium is excreted unchanged by the kidney and reabsorbed alongside sodium in the proximal tubule. Thiazide diuretics must specifically be avoided because they promote sodium loss that paradoxically increases proximal lithium reabsorption, worsening toxicity. In severe toxicity, haemodialysis is used to remove lithium directly, since it is small and not protein-bound.

## answer_b
Loop diuretics

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Loop diuretics are not the class specifically implicated in worsening lithium retention; thiazides are the classic culprit taught for this interaction.

## answer_c
Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors are not the diuretic class specifically associated with worsening lithium toxicity through proximal tubular reabsorption.

## answer_d
Osmotic diuretics

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Osmotic diuretics are not the class taught to worsen lithium retention; the mechanism specifically implicates thiazide-induced proximal sodium/lithium co-reabsorption.

## title
What are two advantages of atypical over typical antipsychotics?

## question
Compared with typical antipsychotics, atypical antipsychotics offer which two advantages?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Fewer extrapyramidal side effects and greater efficacy against negative symptoms

## explanation_a
Correct. Typical antipsychotics act predominantly through high-affinity D2 dopamine receptor antagonism, effectively controlling positive symptoms but, by strongly blocking D2 receptors in the nigrostriatal pathway, producing prominent extrapyramidal side effects and little benefit for negative symptoms. Atypical antipsychotics combine lower or more transient D2 antagonism with serotonin 5-HT2A antagonism, producing fewer extrapyramidal side effects at therapeutic doses and, through this same interaction, more meaningful efficacy against negative symptoms (flat affect, avolition, social withdrawal). These two advantages are the principal reasons atypical antipsychotics are now preferred as first-line therapy in most guidelines.

## answer_b
Lower risk of metabolic side effects and lower cost

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Atypical antipsychotics generally carry a higher, not lower, risk of metabolic side effects (weight gain, dyslipidaemia, new-onset diabetes) than typical agents, and are not generally lower in cost.

## answer_c
Faster onset of antipsychotic effect and no need for blood monitoring

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Atypical antipsychotics do not have a faster onset of action than typical agents, and several (e.g. clozapine) require intensive blood monitoring, not less.

## answer_d
Complete absence of any dopaminergic receptor blockade

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Atypical antipsychotics still block dopamine D2 receptors, just with lower or more transient affinity combined with serotonin antagonism; they do not lack dopaminergic blockade entirely.

## title
What do the anterior cerebral artery's central branches supply?

## question
The anterior cerebral artery's anteromedial central (perforating) branches supply which structures?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The anterior caudate and lentiform nuclei, and the genu/inferior anterior limb of the internal capsule

## explanation_a
Correct. The anterior cerebral artery gives off small anteromedial central (perforating) branches that pierce the anterior perforated substance to supply the anterior parts of the caudate and lentiform nuclei, and the genu together with the lower half of the anterior limb of the internal capsule. The largest of these, the recurrent artery of Heubner, when occluded classically produces contralateral face/arm weakness from internal capsule involvement, sometimes with subtle behavioural change from caudate involvement. These central branches are distinct from the ACA's cortical branches, which instead supply the medial hemispheric surface.

## answer_b
The medial surface of the occipital lobe

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The medial occipital lobe is supplied by the posterior cerebral artery, not the ACA's central branches (and lies outside the ACA's cortical territory too).

## answer_c
The lateral geniculate body and optic tract

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The lateral geniculate body and optic tract are supplied chiefly by branches of the posterior cerebral and anterior choroidal arteries, not the ACA's central branches.

## answer_d
The dentate nucleus and superior cerebellar peduncle

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The dentate nucleus and superior cerebellar peduncle are posterior fossa structures supplied by the superior cerebellar artery, unrelated to the ACA.

## title
What is the cortical territory of the anterior cerebral artery?

## question
The anterior cerebral artery's cortical branches supply which territory?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The medial hemispheric surface (except the occipital lobe), a superolateral strip, and part of the orbital surface

## explanation_a
Correct. The anterior cerebral artery's cortical branches supply almost the entire medial surface of the hemisphere from the frontal to the parietal lobe, sparing the occipital lobe (supplied instead by the posterior cerebral artery). These branches wrap over the superomedial margin to supply a roughly one-inch strip of the adjoining superolateral surface, and supply part of the orbital surface via medial orbital branches. This territory explains the classic ACA infarct picture: contralateral leg-predominant weakness and sensory loss, sparing the face and hand, which lie in the middle cerebral artery's lateral convexity territory.

## answer_b
The entire lateral (convexity) surface of the frontal and parietal lobes

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The entire lateral convexity of the frontal and parietal lobes is chiefly middle cerebral artery territory; the ACA supplies only a narrow superolateral strip of it, not the whole surface.

## answer_c
The occipital pole and inferomedial temporal lobe

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The occipital pole and inferomedial temporal lobe are posterior cerebral artery territory, outside the ACA's distribution.

## answer_d
The insula and superior temporal gyrus

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The insula and superior temporal gyrus are middle cerebral artery territory, not supplied by the ACA.
