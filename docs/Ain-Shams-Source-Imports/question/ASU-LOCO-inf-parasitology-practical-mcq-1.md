## title
In a myiasis fly larva's posterior spiracle plate, what is the arrowed rim structure called?

## question
In the shown figure, the arrowed structure refers to the:

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette
A parasitology practical specimen shows the posterior end of a fly larva collected during species-identification training, photographed under the dissecting microscope with one structure of the spiracle plate indicated by an arrow.

## format
single best answer

## derived_from
Printed question 1 of the ASU Locomotor Parasitology Practical MCQ paper (Dr Ayman Ibrahim, 2025).

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Peritrem.

## explanation_a
Correct. The peritreme is the sclerotised (chitinous) rim that runs around the entire margin of a myiasis fly larva's posterior spiracle plate, framing the button and the spiracular slits within it. Its shape and completeness are part of what a student checks first when comparing spiracle plates between species, before moving on to the slit pattern itself. In the arrowed image this ridge forms the outer border of the plate, which is what distinguishes it from the structures inside the border.

## answer_b
Button.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The button (also called the ecdysial scar or macula) is the small thickened structure sitting inside the peritreme, near the centre of the plate, not the rim itself -- picked by students who have the right general area but the wrong specific structure.

## answer_c
Longitudinal ridge.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. "Longitudinal ridge" is not a standard named structure of a fly larval spiracle plate; this distractor catches a student inventing plausible-sounding anatomy rather than naming the peritreme, button or slits by their correct terms.

## answer_d
Respiratory slit.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The respiratory (spiracular) slits are the narrow openings that pierce the plate itself, arranged in a species-characteristic pattern (straight, curved or sinuous) -- they sit inside the peritreme's border, not as the border itself.

## answer_e
Operculum.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. An operculum is a hinged lid-like structure seen on some parasite eggs (for example some trematode eggs), not a feature of a fly larva's spiracle plate -- picked by a student transferring vocabulary from a different part of the parasitology course.

## title
On an adult flea's thorax, what is the arrowed suture called?

## question
In the shown figure, the arrowed structure refers to the:

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

single best answer

## derived_from
Printed question 2 of the ASU Locomotor Parasitology Practical MCQ paper (Dr Ayman Ibrahim, 2025).

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Mesopleural suture.

## explanation_a
Correct. The mesopleural suture is a line dividing the lateral surface of the flea's mesothorax (the middle of the three thoracic segments), and its presence, position and shape are used alongside the combs as part of species-level identification of fleas. In the arrowed photograph it runs across the thoracic segment rather than along the head margin or the anterior thoracic edge, which is what separates it from the genal and pronotal combs.

## answer_b
Genal comb.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The genal comb is a row of spines along the lower margin of the head (the "cheek"), not a suture line on the thorax -- picked by a student who recognises this is a flea-identification feature but has placed it on the wrong body region.

## answer_c
Post occipital bristle.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A post-occipital bristle would sit at the back of the head capsule, not on the thoracic pleuron where the arrow points -- picked by a student who has confused a head landmark with a thoracic one.

## answer_d
Pronotal comb.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The pronotal comb sits along the posterior margin of the first thoracic segment (the pronotum), a different segment and a comb (row of spines) rather than a suture line -- the commonest confusion with the correct answer because both are thoracic flea-identification features.

## answer_e
Ocular bristle.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. An ocular bristle sits near the eye on the head, not on the thoracic pleuron -- picked by a student transferring a head-region term onto a thoracic structure.

## title
In a fly's sponging mouthparts, what is the arrowed labellar groove called?

## question
In the shown figure, the arrowed structure refers to the:

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette
A parasitology practical specimen shows the sponging mouthparts (labellum) of a fly, photographed under the microscope with one groove on the labellar surface indicated by an arrow.

## format
single best answer

## derived_from
Printed question 3 of the ASU Locomotor Parasitology Practical MCQ paper (Dr Ayman Ibrahim, 2025).

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Antenna.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The antenna is a paired sensory appendage on the head, well away from the labellum where the arrow points -- picked by a student who has not focused on the specific mouthpart structure being tested.

## answer_b
Pharynx.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The pharynx is an internal feeding tube behind the mouth opening, not a surface groove visible on the external labellar pads -- picked by a student who knows the fly feeds by suction but has named an internal rather than a surface structure.

## answer_c
Labium.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The labium is the whole lower-lip structure that carries the labellum at its tip, a larger and more proximal structure than the specific grooved feature the arrow indicates -- picked by a student naming the correct region one level too broad.

## answer_d
Maxillary palp.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The maxillary palps are separate sensory appendages near the base of the proboscis, not part of the labellar surface itself -- picked by a student confusing a sensory palp with the feeding-surface grooves.

## answer_e
Pseudotrachea.

## explanation_e
Correct. The pseudotracheae are the fine, branching grooves on the surface of a sponging fly's labellar lobes (as in Musca domestica) that draw up liquid food by capillary action, functioning like a sieve rather than a piercing structure. Their name reflects a superficial resemblance to the tracheal tubes of the fly's respiratory system, though they carry food, not air. Identifying the pseudotracheae on the labellum is how a sponging (non-biting) mouthpart is told apart on inspection from a piercing-sucking one.

## title
Which ectoparasite is shown in the practical photograph?

## question

## subject

## status

## format
single best answer

## derived_from
Printed question 4 of the ASU Locomotor Parasitology Practical MCQ paper (Dr Ayman Ibrahim, 2025).

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Cimex lectularis.

## explanation_a
Correct. Cimex lectularis (the common bed bug) is identified by a dorsoventrally flattened, broadly oval, wingless, reddish-brown body with a short beak-like proboscis folded beneath the head at rest. It is a nocturnal, non-permanent ectoparasite that hides in mattress seams and furniture by day rather than remaining on the host, unlike a louse. This whole-mount photograph shows exactly that flattened oval body plan, which is what separates it on sight from a flea's laterally flattened body or a louse's narrower, elongated one.

## answer_b
Xenopsylla cheopis.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Xenopsylla cheopis (the Oriental rat flea) is laterally flattened rather than dorsoventrally flattened, an adaptation for moving through fur, and has powerful jumping hind legs that Cimex lacks -- picked by a student who has correctly spotted "wingless ectoparasite" but not the plane of body flattening.

## answer_c
Musca domestica.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Musca domestica (the house fly) is a winged insect with sponging mouthparts, entirely unlike the wingless, piercing-mouthed bed bug shown here -- picked by a student guessing from the general category "insect" rather than the specific body plan.

## answer_d
Pediculus humanus.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Pediculus humanus (the body/head louse) is also wingless and blood-feeding, but its body is narrower and more elongated, and unlike the bed bug it lives permanently on the host or in clothing rather than retreating to a separate daytime harbourage -- the commonest confusion with the correct answer because both are wingless human ectoparasites.

## answer_e
Sarcoptes scabiei.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Sarcoptes scabiei (the scabies mite) is a microscopic, eight-legged arachnid that burrows into the skin, not a visible-to-the-naked-eye, six-legged insect like the bed bug shown here -- picked by a student who has confused a mite with an insect ectoparasite.

## title
On a hard tick, what is the arrowed dorsal shield called?

## question
In the shown figure, the arrowed structure refers to the:

## subject

## status

## owner

## vignette

## format
single best answer

## derived_from
Printed question 5 of the ASU Locomotor Parasitology Practical MCQ paper (Dr Ayman Ibrahim, 2025).

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Scutum.

## explanation_a
Correct. The scutum is the chitinous dorsal shield of a hard (ixodid) tick, small and confined to the area just behind the capitulum in the female (allowing her abdomen to distend hugely with a blood meal) and covering almost the whole dorsum in the male. Its presence at all is the single feature that separates a hard tick from a soft tick, which has no scutum. In the arrowed photograph it sits as a distinct plate immediately behind the mouthparts, which is what distinguishes it from the capitulum in front of it or the festoons at the body's posterior margin.

## answer_b
Capitulum.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The capitulum is the mouthparts-bearing "head" that projects forward from the body, a different and more anterior structure from the dorsal shield the arrow indicates -- picked by a student who has correctly identified a tick landmark but the wrong one.

## answer_c
Festoons.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Festoons are the rectangular cuticular divisions at the tick's posterior margin, not the anterior dorsal shield -- picked by a student who knows festoons are a hard-tick feature but has placed the arrow at the wrong end of the body.

## answer_d
Abdomen.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. "Abdomen" names the whole distensible body region behind the scutum, not the discrete plate the arrow indicates -- too broad a term for the specific structure being tested.

## answer_e
Thorax.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Ticks (like all arachnids) have a fused cephalothorax rather than a separate thorax as insects do, so "thorax" is not a correct term for any tick body region -- picked by a student transferring insect anatomy vocabulary onto an arachnid.

Sarcoptes scabiei (scabies mite).

A

Wohlfahrtia magnifica.

Correct. Bed sores in an immobile, bedridden patient -- living but compromised tissue, often left unattended for hours between turns -- are the classic clinical setting in which Wohlfahrtia myiasis is taught, distinguishing it from flies that only exploit tissue that is already dead. Calliphora species (blue-bottle blowfly).

Musca domestica (house fly).

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Simulium (blackfly).

## explanation_a
Correct. Onchocerciasis (river blindness) is transmitted by the bite of an infected Simulium blackfly, which breeds specifically in fast-flowing, well-oxygenated rivers and streams -- the reason the disease clusters along river valleys and gave the fly-bite exposure history its "near a fast-flowing river" detail. The painless subcutaneous nodules (onchocercomata) form around coiled adult worm pairs deposited by the vector, and the clinical disease itself (itching, skin changes, blindness) comes later from the microfilariae the female worm releases, not from the nodules themselves. Recognising Simulium as the one vector specific to Onchocerca volvulus, rather than any biting fly in general, is what the question tests.

## answer_b
Anopheles mosquito.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Anopheles transmits malaria (and some lymphatic filariasis), not Onchocerca volvulus -- picked by a student who defaults to "mosquito" for any insect-borne tropical disease without checking which vector is specific to this parasite.

## answer_c
Musca domestica (house fly).

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Musca domestica is a mechanical vector of enteric pathogens via contaminated mouthparts and does not transmit filarial nematodes -- picked by a student recalling the most familiar fly without matching it to the specific disease.

## answer_d
Glossina (tsetse fly).

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Glossina transmits African trypanosomiasis (sleeping sickness), a different parasite entirely from the filarial nematode causing subcutaneous nodules here -- picked by a student who has correctly recalled an African vector-borne disease fly but the wrong disease.
