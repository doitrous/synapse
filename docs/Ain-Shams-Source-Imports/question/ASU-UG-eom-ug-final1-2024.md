## title
Ureter crosses the obturator nerve and vessels in the pelvis

## question
In the pelvis, the ureter crosses which of the following structures as it descends toward the bladder along the lateral pelvic wall?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The obturator nerve and vessels

## explanation_a
Correct. As the ureter descends into the pelvis it runs on the medial aspect of the internal iliac vessels and crosses anterior to the obturator nerve and vessels near the lateral pelvic wall, at the point where these structures enter the obturator canal. This obturator crossing is the classic surgical landmark cited when identifying the ureter during pelvic surgery, since the ureter must be traced across this point to avoid inadvertent ligation. The uterine artery in females later crosses anterior to the ureter more medially ('water under the bridge'), but that is a separate, more distal relation from the obturator crossing being tested here.

## answer_b
The inferior epigastric vessels

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The inferior epigastric vessels arise from the external iliac vessels and run superiorly in the anterior abdominal wall; the ureter does not cross them as it descends in the pelvis.

## answer_c
The lumbosacral trunk

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The lumbosacral trunk lies posterior on the pelvic side wall and is not the structure the ureter is described as crossing on its pelvic course.

## answer_d
The internal pudendal nerve

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The internal pudendal nerve leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen and re-enters via the lesser sciatic foramen to reach the perineum; it is not the neurovascular bundle the ureter crosses in the pelvic wall.

## question

## subject
renal

## status

## vignette

## explanation_a
Correct. The right kidney sits slightly lower than the left because of the liver above it, so posteriorly it is related to only the 12th rib, not the 11th rib; the left kidney, which sits higher, is related to both the 11th and 12th ribs posteriorly. Incorrect. The diaphragm IS a posterior relation of the upper pole of the right kidney, so it does not answer an 'except' question about relations that are absent.

Psoas major

Incorrect. Psoas major IS a posterior relation of the right kidney medially, so it does not answer an 'except' question about relations that are absent.

## title
Conivaptan is contraindicated in hypovolemic hyponatremia

## question
Regarding the management of SIADH, which of the following statements about conivaptan is true?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
It is contraindicated in hypovolemic hyponatremia

## explanation_a
Correct. Conivaptan is a non-selective vasopressin V1A/V2-receptor antagonist ('vaptan') used to treat euvolemic or hypervolemic hyponatremia, including SIADH; because it promotes free-water excretion (aquaresis) without retaining sodium, it is contraindicated in hypovolemic hyponatremia, where it would worsen intravascular volume depletion. Vaptans are reserved for states of water excess, not volume depletion, and using one in a hypovolemic patient can precipitate or worsen hypotension. This distinction — euvolemic/hypervolemic hyponatremia versus hypovolemic hyponatremia — determines whether a vaptan is an appropriate treatment.

## answer_b
It is a loop diuretic used first-line in SIADH

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Conivaptan is not a loop diuretic; it is a vasopressin-receptor antagonist that causes aquaresis (free-water excretion) rather than natriuresis, and loop diuretics are not first-line therapy for SIADH.

## answer_c
It is safe and preferred in hypovolemic hyponatremia

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Conivaptan is specifically contraindicated, not preferred, in hypovolemic hyponatremia because it would further reduce effective circulating volume.

## answer_d
It works by directly inhibiting aquaporin-2 gene synthesis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Conivaptan acts by blocking vasopressin V2 receptors in the collecting duct, which secondarily reduces aquaporin-2 trafficking to the luminal membrane; it does not directly inhibit aquaporin-2 gene synthesis.

## title
Glycine is a purely glucogenic amino acid

## question
Which of the following amino acids is classified as purely glucogenic?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Glycine

## explanation_a
Correct. Amino acids are classified by their metabolic fate into pure glucogenic, pure ketogenic, or mixed groups; glycine, like most amino acids, is degraded to intermediates (via serine, then pyruvate) that can feed gluconeogenesis, making it purely glucogenic. Only two amino acids, leucine and lysine, are purely ketogenic, and only four (phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan and isoleucine) are mixed glucogenic-and-ketogenic. Every other amino acid, including glycine, is purely glucogenic.

## answer_b
Leucine

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Leucine is one of only two amino acids (with lysine) that is purely ketogenic, not glucogenic — it is degraded entirely to acetyl-CoA/acetoacetate with no net carbon entering gluconeogenesis.

## answer_c
Lysine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Lysine, like leucine, is purely ketogenic, not glucogenic.

## answer_d
Isoleucine

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Isoleucine is one of the four amino acids classified as glucogenic-and-ketogenic (mixed), not purely glucogenic.

## title
Carnitine deficiency causes muscle weakness and fasting hypoglycemia

## question
A patient presents with proximal muscle weakness and episodes of hypoglycemia during fasting. Which of the following metabolic defects best explains this presentation?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Carnitine deficiency

## explanation_a
Correct. Carnitine is required to shuttle long-chain fatty acyl groups across the inner mitochondrial membrane for beta-oxidation; carnitine deficiency impairs fatty-acid oxidation, so muscle cannot use fat for energy and the liver cannot support ketogenesis and gluconeogenesis as efficiently during fasting, producing proximal muscle weakness together with fasting hypoglycemia. This combination — muscle weakness plus fasting hypoglycemia with low ketones (hypoketotic hypoglycemia) — is the classic presentation of a fatty-acid oxidation defect such as carnitine deficiency. Skeletal and cardiac muscle are especially affected because they rely heavily on fatty-acid oxidation for energy.

## answer_b
Glucose-6-phosphatase deficiency

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Glucose-6-phosphatase deficiency (von Gierke disease) causes fasting hypoglycemia through impaired glycogenolysis/gluconeogenesis output, but its hallmark is severe hepatomegaly with lactic acidosis, hyperuricemia and hyperlipidemia, not primarily proximal muscle weakness from a fat-oxidation defect.

## answer_c
Pyruvate kinase deficiency

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Pyruvate kinase deficiency causes a hereditary hemolytic anemia from a red-cell glycolytic defect, not muscle weakness with fasting hypoglycemia.

## answer_d
Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency causes early-onset emphysema and liver disease, not a muscle-weakness/fasting-hypoglycemia syndrome.

## title
21-alpha-hydroxylase deficiency causes precocious puberty

## question
A defect in the enzyme 21-alpha-hydroxylase is most likely to result in which of the following?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Precocious puberty

## explanation_a
Correct. 21-alpha-hydroxylase deficiency (the most common cause of congenital adrenal hyperplasia) blocks the synthesis of cortisol and aldosterone from their shared precursor, 17-hydroxyprogesterone; the resulting precursor buildup is shunted into the androgen synthesis pathway, causing excess adrenal androgen production. In either sex the chronic androgen excess drives early (precocious) puberty and accelerated but ultimately compromised growth. This is a distinct clinical facet of the same enzyme defect from the salt-wasting, hyponatremic crisis caused by the accompanying aldosterone deficiency.

## answer_b
Delayed puberty with low androgen levels

## explanation_b
Incorrect. 21-alpha-hydroxylase deficiency causes androgen excess, not deficiency, so it produces early puberty with high, not low, androgen levels.

## answer_c
Isolated growth hormone deficiency

## explanation_c
Incorrect. 21-alpha-hydroxylase acts in adrenal steroidogenesis, not the growth hormone axis, and does not cause isolated growth hormone deficiency.

## answer_d
Primary hypothyroidism

## explanation_d
Incorrect. 21-alpha-hydroxylase deficiency affects adrenal cortisol/aldosterone/androgen synthesis, not thyroid hormone synthesis, and does not cause primary hypothyroidism.

## title
Renal glutaminase drives ammoniagenesis for acid-base balance

## question
Which of the following best describes the function of renal glutaminase?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
It deaminates glutamine to generate ammonia for renal acid-base balance

## explanation_a
Correct. Renal glutaminase, located mainly in proximal tubular cell mitochondria, deaminates glutamine to glutamate and ammonia (NH3); the ammonia is secreted into the tubular lumen where it combines with H+ to form ammonium (NH4+), which is excreted, allowing the kidney to excrete acid as ammonium while generating new bicarbonate that is returned to the blood. This ammoniagenesis pathway is upregulated during chronic acidosis and is one of the kidney's principal mechanisms — alongside titratable acid excretion — for maintaining acid-base balance. Without adequate glutaminase activity, the kidney's capacity to excrete a chronic acid load is impaired.

## answer_b
It converts glucose to glutamine in the proximal tubule

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Glutaminase breaks glutamine down (to glutamate and ammonia); it does not synthesize glutamine from glucose.

## answer_c
It reabsorbs filtered bicarbonate in the loop of Henle

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Bicarbonate reabsorption occurs mainly via the sodium-hydrogen exchanger and carbonic anhydrase in the proximal tubule, not via glutaminase, and this process is distinct from ammoniagenesis.

## answer_d
It is the rate-limiting enzyme of the hepatic urea cycle

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Glutaminase is a renal (and hepatic) mitochondrial enzyme involved in acid-base handling and nitrogen metabolism, not the rate-limiting enzyme of the hepatic urea cycle (which is carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I).

## title
Homocystinuria weakens the extracellular matrix via defective lysine cross-linking

## question
In homocystinuria, weakening of the extracellular matrix is best explained by which of the following mechanisms?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Excess homocysteine disrupts normal lysine-dependent cross-linking of collagen

## explanation_a
Correct. In homocystinuria (most commonly from cystathionine beta-synthase deficiency), accumulated homocysteine reacts with the lysine residues that normally participate in the lysyl-oxidase-mediated cross-linking of collagen and elastin, disrupting normal cross-link formation. This produces a structurally weaker extracellular matrix, which underlies the classic features of homocystinuria — lens dislocation (ectopia lentis), skeletal changes resembling Marfan syndrome, and vascular fragility contributing to thromboembolism. The mechanism is therefore an interference with normal cross-linking chemistry rather than a change in collagen synthesis rate.

## answer_b
Excess homocysteine directly degrades elastin via elastase activation

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Homocystinuria's connective tissue weakness comes from disrupted lysine-dependent cross-linking, not from elastase-mediated degradation of elastin.

## answer_c
Excess homocysteine impairs hydroxyproline formation in collagen

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Hydroxyproline formation (via prolyl hydroxylase, which requires vitamin C) is the defect in scurvy, not the mechanism of connective tissue weakness in homocystinuria.

## answer_d
Excess homocysteine increases collagen cross-linking, causing tissue stiffness

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Homocysteine impairs, rather than increases, normal collagen cross-linking; increased cross-linking would make tissue stiffer, not weaker, which is the opposite of what is seen.

## title
Intraglomerular mesangial cells perform basement membrane turnover

## question
Which of the following is a recognized function of the intraglomerular mesangial cells?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Basement membrane turnover

## explanation_a
Correct. Intraglomerular mesangial cells are modified smooth-muscle-like cells that sit between glomerular capillary loops; among their recognized functions is phagocytosis and turnover (synthesis and degradation) of the glomerular basement membrane and trapped macromolecules/immune complexes. They also provide structural support for the capillary tuft and, through their contractile properties, help regulate glomerular capillary surface area and filtration. This turnover function is distinct from the roles of other juxtaglomerular apparatus components.

## answer_b
Production of erythropoietin

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Erythropoietin is produced by renal peritubular interstitial fibroblasts in the cortex and outer medulla, not by intraglomerular mesangial cells.

## answer_c
Renin secretion in response to reduced perfusion

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Renin is secreted by the granular (juxtaglomerular) cells of the afferent arteriole, a separate cell population from the intraglomerular mesangial cells, though both are part of the broader juxtaglomerular apparatus.

## answer_d
Active tubular secretion of organic anions

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Active tubular secretion of organic anions occurs in proximal tubular epithelial cells, not in the mesangial cells of the glomerulus.

## title
Atrial natriuretic peptide compensates for IV-saline-induced overhydration

## question
A patient treated with excessive intravenous saline infusions becomes overhydrated. Which of the following is the expected compensatory response?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Increased atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP) secretion

## explanation_a
Correct. Volume overload from IV saline stretches the cardiac atria, which stimulates release of atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP); ANP promotes natriuresis and diuresis by increasing glomerular filtration rate, inhibiting sodium reabsorption in the collecting duct, and suppressing renin and aldosterone release, all of which act to excrete the excess volume and restore normal circulating volume. This is the physiological counter-regulatory response to hypervolemia, opposite in direction to the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system's response to hypovolemia. ANP is therefore the expected compensation in an overhydrated, volume-expanded state.

## answer_b
Increased aldosterone secretion

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Aldosterone promotes sodium and water retention and would worsen, not compensate for, overhydration; its secretion is suppressed, not increased, in volume overload.

## answer_c
Increased antidiuretic hormone (ADH) secretion

## explanation_c
Incorrect. ADH promotes water retention and would worsen overhydration; volume expansion suppresses, rather than stimulates, ADH release (via low-pressure baroreceptor input).

## answer_d
Increased renin secretion

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Renin release is suppressed by volume expansion (via increased renal perfusion pressure and reduced sympathetic tone), not increased.

## title
The inner medullary collecting duct reabsorbs only a small fraction of filtered water

## question
Which of the following statements regarding renal water reabsorption is true?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Approximately 4.2% of filtered water is reabsorbed in the inner medullary collecting duct

## explanation_a
Correct. Along the nephron, most filtered water (roughly 65%) is reabsorbed in the proximal convoluted tubule, with further reabsorption in the descending thin limb of the loop of Henle; only a small fraction remains to be handled by the distal nephron. Under antidiuretic hormone's influence, only a small percentage of the original filtrate — on the order of a few percent, cited here as approximately 4.2% — is reabsorbed in the inner medullary collecting duct, the final and most tightly regulated site of water reabsorption. This last segment is where the kidney fine-tunes urine concentration to match the body's hydration state.

## answer_b
Approximately 65% of filtered water is reabsorbed in the inner medullary collecting duct

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Approximately 65% of filtered water is reabsorbed in the proximal convoluted tubule, not the inner medullary collecting duct, which reabsorbs only a small residual fraction.

## answer_c
No water is reabsorbed in the inner medullary collecting duct under any condition

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The inner medullary collecting duct does reabsorb water, particularly when antidiuretic hormone is present and aquaporin-2 channels are inserted into the luminal membrane.

## answer_d
Water reabsorption in the inner medullary collecting duct is independent of antidiuretic hormone

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Water reabsorption in the inner medullary collecting duct is critically dependent on antidiuretic hormone, which controls aquaporin-2 channel insertion; without ADH, this segment remains relatively water-impermeable.

## title
Upper motor neuron lesion causes loss of voluntary control over micturition

## question
Regarding abnormal micturition following a suprasacral spinal cord lesion (upper motor neuron lesion), which of the following best describes the resulting change?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Loss of voluntary control over micturition, with an involuntary reflex bladder

## explanation_a
Correct. A lesion above the sacral micturition center (an upper motor neuron lesion) disconnects the pontine and cortical centers that normally allow voluntary initiation and inhibition of voiding from the intact sacral reflex arc; the result is loss of voluntary control over micturition while the basic spinal detrusor reflex persists (and is often exaggerated), producing an involuntary 'reflex' or 'automatic' bladder with reflex incontinence. This contrasts with a lower motor neuron (sacral) lesion, which abolishes the reflex arc itself and produces an atonic, overflow-type bladder. The distinction between upper and lower motor neuron patterns of bladder dysfunction is a standard physiology and clinical teaching point.

## answer_b
Complete loss of the micturition reflex with painless bladder distension

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Complete loss of the micturition reflex with painless bladder distension describes a lower motor neuron (sacral/infrasacral) lesion, which destroys the reflex arc itself, not a suprasacral upper motor neuron lesion, which spares and often exaggerates the reflex.

## answer_c
Increased voluntary control with reduced reflex bladder contractions

## explanation_c
Incorrect. An upper motor neuron lesion above the sacral center reduces, not increases, voluntary control over micturition.

## answer_d
Selective loss of bladder sensation with preserved voluntary voiding

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The characteristic upper motor neuron pattern is loss of voluntary control with a preserved/exaggerated reflex, not an isolated sensory deficit with preserved voluntary voiding.

## title
Acidosis increases cellular potassium efflux and decreases renal potassium secretion

## question
In acidosis, which of the following best describes the effect on potassium handling?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Increased efflux of potassium out of cells and decreased renal potassium secretion

## explanation_a
Correct. In acidosis, excess extracellular hydrogen ions are buffered partly by movement into cells in exchange for intracellular potassium moving out, so cellular potassium efflux increases; at the same time, acidosis reduces the activity of the basolateral Na+/K+-ATPase in the distal nephron's principal cells and lowers luminal potassium secretion, so renal potassium secretion decreases. Both effects combine to predispose to hyperkalemia in acidotic states, particularly metabolic (mineral) acidosis with an inorganic acid load. This is the classic explanation for why acidosis and hyperkalemia are frequently seen together.

## answer_b
Decreased efflux of potassium out of cells and increased renal potassium secretion

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Acidosis increases, not decreases, potassium efflux from cells (via the H+/K+ exchange mechanism), and decreases, not increases, renal potassium secretion.

## answer_c
No effect on cellular potassium efflux, but increased renal potassium secretion

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Acidosis does affect cellular potassium efflux (increasing it via the H+/K+ exchange mechanism), and it decreases, rather than increases, renal potassium secretion.

## answer_d
Increased efflux of potassium out of cells and increased renal potassium secretion

## explanation_d
Incorrect. While acidosis does increase cellular potassium efflux, it decreases, not increases, renal potassium secretion — both changes act in the same (hyperkalemia-promoting) direction, not opposite ones.

## title
Lupus nephritis shows mesangial immune complex deposits

## question
A patient with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) undergoes renal biopsy. Which of the following microscopic findings is characteristic of lupus nephritis?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Mesangial immune complex deposits

## explanation_a
Correct. Lupus nephritis is an immune-complex-mediated glomerulonephritis in which circulating immune complexes (often containing anti-dsDNA antibodies) deposit in the mesangium, and — depending on the class of lupus nephritis — also in the subendothelial and/or subepithelial regions of the glomerular basement membrane. Mesangial deposits are a defining light-microscopic and immunofluorescence finding across the spectrum of lupus nephritis classes, often producing a 'full-house' immunofluorescence pattern (IgG, IgA, IgM, C3, C1q). This immune-complex mechanism distinguishes lupus nephritis from other glomerular diseases with different deposit patterns.

## answer_b
Linear basement membrane deposits of anti-GBM antibody

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Linear basement membrane deposits are characteristic of anti-glomerular basement membrane (anti-GBM) disease (e.g. Goodpasture syndrome), which reflects antibody binding directly to a basement membrane antigen, not the granular immune-complex deposition pattern of lupus nephritis.

## answer_c
Crescents composed purely of neutrophils with no immune deposits

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Crescents in glomerulonephritis are composed mainly of proliferating parietal epithelial cells and macrophages, not purely neutrophils, and lupus nephritis crescents occur alongside immune complex deposits rather than in their absence.

## answer_d
Amyloid deposits staining positive with Congo red

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Amyloid deposits staining with Congo red are characteristic of renal amyloidosis, a different disease process from the immune-complex-mediated injury of lupus nephritis.
