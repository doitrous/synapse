## title
Duration of stored ATP for muscle contraction

## question
The total quantity of ATP stored within the cells of the body is sufficient for contraction for a maximum of how many seconds?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
8

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The stored ATP pool is smaller than this, sufficient for only a few seconds of contraction.

## answer_b
12

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This overstates the tiny directly-stored ATP pool; sustained contraction beyond a few seconds relies on regeneration (via creatine phosphate, then glycolysis/oxidative phosphorylation), not on stored ATP itself.

## answer_c
4

## explanation_c
Correct. The cell's directly stored ATP is enough to fuel contraction for only about 4 seconds; this is precisely why the ATP-ADP cycle must turn over extremely fast and why creatine phosphate exists as an immediate secondary buffer to regenerate ATP as it is spent. This is why regenerating ATP, not simply storing more of it, is the cell's real energetic strategy.

## answer_d
16

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This overstates the stored ATP pool by a wide margin.

## title
Oxidative system's fuel sources

## question
In muscle, the oxidative system depends on the supply of reduced equivalents from:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Complete oxidation of glucose in the TCA cycle

## explanation_a
Incorrect alone. Complete glucose oxidation via the TCA cycle does supply reduced equivalents, but it is not the only source feeding the oxidative system.

## answer_b
Fatty acid oxidation

## explanation_b
Incorrect alone. Fatty acid oxidation likewise supplies reduced equivalents but is not the sole source.

## answer_c
Oxidation of ketone bodies

## explanation_c
Incorrect alone. Ketone-body oxidation contributes reduced equivalents too, but again is not exclusive.

## answer_d
All of the above

## explanation_d
Correct. The mitochondrial oxidative (aerobic) system draws its reduced equivalents (NADH, FADH2) from all three fuel classes together -- complete oxidation of glucose through the TCA cycle, beta-oxidation of fatty acids, and oxidation of ketone bodies -- which is exactly why the aerobic system can sustain far longer, more efficient ATP regeneration than the anaerobic glycolysis or phosphagen systems alone. No single fuel class alone accounts for the oxidative system's full capacity.
