Elimination of self-reactive T cells during thymic development is called:

## question
Elimination of self-reactive T cells during thymic development is called:

## subject
imm

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Apoptosis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Apoptosis is the execution mechanism by which self-reactive clones are removed, but the process/checkpoint being named here is negative selection, not apoptosis in general.

## answer_b
Positive selection

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Positive selection is the earlier thymic checkpoint that rescues cells recognising self MHC; it does not eliminate self-reactive cells.

## answer_c
Negative selection

## explanation_c
Correct. Negative selection is the thymic checkpoint at which developing T cells whose receptor binds self-peptide/self-MHC too strongly are deleted by apoptosis. This clonal deletion of self-reactive clones is the central mechanism of thymic (central) self-tolerance, and it occurs after positive selection, mainly at the corticomedullary junction and in the medulla.

## answer_d
Clonal anergy

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Clonal anergy is a peripheral tolerance mechanism that functionally silences a self-reactive cell that has already left the thymus; it is not the thymic elimination process described here.

Incorrect. The TCR is expressed at high, not low, copy number on the surface of every mature T cell, including Th cells, since it is the cell's primary antigen-recognition receptor.

## question
Anergy of T cells occur if:

## subject
imm

## status

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Antigen doesn't bind to TCR

## explanation_a
Incorrect alone. If antigen never binds the TCR at all, there is simply no recognition event; but anergy specifically describes recognition without costimulation, so this alone is incomplete as the trigger described.

## answer_b
MHC class II doesn't bind to CD4

## explanation_b
Incorrect alone. Failure of the CD4 co-receptor interaction weakens signalling, but is only part of the fuller costimulation-failure picture that produces anergy.

## answer_c
B7 doesn't bind CD28

## explanation_c
Correct answer requires this option and is captured fully in D. When B7 fails to engage CD28 — i.e., signal 1 (antigen via TCR) occurs without signal 2 (costimulation) — the T cell becomes anergic rather than activated.

## answer_d
All of the above

## explanation_d
Correct. T-cell anergy is the general outcome of incomplete activation signalling: it can follow from antigen failing to engage the TCR, from the CD4-MHC-II interaction failing, or — most classically — from B7 failing to engage CD28 so that costimulation never arrives. In every one of these scenarios the T cell ends up functionally unresponsive rather than activated, which is exactly what anergy means.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Option D correctly describes negative selection, so 'none of the above' does not apply.
