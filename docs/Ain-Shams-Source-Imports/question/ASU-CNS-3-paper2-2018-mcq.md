## title
Which nucleus lies in the floor of the fourth ventricle and is vulnerable to a fourth-ventricle tumour?

## question
A tumour expanding within the fourth ventricle classically affects which cranial nerve nucleus first, because of its location in the ventricular floor?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The abducens (CN VI) nucleus

## explanation_a
Correct. The abducens nucleus lies in the caudal pons, directly beneath the facial colliculus in the floor of the fourth ventricle. This superficial position makes it especially vulnerable to compression by an expanding fourth-ventricle mass, producing an ipsilateral lateral rectus palsy, sometimes with associated facial weakness from the adjacent facial nerve genu.

## answer_b
The oculomotor (CN III) nucleus

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The oculomotor nucleus lies in the midbrain tegmentum, at the level of the superior colliculus, well away from the fourth ventricle floor.

## answer_c
The trochlear (CN IV) nucleus

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The trochlear nucleus lies in the midbrain, at the inferior collicular level, not in the fourth ventricle floor.

## answer_d
The hypoglossal (CN XII) nucleus

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The hypoglossal nucleus does lie near the floor of the fourth ventricle but more caudally, in the medulla; the classically taught landmark directly beneath the facial colliculus is the abducens nucleus.

## title
Which cortical area is classically described as formulating thoughts prior to their conversion into speech?

## question
Which cortical language area is classically described as the site where thoughts are formulated, before being relayed to Broca's area for conversion into speech?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Wernicke's area

## explanation_a
Correct. Wernicke's area, in the posterior superior temporal gyrus of the dominant hemisphere, is classically described as the general interpretative (gnostic) area where language content is analysed for meaning and coherent thoughts are formulated, before being relayed via the arcuate fasciculus to Broca's area for conversion into articulate speech. A lesion here produces fluent but meaningless (receptive) aphasia.

## answer_b
Broca's area

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Broca's area converts already-formulated thoughts into the motor programme for speech articulation; it does not itself formulate the thought content.

## answer_c
The angular gyrus

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The angular gyrus is a parietal association area involved in reading and cross-modal language association, not primary thought formulation.

## answer_d
The primary motor cortex

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The primary motor cortex executes the motor commands for speech muscles; it plays no role in formulating linguistic thought content.

## question
A patient presents with an acute panic attack. Which drug is used to rapidly abort the episode?

Correct. Alprazolam, a short-acting, high-potency benzodiazepine, rapidly potentiates GABA-A receptor-mediated inhibition and has a fast enough onset to abort an acute panic attack, unlike the delayed-onset agents used for chronic panic-disorder prophylaxis. ## answer_d
Buspirone

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Buspirone is a non-benzodiazepine anxiolytic with a slow onset of action over one to two weeks, unsuitable for acute panic attacks.

## title
The great cerebral vein of Galen is formed by the union of which two veins?

## question
The great cerebral vein (of Galen) is formed beneath the splenium of the corpus callosum by the union of which pair of veins?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The two internal cerebral veins

## explanation_a
Correct. The great cerebral vein of Galen is formed beneath the splenium of the corpus callosum by the union of the two internal cerebral veins, which drain the deep structures of the cerebral hemispheres; it also receives the paired basal veins of Rosenthal before joining the inferior sagittal sinus to form the straight sinus. This deep venous confluence is clinically important because thrombosis here has serious consequences distinct from superficial cortical vein pathology.

## answer_b
The two basal veins of Rosenthal

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The basal veins of Rosenthal drain into the great cerebral vein as tributaries; they are not the pair of veins whose union directly forms it.

## answer_c
The two superior cerebral veins

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The superior cerebral veins are superficial cortical veins that drain into the superior sagittal sinus, unrelated to the deep venous system forming the vein of Galen.

## answer_d
The two inferior cerebral veins

## explanation_d
Incorrect. There is no paired "inferior cerebral vein" that forms the great cerebral vein; deep cerebral venous drainage is via the internal cerebral veins.

## title
Which vascular lesion, caused by chronic hypertension, classically ruptures to cause deep hypertensive intracerebral haemorrhage?

## question
Chronic, poorly controlled hypertension classically produces which microscopic vascular lesion on the small penetrating arteries of the basal ganglia, thalamus and pons, whose rupture causes deep hypertensive intracerebral haemorrhage?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Charcot-Bouchard microaneurysms

## explanation_a
Correct. Charcot-Bouchard microaneurysms are tiny aneurysmal dilatations of the small penetrating (lenticulostriate and other perforating) arteries, caused directly by chronic hypertension weakening the vessel media; their rupture is the classic mechanism of primary hypertensive intracerebral haemorrhage, typically deep in the basal ganglia, thalamus, pons or cerebellum. Their characteristic basal ganglia/thalamic distribution is why hypertensive bleeds are classically described as deep haemorrhages.

## answer_b
Berry (saccular) aneurysms

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Berry (saccular) aneurysms occur at arterial branch points of the circle of Willis and classically cause subarachnoid haemorrhage, not deep hypertensive intraparenchymal bleeding.

## answer_c
Cerebral amyloid angiopathy

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Cerebral amyloid angiopathy causes lobar (peripheral cortical) haemorrhage in normotensive elderly patients, a distinct mechanism and distribution from hypertensive deep haemorrhage.

## answer_d
Arteriovenous malformation

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Arteriovenous malformations are congenital vascular anomalies that can bleed at any age, unrelated to chronic hypertensive vessel injury.

Sleepwalking is a disorder of arousal that arises out of slow-wave (stage N3) non-REM sleep, typically in the first third of the night when slow-wave sleep predominates, with motor tone preserved and impaired consciousness during the episode. Stage N1 sleep

## answer_d
Stage N2 sleep

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Stage N2 is an intermediate non-REM stage; sleepwalking is specifically linked to deep slow-wave (N3) sleep.
