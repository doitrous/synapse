QST-DEV-ASU-AE-EMBRYO3-PRIMITIVE-STREAK-EPIBLAST

## title
Layer the primitive streak develops in

## question
The primitive streak develops in which layer of the embryonic disc?

## subject
dev

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Epiblast

## explanation_a
Correct. The primitive streak forms at the start of the third week as a median, narrow groove with bulging sides, appearing within the epiblast at the caudal end of the bilaminar embryonic disc. It is the first visible sign of gastrulation, and its epiblast cells go on to invaginate through it, giving rise to every layer of the trilaminar disc.

## answer_b
Hypoblast

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The hypoblast is the other bilaminar-disc layer, but it is displaced rather than converted as gastrulation proceeds -- it does not form the primitive streak, and it contributes no cells to the trilaminar disc's own three layers.

## answer_c
Mesoderm

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Mesoderm is one of the products of gastrulation (formed by epiblast cells invaginating through the streak), not the layer the streak itself develops within.

## answer_d
Endoderm

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Endoderm, likewise, is a product of gastrulation -- invaginating epiblast cells displace and replace the hypoblast to form it -- not the layer the streak develops in.

## answer_e
Notochord

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The notochord is a separate axial structure that forms from epiblast cells migrating cranially through the primitive node, at the streak's cranial end -- it is a product of the streak's activity, not the layer the streak itself forms within.

## title
Week gastrulation occurs

## question
On what week of intrauterine life does gastrulation occur?

## subject
dev

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The second week is dominated by implantation and formation of the bilaminar disc (epiblast and hypoblast) -- gastrulation, which converts the disc from bilaminar to trilaminar, has not yet begun.

## answer_b
4th

## explanation_b
Incorrect. By the fourth week gastrulation is complete and folding of the embryonic disc is underway -- the fourth week is too late for the process the question is asking about.

## answer_c
5th

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The fifth week is well into organogenesis, long after gastrulation (and the folding that follows it) has finished.

## answer_d
3rd

## explanation_d
Correct. Gastrulation is the third week's characteristic event. The primitive streak appears at the start of the week, and through it epiblast cells invaginate to form the endoderm, the intraembryonic mesoderm and the notochord, converting the bilaminar disc into the trilaminar one. The chorionic villi also make their first appearance this same week, alongside gastrulation.

## answer_e
1st

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The first week covers fertilisation through the start of implantation -- far too early for gastrulation, which needs the bilaminar disc (itself a second-week structure) already in place.

Sites where intraembryonic mesoderm is absent

Intraembryonic mesoderm is absent in:

## explanation_b

## title
Germ layers of origin for skin

## question
Skin develops from:

## subject
dev

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect as the complete answer -- surface ectoderm does give the epidermis (and its appendages: hair, nails, glands), but the skin as a whole has a second contributing layer this option leaves out.

## answer_b
Endoderm

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Endoderm gives the linings of the gut and respiratory tract and the parenchyma of organs such as the liver and pancreas -- it plays no part in forming the skin.

## answer_c
Mesoderm

## explanation_c
Incorrect as the complete answer -- mesoderm (via the somite's dermatome) does give the dermis, but the skin as a whole has a second contributing layer this option leaves out.

## answer_d
Ectoderm & mesoderm

## explanation_d
Correct. Skin has two components from two different germ layers: the epidermis, the surface epithelial layer, is ectodermal, while the dermis beneath it, the connective-tissue layer, is mesodermal (from the dermatome, the dorsolateral part of each somite's dermomyotome). Together these two layers, from two different germ layers, make up the skin -- the fact that the individual dermis-from-somites and epidermis-from-ectoderm questions elsewhere in this bank are each testing one half of.

## answer_e
Ectoderm & Endoderm

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Endoderm plays no part in forming the skin -- the second contributing layer is mesoderm (the dermis), not endoderm.

## answer_a
Notochord

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The notochord is not itself the source of the neural plate -- it lies beneath the ectoderm and induces the overlying ectoderm to thicken into the neural plate, an inductive role rather than a direct-origin one.

## answer_b
Mesoderm

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Mesoderm is not the neural plate's germ layer of origin -- the neural plate is an ectodermal structure.

## answer_c
Endoderm

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Endoderm is not the neural plate's germ layer of origin either -- again, the neural plate is ectodermal.

## answer_d
Ectoderm dorsal to the notochord

## explanation_d
Correct. The neural plate is ectoderm, but not just any ectoderm -- specifically the median strip lying directly over (dorsal to) the notochord. It is induced by the notochord immediately beneath it. This precise positional relationship is what marks out which part of the dorsal ectoderm thickens into the neural plate, and which stays as ordinary surface ectoderm.

## answer_e
Ectoderm on the whole dorsal aspect of the embryo

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The neural plate is not the whole of the dorsal ectoderm -- only the median strip overlying the notochord thickens into neural plate; the ectoderm lateral to it on the dorsal aspect remains surface ectoderm, destined to become epidermis instead.

QST-DEV-ASU-AE-EMBRYO3-LATERAL-FOLDING-GUT-TUBE

## title
Result of lateral folding

## question
Lateral folding leads to the formation of an elongated:

## subject
dev

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Yolk sac

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Folding compresses the secondary yolk sac into the vitelline duct rather than elongating it -- the yolk sac shrinks with folding, it does not become the elongated structure this question is asking about.

## answer_b
Gut tube

## explanation_b
Correct. The flat trilaminar disc folds cephalocaudally, driven by the growing neural tube and somites, and laterally, driven by the expanding amniotic cavity. As it folds, part of the endoderm-lined secondary yolk sac is incorporated into the embryo as an elongated tube. This gut tube is divided along its length into foregut (enclosed by the head fold), midgut (enclosed by the lateral folds) and hindgut (enclosed by the tail fold).

## answer_c
Allantois

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The allantois is a small diverticulum budding into the connecting stalk -- folding does not elongate it into a tube; if anything it is drawn along with the connecting stalk as the umbilical ring forms.

## answer_d
Spinal tube

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The 'spinal tube' (neural tube caudally) forms by fusion of the neural folds during neurulation, a process that runs alongside but is distinct from lateral/cephalocaudal folding of the disc as a whole -- folding is not what elongates the neural tube.

## answer_e
Heart tube

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The heart tube forms from the fusion of paired cardiogenic mesoderm as the head fold swings the future heart region ventrally and caudally -- folding repositions it, but 'elongated gut tube' specifically describes the endoderm-derived structure, not the heart tube.
