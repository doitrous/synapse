## title
The placental barrier does not include which of the following?

## question
The placental barrier does not include which of the following?

## subject
dev

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
The syncytiotrophoblast.

## explanation_a
Incorrect as an answer to "does not include" -- the syncytiotrophoblast is the outermost, maternal-blood-facing layer of the barrier and is very much part of it.

## answer_b
The cytotrophoblast.

## explanation_b
Incorrect as an answer -- the cytotrophoblast is the second, inner trophoblastic layer of the early barrier, even though it thins away in the second half of pregnancy.

## answer_c
The primary mesoderm.

## explanation_c
Incorrect as an answer -- the primary (villous) mesoderm forms the connective-tissue core of the barrier, sitting between the trophoblast and the fetal capillaries.

## answer_d
The endothelium of maternal blood vessels.

## explanation_d
Correct. The human placenta is hemochorial, meaning maternal blood bathes the syncytiotrophoblast directly rather than flowing through any maternal vessel wall -- by definition there is no maternal endothelium anywhere in the placental barrier. The four real components, from maternal blood inward to fetal blood, are the syncytiotrophoblast, the cytotrophoblast, the connective-tissue core (primary/villous mesoderm), and the endothelium of the fetal capillaries -- all four of which are part of the barrier and appear as the other four options here. Because maternal blood contacts syncytiotrophoblast without any intervening maternal vessel wall, "endothelium of maternal blood vessels" is the one item on this list that was never a barrier component to begin with.

## answer_e
The endothelium of fetal blood vessels.

## explanation_e
Incorrect as an answer -- the fetal capillary endothelium is the innermost, fetal-blood-facing layer of the barrier and is very much part of it.

## title
Which layers of the placental barrier disappear in the second half of pregnancy?

## question
The following layers of placental barrier disappear in the second half of pregnancy:

## subject
dev

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Cytotrophoblast + syncytiotrophoblast.

## explanation_a
Incorrect -- syncytiotrophoblast is one of the two layers that persists throughout pregnancy, not one that disappears.

## answer_b
1ry mesoderm + fetal endothelium.

## explanation_b
Incorrect -- fetal capillary endothelium is the other layer that persists; only the mesoderm (not the endothelium) is lost.

## answer_c
Cytotrophoblast + 1ry mesoderm.

## explanation_c
Correct. As pregnancy advances, the placental barrier thins from its early four-layer form down to just two, to speed maternal-fetal exchange as fetal demand rises. The two components that disappear are the cytotrophoblast (which becomes discontinuous and then absent by the second half of pregnancy) and the primary/villous mesoderm (the connective-tissue core, which thins as the fetal capillaries come to lie almost directly beneath the remaining trophoblast). What remains -- syncytiotrophoblast on the maternal side and fetal capillary endothelium on the fetal side -- is exactly the two-layer late-pregnancy barrier this same paper tests directly in its next relevant question.

## answer_d
1ry mesoderm + syncytiotrophoblast.

## explanation_d
Incorrect -- this again pairs a persisting layer (syncytiotrophoblast) with a disappearing one (mesoderm), which is not what the question asks for.

## answer_e
Syncytiotrophoblast + fetal endothelium.

## explanation_e
Incorrect -- this is precisely the pair that remains late in pregnancy, not the pair that disappears.

## title
What makes up the placental barrier late in pregnancy?

## question
Placental barrier late in pregnancy includes:

## subject
dev

## status

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect -- cytotrophoblast and primary mesoderm are exactly the two layers that disappear in late pregnancy, the opposite of what remains.

## answer_b
Intraembryonic mesoderm + syncytiotrophoblast.

## explanation_b
Incorrect -- intraembryonic mesoderm has no role in the placental barrier at all, which is built entirely from extraembryonic tissues.

## answer_c

## explanation_c
Incorrect -- extraembryonic mesoderm (the villous core) is one of the layers that thins away, not one that remains as the late barrier.

This thinning happens because the cytotrophoblast becomes discontinuous and the villous (primary) mesoderm thins out, shortening the diffusion distance between maternal and fetal blood at exactly the point in pregnancy when fetal oxygen and nutrient demand is rising fastest.

## explanation_b
Incorrect -- ovarian ectopic pregnancy does occur, but it is rare compared with the uterine tube.

## answer_c

## explanation_c
Incorrect -- abdominal/peritoneal ectopic pregnancy is the rarest of the recognized sites, not the commonest.

## explanation_e
Incorrect -- cervical ectopic pregnancy is a recognized but uncommon site, far behind the uterine tube.

## title
Which of the following is not a layer a sperm must penetrate to fertilize an ovum?

## question
The layers that the sperm has to penetrate in order to fertilize an ovum include the following EXCEPT:

## subject
dev

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Vitelline membrane.

## explanation_a
Incorrect as an answer -- the vitelline (oolemma) membrane is the final true barrier the sperm fuses with/penetrates.

## answer_b
Cummulus oophorus.

## explanation_b
Incorrect as an answer -- the cumulus oophorus, the outermost cell mass around the ovulated oocyte, is the first layer a sperm must get through.

## answer_c
Corona radiata.

## explanation_c
Incorrect as an answer -- the corona radiata, the layer of follicle cells beneath the cumulus, is a genuine barrier the sperm penetrates using its acrosomal hyaluronidase.

## answer_d
Zona pellucida.

## explanation_d
Incorrect as an answer -- the zona pellucida, the glycoprotein coat carrying the sperm-binding receptors, is the barrier whose crossing triggers the acrosome/zona reaction and is very much a true penetration barrier.

## answer_e
Perivitelline space.

## explanation_e
Correct. To fertilize the oocyte, a capacitated sperm must cross the cumulus oophorus, the corona radiata and the zona pellucida in turn before fusing with the oolemma (vitelline membrane); the perivitelline space, however, is not a barrier at all -- it is the fluid-filled gap between the oolemma and the zona pellucida, and the sperm only enters this space after the true barrier (the zona) has already been breached, on its final short approach to the oocyte membrane. Because it is a space the sperm crosses rather than a structure it must digest through, "perivitelline space" is the one item on this list that does not belong with the other four genuine penetration barriers.

## explanation_a

## answer_b
Maternal blood.

## explanation_b
Correct. The intervillous spaces are the maternal-blood-filled spaces of the placenta into which spiral arteries empty and from which uterine veins drain; chorionic villi (carrying fetal blood in their own separate capillaries) float within this maternal blood, but the two circulations never mix. This is the essence of the hemochorial placenta: syncytiotrophoblast is bathed directly in maternal blood, with fetal blood always kept separate inside the villous capillaries.

A mixture of foetal & maternal blood.

Incorrect -- maternal and fetal blood never mix in the normal placenta; only maternal blood occupies the intervillous space.

## answer_d
Blood with high CO2 content.

## explanation_d
Incorrect -- the intervillous space is filled with fresh maternal arterial blood, relatively oxygen-rich rather than defined by high CO2, as it arrives from the spiral arteries.

## answer_e
Blood with low O2 content.

## explanation_e
Incorrect -- incoming maternal blood in the intervillous space is arterial and oxygen-rich before it gives up oxygen to the villi, not low in oxygen.

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Correct -- this is the false statement. Extraembryonic mesoderm is in fact the primary (1ry) mesoderm -- it appears before any intraembryonic (secondary) mesoderm forms during gastrulation in the third week -- so calling it "secondary" mesoderm reverses the correct sequence. It forms the connecting stalk.

## explanation_b
This statement is true, not the answer -- extraembryonic mesoderm forms the connecting stalk that will become the umbilical cord.

## answer_c
It shares in formation of chorion.

## explanation_c
This statement is true, not the answer -- extraembryonic mesoderm (as somatic extraembryonic mesoderm, lining the trophoblast) contributes to the chorion together with the cyto- and syncytiotrophoblast.

## answer_d
It forms extraembryonic coelom.

## explanation_d
This statement is true, not the answer -- spaces that appear within the extraembryonic mesoderm coalesce to form the extraembryonic coelom (chorionic cavity).

## answer_e
It surrounds the yolk sac.

## explanation_e
This statement is true, not the answer -- splanchnic extraembryonic mesoderm covers the primary/secondary yolk sac, surrounding it externally.
