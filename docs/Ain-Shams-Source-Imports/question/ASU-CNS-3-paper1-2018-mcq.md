## title
Which vitamin deficiency causes pellagra, presenting with dermatitis, diarrhoea and dementia?

## question
A patient presents with a photosensitive, symmetrical dermatitis over sun-exposed skin, diarrhoea and progressive dementia/confusion. Deficiency of which vitamin produces this classic triad (pellagra)?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Niacin (vitamin B3)

## explanation_a
Correct. Niacin (vitamin B3, nicotinic acid/nicotinamide) is a precursor of the coenzymes NAD and NADP used throughout cellular energy metabolism. Its deficiency causes pellagra, classically presenting with the triad of the "3 Ds": photosensitive dermatitis in sun-exposed skin, diarrhoea from gastrointestinal mucosal atrophy, and dementia/confusion from CNS energy failure (a 4th D, death, follows if uncorrected). Because niacin can also be synthesised from tryptophan, pellagra can arise from isolated niacin deficiency or from conditions that deplete tryptophan, and it is treated with niacin/nicotinamide replacement.

## answer_b
Thiamine (vitamin B1)

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Thiamine (B1) deficiency causes Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome, presenting with a different triad of confusion, ophthalmoplegia/nystagmus and ataxia, classically in chronic alcoholics — not pellagra's dermatitis-diarrhoea-dementia picture.

## answer_c
Cobalamin (vitamin B12)

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Cobalamin (B12) deficiency classically causes subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord and megaloblastic anaemia, not pellagra.

## answer_d
Pyridoxine (vitamin B6)

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Pyridoxine (B6) deficiency classically causes peripheral neuropathy and sideroblastic anaemia, not the pellagra triad.

## title
Which metabolite, formed by astrocytic ammonia detoxification, contributes to cerebral oedema in hyperammonaemic encephalopathy?

## question
In a patient with a urea cycle defect (or severe liver failure), ammonia accumulates in the blood. Astrocytes detoxify this ammonia using glutamine synthetase, and excess accumulation of the resulting osmotically active product causes astrocyte swelling, contributing to cerebral oedema. Which metabolite is this?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Glutamine

## explanation_a
Correct. Astrocytic glutamine synthetase combines the excess ammonia with glutamate to form glutamine, the brain's main route of ammonia detoxification. When ammonia delivery is high and sustained, as in a urea cycle defect or hepatic failure, this drives excessive intra-astrocytic glutamine accumulation; because glutamine is osmotically active, it draws water into the astrocyte, causing astrocyte swelling and cerebral oedema, the core mechanism of hyperammonaemic (hepatic) encephalopathy.

## answer_b
Glutamate

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Glutamate is the substrate consumed by glutamine synthetase (combined with ammonia to form glutamine), not the accumulating osmotically active product responsible for astrocyte swelling.

## answer_c
GABA

## explanation_c
Incorrect. GABA is an inhibitory neurotransmitter synthesised from glutamate by glutamate decarboxylase; it is not part of the astrocytic ammonia-detoxification pathway that drives cerebral oedema here.

## answer_d
Urea

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Urea is the product of the hepatic urea cycle itself, whose impairment is precisely the problem in this scenario; astrocytes do not synthesise urea as their route of ammonia detoxification.

## title
Which pharmacokinetic property of methadone underlies its use in opioid substitution therapy?

## question
Compared with morphine's relatively short elimination half-life, methadone has a much longer half-life. Which clinical consequence of this property is the basis for using methadone as maintenance (substitution) therapy in opioid use disorder?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Fewer/milder withdrawal symptoms with stable once-daily dosing

## explanation_a
Correct. Methadone's much longer half-life (roughly 15-60 hours) than morphine's produces stable plasma levels with once-daily oral dosing, smoothing out the peaks and troughs in mu-receptor occupancy that drive withdrawal symptoms. This pharmacokinetic property, not any difference in receptor selectivity, is why methadone is used as maintenance (substitution) therapy: it steadily satisfies mu-receptor demand to suppress withdrawal and craving while avoiding the rapid on/off reinforcing effect of shorter-acting opioids such as morphine or heroin.

## answer_b
Complete blockade of opioid receptors, preventing any opioid effect

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Complete opioid-receptor blockade without agonist effect describes naltrexone, a pure antagonist used for relapse prevention after detoxification, not methadone, which is itself a full mu-agonist.

## answer_c
Rapid reversal of opioid-induced respiratory depression

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Rapid reversal of opioid-induced respiratory depression describes naloxone, a short-acting antagonist used for acute overdose reversal, not methadone's role in maintenance therapy.

## answer_d
A ceiling effect on respiratory depression at high doses

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A ceiling effect on respiratory depression at high doses is a property of buprenorphine, a partial mu-agonist, not methadone, which is a full agonist without that ceiling.
