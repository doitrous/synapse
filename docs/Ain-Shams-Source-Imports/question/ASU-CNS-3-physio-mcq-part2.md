# Item

QST-ASUCNS3-PHYSIO-Q49

## title
Increased pain sensitivity in the normal skin surrounding an area of injury is called what?

## question
A patch of normal, uninjured skin surrounding a burn becomes painfully sensitive to light touch, even though the burn itself is a short distance away. What is this phenomenon called?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Secondary hyperalgesia

## explanation_a
Correct. Secondary hyperalgesia is exaggerated pain sensitivity in normal, uninjured skin surrounding (not within) an area of tissue damage. It results from central sensitisation — increased excitability of dorsal horn neurons receiving convergent input from the surrounding skin — rather than from any direct injury to that skin's own nociceptors.

## answer_b
Primary hyperalgesia

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Primary hyperalgesia is exaggerated pain sensitivity within the injured tissue itself, from peripheral nociceptor sensitisation by locally released inflammatory mediators — not in the surrounding normal skin.

## answer_c
Allodynia of the burn itself

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Allodynia (pain from a normally non-painful stimulus) can accompany secondary hyperalgesia in the surrounding skin, but the named phenomenon being tested here, exaggerated pain in tissue outside the injury, is specifically secondary hyperalgesia.

## answer_d
Referred pain

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Referred pain is pain perceived at a site distant from its true (often visceral) origin, due to convergent afferent input — a different mechanism from the local spread of sensitisation around a skin injury.

## title
A sunburned patient feels pain from the light pressure of a jacket. What mechanism explains this?

## question
A woman who sunbathed develops a sunburn and, later, feels pain simply from the light pressure of putting on her jacket over the burned skin. Which mechanism best explains this increased pain sensitivity at the site of the burn itself?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Sensitisation of peripheral nociceptors by locally released inflammatory tissue products

## explanation_a
Correct. This is primary hyperalgesia: chemical mediators released by damaged tissue at the burn site — prostaglandins, bradykinin, histamine, and other inflammatory products — lower the activation threshold of the peripheral nociceptors in that tissue. Because those nociceptors are now sensitised, a previously innocuous stimulus, such as the light pressure of clothing, is perceived as painful. The sensitisation is confined to the injured tissue itself, which is the defining feature of primary (as opposed to secondary) hyperalgesia.

## answer_b
Central sensitisation of dorsal horn neurons receiving input from unburned skin

## explanation_b
Incorrect. That describes secondary hyperalgesia, which occurs in the normal skin surrounding an injury, not at the injury site itself — the vignette specifically places the pain at the burned skin.

## answer_c
Loss of descending inhibition from the periaqueductal gray

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Loss of descending inhibitory control would produce a more generalised increase in pain sensitivity, not the localised sensitisation confined to the site of tissue damage described here.

## answer_d
Increased conduction velocity in A-delta fibres

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Hyperalgesia at an injury site reflects a lowered activation threshold of nociceptors, not a change in axonal conduction velocity.

## title
A lesion of one cerebellar hemisphere produces incoordination on which side of the body?

## question
A lesion confined to one cerebellar hemisphere produces limb incoordination (ataxia) on which side of the body?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The same (ipsilateral) side as the lesion

## explanation_a
Correct. Each cerebellar hemisphere coordinates voluntary movement on its own (ipsilateral) side, because cerebellar efferents cross twice — once leaving the deep cerebellar nuclei toward the contralateral red nucleus/thalamus, and again when the corticospinal tract they influence decussates in the medulla — restoring a net ipsilateral relationship. A unilateral cerebellar lesion therefore produces ataxia and dysmetria on the same side as the lesion.

## answer_b
The opposite (contralateral) side to the lesion

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Contralateral control is the rule for the cerebral motor cortex (single decussation at the pyramids), not the cerebellum, which has a net double decussation.

## answer_c
Both sides equally

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A unilateral cerebellar hemisphere lesion produces lateralised, not bilateral, incoordination — confined to the ipsilateral limbs.

## answer_d
Neither side; cerebellar lesions do not affect limb coordination

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The cerebellum is central to coordinating the timing, force, and accuracy of voluntary movement; a hemisphere lesion produces clear ipsilateral incoordination (ataxia, dysmetria, intention tremor).

## title
An infarction of the blood vessels supplying the cerebral cortex, involving the primary motor area, produces which pattern of paralysis?

## question
An ischaemic infarction involving the blood vessels that supply the primary motor cortex produces which pattern of weakness?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Contralateral spastic paralysis

## explanation_a
Correct. The primary motor cortex gives rise to corticospinal fibres that decussate at the medullary pyramids in the lower medulla. A lesion of the cortex itself — including ischaemic infarction of the vessels supplying it — produces an upper motor neuron syndrome of spastic paralysis, exaggerated reflexes, and an extensor plantar response on the contralateral side of the body. The weakness is contralateral precisely because the corticospinal fibres cross below the level of the cortical lesion, at the pyramids.

## answer_b
Ipsilateral spastic paralysis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The corticospinal tract decussates below the level of a cortical lesion (at the medullary pyramids), so the resulting weakness is contralateral, not ipsilateral, to the lesion.

## answer_c
Contralateral flaccid paralysis with no reflex change

## explanation_c
Incorrect. An upper motor neuron lesion (cortical infarction) produces spastic paralysis with exaggerated, not absent, reflexes; flaccid paralysis with no reflex change is the pattern of a lower motor neuron lesion instead.

## answer_d
Bilateral symmetrical weakness

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A focal unilateral cortical infarction produces lateralised, contralateral weakness, not bilateral symmetrical weakness.

## title
A lesion of the right fasciculus gracilis causes loss of vibration sense in the right tibia. Why the same side?

## question
A patient with a lesion confined to the right fasciculus gracilis loses vibration sense over the right tibia, below the level of the lesion. Why is the sensory loss on the same (ipsilateral) side as the lesion, rather than the opposite side?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Dorsal column fibres ascend uncrossed and decussate only in the lower medulla

## explanation_a
Correct. Fibres in the fasciculus gracilis (lower limb) and fasciculus cuneatus (upper limb) ascend on the same side of the spinal cord and do not cross the midline until they form internal arcuate fibres in the lower medulla. A unilateral lesion below that decussation therefore produces ipsilateral, not contralateral, loss of vibration and proprioceptive sense.

## answer_b
Spinothalamic fibres ascend uncrossed and decussate only in the thalamus

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This describes the (crossed) spinothalamic tract, which carries pain and temperature and decussates in the spinal cord itself — the opposite pattern to the dorsal columns, and not the pathway carrying vibration sense.

## answer_c
The fasciculus gracilis crosses at the level of entry, then re-crosses in the medulla

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The fasciculus gracilis does not cross at the segmental level of entry; it ascends uncrossed the entire length of the spinal cord and crosses only once, in the medulla.

## answer_d
Vibration sense is represented bilaterally at every spinal level

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Vibration sense in each dorsal column is a unilateral (ipsilateral, pre-decussation) representation, not a bilateral one — this is exactly why a unilateral lesion produces a unilateral (ipsilateral) deficit.

## title
Which receptor type carries the afferent limb of the flexor withdrawal reflex?

## question
The afferent (sensory) limb of the flexor withdrawal reflex, triggered by touching a hot surface, is carried by which receptor type?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Free nerve endings (nociceptors)

## explanation_a
Correct. The flexor withdrawal reflex is triggered by a noxious stimulus, and its afferent limb is carried by free nerve endings — the nociceptors that transduce noxious mechanical, thermal, or chemical stimuli. These afferents synapse on spinal interneurons that excite flexor motor neurons of the stimulated limb, producing withdrawal. The same interneurons also drive, via the crossed extensor reflex, extension of the opposite limb.

## answer_b
Muscle spindles

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Muscle spindles are proprioceptors signalling muscle length/stretch; they are the afferent limb of the (unrelated) stretch reflex, not the nociceptive withdrawal reflex.

## answer_c
Golgi tendon organs

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Golgi tendon organs signal muscle tension and drive the inhibitory autogenic reflex on the same muscle, not the noxious-stimulus-triggered withdrawal reflex.

## answer_d
Pacinian corpuscles

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Pacinian corpuscles are rapidly adapting mechanoreceptors for vibration, not the nociceptive receptor type that initiates withdrawal.

## title
Why are superficial (e.g. abdominal) reflexes lost in an upper motor neuron lesion, while deep tendon reflexes become exaggerated?

## question
In an upper motor neuron lesion, the superficial abdominal reflexes are typically lost, while the deep tendon reflexes become exaggerated. What explains the loss of the superficial reflexes specifically?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Superficial reflexes depend on an intact corticospinal tract, which the lesion has interrupted

## explanation_a
Correct. Superficial (cutaneous) reflexes such as the abdominal reflex require a functioning corticospinal tract input to the spinal segment, unlike deep tendon reflexes, which do not share this dependency. An upper motor neuron lesion that interrupts the corticospinal tract therefore abolishes the superficial reflexes below the lesion, even as it simultaneously exaggerates the deep tendon reflexes (loss of descending inhibition).

## answer_b
Superficial reflexes are purely spinal reflexes with no supraspinal input at all

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Superficial reflexes are not purely spinal — their preservation specifically requires corticospinal (supraspinal) input, which is exactly why they are lost when that input is interrupted.

## answer_c
Superficial reflexes require an intact peripheral nerve, which the lesion has damaged

## explanation_c
Incorrect. An upper motor neuron lesion is central (corticospinal tract), not peripheral; the peripheral nerve carrying the reflex's afferent and efferent limbs is intact.

## answer_d
Superficial reflexes are mediated by the same circuit as deep tendon reflexes

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Superficial and deep tendon reflexes are not mediated by the same circuit — that is precisely why an upper motor neuron lesion affects them in opposite directions (superficial reflexes lost, deep reflexes exaggerated).

C5a

Incorrect. C5a is chiefly an anaphylatoxin and chemotactic factor for inflammatory cells, not a direct nociceptor-activating pain mediator.

## title
Which receptor is chiefly responsible for detecting vibration?

## question
Which mechanoreceptor is chiefly responsible for detecting vibration?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Pacinian corpuscle

## explanation_a
Correct. The Pacinian corpuscle is a large, encapsulated, rapidly adapting mechanoreceptor found in the deep dermis and subcutaneous tissue. Its onion-like lamellated capsule filters out sustained pressure and selectively tunes it to high-frequency mechanical oscillation. This makes it the receptor chiefly responsible for detecting vibration, distinct from the other cutaneous mechanoreceptors tuned to other modalities.

## answer_b
Muscle spindle

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The muscle spindle signals muscle length and the rate of change of length (stretch), not cutaneous vibration.

## answer_c
Golgi tendon organ

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The Golgi tendon organ signals muscle tension, not vibration.

## answer_d

## explanation_d

Golgi tendon organ (Ib) afferents ultimately inhibit which neurons, and by what kind of circuit?

## question
Golgi tendon organ Ib afferents, signalling increased muscle tension, ultimately inhibit which neurons in the spinal cord, and by what kind of circuit?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The alpha motor neurons of the same muscle, via a disynaptic circuit through an inhibitory interneuron

## explanation_a
Correct. The Golgi tendon organ's Ib afferents enter the spinal cord and synapse on an inhibitory interneuron. That interneuron, in turn, inhibits the alpha motor neurons supplying the same (homonymous) muscle — a disynaptic circuit, not a direct synapse. The net effect is that excessive tension in a muscle reflexively reduces that same muscle's own contraction, a protective reflex called autogenic (inverse myotatic) inhibition.

## answer_b
The alpha motor neurons of the same muscle, via a direct monosynaptic synapse

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The Golgi tendon organ reflex is disynaptic, routed through an inhibitory interneuron; the monosynaptic reflex arc (direct Ia afferent-to-alpha-motor-neuron synapse) instead describes the stretch reflex.

## answer_c
The alpha motor neurons of the antagonist muscle, via a direct monosynaptic synapse

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The reflex inhibits the same (homonymous), not the antagonist, muscle's alpha motor neurons — it is an autogenic (self), not a reciprocal, inhibitory reflex.

## answer_d
The gamma motor neurons of the same muscle only, with no effect on alpha motor neurons

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The tendon organ reflex's principal action is inhibition of the alpha motor neurons of the same muscle; it is not restricted to the gamma motor neurons.
