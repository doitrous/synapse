A triploid mouse's chromosome number, given a haploid number of 20

## question
If the haploid chromosome number in a mouse is 20, what is the chromosome number of a triploid mouse?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
60

## explanation_a
Correct. A triploid cell carries three complete haploid sets of chromosomes rather than the usual two, so its chromosome number is three times the haploid number: 3 x 20 = 60. Triploidy is a form of euploidy — the whole-set multiple is exact, unlike aneuploidy, where only one chromosome is gained or lost. Recognising this distinction, and being able to calculate a chromosome count from a stated ploidy level, is the basic arithmetic every karyotype-interpretation question in this chapter builds on.

## answer_b
21

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This picks the student who adds one to the haploid number, as though "triploid" meant "haploid plus one extra chromosome" rather than three whole haploid sets.

## answer_c
40

## explanation_c
Incorrect. 2 x 20 = 40 is the normal diploid chromosome number for this species, not the triploid number — this distractor catches a student who forgot the question asked about triploidy specifically.

## answer_d
19

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This is one less than the haploid number and corresponds to no meaningful ploidy state; it catches a student guessing rather than calculating.

## answer_e
10

## explanation_e
Incorrect. This is half the haploid number, again corresponding to no meaningful ploidy state for this species.

## title
The mode of inheritance in which a father can transmit a trait to his son

## question
Which of the following modes of inheritance is characterised by male-to-male transmission (father to son)?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Mitochondrial inheritance

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Mitochondrial inheritance passes only from an affected mother to her children; an affected father transmits it to none of his children, sons included, so this pattern shows the opposite of male-to-male transmission.

## answer_b
X-linked recessive inheritance

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A son receives his father's Y chromosome, never his X, so an X-linked recessive allele can never pass from father to son — it catches a student who has not yet linked "X-linked" to "no father-to-son transmission."

## answer_c
X-linked dominant inheritance

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The same chromosome logic applies regardless of dominance: an affected father transmits an X-linked dominant condition to all of his daughters and none of his sons.

## answer_d
Autosomal dominant inheritance

## explanation_d
Correct. Autosomal dominant inheritance runs through the autosomes, not the sex chromosomes, so a father's mutant allele can land on either the copy he gives a son or the copy he gives a daughter with equal likelihood. Because a son can therefore inherit an autosomal dominant condition directly from his affected father, male-to-male transmission is possible — and its presence in a pedigree is one of the clearest clues that the trait is autosomal rather than X-linked or mitochondrial. This is the single feature examiners lean on hardest to separate autosomal dominant inheritance from every sex-linked pattern in this chapter.

## title
The usual explanation for achondroplasia in a child of unaffected parents

## question
If a normal-stature couple has a child with achondroplasia, this is most often caused by:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
New mutation

## explanation_a
Correct. Achondroplasia is autosomal dominant, but most cases arise in families with no prior history of the condition, from a new mutation occurring in a parental germ cell at conception — most often traced to the paternal germline and associated with older paternal age. Because the mutation is new, neither parent carries it in their own body cells, which is exactly why both parents are of normal stature despite having an affected child. This is the standard teaching explanation for a sporadic autosomal dominant disorder, and achondroplasia is the example used precisely because it illustrates the point so cleanly.

## answer_b
Multifactorial inheritance

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Achondroplasia is a well-characterised single-gene (FGFR3) autosomal dominant condition, not a multifactorial one shaped by many genes and environmental factors together.

## answer_c
Genomic imprinting

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Genomic imprinting explains why the parental origin of a lost gene copy matters, as in Prader-Willi and Angelman syndromes — it is not the mechanism behind a sporadic single-gene mutation like this one.

## answer_d
Anticipation

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Anticipation describes a disorder worsening across generations of an already-affected family; it does not explain a first occurrence in a family with no prior history.

## answer_e
Trinucleotide repeat expansion

## explanation_e
Incorrect. This mechanism explains disorders such as Huntington's disease, not achondroplasia, which results from a point mutation in FGFR3 rather than a repeat expansion.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. X-linked recessive inheritance likewise never shows male-to-male transmission, since a son never receives his father's X chromosome.

## explanation_c
Correct. "Holandric" is simply another name for Y-linked inheritance — a trait carried on the Y chromosome, and therefore transmitted only from father to son, since only sons inherit a Y chromosome and daughters never do. This vocabulary question exists to make sure a student recognises the term "holandric" on sight, since an examiner may use either word interchangeably in a later question without warning.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Autosomal recessive inheritance is unrelated to the sex chromosomes at all and does not show the obligatory father-to-son-only pattern that defines holandric inheritance.

## title
The inheritance pattern in which male-to-male transmission is obligatory

## question
Male-to-male transmission must occur in:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Y-linked inheritance

## explanation_a
Correct. In Y-linked (holandric) inheritance, every son of an affected father inherits the father's Y chromosome and therefore the trait, without exception — there is no way for a son to escape it, and no daughter can ever inherit it, since daughters never receive a Y chromosome. This makes Y-linked inheritance unique among every pattern in this chapter: male-to-male transmission is not merely possible, as in autosomal dominant inheritance, but obligatory in every single affected lineage. This distinction between "possible" (autosomal dominant) and "obligatory" (Y-linked) is precisely what this question is testing.

## answer_b
X-linked dominant inheritance

## explanation_b
Incorrect. X-linked dominant inheritance never shows male-to-male transmission at all, since a son never receives his father's X chromosome.

## answer_c
Sex-linked recessive inheritance

## explanation_c
Incorrect. X-linked recessive inheritance also never shows male-to-male transmission, for the same chromosomal reason as X-linked dominant inheritance.

## answer_d
Autosomal recessive

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Male-to-male transmission is possible in autosomal recessive inheritance (an affected father can have an affected son), but it is not obligatory — many affected fathers will have no affected sons at all, depending on the mother's carrier status, so this pattern does not fit "must occur."
