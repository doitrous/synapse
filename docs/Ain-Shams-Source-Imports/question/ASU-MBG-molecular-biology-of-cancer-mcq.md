## title
Cell-cycle event at the G1 restriction point

## question
Which of the following occurs in G1's restriction point?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Growth factor inhibits cyclin transcription.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Growth factor signalling induces, rather than inhibits, cyclin transcription — this is the opposite of what actually initiates cell-cycle passage.

## answer_b
Cyclin inactivates CDK

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Cyclins activate CDKs, not the other way around; a cyclin does not inactivate its own kinase partner.

## answer_c
The protein P15, P16 activate CDK

## explanation_c
Incorrect. p15 and p16 are CDK inhibitors that restrain, not activate, CDK activity — they are tumor suppressors, not activators, of the restriction point.

## answer_d
RB protein is phosphorylated.

## explanation_d
Correct. At the G1 restriction point, a mitogenic growth-factor signal drives cyclin-CDK complexes to phosphorylate the retinoblastoma protein (Rb). In its unphosphorylated state Rb binds and sequesters the E2F transcription factor, blocking S-phase entry; phosphorylating Rb inactivates this brake and releases E2F to activate the genes needed for DNA synthesis. Passing the restriction point in this phosphorylated state commits the cell to complete the rest of the cycle even if growth factor is later withdrawn.

## answer_e
E2F transcription factor is inactive.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. E2F is inactive only while it remains bound by unphosphorylated Rb; at the restriction point itself, Rb phosphorylation has already released and activated E2F.

## title
Mechanism that does NOT activate a proto-oncogene

## question
Proto-oncogenes are not converted to oncogene by which of the following?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Chromosomal translocation

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Chromosomal translocation is a genuine activating mechanism — it can place a proto-oncogene under a stronger regulatory element or fuse it to another gene, as in the Philadelphia chromosome's BCR-ABL fusion.

## answer_b
Proto-oncogene duplication

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Gene duplication (a form of amplification) raises a proto-oncogene's copy number and expression, which is a genuine activating mechanism.

## answer_c
Insertion mutagenesis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Insertion mutagenesis — a foreign sequence such as a retroviral promoter landing next to the gene — is a genuine activating mechanism, driving the proto-oncogene's overexpression.

## answer_d
Proto-oncogene deletion

## explanation_d
Correct. Deletion of a proto-oncogene removes its growth-promoting function entirely rather than activating it — the opposite of what oncogenic conversion requires, which is an increase in the gene's expression or activity. Chromosomal translocation, gene duplication/amplification, insertion mutagenesis and point mutation all raise a proto-oncogene's expression or alter its product to become constitutively active, which is why each of those, unlike deletion, is a genuine activating mechanism. This is the standard contrast with tumor suppressor genes, where deletion of the remaining functional copy is exactly the mechanism that contributes to cancer.

## answer_e
Point mutation of proto-oncogene.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A point mutation in the coding sequence (the classical RAS example) can make the gene product constitutively active, which is a genuine activating mechanism.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. M-to-G1 is not the checkpoint associated with Rb or p53; their regulatory role is specifically at the G1/S boundary.

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Not the false statement — this is true. Proto-oncogenes, in their normal form, do encode proteins (growth factors, receptors, signal-transduction proteins, cyclins) that promote passage through the cell cycle.

Not the false statement — this is true. Tumor suppressor genes, by definition, encode proteins that restrain cell-cycle progression or eliminate damaged cells.

## title
The exception among tumor-suppression-producing events (Rb hyperphosphorylation)

## question
All of the following can result in tumor suppression except

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Inhibition of CDK.

## explanation_a
Not the exception — this is true. Inhibiting CDK activity restrains cell-cycle progression, a genuine tumor-suppressive effect.

## answer_b
Activation of Rb.

## explanation_b
Not the exception — this is true. Activating Rb (keeping it in its restraining, hypophosphorylated state) suppresses passage through the restriction point, a genuine tumor-suppressive effect.

## answer_c
Hypophosphorylation of Rb.

## explanation_c
Not the exception — this is true. Hypophosphorylated Rb is the active, E2F-sequestering state that restrains the cycle, a genuine tumor-suppressive effect — Rb "activation" and "hypophosphorylation" describe the same restraining state.

## answer_d
Release of mdm away from p53.

## explanation_d
Not the exception — this is true. Releasing Mdm2 away from p53 frees p53 to act — halting the cycle or triggering apoptosis on damage — a genuine tumor-suppressive effect.

## answer_e
Hyperphosphorylation of Rb.

## explanation_e
Correct — this is the exception. Hyperphosphorylation of Rb inactivates it, releasing the E2F transcription factor and permitting S-phase entry — the opposite of tumor suppression. It is hypophosphorylated (active) Rb that restrains the cycle by sequestering E2F, so driving Rb toward the hyperphosphorylated state promotes, rather than suppresses, proliferation. This is the same trap as option b/c stated in reverse: "activation" and "hypophosphorylation" of Rb are the same protective state, while "hyperphosphorylation" is the inactivating event a mitogenic signal produces.

## title
Cell-cycle checkpoint halted by DNA damage

## question
At which cell cycle checkpoint, cell cycle is halted if cells DNA is damaged:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
G0 – G1

## explanation_a
Incorrect. There is no standard "G0-G1" DNA-damage checkpoint named in this chapter's teaching; the relevant halt point is G1-S.

## answer_b
G2- S

## explanation_b
Incorrect. "G2-S" is not a real, sequential cell-cycle transition (S precedes G2, not the reverse), and it is not the DNA-damage checkpoint this question tests.

## answer_c
G2 – M

## explanation_c
Incorrect. G2-M is a genuine checkpoint but is not the one this chapter associates with p53's response to DNA damage; that role is assigned specifically to the G1-S checkpoint.

## answer_d
G1 – S

## explanation_d
Correct. The G1-S checkpoint is where the cell cycle halts if the cell's DNA is damaged: p53, stabilised by the damage, arrests the cycle at this point through p21 so that damaged DNA is not copied into two daughter cells, and stimulates DNA-repair enzyme activity — or, if the damage is irreparable, triggers apoptosis instead. This is the same G1-S checkpoint that Rb and p53 are both taught to regulate elsewhere in this chapter.

## answer_e
S – G2.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. "S-G2" is not the checkpoint this question tests; the DNA-damage halt point taught here is G1-S.

## title
Mechanism that does NOT activate a proto-oncogene (promoter deletion)

## question
One of these mechanisms dose not result in activation of a protooncogene to an oncogene?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Single base mutation.

## explanation_a
Not the answer — this is a true activating mechanism. A single base (point) mutation can make a proto-oncogene's product constitutively active.

## answer_b
Gene amplification.

## explanation_b
Not the answer — this is a true activating mechanism. Gene amplification raises a proto-oncogene's copy number and expression.

## answer_c
Insertion of a viral sequence.

## explanation_c
Not the answer — this is a true activating mechanism. Insertion of a viral sequence next to a proto-oncogene (insertional mutagenesis) can drive its overexpression via a strong viral promoter.

## answer_d
Chromosomal translocation.

## explanation_d
Not the answer — this is a true activating mechanism. Chromosomal translocation can place a proto-oncogene under new regulatory control or fuse it to another gene.

## answer_e
Promotor deletion.

## explanation_e
Correct. Deleting a proto-oncogene's promoter would reduce or abolish its expression rather than activate it, so promoter deletion is not a mechanism of proto-oncogene-to-oncogene activation — activation requires increasing expression or activity, and removing the promoter does the opposite. This mirrors the earlier questions' pattern: any mechanism that silences rather than enhances a proto-oncogene is the correct "does NOT activate" answer.
