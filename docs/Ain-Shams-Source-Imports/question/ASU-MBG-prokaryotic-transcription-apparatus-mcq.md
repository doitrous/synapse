# Item

## id

## title
The function of rho factor in bacterial transcription

## question
The function of rho factor is to:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Enhance the rate of transcription

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Rho factor's role is to bring transcription to an end, not to speed up the rate at which RNA polymerase adds nucleotides during elongation.

## answer_b
Required for initiation of transcription

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Initiation is sigma factor's job — sigma directs the holoenzyme to the promoter and starts transcription — not rho's, which acts only at the far end of the process.

## answer_c
Required for termination of transcription

## explanation_c
Correct. Rho factor is the protein that carries out rho-dependent termination: it follows behind RNA polymerase along the growing RNA transcript and eventually catches up to it, helping to unwind the RNA-DNA hybrid and release both the polymerase and the finished RNA. This is one of the two ways bacterial transcription can end — the other being intrinsic (rho-independent) termination, which needs no protein factor at all and instead relies on a G-C-rich hairpin in the transcript itself. Recognising rho factor as a termination protein, not an initiation or elongation factor, is the basic fact this question and its neighbours in the chapter are built around.

## answer_d
Release RNA polymerase from the promoter region

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Rho factor acts at the end of transcription, releasing the polymerase from the finished transcript — it plays no role at the promoter, which is where sigma factor and the holoenzyme act at the start of transcription.

# Item

## id

## title
The enzyme not required for transcription

## question
Which of the following enzymes is NOT present in (required for) transcription?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Topoisomerase I

## explanation_a
Incorrect as an answer to this "NOT" question, because topoisomerase genuinely is used during transcription, to relieve the supercoiling that builds up ahead of and behind RNA polymerase as it unwinds and re-winds the DNA.

## answer_b
Helicase activity

## explanation_b
Correct — this is the enzyme activity NOT required for transcription. Unlike DNA replication, which depends on a dedicated helicase (DnaB in E. coli) to unwind the double helix ahead of the replication fork, transcription needs no separate helicase enzyme at all: RNA polymerase itself locally opens the short stretch of DNA it is currently transcribing, using its own structure rather than recruiting a distinct unwinding enzyme. This is a genuinely tested distinction between the two processes, and it is exactly the kind of fact that is easy to get backwards if a student assumes every DNA-unwinding step must involve "a helicase" by name.

## answer_c
Ligase

## explanation_c
Incorrect as an answer to this "NOT" question, because RNA ligase activity (or an equivalent joining step) is genuinely involved in RNA processing associated with transcription, such as intron removal during splicing.

## answer_d
RNA polymerase

## explanation_d
Incorrect as an answer to this "NOT" question, because RNA polymerase is, by definition, the central enzyme of transcription — it cannot be the one enzyme excluded from the process.

## answer_e
Topoisomerase II

## explanation_e
Incorrect as an answer to this "NOT" question, because topoisomerase II, like topoisomerase I, genuinely helps manage the supercoiling generated during transcription.

# Item

## id

## title
The mechanism of intrinsic (rho-independent) transcription termination

## question
Intrinsic termination of bacterial transcription is characterised by:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Needing rho factor

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Needing rho factor is precisely what distinguishes rho-*dependent* termination from *intrinsic* termination — the stem is asking about the mechanism that does not need rho.

## answer_b
Needing ATP

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Intrinsic termination is a purely structural, sequence-driven event — the hairpin plus poly-U run destabilises the RNA-DNA hybrid without any enzymatic, ATP-consuming step.

## answer_c
A sequence rich in G-C, inverted and repeated, near the 3' end of the transcript

## explanation_c
Correct. Intrinsic (rho-independent) termination relies on a self-complementary, G-C-rich inverted repeat near the 3' end of the newly made RNA folding back on itself into a stable hairpin structure; combined with a following stretch of U residues (which pair only weakly with the DNA template), this hairpin destabilises the RNA-DNA hybrid enough to release the transcript without any protein factor being required at all. This is the structural, self-contained counterpart to rho-dependent termination, and recognising the G-C-rich-hairpin-plus-poly-U signature is what identifies a terminator as intrinsic rather than rho-dependent.

## answer_d
Helicase and ATPase activity

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Rho factor itself has ATPase and helicase-like activity that it uses to catch up to the polymerase in rho-*dependent* termination — but the question asks about *intrinsic* termination, which needs neither rho nor any comparable enzyme activity.

## title
Identifying sigma factor by its role in RNA polymerase

## question
Sigma factor is:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
A subunit of RNA polymerase responsible for the initiation of transcription

## explanation_a
Correct. Sigma factor is the detachable subunit of the bacterial RNA polymerase holoenzyme that gives the enzyme the ability to recognise a promoter and begin transcription at the correct site — without it, the core enzyme alone can extend an RNA chain but cannot reliably find where to start one. Once initiation has succeeded and the polymerase moves into elongation, sigma factor can dissociate from the complex, leaving the core enzyme to continue on its own. This makes sigma the single most important subunit for answering "why does RNA polymerase start transcribing exactly here and not somewhere else."

## answer_b
A subunit of DNA polymerase required for Okazaki fragment synthesis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This describes a role in DNA replication (Okazaki fragment synthesis on the lagging strand), a different process from transcription, and sigma factor is not a DNA polymerase subunit at all.

## answer_c
A subunit of the 50S ribosome that catalyses peptide bond synthesis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This describes peptidyl transferase activity in translation, an entirely different process and molecular machine from RNA polymerase and transcription.

## answer_d
A subunit of the 30S ribosomal subunit to which mRNA binds

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This describes a component of the ribosome's small subunit involved in translation initiation, not a subunit of RNA polymerase involved in transcription initiation.

The molecule that recognises the transcription initiation site

## question
The initiation site for transcription is recognised by:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
The alpha subunit of DNA-dependent RNA polymerase

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The alpha subunits contribute to holoenzyme assembly and interact with regulatory proteins at some promoters, but promoter recognition and initiation-site selection is specifically sigma factor's role, not the alpha subunit's.

## answer_b
The beta subunit of DNA-dependent RNA polymerase

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The beta and beta-prime subunits form the catalytic core that carries out RNA synthesis once transcription is under way — they are not what recognises the promoter sequence in the first place.

## explanation_c
Correct. Sigma factor is the RNA polymerase holoenzyme's subunit dedicated to finding the promoter: it recognises the Pribnow box (consensus TATAAT, about ten base pairs upstream of the start site) and directs the holoenzyme to begin transcription there. This is the same fact tested directly at Q49 ("what is sigma factor"), now asked from the angle of "what recognises the initiation site" — a student who can answer one should be able to answer both.

## answer_d
Rho factor

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Rho factor acts at the opposite end of transcription, terminating it — it plays no role in recognising where transcription should begin.

# Item

## title
Why RNA polymerase is called a holoenzyme

## question
RNA polymerase is actually an aggregate of several different subunits, so it is often called:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
A holoenzyme

## explanation_a
Correct. "Holoenzyme" is the name for the complete, multi-subunit bacterial RNA polymerase — the core enzyme (the catalytic subunits, sufficient for elongation) plus the detachable sigma subunit (needed for accurate initiation). Naming the whole assembly a holoenzyme, rather than simply "an enzyme," reflects the fact that it is built from several distinct polypeptides working together, each contributing a different function to the overall process of transcription. This same aggregate-of-subunits fact is what makes it possible for sigma factor to detach after initiation and leave the core enzyme to finish the job alone.

## answer_b
A primosome

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A primosome is the multi-protein complex that synthesises RNA primers during DNA replication — a different process and a different molecular machine from RNA polymerase and transcription.

## answer_c
Reverse transcriptase

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Reverse transcriptase is the enzyme that synthesises DNA from an RNA template, the opposite direction of information flow from transcription, and is not another name for RNA polymerase.

## answer_d
All of the above

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Only "holoenzyme" correctly names the multi-subunit RNA polymerase assembly; "primosome" and "reverse transcriptase" name entirely different molecular machines, so this option is not correct.

The number of RNA polymerases in a bacterial cell

## question
How many RNA polymerases are present in a bacterial system?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
4

## explanation_a
Incorrect. No organism taught in this course uses four separate RNA polymerases; this overstates even the eukaryotic figure.

## answer_b
2

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Two is neither the bacterial figure (one) nor the eukaryotic figure (three) taught in this course.

## answer_c
1

## explanation_c
Correct. Bacteria transcribe every class of RNA — messenger, ribosomal and transfer RNA alike — with a single RNA polymerase, which switches which genes it transcribes by swapping which sigma factor is bound to its core enzyme. This is a direct contrast with eukaryotes, which use three separate, dedicated polymerases (RNA polymerase I for the large rRNAs, II for mRNA, III for tRNA and small RNAs), a fact this module already tests elsewhere for the eukaryotic side. Knowing that bacteria need only one enzyme, not three, for this job is precisely the "prokaryote versus eukaryote" contrast this question exists to test.

## answer_d

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Three is the eukaryotic figure (RNA polymerases I, II and III), not the bacterial one — this distractor catches a student who answers with the eukaryotic fact instead of the bacterial one the question actually asks about.

## title
The consensus sequence of the Pribnow box

## question
What is the consensus sequence of the Pribnow box?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
TATATA

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This is not the accepted Pribnow box consensus — it resembles the correct sequence but repeats "TA" rather than ending in "AT," and mixing it up with the true consensus is exactly the trap this distractor sets.

## answer_b
TATAAT

## explanation_b
Correct. TATAAT is the consensus sequence of the Pribnow box (also called the -10 box), a short DNA element centred about ten base pairs upstream of the bacterial transcription start site. Sigma factor recognises this sequence — or, more precisely, its resemblance to it, since real promoters vary somewhat from the exact consensus — to position the RNA polymerase holoenzyme correctly and begin transcription. This is the same -10 element referred to more generally in this concept's teaching of how sigma factor finds the correct initiation site.

## answer_c
TAATAA

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This sequence reorders the correct consensus's letters and is not the accepted Pribnow box sequence.

## answer_d
TTAAT

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This sequence is one base short of the correct six-base consensus and is not the accepted Pribnow box sequence.
