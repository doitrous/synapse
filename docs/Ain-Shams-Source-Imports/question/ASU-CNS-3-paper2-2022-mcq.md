# Item

## id
QST-ASUCNS3P22022-BERRY-ANEURYSM

## title
Which vessel abnormality is the classic cause of subarachnoid haemorrhage?

## question
A young man collapses with a sudden, severe ('thunderclap') headache after physical exertion, and lumbar puncture shows blood in the cerebrospinal fluid. Rupture of which vessel abnormality at the circle of Willis classically causes this presentation?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
A congenital berry (saccular) aneurysm

## explanation_a
Correct. Congenital berry (saccular) aneurysms form at branch points of the circle of Willis — commonly the anterior communicating artery, the origin of the posterior communicating artery, or the middle cerebral artery bifurcation — where the vessel wall's muscular and elastic layers are congenitally deficient. Their rupture is the leading cause of spontaneous subarachnoid haemorrhage, classically presenting as a sudden, severe ('thunderclap') headache, sometimes precipitated by exertion, with blood found in the CSF on lumbar puncture.

## answer_b
A Charcot-Bouchard microaneurysm

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Charcot-Bouchard microaneurysms form on small penetrating vessels deep within the brain parenchyma as a consequence of chronic hypertension, and their rupture causes intracerebral, not subarachnoid, haemorrhage.

## answer_c
An arteriovenous malformation of the middle cerebral artery

## explanation_c
Incorrect. An arteriovenous malformation can also bleed into the subarachnoid space, but it is a distinct developmental vascular lesion, not the classic cause tested here, and is far less common than aneurysmal rupture as a cause of spontaneous subarachnoid haemorrhage.

## answer_d
Atherosclerotic stenosis of the basilar artery

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Atherosclerotic stenosis narrows a vessel and predisposes to ischaemic stroke, not to the sudden rupture and haemorrhage described.

QST-ASUCNS3P22022-DECORTICATE-POSTURING

## title
Which posturing pattern localises a lesion above the level of the red nucleus?

## question
A comatose patient shows bilateral flexion of the upper limbs at the elbow and wrist, with extension of both lower limbs, in response to a painful stimulus. This posturing localises the lesion to which level of the neuraxis?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
At or above the level of the red nucleus

## explanation_a
Correct. This is decorticate posturing — bilateral upper-limb flexion with lower-limb extension — which results from a severe lesion at or above the level of the red nucleus (cerebral hemispheres, internal capsule or upper midbrain) that interrupts the corticospinal and corticorubral pathways while sparing the rubrospinal tract, which continues to drive upper-limb flexor tone. It therefore localises the lesion to this rostral level, above where the rubrospinal tract itself is interrupted.

## answer_b
At the level of the pons, below the red nucleus

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A lower, pontine-level lesion that also interrupts the rubrospinal tract produces decerebrate posturing instead — extension of all four limbs, not upper-limb flexion.

## answer_c
At the level of the lower medulla

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A lower medullary lesion is generally incompatible with this reflex posturing pattern and points toward flaccidity or different brainstem signs, not decorticate posturing.

## answer_d
At the level of the lumbar spinal cord

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A lumbar spinal cord lesion would not produce this pattern of upper-limb flexion with lower-limb extension, which requires a supraspinal lesion.

QST-ASUCNS3P22022-PILOCYTIC-ASTROCYTOMA

## title
Which childhood cerebellar tumour is characterised by Rosenthal fibres?

## question
A 7-year-old child presents with ataxia and signs of raised intracranial pressure. Imaging shows a well-circumscribed cerebellar mass, and biopsy shows compact, elongated bipolar cells with corkscrew-shaped eosinophilic structures. Which tumour is this?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Pilocytic astrocytoma

## explanation_a
Correct. Pilocytic astrocytoma (WHO grade 1) is the most common paediatric brain tumour and characteristically arises in the cerebellum. It shows a biphasic pattern of compact, elongated ('hair-like', pilocytic) bipolar cells alternating with looser microcystic areas, and its histological hallmark is the Rosenthal fibre — an eosinophilic, corkscrew-shaped, degenerating astrocytic process — matching the biopsy described.

## answer_b
Medulloblastoma

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Medulloblastoma is also a childhood posterior-fossa tumour, but it is composed of sheets of primitive small round blue cells, not pilocytic bipolar cells with Rosenthal fibres.

## answer_c
Glioblastoma

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Glioblastoma (WHO grade 4) shows necrosis and microvascular proliferation, and is rare in children and rarely well-circumscribed, unlike the tumour described.

## answer_d
Oligodendroglioma

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Oligodendroglioma shows a characteristic clear perinuclear halo ('fried-egg' appearance) on histology, not Rosenthal fibres, and is not a classic childhood cerebellar tumour.

QST-ASUCNS3P22022-STRIA-TERMINALIS

## title
Which fibre bundle carries the amygdala's principal efferent output to the hypothalamus?

## question
Which white-matter tract follows the curve of the lateral ventricle to connect the amygdala with the hypothalamus, carrying the amygdala's principal efferent output?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The stria terminalis

## explanation_a
Correct. The stria terminalis is a thin, C-shaped fibre bundle that follows the curve of the lateral ventricle from the amygdala forward to the hypothalamus (chiefly its anterior and ventromedial nuclei) and the septal area, carrying the amygdala's principal efferent output. It mediates the autonomic and neuroendocrine components of emotion and fear responses generated in the amygdala. This makes it the amygdala's own major projection pathway, distinct from the Papez circuit's hippocampus-based loop.

## answer_b
The fornix

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The fornix is the Papez circuit's pathway from the hippocampus to the mammillary bodies, a separate limbic tract arising from the hippocampus, not the amygdala.

## answer_c
The cingulum

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The cingulum runs within the cingulate gyrus, completing the Papez circuit by returning fibres to the hippocampal formation; it does not connect the amygdala to the hypothalamus.

## answer_d
The mammillothalamic tract

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The mammillothalamic tract connects the mammillary bodies to the anterior thalamic nucleus, another Papez-circuit link unrelated to the amygdala's own efferent pathway.

QST-ASUCNS3P22022-ALZHEIMER-PATHOLOGY

## title
Which two histological hallmarks characterise Alzheimer disease?

## question
Post-mortem examination of a patient who died with progressive dementia shows extracellular deposits with a beta-amyloid core and intracellular paired helical filaments of hyperphosphorylated tau protein. Which disease do these two findings together characterise?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Alzheimer disease

## explanation_a
Correct. Alzheimer disease, the most common cause of dementia, is characterised histologically by two hallmark lesions: extracellular neuritic (senile) plaques with a beta-amyloid core, and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles composed of paired helical filaments of hyperphosphorylated tau protein. These accumulate progressively in the hippocampus, entorhinal cortex and neocortex, driving the cortical atrophy and memory loss described.

## answer_b
Huntington disease

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Huntington disease is characterised by atrophy and gliosis of the caudate nucleus from a CAG repeat expansion in the huntingtin gene, not amyloid plaques or tau tangles.

## answer_c
Parkinson disease

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Parkinson disease is characterised by intracytoplasmic Lewy bodies (alpha-synuclein) and substantia nigra dopaminergic neuron loss, not beta-amyloid plaques or tau tangles.

## answer_d
Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease is a prion disease showing spongiform change on histology, not neuritic plaques or neurofibrillary tangles.
