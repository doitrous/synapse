## title
Which of the following is considered a primary lymphoid organ?

## question
Which of the following is considered a primary lymphoid organ?

## subject
imm

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Bone marrow

## explanation_a
Correct. Bone marrow is a primary (central) lymphoid organ, along with the thymus — together these are the sites where B and T lymphocytes develop and mature before ever meeting antigen. This is what distinguishes a primary lymphoid organ from a secondary one: primary organs generate and mature naive lymphocytes, while secondary organs (lymph nodes, spleen, tonsils, MALT) are where those already-mature lymphocytes encounter antigen and mount a response. Bone marrow additionally performs haematopoiesis, generating every blood cell lineage.

## answer_b
Lymph nodes

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Lymph nodes are a secondary lymphoid organ — a site where mature lymphocytes are activated by antigen, not where they develop.

## answer_c
Spleen

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The spleen is a secondary lymphoid organ that filters blood and initiates responses to blood-borne antigen, not a site of lymphocyte development.

## answer_d
Tonsils

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Tonsils are secondary lymphoid (mucosa-associated) tissue that samples antigen at the pharynx, not a primary developmental organ.

## title
A cell utilizing lysosomal enzymes to kill pathogens is:

## question
A cell utilizing lysosomal enzymes to kill pathogens is:

## subject
imm

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
NK cells

## explanation_a
Incorrect. NK cells kill their targets by releasing perforin and granzymes, not lysosomal enzymes.

## answer_b
Tc cells

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Cytotoxic T cells (Tc) also kill via perforin and granzyme release, a different mechanism from lysosomal-enzyme killing.

## answer_c
Neutrophils

## explanation_c
Correct. After phagocytosing a pathogen, the neutrophil's principal killing mechanism is degranulation of lysosomal (azurophilic and specific granule) enzymes into the phagosome, working alongside the oxidative burst to destroy the ingested organism. This distinguishes phagocyte killing of ingested extracellular pathogens from the perforin/granzyme killing NK and cytotoxic T cells use against infected or tumour host cells.

## answer_d
Th cells

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Helper T cells (Th) coordinate the immune response via cytokines and costimulation; they do not themselves kill pathogens using lysosomal enzymes.

## title
The role of primary lymphoid organs in the immune system:

## question
The role of primary lymphoid organs in the immune system:

## subject
imm

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Expose T cells to foreign antigens

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Exposing T cells to foreign antigen is a secondary-lymphoid-organ role (lymph nodes, spleen), not a primary-organ one.

## answer_b
Primary site for Ab synthesis & release

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Antibody synthesis and release happens in secondary lymphoid organs, where activated plasma cells reside, not in the primary organs.

## answer_c
Filter blood & trap blood borne antigens

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Filtering blood and trapping blood-borne antigen describes the spleen, a secondary lymphoid organ.

## answer_d
Provide an environment for maturation of B & T cells

## explanation_d
Correct. Bone marrow and thymus are primary lymphoid organs and are identified as the sites of B- and T-cell development and maturation — providing the environment in which lymphocytes complete this process before ever meeting antigen. This developmental role is what distinguishes primary lymphoid organs from secondary ones, which instead activate already-mature lymphocytes.

## answer_e
Line mucosal surfaces of the body for efficient Ag contact

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Lining mucosal surfaces for antigen contact describes mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT), a secondary lymphoid tissue.

## title
Haematopoietic stem cells are pluripotent, in that:

## question
Haematopoietic stem cells are pluripotent, in that:

## subject
imm

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
They are Ag specific cells

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Antigen specificity is a property of mature T and B lymphocyte receptors, not of haematopoietic stem cells, which have no antigen receptor at all.

## answer_b
They are T & B cells of different specificities

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Haematopoietic stem cells are not themselves T and B cells; T and B lymphocytes are just two of the many lineages a stem cell can ultimately give rise to.

## answer_c
They can develop into any blood cell

## explanation_c
Correct. Haematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow are pluripotent. A single stem cell can give rise to any blood cell lineage, both myeloid (granulocytes, monocytes, red cells, platelets) and lymphoid (T cells, B cells, NK cells), rather than being restricted to one lineage. This is a property of the progenitor cell itself, quite separate from the antigen specificity that mature T- and B-cell receptors later acquire.

## answer_d
They produce cells of a single lineage

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Producing cells of a single lineage describes a unipotent progenitor, not a pluripotent stem cell — pluripotency means the opposite, the ability to give rise to multiple/any lineage.
