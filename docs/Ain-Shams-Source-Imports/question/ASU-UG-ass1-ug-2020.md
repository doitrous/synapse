## title
Genitofemoral nerve injury during radical nephroureterectomy

## question
A patient with renal cell carcinoma undergoes radical nephroureterectomy with ligation of the renal artery, renal vein, and ureter. Which structure is most likely to be injured during the ureteric part of the procedure?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Psoas major

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Psoas major is the large muscle the genitofemoral nerve and ureter both travel across; it is a landmark for the dissection, not the structure typically injured.

## answer_b
Genitofemoral nerve

## explanation_b
Correct. The genitofemoral nerve runs on the anterior surface of psoas major, in close proximity to the gonadal vessels, ureter, and lower pole of the kidney, making it the structure most at risk when the renal vessels and ureter are mobilized and ligated for radical nephroureterectomy. Injury produces sensory loss over the anterior scrotum/labia and upper thigh (genital and femoral branches) and, in males, loss of the cremasteric reflex. Surgeons dissecting along psoas major during ureterolysis or gonadal vessel ligation are specifically taught to identify and preserve this nerve.

## answer_c
Gonadal veins

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The gonadal veins run parallel to the ureter and can be ligated deliberately as part of the procedure, but they are vessels, not the nerve most at risk of inadvertent injury described here.

## answer_d
Inferior vena cava

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The inferior vena cava lies medial to the right kidney and is a major structure to protect during caval-side dissection, but it is not the structure classically injured during ureteric mobilization on either side.

## answer_e
Sympathetic trunk

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The sympathetic trunk lies more medially along the vertebral bodies, well away from the lateral retroperitoneal plane where the ureter and renal vessels are typically ligated.

## title
Acute interstitial nephritis from antibiotic hypersensitivity

## question
A patient develops fever, a maculopapular rash, and eosinophilia with red and white cells in the urine within about 3 days of starting a new antibiotic. Which mechanism best explains this presentation?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Acute interstitial nephritis due to a hypersensitivity reaction to the antibiotic

## explanation_a
Correct. Acute interstitial nephritis caused by a hypersensitivity reaction to a drug, classically an antibiotic such as a penicillin, characteristically presents with peripheral and urinary eosinophilia, fever, and a maculopapular rash arising within days of exposure, together with hematuria and pyuria from interstitial inflammation. This drug-hypersensitivity pattern, with its rapid onset relative to a new drug and its allergic features, is the classic teaching vignette for AIN and is distinguished from other causes of acute kidney injury by exactly these features.

## answer_b
Ischemic acute tubular necrosis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Ischemic acute tubular necrosis follows a hypotensive or nephrotoxic insult and produces muddy-brown granular casts, not eosinophilia, fever, or a rash.

## answer_c
Postinfectious (poststreptococcal) glomerulonephritis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Postinfectious glomerulonephritis follows a streptococcal infection by 1-3 weeks and presents with a nephritic picture (hematuria, hypertension, edema), not the eosinophilia-plus-rash pattern of a drug hypersensitivity reaction.

## answer_d
IgA nephropathy flare

## explanation_d
Incorrect. IgA nephropathy classically flares with gross hematuria concurrent with (not days after) a mucosal infection, and does not produce eosinophilia or a rash.

## title
Macula densa basal Golgi apparatus

## question
On electron microscopy, macula densa cells of the distal tubule characteristically show which ultrastructural feature, reflecting their sensory/paracrine rather than absorptive function?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
An apically located Golgi apparatus, as in typical absorptive tubular epithelium

## explanation_a
Incorrect. An apical Golgi is the arrangement seen in typical absorptive tubular epithelium (e.g., proximal tubule), not in the macula densa, which shows the reverse.

## answer_b
A basally located Golgi apparatus

## explanation_b
Correct. Macula densa cells show a reversed polarity compared with most tubular epithelium, with the Golgi apparatus located basally, facing the extraglomerular mesangium and juxtaglomerular apparatus, consistent with their role sensing luminal sodium chloride and signalling paracrine mediators toward the JGA rather than absorbing filtrate. This basal Golgi location is a distinctive, frequently tested exception to the usual apical-Golgi pattern of tubular cells.

## answer_c
Numerous apical microvilli forming a prominent brush border

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A prominent apical brush border of microvilli is characteristic of the proximal convoluted tubule, which maximizes surface area for bulk reabsorption, not the macula densa.

## answer_d
Abundant basal infoldings with interdigitating mitochondria, as in principal cells

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Extensive basal infoldings with interdigitating mitochondria are characteristic of principal cells of the collecting duct (an ion-transporting, energy-intensive epithelium), not the sensory macula densa.

## title
Tram-track splitting in type 1 MPGN

## question
Which glomerular lesion is characterized by tram-track (double-contour) splitting of the glomerular basement membrane with subendothelial immune-complex deposits?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Type 1 membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis

## explanation_a
Correct. Type 1 membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis shows mesangial and endocapillary proliferation with mesangial cell interposition between the endothelium and basement membrane, producing the classic "tram-track" double-contour appearance of the GBM on light and electron microscopy, together with subendothelial immune-complex deposits. It is a well-recognized cause of both nephrotic-range proteinuria and hypocomplementemia and is a frequently tested histologic pattern for exactly this reason.

Membranous nephropathy

Incorrect. Membranous nephropathy shows subepithelial (not subendothelial) deposits producing a "spike-and-dome" appearance on silver stain, without tram-track splitting.

## answer_c
Minimal change disease

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Minimal change disease shows normal glomeruli by light microscopy with only diffuse podocyte foot-process effacement on electron microscopy; there is no basement membrane splitting or immune deposits.

## answer_d
Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis shows segmental scarring of some glomeruli, not diffuse tram-track GBM splitting.

## title
Afferent arteriolar dilatation increases renal blood flow

## question
Dilatation of the afferent arteriole, at constant efferent arteriolar tone, produces which effect on renal blood flow?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Increases renal blood flow

## explanation_a
Correct. Dilatation of the afferent arteriole lowers preglomerular resistance, which raises glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure and increases both renal plasma flow and, at constant efferent tone, glomerular filtration rate. This is why physiologic states that dilate the afferent arteriole (such as prostaglandin-mediated autoregulation, or NSAIDs blocking that dilatation) directly affect renal blood flow, not just GFR.

## answer_b
Decreases renal blood flow

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Afferent arteriolar constriction, not dilatation, is what reduces renal blood flow by raising preglomerular resistance.

## answer_c
No change in renal blood flow, only in GFR

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Afferent arteriolar tone affects total renal vascular resistance directly, so renal blood flow changes together with GFR, not independently of it.

## answer_d
Decreases renal blood flow but increases GFR

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This describes the effect of efferent arteriolar constriction (GFR rises while RBF may fall at higher degrees of constriction), the opposite scenario from afferent dilatation.

## title
Sodium bicarbonate can cause hypokalemia

## question
A recognized adverse effect of sodium bicarbonate administration is which electrolyte disturbance?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Hypokalemia

## explanation_a
Correct. Sodium bicarbonate raises extracellular pH, and the resulting alkalosis drives potassium into cells in exchange for hydrogen ions leaving the intracellular compartment, lowering serum potassium. This intracellular shift is a recognized adverse effect of bicarbonate therapy and is, in fact, exploited therapeutically as one of the shift-based treatments for hyperkalemia.

## answer_b
Hyperkalemia

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Hyperkalemia is caused by acidosis (the opposite process, hydrogen ions moving into cells and potassium moving out), not by bicarbonate-induced alkalosis.

## answer_c
Hypercalcemia

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Sodium bicarbonate does not characteristically cause hypercalcemia; its major relevant electrolyte effect is the potassium shift described.

## answer_d
Hypernatremia only, with no effect on potassium

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Sodium bicarbonate does have a well-recognized effect on potassium (causing hypokalemia via the alkalosis-driven intracellular shift), so this option is incomplete/incorrect.

## title
Safe correction ceiling for severe hyponatremia

## question
A patient presents with a serum sodium of 116 mEq/L. To avoid osmotic demyelination syndrome, to what level should the serum sodium safely be raised within the first 24 hours of correction?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
No more than about 124-126 mEq/L (a rise of about 8-10 mEq/L)

## explanation_a
Correct. To avoid osmotic demyelination syndrome, correction of severe or chronic hyponatremia is limited to a rise in serum sodium of no more than about 8-10 mEq/L in the first 24 hours, so a patient starting at 116 mEq/L should not be raised above roughly 124-126 mEq/L within the first day. This rate-limited approach is standard teaching precisely because overly rapid correction, not the hyponatremia itself, is what precipitates central pontine/extrapontine myelinolysis.

## answer_b
All the way to a normal value of about 140 mEq/L

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Correcting all the way to a normal serum sodium within 24 hours substantially exceeds the safe rate ceiling and risks osmotic demyelination syndrome.

## answer_c
No more than about 118 mEq/L (a rise of about 2 mEq/L)

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A rise of only about 2 mEq/L is overly conservative relative to the accepted 8-10 mEq/L/24h ceiling and would undertreat a genuinely severe, symptomatic hyponatremia.

## answer_d
It should be lowered further before any correction is attempted

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Severe hyponatremia should be corrected, not further lowered; the teaching point concerns the rate and ceiling of correction, not withholding it.

Foot process effacement without proliferation

## title
E. coli causes 90% of uncomplicated UTIs

## question
In a patient with a structurally and functionally normal (anatomically unremarkable) urinary tract, which organism causes approximately 90% of uncomplicated urinary tract infections?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Escherichia coli

## explanation_a
Correct. Uropathogenic Escherichia coli accounts for approximately 90% of uncomplicated urinary tract infections in patients with a normal urinary tract, far more than any other single organism, owing to its adhesins (type 1 and P fimbriae) that allow it to colonize the periurethral area and ascend into the bladder. This dominance is why empiric therapy for uncomplicated cystitis is chosen to reliably cover E. coli.

## answer_b
