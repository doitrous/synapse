## title
Huntington disease is caused by which genetic abnormality, with what microscopic feature?

## question
Huntington disease is caused by a CAG trinucleotide repeat expansion in which gene, and what is its characteristic microscopic brain feature?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The huntingtin gene, with atrophy and gliosis of the caudate nucleus

## explanation_a
Correct. Huntington disease results from a CAG trinucleotide repeat expansion in the huntingtin (HTT) gene; its characteristic pathological feature is atrophy of the striatum, especially the caudate nucleus, with accompanying gliosis, reflecting selective early neuronal loss there. Longer CAG repeat expansions correlate with earlier disease onset, a phenomenon known as anticipation across generations.

## answer_b
The huntingtin gene, with Lewy bodies in the substantia nigra

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Lewy bodies in the substantia nigra are the characteristic feature of Parkinson disease, not Huntington disease.

## answer_c
The APP gene, with amyloid plaques in the cortex

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The APP gene and cortical amyloid plaques are characteristic of Alzheimer disease, not Huntington disease.

## answer_d
The SOD1 gene, with anterior horn cell loss

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The SOD1 gene and anterior horn cell loss are associated with familial amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, not Huntington disease.

## title
Which tongue muscle is supplied by contralateral corticobulbar fibres only?

## question
A patient with a unilateral upper motor neuron lesion has tongue deviation on protrusion. Which tongue muscle's unique contralateral-only corticobulbar supply explains this?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Genioglossus

## explanation_a
Correct. Genioglossus is the exception among tongue muscles in receiving corticobulbar (upper motor neuron) input from the contralateral cerebral hemisphere only; a unilateral upper motor neuron lesion therefore weakens it selectively, causing the protruded tongue to deviate toward the side of the lesion. This is the anatomical basis of the classic clinical rule that the tongue deviates toward the side of the lesion in an upper motor neuron lesion, but away from it in a lower motor neuron (hypoglossal nerve) lesion.

## answer_b
Styloglossus

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Styloglossus, like most tongue muscles, receives bilateral corticobulbar supply, so a unilateral upper motor neuron lesion does not selectively weaken it the way it weakens genioglossus.

## answer_c
Hyoglossus

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Hyoglossus also receives bilateral corticobulbar supply, unlike genioglossus's unique contralateral-only pattern.

## answer_d
Palatoglossus

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Palatoglossus is functionally and developmentally distinct (innervated by the vagus/pharyngeal plexus, not the hypoglossal nerve), and is not the muscle responsible for this classic tongue-deviation sign.

Which is the principal excitatory neurotransmitter of the CNS?

## question
Which amino acid is the principal excitatory neurotransmitter of the central nervous system?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Glutamate

## explanation_a
Correct. Glutamate is the principal excitatory neurotransmitter of the central nervous system, acting on ionotropic (NMDA, AMPA, kainate) and metabotropic receptors to mediate fast excitatory transmission at the great majority of excitatory CNS synapses. This excitatory role complements GABA, the CNS's principal inhibitory neurotransmitter, and the balance between the two underlies normal CNS signalling.

## answer_b
GABA

## explanation_b
Incorrect. GABA is the principal inhibitory, not excitatory, neurotransmitter of the CNS.

## answer_c
Glycine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Glycine is also chiefly an inhibitory neurotransmitter, especially in the spinal cord and brainstem, not the principal excitatory one.

## answer_d
Taurine

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Taurine has some modulatory/inhibitory roles but is not recognised as the CNS's principal excitatory neurotransmitter.

## title
The substantia gelatinosa (of Rolando) corresponds to which Rexed lamina?

## question
In the spinal cord grey matter's dorsal horn, the substantia gelatinosa (of Rolando) corresponds to which Rexed lamina?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Lamina II

## explanation_a
Correct. The substantia gelatinosa (of Rolando), a band of small, densely packed neurons important for modulating pain and temperature signals, corresponds to Rexed lamina II, immediately deep to lamina I, present in the dorsal horn at all spinal cord levels including the cervical segments. This lamina lies immediately deep to lamina I (the posteromarginal nucleus), together forming the anatomical substrate of the gate control theory of pain modulation.

## answer_b
Lamina I

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Lamina I is the posteromarginal nucleus, the most dorsal lamina, immediately superficial to the substantia gelatinosa, not the substantia gelatinosa itself.

## answer_c
Lamina IV

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Lamina IV lies deeper in the dorsal horn and is not the substantia gelatinosa.

## answer_d
Lamina IX

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Lamina IX is in the ventral horn, containing motor neurons, an entirely different location from the substantia gelatinosa.

## title
In the Pacinian corpuscle, how does the sensory nerve fibre run structurally?

## question
Which structural feature distinguishes the Pacinian corpuscle from the Meissner corpuscle?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Its single sensory nerve fibre runs axially through the centre of concentric onion-skin lamellae

## explanation_a
Correct. The Pacinian corpuscle's defining structural feature is a single sensory nerve fibre running axially, straight through the centre of the receptor's concentric "onion-skin" lamellae — a histological distinction from the Meissner corpuscle's different internal architecture. This axial arrangement, within the receptor's onion-skin lamellae, underlies the Pacinian corpuscle's particular sensitivity to vibration and rapidly changing deep pressure.

## answer_b
Its nerve terminal coils among interdigitating lamellar cells near the epidermis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This coiled, interdigitating architecture near the epidermis describes the Meissner corpuscle, not the Pacinian corpuscle's axial nerve fibre.

## answer_c
It has no capsule surrounding the nerve terminal

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The Pacinian corpuscle is a large, well-encapsulated receptor with prominent concentric lamellae, not an unencapsulated ending.

## answer_d
It contains multiple nerve fibres arranged in a branching network

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The Pacinian corpuscle has a single axial sensory nerve fibre, not multiple branching fibres.
