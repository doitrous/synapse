Antiphospholipid syndrome is an autoimmune disease against

## question
Antiphospholipid syndrome, is an autoimmune disease against

## subject
pharm

## status

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Fatty acids

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Antibodies in this syndrome are not directed against free fatty acids; fatty acids are not the antigenic target the disease is named for.

## answer_b
Surfactant

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Lung surfactant is a different clinical topic entirely (relevant to neonatal respiratory distress syndrome), not the autoimmune target in antiphospholipid syndrome.

## answer_c
Acidic phospholipids

## explanation_c
Correct. Antiphospholipid syndrome is an autoimmune disease in which the body forms antibodies against acidic (negatively charged) phospholipids — a class that includes phosphatidylserine, phosphatidylinositol, phosphatidylglycerol and cardiolipin. These autoantibodies predispose to abnormal blood clotting (thrombosis) and to pregnancy complications such as recurrent miscarriage.

## answer_d
Cholesterol

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Cholesterol is a neutral, uncharged lipid, not one of the acidic phospholipids this syndrome's antibodies target; it is not the antigen this disease is defined by.

## question
Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS) occurs in premature infant due to deficiency of

## answer_a
Dipalmitoyl Phosphatidyl Serine

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Phosphatidylserine is an acidic phospholipid relevant to blood clotting and cell signalling (and to antiphospholipid syndrome), not the specific surfactant phospholipid whose deficiency causes RDS.

## answer_b
Dipalmitoyl Phosphatidyl Ethanolamine

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Phosphatidylethanolamine (cephalin) is a major membrane phospholipid with roles in blood clotting and the myelin sheath, but it is not the main surface-tension-lowering component of lung surfactant.

## answer_c
Dipalmitoyl Phosphatidyl Choline

## explanation_c
Correct. Dipalmitoyl phosphatidylcholine (dipalmitoyl lecithin, DPPC) is the major surface-tension-lowering phospholipid of lung surfactant. In premature infants, the type II pneumocytes that synthesise DPPC are often not yet mature enough to produce sufficient amounts, so the alveoli cannot maintain the low surface tension needed to stay open at the end of expiration, causing respiratory distress syndrome.

## answer_d
Diphosphatidyl glycerol

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Diphosphatidylglycerol (cardiolipin) is a mitochondrial inner-membrane phospholipid important for electron transport chain function, not the primary determinant of alveolar surface tension in neonatal lungs.

## title
Which out of the following fatty acids is a precursor of prostaglandins?

## question
Which out of the following fatty acids is a precursor of prostaglandins?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Arachidonic acid

## explanation_a
Correct. Arachidonic acid, a 20-carbon omega-6 polyunsaturated fatty acid released from membrane phospholipids by phospholipase A2, is the direct substrate for prostaglandin H synthase (cyclo-oxygenase), which converts it into the cyclic eicosanoids — the prostaglandins and thromboxanes. Lipoxygenase acts on the same arachidonic acid pool to make the acyclic leukotrienes, so arachidonic acid is the single starting point for the whole eicosanoid family.

## answer_b
Oleic acid

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Oleic acid is a monounsaturated omega-9 fatty acid that is not a substrate for prostaglandin H synthase; it plays no role in eicosanoid synthesis.

## answer_c
Linoleic acid

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Linoleic acid is the essential omega-6 dietary precursor that the body elongates and desaturates to make arachidonic acid, but linoleic acid itself is one metabolic step removed from being the direct eicosanoid precursor — that role belongs to arachidonic acid.

## answer_d
Linolenic acid

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Alpha-linolenic acid is the essential omega-3 precursor of a separate eicosanoid family (via eicosapentaenoic acid), not the omega-6 pathway that produces the classic prostaglandins this question asks about.

## title
Cholesterol is a precursor for all of the following except

## question
Cholesterol is a precursor for all of the following except

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Bile acids

## explanation_a
Incorrect as the "except" answer. Bile acids (such as cholic acid) are synthesised from cholesterol in the liver, so cholesterol is genuinely their precursor.

## answer_b
Steroid hormones

## explanation_b
Incorrect as the "except" answer. Every steroid hormone (cortisol, aldosterone, testosterone, oestrogen, progesterone) is synthesised starting from cholesterol; the steroid nucleus itself comes from cholesterol's own ring structure.

## answer_c
Sex hormones

## explanation_c
Incorrect as the "except" answer. The sex hormones (testosterone, oestrogen, progesterone) are a subset of the steroid hormones and are made from cholesterol via the same steroidogenic pathway.

## answer_d
Vitamin D

## explanation_d
Incorrect as the "except" answer. Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) is synthesised in the skin from 7-dehydrocholesterol, itself derived from cholesterol, under the action of UV light — cholesterol is genuinely vitamin D's precursor.

## answer_e
Glycerol

## explanation_e
Correct. Glycerol is a simple three-carbon alcohol that forms the backbone of triacylglycerols and glycerophospholipids; it is synthesised from glycolytic or gluconeogenic intermediates (such as dihydroxyacetone phosphate), not from cholesterol. Because cholesterol's steroid ring system has no metabolic path to glycerol's simple carbon backbone, glycerol is the one substance in this list that cholesterol is not a precursor of.

## question
Which out of the following is a storage form of lipid?

## subject

## status

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Phospholipid

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Phospholipids are primarily structural lipids that build cell membrane bilayers, not the body's main energy-storage lipid.

## answer_b
Lipoprotein

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A lipoprotein is a transport particle that carries lipids (including triacylglycerol and cholesterol) through the aqueous bloodstream; it is a delivery vehicle, not itself the stored energy form.

## answer_c
Triacylglycerol

## explanation_c
Correct. Triacylglycerol (three fatty acids esterified to a glycerol backbone) is the body's major storage form of lipid, packed into adipocytes in adipose tissue as a dense, anhydrous energy reserve that can be mobilised by lipolysis when needed. Gram for gram it stores roughly twice the energy of glycogen, precisely because it carries no bound water the way glycogen does.

## answer_d
Sulfo-lipid

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Sulfolipids are minor structural components found in specific tissues (such as sulfatides in the myelin sheath); they are not the body's major lipid storage form.

## question
Which of the following is a Sphingo-phospholipid?

## subject

## answer_a
Cardiolipin

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Cardiolipin (diphosphatidylglycerol) is a glycerophospholipid built on a glycerol backbone, not a sphingosine backbone, so it is not a sphingo-phospholipid.

## answer_b
Cephalin

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Cephalin (phosphatidylethanolamine) is also a glycerophospholipid, built on the same glycerol-based phosphatidic acid backbone as lecithin, not on sphingosine.

Sphingomyelin

## explanation_d
Correct. Sphingomyelin is built by joining a fatty acid to sphingosine through an amide bond to form ceramide, and then joining ceramide to phosphocholine — giving it a sphingosine-based backbone rather than glycerol's. This makes it the one phosphosphingolipid among the options, and, together with the glycosphingolipids, part of the sphingolipid family found especially in the cell membranes of lung and brain tissue, most notably the myelin sheath.
