QST-DEV-ASU-AE-EMBRYO3-NOTOCHORD-FROM-EPIBLAST

## title
Origin of the notochord

## question
The notochord is derived from:

## subject
dev

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Endoderm

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Endoderm is what the invaginating epiblast cells become as they replace the hypoblast during gastrulation -- the notochord forms from a separate, more medial stream of epiblast cells and is not itself endoderm.

## answer_b
Hypoblast

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The hypoblast is displaced rather than converted during gastrulation -- it does not contribute cells to the notochord (or to any of the trilaminar disc's three layers, all of which trace back to the epiblast alone).

## answer_c
Mesoderm

## explanation_c
Incorrect as the direct source, though the notochord is often loosely grouped with mesoderm functionally -- in this bank's own teaching framework the notochord is one of the structures the invaginating epiblast forms directly, alongside the endoderm and the intraembryonic mesoderm, rather than a further derivative of the mesoderm layer itself.

## answer_d
Ectoderm

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The ectoderm is what remains of the epiblast once the invaginating cells have left to form the other layers -- the notochord forms from cells that migrate cranially through the primitive node before that remainder becomes 'ectoderm' as a named layer.

## answer_e
Epiblast

## explanation_e
Correct. Gastrulation begins with epiblast cells proliferating and invaginating through the primitive streak and node. Cells migrating cranially in the midline from the primitive pit form the prenotochordal process, which becomes the notochordal plate and then folds into the definitive notochord. Every layer and axial structure formed during gastrulation -- endoderm, intraembryonic mesoderm, ectoderm and the notochord alike -- traces back to the epiblast as its single source.

QST-DEV-ASU-AE-EMBRYO3-NEURENTERIC-CANAL

## title
Neurenteric canal connection

## question
Amniotic cavity is temporarily connected to the yolk sac cavity via:

## subject
dev

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Breakdown of the oral membrane

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The oral (oropharyngeal) membrane is where ectoderm and endoderm fuse at the cranial end of the embryonic disc -- its breakdown later opens the mouth into the foregut, not a connection between the amniotic cavity and the yolk sac.

## answer_b
Allantois

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The allantois is an outpouching of the hindgut into the connecting stalk, involved in early waste handling and later contributing to bladder development -- it is not the structure that links the amniotic cavity to the yolk sac.

## answer_c
Neurenteric canal

## explanation_c
Correct. During notochord formation, the roof of the notochordal canal lies against ectoderm and its floor is fused with endoderm; when that floor degenerates, it opens the neurenteric canal, a temporary communication running through the region of the primitive node that connects the amniotic cavity above with the yolk sac cavity below. It closes again once the notochordal plate folds into the definitive notochord and the endoderm re-fuses beneath it.

## answer_d
Vitello-intestinal duct

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The vitello-intestinal (vitelline) duct connects the secondary yolk sac to the midgut later in development, once the gut tube has formed by folding -- a different structure from the neurenteric canal, which exists earlier and connects the amniotic cavity to the yolk sac, not the yolk sac to the gut.

## answer_e
Breakdown of the cloacal membrane

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The cloacal membrane is where ectoderm and endoderm fuse at the caudal end of the embryonic disc -- its later breakdown opens the anorectal canal and urogenital sinus to the surface, not a connection between the amniotic cavity and the yolk sac.

QST-DEV-ASU-AE-EMBRYO3-NOTOCHORD-NUCLEUS-PULPOSUS

## title
The notochord's fate

## question
The notochord:

## subject
dev

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Is derived from the roof of the yolk sac

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The notochord traces back to epiblast cells migrating through the primitive pit and node, not to the roof of the yolk sac -- the yolk sac's own roof lies beneath the notochordal plate at one stage, but is not its source.

## answer_b
Forms the nucleus pulposus of the intervertebral disc

## explanation_b
Correct. The vertebral bodies form around the notochord from the sclerotome, the ventromedial part of each somite, and once they do, most of the notochord itself degenerates. The notochordal tissue trapped within each developing intervertebral disc is the exception -- it persists rather than degenerating, becoming the nucleus pulposus, the gel-like core of the adult disc.

## answer_c
Is found caudal to the cloacal membrane

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The notochord runs the length of the embryonic axis, cranial to caudal, and specifically lies cranial to (not caudal to) the cloacal membrane, extending as far forward as the oropharyngeal membrane.

## answer_d
Forms the spinal cord

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The spinal cord forms from the neural tube, an ectodermal derivative -- the notochord's role there is inductive (it induces the overlying ectoderm to thicken into the neural plate), not a direct contribution of notochordal tissue to the cord itself.

## answer_e
Forms the bodies of the vertebrae

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The vertebral bodies form from sclerotome cells of the somites, which migrate to surround the notochord -- the column forms around the notochord rather than from it, and the notochord itself mostly degenerates except for its nucleus-pulposus remnant.

## title
What is NOT found in the umbilical cord at full term

## question
Which of the following is not found in the normal umbilical cord at full term?

## subject
dev

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Right umbilical artery

## explanation_a
Incorrect as an answer -- the definitive cord retains both umbilical arteries, so a right umbilical artery is normally present.

## answer_b
Wharton's jelly

## explanation_b
Incorrect as an answer -- Wharton's jelly, the mucoid connective tissue that packs and protects the cord's vessels, is a normal component of the definitive cord.

## answer_c
Left umbilical vein

## explanation_c
Incorrect as an answer -- the left umbilical vein is the one vein that persists to term; the definitive cord is normally found to contain it.

## answer_d
Allantois (remnant)

## explanation_d
Incorrect as an answer -- a remnant of the allantois persists within the connecting-stalk-derived core of the definitive cord, so some allantoic remnant is normally present, even though its lumen degenerates functionally.

## answer_e
Right umbilical vein

## explanation_e
Correct. Early in development the primitive cord carries two umbilical veins, but as the cord matures the right umbilical vein degenerates and obliterates, leaving only the left umbilical vein to carry oxygenated blood back to the fetus at full term. So the right umbilical vein -- unlike the right umbilical artery, which persists -- is the one vessel that is normally absent from the umbilical cord at full term, and that is exactly what this question is testing.

## title
Location of the allantois

## question
The allantois is present in the:

## subject
dev

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Septum transversum

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The septum transversum is a separate mass of mesoderm contributing to the diaphragm and the fibrous tissue of the liver -- it does not house the allantois.

## answer_b
Chorionic villi

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Chorionic villi are the placental exchange structures growing from the chorion into the intervillous space -- the allantois runs through the connecting stalk toward the chorion, but is not itself located within the villi.

## answer_c
Connecting stalk

## explanation_c
Correct. The allantois is an outpouching of the hindgut that extends into the connecting stalk -- the structure that anchors the embryo to the chorion and later becomes part of the umbilical cord as the disc folds. As the primitive umbilical ring forms, the allantois, together with the umbilical vessels, passes through it within the connecting stalk, and its remnant persists there as part of the definitive cord's core.

Amniotic cavity

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The amniotic cavity is the fluid-filled space that comes to surround the embryo as folding proceeds -- the allantois is a solid-walled outpouching within the connecting stalk, not a location inside the amniotic cavity itself.

## answer_e
Placenta

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The placenta is the site of maternal-fetal exchange, formed from chorionic villi and decidua -- the allantois is a cord structure, not a placental one, though its vessels do extend toward the placenta as the umbilical vessels.

QST-DEV-ASU-AE-EMBRYO3-WHARTON-JELLY-FALSE-KNOT

## title
Umbilical cord anomalies EXCEPT

## question
As regards anomalies of the umbilical cord, the following are true EXCEPT:

## subject
dev

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
A long cord may coil around the neck of the fetus

## explanation_a
Incorrect as an answer -- an excessively long cord can indeed coil around the fetal neck (nuchal cord), a genuine anomaly of length.

## answer_b
A short cord may cause early placental separation

## explanation_b
Incorrect as an answer -- a short cord can restrict fetal movement and, during delivery, can pull and separate the placenta prematurely, a genuine anomaly of length.

## answer_c
A true knot is due to local accumulation of Wharton's jelly

## explanation_c
Correct. Local accumulation of Wharton's jelly, or a wider curve of an umbilical vessel, produces a FALSE knot -- a benign, harmless swelling that only looks like a knot -- not a TRUE knot, which is an actual loop of the cord pulled tight, from excessive fetal movement in a long cord. This statement reverses that pairing, attributing Wharton's-jelly accumulation to the true knot instead of the false one, which is why it is the false statement here.

## answer_d
A false knot does not affect the fetus

## explanation_d
Incorrect as an answer -- a false knot (unlike a tightened true knot, which can compress the vessels and compromise the fetus) causes the fetus no stress, so this statement is true.

## answer_e
In battledore placenta, the cord is attached to the margin of the placenta

## explanation_e
Incorrect as an answer -- battledore placenta, marginal cord attachment, is a genuine and correctly described anomaly of attachment (the same fact tested directly at embryo3-q004).

## title
The definitive yolk sac

## question
The definitive yolk sac:

## subject
dev

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Is lined by mesoderm

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The yolk sac's cavity is lined by endoderm at every stage (primary, secondary and definitive), not mesoderm -- mesoderm covers its outer surface (the splanchnopleuric layer) rather than lining its lumen.

## answer_b
Has a wall made of ectoderm

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The yolk sac wall is endodermal in its lining, with an outer mesodermal covering -- not ectodermal at any stage.

## answer_c
Is found in the umbilical cord

## explanation_c
Correct. As the secondary yolk sac shrinks with folding, its remaining connection to the midgut narrows down to the vitelline duct. What is left outside the abdomen, distal to that narrowing, becomes the definitive yolk sac -- a small vestigial remnant. It persists as part of the umbilical cord's contents, still identifiable near the cord within the membranes at term.

## answer_d
May be found in the amniotic cavity

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The definitive yolk sac is a remnant located within the umbilical cord/chorionic membranes, not free within the amniotic cavity itself.

## answer_e
Is derived from the trophoblast

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The yolk sac derives from the hypoblast/endoderm lineage (via Heuser's membrane lining the blastocoele, then the secondary yolk sac forming from endoderm), not from the trophoblast, which instead gives rise to the placenta's own epithelial components.

QST-DEV-ASU-AE-EMBRYO3-ALLANTOIS-HINDGUT-DIVERTICULUM

## title
The allantois's own origin

## question
As regards the allantois, choose the correct statement:

## subject
dev

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Is a diverticulum of the hindgut

## explanation_a
Correct. The allantois begins as a diverticulum, an outpouching, of the hindgut. It buds from the endoderm of the caudal part of the yolk sac/hindgut region into the connecting stalk. This gives it an endodermal origin, distinct from the umbilical vessels and the Wharton's jelly it simply runs alongside within the cord.

## answer_b
Completely degenerates postnatally

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The allantois does not completely degenerate -- its lumen becomes non-functional and most of it regresses (contributing fibrously to the urachus/median umbilical ligament postnatally), but a remnant persists rather than vanishing entirely.

## answer_c
Is an outpouching of the coelom into the connecting stalk

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The allantois is a diverticulum of the hindgut (endoderm), not an outpouching of the coelom -- the extraembryonic coelom is a separate, mesoderm-lined cavity that happens to run through the same connecting stalk alongside the allantois, but the allantois itself is not made of coelomic outpouching.

## answer_d
Is not found in the umbilical cord at any time

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The allantois, or its remnant, is found in the umbilical cord from the primitive-cord stage onward (tested directly at embryo3-q022 and embryo3-q094) -- it is not absent from the cord at every stage.

## answer_e
All the above

## explanation_e
Incorrect as an answer -- since B, C and D are each false, 'all the above' cannot be the correct choice; only A is correct.

## title
Functions of amnion EXCEPT

## question
As regards amnion, all the following are true EXCEPT:

## subject
dev

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Amniotic cavity contains watery fluid

## explanation_a
Incorrect as an answer -- amniotic fluid is indeed a clear watery fluid, so this statement is true.

## answer_b
It acts as a protective water jacket for the body

## explanation_b
Incorrect as an answer -- in early pregnancy especially, amniotic fluid genuinely acts as a shock-absorbing, protective water jacket around the embryo/fetus, so this statement is true.

## answer_c
It prevents fetal movements

## explanation_c
Correct. In late pregnancy amniotic fluid does the opposite of preventing fetal movement -- it provides the space the fetus needs to move freely, and those movements are what help develop fetal muscle tone and strength. So 'it prevents fetal movements' reverses the real relationship and is the false statement here.

## answer_d
It is a medium for excretion of urine

## explanation_d
Incorrect as an answer -- amniotic fluid is genuinely a medium into which fetal urine is excreted from mid-pregnancy onward, so this statement is true.

## answer_e
It forms the bag of forewaters during labor

## explanation_e
Incorrect as an answer -- the fore bag of amniotic fluid, ahead of the presenting part, genuinely helps the cervix dilate gradually during labour and is called the bag of forewaters, so this statement is true.
