## title
What is the approximate resting membrane potential of skeletal muscle?

## question
Resting membrane potential of skeletal muscle is about:

## subject

## status

## owner
Claude

## vignette
A student is comparing the resting membrane potential of skeletal muscle to that of a typical neuron and must recall the correct value in millivolts.

## format
single best answer

## derived_from
Printed question 38 of the ASU Locomotor Physiology MCQ paper.

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Positive 90 mV

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This value has the correct magnitude but the wrong sign; the resting interior of a skeletal muscle fibre is negative relative to the outside, not positive.

## answer_b
Positive 30 mV

## explanation_b
Incorrect. +30 mV is close to the peak of the action potential's overshoot, not the resting potential.

## answer_c
O mV

## explanation_c
Incorrect. 0 mV describes the membrane briefly during the rising phase of an action potential as it crosses from negative to positive, not the resting state.

## answer_d
Negative 30 mV

## explanation_d
Incorrect. -30 mV is too small a magnitude; it is closer to a partially depolarised (threshold-range) potential than the true resting value.

## answer_e
Negative 90 mV

## explanation_e
Correct. Skeletal muscle fibres, like large nerve fibres, have a resting membrane potential of about -90 mV, among the most negative (most polarised) of excitable cells, reflecting their high resting potassium permeability.

## title
What defines the absolute refractory period of skeletal muscle?

## question
Absolute refractory period in the skeletal muscle is:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette
A student is asked which phase(s) of the action potential correspond to the period during which no stimulus, however strong, can produce a second action potential.

## format
single best answer

## derived_from
Printed question 39 of the ASU Locomotor Physiology MCQ paper.

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
The depolarization phase

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Naming only the depolarisation phase understates the period; the absolute refractory period actually extends a little into the start of repolarisation as well, since sodium channels are still inactivated at that point.

## answer_b
The repolarization phase

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The absolute refractory period includes depolarisation, not just repolarisation; naming repolarisation alone omits its start.

## answer_c
The depolarization and upper third of the repolarization phases

## explanation_c
Correct. The absolute refractory period spans the whole depolarisation phase plus the early (upper) third of repolarisation -- the interval during which essentially all voltage-gated sodium channels are open-then-inactivated and therefore unavailable to open again no matter how strong the stimulus.

## answer_d
The depolarization and the repolarization phases

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This overstates the period: it would include the whole of repolarisation, but by its later two-thirds enough sodium channels have already recovered from inactivation that a strong enough stimulus CAN produce a second action potential (the relative refractory period), so this option is too broad.

## answer_e
The upper half of depolarization and the whole repolarization.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. This starts the period too late (upper half of depolarisation, rather than from its very start) and also extends it across the whole repolarisation phase, which again overstates the true absolute refractory window.

Which phase of a muscle twitch is the longest?

## question
Which is the longest phase of a muscle twitch?

## subject

## status

Claude

single best answer

Printed question 78 of the ASU Locomotor Physiology MCQ paper.

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Latent

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The latent period is brief -- roughly the 2 msec delay between the arrival of the stimulus/action potential and the start of visible tension, the shortest of the named phases.

## answer_b
Contraction

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The contraction phase is shorter than the relaxation phase in a typical skeletal muscle twitch.

Correct. The relaxation phase, during which calcium is actively pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum and tension declines back to baseline, takes longer than either the latent period or the contraction phase in a standard skeletal muscle twitch.

## answer_e
Initial

## explanation_e
Incorrect. "Initial" is not a standard named phase of the twitch; the recognised phases are latent, contraction and relaxation, of which relaxation is the longest.

Load is less than the force generated by the muscle

## explanation_e
Incorrect. If load were less than the force generated, the muscle would shorten and move the load -- that describes isotonic (concentric) contraction, not isometric.

## subject

## format
single best answer

## derived_from
Printed question 60 of the ASU Locomotor Physiology MCQ paper.

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Sarcomere length inside the body is lower than their maximal length (L-max)

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Resting sarcomere length in the body sits close to L-max (the optimal length for actin-myosin overlap, about 2.2 micrometres), not below it -- this is precisely why the body's normal working range sits near the peak of the length-tension curve.

## answer_b
The greater initial length of a muscle produce a more tension during contraction, within limits.

## explanation_b
Correct. Within limits, stretching the muscle to a greater initial (preload) length increases the active tension it can generate on stimulation, because it improves the overlap between actin and myosin filaments up to the optimal length -- this is the length-tension relationship. Beyond the optimal length, further stretch reduces tension again.

## answer_c
Overstretch of the muscle fibers leads to overlap of actin filaments

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Overstretching PULLS filaments apart, reducing their overlap and thus reducing the number of cross-bridges that can form -- it does not increase overlap.

## answer_d
The active tension represents the resistance of muscle elastic elements to stretch.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This describes PASSIVE tension (from the muscle's connective-tissue elastic elements resisting stretch), not active tension, which is generated by the contractile machinery (actin-myosin cross-bridges) itself.

single best answer

## derived_from
Printed question 82 of the ASU Locomotor Physiology MCQ paper.

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The amount of calcium released by the sarcoplasmic reticulum is reduced as length increases

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Calcium release by the sarcoplasmic reticulum is an excitation-contraction coupling event and is not what changes with sarcomere stretch beyond optimum; the length-tension effect is purely mechanical (filament overlap), not calcium availability.

## answer_b
The thin filaments are pulled away from one another, thereby reducing their ability to interact with myosin

## explanation_b
Correct. Beyond the optimum sarcomere length, the thin (actin) filaments from opposite ends of the sarcomere are pulled progressively further apart from the central thick (myosin) filament region, reducing the zone of overlap available for cross-bridge formation and so reducing active tension.

## answer_c
The thick filaments are pulled away from one another, thereby reducing their ability to interact with actin

## explanation_c
Incorrect. It is the thin (actin), not the thick (myosin) filaments, that are pulled apart from each other as the sarcomere is stretched; the thick filaments themselves do not change length or position relative to each other in this way.

## answer_d
The thick filaments overlap one another, thereby reducing their ability to interact with actin

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This describes the opposite (shortened, not stretched) end of the length-tension curve, where the thick filaments can crumple against the Z line at very short sarcomere lengths -- not the stretched condition this question asks about.

## answer_e
The thin filaments overlap one another, thereby reducing their ability to interact with Myosin

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Thin filament overlap with each other (rather than being pulled apart) describes what happens at very SHORT sarcomere lengths, not at lengths beyond the optimum (stretched) condition asked about here.
