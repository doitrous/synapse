## title
What is the classic clinical presentation of multiple sclerosis?

## question
A young adult presents with a relapsing course of motor weakness and sensory paraesthesia, and MRI shows white-matter lesions consistent with demyelination. Which disease does this describe?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Multiple sclerosis

## explanation_a
Correct. Multiple sclerosis classically presents with relapsing-remitting attacks of motor weakness and sensory paraesthesia, correlating with demyelinating plaques disseminated in time and space, seen on MRI as characteristic white-matter lesions. Recognising this pattern is central to the clinical/radiological diagnostic criteria for MS, which require lesions disseminated in both time and space.

## answer_b
Guillain-Barre syndrome

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Guillain-Barre syndrome is an acute, monophasic, ascending peripheral polyneuropathy following infection, not a relapsing CNS demyelinating disease with MRI plaques.

## answer_c
Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis is a progressive motor neuron disease with combined upper and lower motor neuron signs, not a relapsing sensory-motor disease with demyelinating plaques.

## answer_d
Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease is an inherited peripheral neuropathy with a chronic progressive course, not a relapsing CNS disease with plaques on imaging.

## answer_a
Medulloblastoma

## explanation_a
Correct. Medulloblastoma is the most common malignant paediatric brain tumour, classically arising midline in the cerebellum/posterior cranial fossa around age 5, composed histologically of sheets of small, primitive (embryonal) round blue cells. Meningioma

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Meningioma arises from arachnoid cap cells, typically in adults, and is not composed of sheets of primitive embryonal cells.

## answer_d
Craniopharyngioma

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Craniopharyngioma is a suprasellar (not posterior fossa) tumour arising from Rathke's pouch remnants, with a different histological appearance.

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The optic tract

## explanation_a
Correct. The optic tract, formed after the partial decussation of fibres at the optic chiasm, carries the contralateral visual field's information and terminates in the lateral geniculate body, its principal input. From there, geniculocalcarine (optic radiation) fibres continue on to the primary visual cortex in the occipital lobe.

The optic nerve

Incorrect. The optic nerve is the pre-chiasmal segment, carrying one eye's entire visual field; it ends at the chiasm, not at the lateral geniculate body.

The optic radiation

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The optic radiation carries output from the lateral geniculate body onward to the visual cortex, the step after, not the input to it.

## answer_d
The superior colliculus

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The superior colliculus receives some visual input for reflex eye movements, but the lateral geniculate body's principal, conscious-vision input is the optic tract.

## answer_a
Forceps major

## explanation_a
Correct. Forceps major is formed by splenial fibres of the corpus callosum sweeping into each occipital lobe, connecting the two occipital lobes across the midline, making it a commissural fibre tract. The internal capsule

The superior longitudinal fasciculus

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The superior longitudinal fasciculus is an association fibre tract connecting areas within the same hemisphere, not a commissural tract.

Incorrect. Chronic kidney disease is not the condition specifically associated with isoflurane's coronary steal risk.

Well-controlled hypertension

Incorrect. Well-controlled hypertension without coronary artery stenosis does not carry the same coronary steal risk as established ischaemic heart disease.

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Tardive dyskinesia

## explanation_a
Correct. Long-term (chronic) haloperidol therapy causes tardive dyskinesia, involuntary repetitive orofacial movements (lip smacking, tongue protrusion, chewing), attributed to dopamine receptor supersensitivity after prolonged blockade, and often irreversible even after stopping the drug. Akathisia

Incorrect. Akathisia is a subjective restlessness occurring early in antipsychotic treatment, not the late orofacial dyskinesia of chronic use.

## answer_d
Drug-induced parkinsonism

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Drug-induced parkinsonism (rigidity, bradykinesia) occurs early to mid-treatment and is reversible, distinct from the late, often-irreversible orofacial movements of tardive dyskinesia.

Which antiepileptic drug is considered safest for a child with uncomplicated absence epilepsy, with the least effect on cognition and learning?

## subject
neuro

## status

## source

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Ethosuximide

## explanation_a
Correct. Ethosuximide blocks T-type calcium channels in thalamic neurons, the mechanism underlying absence seizures, and is regarded as the safest option for uncomplicated childhood absence epilepsy, with a more favourable cognitive/learning profile than the alternatives. This favourable safety profile makes it the preferred choice when sparing a child's cognition and learning during long-term antiepileptic therapy is a priority.

## answer_b
Valproate

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Valproate also covers absence seizures but carries a broader systemic and, in females, teratogenic/reproductive safety burden, making it a less favoured first choice when sparing cognition is the priority.

## answer_c
Phenytoin

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Phenytoin is not effective for, and can worsen, absence seizures, and is not the safest choice here.

## answer_d
Carbamazepine

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Carbamazepine can worsen absence seizures and is not indicated for this seizure type.

## answer_a
REM sleep

## explanation_a
Correct. Growth hormone's largest, most reliable secretory pulse occurs during slow-wave (deep, N3) sleep; correspondingly, its secretion decreases during REM sleep, when many neuroendocrine and autonomic patterns shift away from the deep non-REM state. Stage N1 sleep

## explanation_b

Stage N2 sleep

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Stage N2 is an intermediate non-REM stage; growth hormone's characteristic decrease is specifically described relative to REM sleep, not N2.

## answer_d
The transition into sleep (sleep onset)

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Sleep onset itself is not the stage of decreased growth hormone secretion; the described decrease is specific to REM sleep.

## answer_a
The anterior hypothalamus

## explanation_a
Correct. The anterior hypothalamus (anterior/preoptic area) functions as the heat-loss centre, triggering cooling responses (cutaneous vasodilation, sweating) when core temperature rises; direct injury to this region can raise temperature through heat-loss failure rather than true infective fever. Because the anterior hypothalamus is not itself an infection sensor, direct injury to it can raise temperature through heat-loss failure rather than a true infective fever.

The posterior hypothalamus

The lateral hypothalamus

Incorrect. The lateral hypothalamus is chiefly associated with the hunger/feeding centre, not thermoregulatory heat loss.

## answer_d
The paraventricular nucleus

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The paraventricular nucleus is chiefly associated with oxytocin/vasopressin synthesis and autonomic/neuroendocrine output, not the primary heat-loss centre.

## answer_a
Delta waves

## explanation_a
Correct. Delta waves are the lowest-frequency (under 4 Hz), highest-amplitude EEG rhythm, and they characterise deep, slow-wave sleep (stage N3), the stage with the highest arousal threshold. Alpha waves

Beta waves

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Beta waves are the fastest, lowest-amplitude rhythm, seen in alert wakefulness and REM sleep, not deep slow-wave sleep.

## answer_d
Theta waves

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Theta waves are seen in light (stage N1) sleep and drowsiness, an intermediate frequency band, not the signature of deep slow-wave sleep.

The limbic system

Correct. Memory consolidation depends on the limbic system, chiefly the hippocampus and adjacent medial temporal lobe structures together with the Papez circuit, which bind distributed cortical representations of an experience into a lasting memory over time. The cerebellum

The reticular activating system

Incorrect. The reticular activating system governs arousal and consciousness level, not the specific process of memory consolidation.
