# Item

## title
What does the karyotype 46,XY represent?

## question
The 46,XY chromosomal pattern in a human represents:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Diploid number of chromosomes

## explanation_a
Correct. Human somatic cells normally carry 46 chromosomes — 22 pairs of autosomes plus one pair of sex chromosomes — which is the diploid (2n) number: two complete sets, one from each parent. Writing the karyotype as 46,XY states the total chromosome count first, then the sex chromosome constitution, so 46,XY names a normal diploid male. Recognising 46 as the expected diploid count is the baseline every other karyotype in this set is compared against — anything above or below it, or any extra notation after the sex chromosomes, signals a numerical or structural aberration.

## answer_b
Tetraploid number of chromosomes

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Tetraploid means four complete chromosome sets (4n = 92 in humans), double the diploid number; 46 is one diploid set, not four.

## answer_c
Haploid number of chromosomes

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Haploid means one complete set (n = 23 in humans), the number found in a gamete; 46 is twice that, the diploid somatic-cell number.

## answer_d
Example for aneuploid

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Aneuploidy means a chromosome count that is not an exact multiple of the haploid number — one chromosome too many or too few, such as 45 or 47. 46 is exactly the expected diploid count, so it is the normal (euploid) pattern, not an example of aneuploidy.

## answer_e
Triploid number of chromosomes

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Triploid means three complete chromosome sets (3n = 69 in humans); 46 is one set (diploid), not three.

## title
What describes the movement of a chromosome segment onto a non-homologous chromosome?

## question
The movement of a segment of a chromosome from one chromosome to another non-homologous chromosome is called:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Inversion

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Inversion is a segment of a single chromosome breaking out and reinserting in the reverse orientation within the same chromosome; it does not move material to a different, non-homologous chromosome.

## answer_b
Translocation

## explanation_b
Correct. Translocation is the movement of a chromosome segment from one chromosome onto a different, non-homologous chromosome. When the exchange is reciprocal and no genetic material is gained or lost overall, it is a balanced translocation, which usually leaves the carrier phenotypically normal but at risk of producing unbalanced gametes; when material is genuinely gained or lost, it is unbalanced and typically causes a clinical syndrome. Recognising translocation as specifically an inter-chromosomal (not intra-chromosomal) rearrangement is what separates it from inversion, deletion and duplication, which all happen within a single chromosome.

## answer_c
Deletion

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Deletion is the loss of a segment of a chromosome; it does not involve that segment relocating onto a different chromosome, it is simply removed.

## answer_d
Duplication

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Duplication is an extra copy of a segment appearing on the same chromosome; it does not describe material moving to a non-homologous chromosome.

## title
What change to the procedure produces high-resolution chromosome banding?

## question
To do high resolution banding of chromosomes, what change to the procedure must be made relative to conventional karyotype?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Use more cells

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The number of cells examined is not what changes the resolution of banding; the printed key names a change in timing within the cell cycle, not a change in sample quantity.

## answer_b
Lengthen the staining procedure

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Extending how long the banding stain is applied does not produce the earlier-stage, less-condensed chromosomes that high-resolution banding relies on; the key change is in when division is stopped, not how long staining takes.

## answer_c
Amplify the chromosomes

## explanation_c
Incorrect. High-resolution karyotyping does not amplify (make additional copies of) the chromosomal material; it examines the same chromosomes at an earlier, less-condensed stage of mitosis.

## answer_d
Use a more sensitive stain

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The improvement in high-resolution karyotyping comes from the state of the chromosomes examined (less condensed, so more bands are resolvable along their length), not from a more sensitive staining reagent.

## answer_e
Arrest the cells earlier in mitosis

## explanation_e
Correct. High-resolution karyotyping arrests dividing cells at an earlier stage of mitosis — prophase or prometaphase — instead of the metaphase arrest used for a conventional karyotype. At this earlier stage the chromosomes are still relatively uncondensed and therefore longer, which spreads out the banding pattern along each chromosome's length and allows more, finer bands to be resolved than a fully condensed metaphase chromosome can show. This single change in timing is the entire mechanistic basis of "high-resolution" banding.
