## title
Example of a secondary cartilaginous joint

## question
Which of the following is an example of a secondary cartilaginous joint?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Symphysis pubis

## explanation_a
Correct. The symphysis pubis is the standard worked example of a secondary cartilaginous joint (a symphysis): the two pubic bones are covered by hyaline cartilage and united across the midline by a pad of fibrocartilage, giving a joint that is slightly mobile rather than fully fixed or freely synovial. Secondary cartilaginous joints characteristically lie in the median plane of the body -- the symphysis pubis and the intervertebral discs are the two classic examples -- which distinguishes them from primary cartilaginous joints (temporary, hyaline-only, growth-plate type) and from the three named fibrous-joint types.

## answer_b
Sternocostal joint

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The sternocostal joints (between the costal cartilages and the sternum) are mostly synovial plane joints, not secondary cartilaginous joints.

## answer_c
Costochondral joint

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The costochondral joints (between rib and costal cartilage) are primary cartilaginous joints, a continuous bar of hyaline cartilage with no joint cavity, not secondary cartilaginous ones.

## answer_d
Sacroiliac joint

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The sacroiliac joint is a synovial joint (with an accessory fibrous component), not a secondary cartilaginous joint.

## answer_e
Sternoclavicular joint

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The sternoclavicular joint is a synovial saddle joint with an intra-articular disc, not a secondary cartilaginous joint.

Type of the interphalangeal joints

## question
What is the type of the interphalangeal joints?

## subject
msk

## status

## owner
Claude

## vignette

C

## answer_a
Condyloid

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Condyloid (ellipsoid) describes the metacarpophalangeal and wrist joints, which add abduction/adduction to flexion/extension -- a movement the interphalangeal joints do not have.

## answer_b
Plane

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Plane describes joints such as the acromioclavicular joint, whose flat surfaces glide on one another, not the interphalangeal joints.

## answer_c
Hinge

## explanation_c
Correct. The interphalangeal joints are synovial hinge joints: uniaxial, permitting flexion and extension only, never abduction, adduction or rotation. This is the same joint-shape classification scheme that places the elbow among the hinge joints and the wrist among the ellipsoid joints -- each named joint of the limb has one shape class, and no two neighbouring joints in the same digit share a type.

## answer_d
Pivot

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Pivot describes the superior and inferior radio-ulnar joints, built for rotation (pronation/supination), not the interphalangeal joints.

## answer_e
Saddle

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Saddle describes the carpometacarpal joint of the thumb, not the interphalangeal joints.

## title
Function of the tendon

## question
The function of the tendon is to link:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
A muscle to a bone

## explanation_a
Correct. A tendon is a cord of dense regular connective tissue that attaches a muscle indirectly to bone, transmitting the muscle's pull to its skeletal insertion. This is one of a muscle's three possible modes of attachment -- direct (fibres inserting straight into periosteum), by tendon (a cord, for a concentrated pull), or by aponeurosis (a flattened sheet, for a pull spread over a wide area) -- and it is distinct from a ligament, which instead connects one bone to another rather than muscle to bone.

## answer_b
A bone to a cartilage

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Linking a bone to a cartilage is not a tendon's role; cartilage-to-bone continuity is how a primary cartilaginous joint (such as a costochondral joint) is built, not a soft-tissue attachment.

## answer_c
A muscle to skin

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A small number of muscles (mostly facial muscles of expression) do insert into skin rather than bone, but this is not the defining function of a tendon in general.

## answer_d
A cartilage to skin

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Cartilage-to-skin is not a recognised anatomical attachment at all.

## answer_e
A bone to a bone

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Bone-to-bone is the role of a ligament, not a tendon.

Example of a multipennate muscle

## question
Which one of the following muscles is a multipennate muscle?

## subject

## vignette

## correct_answer

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Flexor pollicis longus is the standard worked example of a unipennate muscle (tendon down one side only), not multipennate.

## answer_b
Rectus femoris

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Rectus femoris is the standard worked example of a bipennate muscle (a central tendon with fibres on both sides), not multipennate.

## answer_c
Dorsal interossei

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The dorsal interossei are small bipennate hand muscles, not multipennate.

## answer_e
Deltoid

## explanation_e
Correct. Deltoid is the standard worked example of a multipennate muscle: several tendinous septa run through its substance, each with fibres attaching obliquely on both sides, like several feathers stacked side by side. Pennation of any degree packs more, shorter fibres into a given muscle volume than a parallel/strap arrangement would, trading range of movement for extra power -- multipennate muscles like deltoid are built for strength across a wide arc, not for a long excursion.

## title
Muscle aiding another in the same movement

## question
What is the term for a muscle that aids another in the same movement by cancelling its unwanted side effects at other joints? (matching set: opposite action, prevents movement of another joint, initiates a movement, aids another in the same movement)

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Synergist

## explanation_a
Correct. The synergist contracts to eliminate unwanted movements at the other joints the prime mover crosses, so that the prime mover's action on the joint that actually matters becomes maximal -- for example, the wrist extensors acting as synergists to the long finger flexors, preventing the wrist from flexing as the fingers grip. This is the role most easily confused with the fixator: a fixator holds the prime mover's own origin still, while a synergist cancels the prime mover's own unwanted effect at a joint it crosses on its way to the joint it is meant to act on.

## answer_b
Agonist

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The agonist (prime mover) is the muscle initiating the movement itself, not the one cancelling its side effects elsewhere.

## answer_c
Antagonist

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The antagonist opposes the prime mover's action; it does not aid it.

## answer_d
Fixator

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A fixator stabilises the prime mover's own origin or the joint it acts on directly, a related but distinct role from cancelling the prime mover's unwanted effect at a different joint it crosses.

## explanation_b
