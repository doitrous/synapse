## title
In a double-stranded DNA molecule, what is the ratio of purines to pyrimidines?

## question
In a double-stranded DNA molecule, what is the ratio of purines to pyrimidines?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Variable

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This is the answer of a student who has not connected Watson-Crick base pairing to base class: because every purine (A or G) pairs with exactly one pyrimidine (T or C respectively) across the duplex, the ratio is fixed, not variable.

## answer_b
Determined by the base sequence in RNA

## explanation_b
Incorrect. RNA sequence has no bearing on a DNA molecule's own purine:pyrimidine ratio; this option is a distractor naming an unrelated molecule to test whether the student is reasoning about DNA pairing at all.

## answer_c
Cannot be predicted

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The ratio can always be predicted from the pairing rule itself, regardless of the specific sequence — this option picks a student who has not realised that base pairing constrains composition independently of sequence.

## answer_d
Always 1:1

## explanation_d
Correct. In double-stranded DNA, adenine (a purine) pairs only with thymine (a pyrimidine), and guanine (a purine) pairs only with cytosine (a pyrimidine). Every purine on one strand is matched by exactly one pyrimidine on the same base pair, so across the whole duplex the total number of purines always equals the total number of pyrimidines, whatever the actual A, T, G and C percentages are. This is a direct structural consequence of Watson-Crick pairing and holds even though the A:T and G:C ratios individually can vary between species and even between regions of the same genome.

## answer_e
Always 2:1

## explanation_e
Incorrect. This ratio does not correspond to any real base-pairing rule; it may be picked by a student guessing at a fixed but wrong number rather than reasoning from how purines and pyrimidines pair.

## title
How do histones interact with DNA to package it into the nucleus?

## question
In order to package the roughly 3x10^9 nucleotides of DNA into the nucleus, DNA is wound around histones. What kind of interaction underlies histone-DNA binding?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Hydrophobic interaction between histone residues and phosphates

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The DNA backbone's phosphate groups are strongly polar and negatively charged, not hydrophobic, so a hydrophobic interaction cannot be what holds histones to the backbone; this catches a student who has confused the phosphate backbone with the hydrophobic base-stacking that occurs inside the helix.

## answer_b
Hydrogen-bonding between histone residues and nitrogenous bases

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The nitrogenous bases are already committed to hydrogen bonding with each other across the duplex (Watson-Crick pairing) and are tucked inside the helix, not exposed for histones to bind; this distractor catches a student who assumes any DNA-protein contact must involve the bases.

## answer_c
Electrostatic attraction between histone residues and phosphates

## explanation_c
Correct. Histones are rich in the basic amino acids lysine and arginine, which carry positively charged side chains at physiological pH. The DNA backbone's phosphate groups are negatively charged. The resulting electrostatic (ionic) attraction between the positively charged histone residues and the negatively charged phosphate backbone is what lets roughly two turns of DNA wrap around the histone octamer to form a nucleosome, the basic packaging unit that condenses the genome to fit inside the nucleus. This is why histones must be basic proteins — an acidic or neutral protein could not bind the backbone this way.

## answer_d
Phospho-ester-bonding between histone residues and phosphates

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A phosphoester bond is a covalent linkage (the kind that joins nucleotides to each other within a strand); histone-DNA binding is non-covalent and reversible, which is exactly what lets chromatin be repeatedly opened and re-condensed, so covalent bonding would be a mechanistic error.

## answer_e
Salt ionic bridges between histone residues and nitrogenous bases

## explanation_e
Incorrect. This half-correctly names an ionic interaction but attaches it to the wrong partner — the bases, not the phosphate backbone — catching a student who has the right kind of bond but the wrong location on the DNA molecule.

# Item

## title
Which statement about a double-helical molecule of DNA is true?

## question
Which of the following statements regarding a double-helical molecule of DNA is true?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
All hydroxyl groups of pentoses are involved in linkages

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The deoxyribose sugar has a free 3'-hydroxyl at the growing end of a strand (until it is joined to the next nucleotide) and, on the terminal residue of a linear strand, a free 3'-OH that is never linked; not every hydroxyl is tied up, so this overstates how completely the pentose is consumed by backbone linkages.

## answer_b
Base pairs are perpendicular to the DNA axis

## explanation_b
Correct. In the double helix, the sugar-phosphate backbones form the outer, twisting rails of the structure, while the nitrogenous bases project inward and pair with their complement on the opposite strand. Each base pair lies roughly flat, stacked on the one above and below it, and is oriented perpendicular to the long axis of the helix — like rungs set at right angles to the rails of a twisted ladder. This perpendicular stacking is what allows the bases to hydrogen-bond to each other while the backbones wind around the outside, and it is also what gives the helix its major and minor grooves.

## answer_c
Both strands are identical in sequence

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The two strands are complementary, not identical — where one strand reads A, the other reads T at that position, and where one reads G, the other reads C; a student who picks this has confused "complementary" with "identical".

## answer_d
Each strand has parallel, 5' to 3' direction

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The two strands run antiparallel: one strand runs 5' to 3' while the other runs 3' to 5' across the same span of helix, not parallel in the same direction — this is the fact this distractor is testing in reverse.

## answer_e
DNA denaturation is an irreversible process

## explanation_e
Incorrect. DNA denaturation (strand separation by heat or chemical treatment) is reversible under the right conditions — the complementary strands can reanneal on cooling, which is the basis of techniques like PCR and hybridisation; calling it irreversible is a factual error, not a subtle trap.

## title
Why does a GC-rich region of DNA need a higher temperature to melt?

## question
A sample of human DNA is heated, disrupting the double helix. One fraction within the molecule required a much higher temperature to melt than the rest. What is this fraction most likely to have a higher content of?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Adenine plus cytosine

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Adenine and cytosine are not a Watson-Crick base pair, so pairing them together as "the GC-rich-like" answer is not meaningful; this distractor catches a student pattern-matching on the letter C without checking which two bases actually pair together.

## answer_b
Cytosine plus guanine

## explanation_b
Correct. A guanine-cytosine (G-C) base pair is held together by three hydrogen bonds, one more than the two hydrogen bonds holding an adenine-thymine (A-T) pair together. More hydrogen bonds mean the base pair is harder to pull apart, so a DNA region with a higher proportion of G-C pairs needs more thermal energy — a higher melting (denaturation) temperature — to separate its two strands than an equivalent A-T-rich region. This relationship is the basis of using melting temperature to estimate GC content, and it is also why GC-rich genomic regions (and PCR primers) behave differently under heating than AT-rich ones.

## answer_c
Adenine plus thymine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This is the reverse of the correct relationship: A-T pairs have fewer hydrogen bonds (two) than G-C pairs (three), so an AT-rich region melts at a lower temperature, not a higher one — this catches a student who has the concept the wrong way round.

## answer_d
Cytosine plus thymine

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Cytosine and thymine are both pyrimidines and never pair with each other in normal Watson-Crick base pairing, so this combination is not a real determinant of melting temperature at all.

## answer_e
Adenine plus guanine

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Adenine and guanine are both purines and, like cytosine plus thymine, never pair with each other; this distractor tests the same "two bases that don't actually pair" misconception as option D.

## title
On which strand, and in which direction, do Okazaki fragments elongate?

## question
During DNA replication, Okazaki fragments elongate in which direction?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Leading strand towards the replication fork

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The leading strand is synthesised continuously, not in the short fragments called Okazaki fragments — this option puts the right process (synthesis towards the fork) on the wrong strand.

## answer_b
Lagging strand towards the replication fork

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This gets the strand right but the direction wrong: because DNA polymerase can only add nucleotides 5' to 3', and the lagging-strand template runs in the opposite orientation to fork movement, each Okazaki fragment must be synthesised away from the fork, not towards it.

## answer_c
Leading strand away from the replication fork

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This combines the wrong strand (leading, which is continuous) with the direction that actually belongs to the lagging strand — a student choosing this has swapped which strand is discontinuous.

## answer_d
Lagging strand away from the replication fork

## explanation_d
Correct. DNA polymerase can only synthesise new DNA in the 5' to 3' direction. On the leading strand, the template runs so that continuous synthesis proceeds in the same direction the replication fork opens. On the lagging strand, the template runs the opposite way, so DNA polymerase must synthesise short stretches — Okazaki fragments — starting near the fork and extending back away from it, each primed separately as the fork exposes more template. This is why the lagging strand is replicated discontinuously while the leading strand is replicated continuously, and it is also why the lagging strand needs its own set of RNA primers, primase activity and later ligation by DNA ligase.

## answer_e
Both strands towards the replication fork

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Only the leading strand is synthesised continuously towards the fork; describing both strands this way ignores the entire reason Okazaki fragments exist, which is the discontinuous, away-from-the-fork synthesis unique to the lagging strand.

## title
Which enzyme nicks and reseals the DNA double helix ahead of the replication fork?

## question
Which of the following enzymes could nick and seal the eukaryotic DNA double helix in front of the replication fork?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Topoisomerase

## explanation_a
Correct. As helicase unwinds the double helix at the replication fork, the DNA ahead of the fork becomes overwound (positively supercoiled), because the strands cannot simply spin freely inside the cell. Topoisomerase relieves this strain by transiently cutting one or both strands (nicking), allowing the DNA to swivel and release the supercoiling, and then resealing the cut. Without this activity, the mounting torsional stress ahead of the fork would stall replication; this is also why topoisomerase inhibitors (such as fluoroquinolone antibiotics acting on the bacterial version, DNA gyrase) can block DNA replication.

## answer_b
DNA polymerase-I

## explanation_b
Incorrect. DNA polymerase I extends and processes DNA strands (and removes RNA primers) but does not nick and reseal the helix to relieve supercoiling; this option tests whether the student has confused strand-synthesis enzymes with the topological-relief enzyme.

## answer_c
DNA gyrase

## explanation_c
Incorrect. DNA gyrase is itself a type of topoisomerase, but it is specifically the bacterial (prokaryotic) enzyme; the question asks about the eukaryotic replication fork, where the equivalent activity is provided by eukaryotic topoisomerases, not gyrase by name.

## answer_d
DNA ligase

## explanation_d
Incorrect. DNA ligase seals nicks left between Okazaki fragments on the lagging strand after primer removal and gap-filling — a different nick, at a different location, for a different reason — not the supercoiling relief ahead of the fork.

## answer_e
Polymerase III

## explanation_e
Incorrect. DNA polymerase III is the main replicative polymerase that extends new DNA strands; it has no role in nicking and resealing the helix to relieve torsional strain ahead of the fork.

## title
What activity of DNA polymerase maintains the fidelity of DNA synthesis?

## question
Which activity is responsible for the proofreading that maintains the fidelity of DNA synthesis?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Occurs after the DNA synthesis has been completed to the end

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Proofreading happens continuously, immediately after each nucleotide is added, not only once the whole strand is finished — waiting until the end would let far more errors accumulate and be replicated before any were caught.

## answer_b
Is a function of 3'-5' exonuclease activity of DNA polymerases

## explanation_b
Correct. As DNA polymerase adds each new nucleotide, it briefly checks whether the base just added is correctly paired with the template. If it is not, the polymerase's own 3'-to-5' exonuclease activity removes the mispaired nucleotide from the growing 3' end before synthesis resumes. This proofreading step happens during synthesis, immediately after each addition, and is what keeps the replication error rate far lower than base pairing alone would achieve — errors that escape this step are the ones later corrected by the separate mismatch repair system.

## answer_c
Requires the presence of a specific enzyme other than DNA polymerase

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Proofreading is an intrinsic activity of the DNA polymerase itself (its own exonuclease domain), not a separate enzyme working alongside it — this distractor catches a student who assumes every distinct function needs its own dedicated enzyme.

## answer_d
Occurs in prokaryotes but not in eukaryotes

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Proofreading exonuclease activity is present in replicative polymerases of both prokaryotes and eukaryotes; restricting it to one domain of life is a factual error.

## answer_e
Occurs in eukaryotes but not in prokaryotes

## explanation_e
Incorrect. This is the mirror-image error of option D — proofreading is not exclusive to eukaryotes either; it is a general feature of high-fidelity replicative DNA polymerases across both domains.

## subject

DNA gyrase

## explanation_b
Incorrect. DNA gyrase is a bacterial topoisomerase that relieves supercoiling, like its eukaryotic counterparts — it does not carry exonuclease activity of either polarity.

# Item

## title
What kind of enzyme is telomerase?

## question
What kind of enzyme is the telomerase protein?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Terminal transferase

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase adds nucleotides without needing a template, in a different biological context (notably V(D)J recombination in lymphocytes); telomerase, in contrast, carries its own RNA template, which is the defining feature this question is testing.

## answer_b
DNA polymerase δ

## explanation_b
Incorrect. DNA polymerase δ is a template-directed DNA-synthesising enzyme used in ordinary lagging-strand replication; it uses a DNA template, not the RNA template telomerase carries, so it cannot be what extends telomeres by copying an internal RNA sequence.

## answer_c

## question

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The amount of Adenine and thymine [is equal], [and] the amount of guanine and cytosine [is equal]

## explanation_a
Correct. Chargaff's rule states that in double-stranded DNA, the amount of adenine always equals the amount of thymine, and the amount of guanine always equals the amount of cytosine — a direct consequence of each purine pairing with exactly one specific pyrimidine across the duplex (A with T, G with C). Once one base's percentage is known, this rule lets every other base's percentage be calculated: for example, if adenine is 20% of bases, thymine must also be 20%, leaving 60% split equally between guanine and cytosine (30% each). The amount of cytosine and Guanine [is equal to] the amount of adenine and cytosine

## explanation_b
Incorrect. ## answer_c
The amount of Adenine and Guanine [is equal to] the amount of thymine and cytosine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This groups the two purines (A, G) together and compares them to the two pyrimidines (T, C) together, which happens to be numerically true only because purines always equal pyrimidines overall in a duplex — but it is not how Chargaff's rule is actually stated, and it skips over the more specific and more testable individual equalities (A=T and G=C) that this option's own paired bases obscure.

All of these

Incorrect. Since options B and C do not correctly state Chargaff's rule in the way option A does, "all of these" cannot be right; this option catches a student who assumes a "combine everything" answer is always the safe choice.

Both ends of the strand carry a free hydroxyl group

Incorrect.
