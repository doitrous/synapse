## title
Periorbital oedema and splinter haemorrhages: what stage is diagnostic on muscle biopsy?

## question
A patient has periorbital oedema and splinter haemorrhages under the nails, and trichinosis is suspected. What diagnostic stage of the parasite is found on muscle biopsy?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette
A female patient presents with facial and periorbital swelling together with linear haemorrhages under several fingernails, following a history of eating undercooked meat several days earlier. A muscle biopsy is taken to confirm the diagnosis.

## format
single best answer

## derived_from
Adapted from printed question 14 of the ASU Locomotor Parasitology Practical MCQ paper (Dr Ayman Ibrahim, 2025). The original item presented three unlabelled photographs (marked A/B/C: an adult worm, an encysted structure, and a stained tissue field) as the answer options; those three photographs are reproduced here as named text options describing the same three stages, since the underlying teaching fact (which stage is diagnostic on muscle biopsy) is unambiguous from the printed vignette -- see author_notes.

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Encysted larva within striated muscle.

## explanation_a
Correct. After Trichinella spiralis larvae hatch in the intestine and migrate via the bloodstream, they penetrate individual striated muscle fibres and coil there, and the fibre reorganises around each larva into a collagen-walled "nurse cell" capsule -- this encysted larva within its capsule is exactly what a muscle biopsy is taken to find, and finding it confirms the diagnosis. The periorbital oedema and splinter haemorrhages in the vignette both reflect this same migratory/invasive phase reaching highly vascular tissue (extraocular/facial muscle and nail-bed capillaries) rather than being caused by the encysted stage itself. Muscle biopsy (together with serology) is the standard confirmatory test precisely because encysted larvae, not adult worms or free larvae, are what is actually present in muscle at this stage of disease.

## answer_b
Adult female worm.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The adult female Trichinella lives briefly in the small intestine, not in striated muscle, and is not what a muscle biopsy is taken to find -- picked by a student who has correctly recalled "female worm releases larvae" without tracking where those larvae end up.

## answer_c
Adult male worm.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Like the female, the adult male Trichinella is an intestinal-phase form and is never found encysted in muscle -- picked by a student generalising "adult worm" as the diagnostic form without distinguishing the intestinal and muscle phases of the life cycle.

## answer_d
Free first-stage larva circulating in blood.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Larvae migrate through the bloodstream only transiently on their way to striated muscle; a muscle biopsy samples tissue, not blood, and by the time clinical signs like periorbital oedema appear the larvae have already reached and begun encysting in muscle -- picked by a student confusing the migratory route with the diagnostic sample site.

## title
An ulcerating nodule in a patient from Sinai: what transmits this parasite?

## question
A patient from Sinai presents with an ulcer on the upper limb that began as a nodule a month earlier. What is the vector responsible for disease transmission?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette
A patient from Sinai, Egypt, presents with a single ulcerated skin lesion on the upper limb. The lesion started as a small painless nodule about a month before, which has since ulcerated centrally with a raised, indurated border.

## format
single best answer

## derived_from
Adapted from printed question 15 of the ASU Locomotor Parasitology Practical MCQ paper (Dr Ayman Ibrahim, 2025). The original item presented three unlabelled insect photographs (marked A/B/C, no printed species names) and asked for the vector; those three photographs could not be reliably matched to a named species without a caption, so this item is reformatted here as a standard named-option question testing the same standard, independently well-established teaching fact carried by the printed clinical vignette -- see author_notes.

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Phlebotomus (sandfly).

## explanation_a
Correct. Cutaneous leishmaniasis is transmitted by the bite of an infected female Phlebotomus sandfly (the Old World vector, found in Egypt including Sinai), which injects the motile promastigote form into the skin while feeding. The lesion then evolves over weeks from a papule to a painless nodule, which characteristically ulcerates centrally with a raised, indurated border and heals slowly over months -- exactly the "nodule a month before, now an ulcer" progression in the vignette. Recognising Sinai as an endemic focus and Phlebotomus as the vector specific to Leishmania, rather than any biting insect in general, is what the question tests.

## answer_b
Simulium (blackfly).

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Simulium transmits Onchocerca volvulus, producing painless subcutaneous nodules that do not typically ulcerate in this way, and its bite is associated with river-valley exposure rather than the Sinai desert/oasis setting -- picked by a student who has recalled "nodule-forming vector-borne disease" without matching the correct organism to the ulcerating course described.

## answer_c
Anopheles mosquito.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Anopheles transmits malaria, which does not produce a localised skin nodule or ulcer -- picked by a student defaulting to the most familiar mosquito vector without matching it to a skin-lesion disease.

## answer_d
Culex mosquito.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Culex transmits lymphatic filariasis and some arboviruses, neither of which produces this papule-to-ulcerating-nodule skin progression -- picked by a student recalling a mosquito genus without matching it to the specific clinical picture described.

## title
Is this tick specimen a hard tick or a soft tick, and adult or larva?

## question
The parasite in the shown figure is:

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette
A parasitology practical specimen shows a whole-mount tick, photographed to show its overall body shape, cuticle texture and leg count.

## format
single best answer

## derived_from
Printed question 16 of the ASU Locomotor Parasitology Practical MCQ paper (Dr Ayman Ibrahim, 2025).

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Hard tick adult.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A hard (ixodid) tick adult would show a visible dorsal scutum -- small in the female, covering the whole dorsum in the male -- which is absent from the smooth, leathery cuticle shown in this specimen, and would have four pairs of legs like the specimen shown, so leg count alone does not rule this out; the missing scutum is what does.

## answer_b
Soft tick larva.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A soft tick larva, like all tick larvae, would have only three pairs of legs (six legs total), one fewer pair than the nymphal and adult stages -- picked by a student who has correctly identified "soft tick" from the lack of a scutum but has miscounted the legs as a larva's rather than an adult's.

## answer_c
Hard tick larva.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This combines two errors: a hard tick larva would still show the beginnings of a scutum (present from the larval stage onward in Ixodidae) and would have only three pairs of legs, neither of which matches a specimen with no scutum and four pairs of legs.

## answer_d
Soft tick adult.

## explanation_d
Correct. Soft ticks (family Argasidae) lack a scutum entirely at every life stage, giving them a uniformly leathery, wrinkled cuticle rather than a shielded dorsum, and their capitulum (mouthparts) is tucked underneath the body and invisible from directly above -- both features distinguishing them from hard ticks (Ixodidae). The four pairs of legs visible here mark this specimen as past the six-legged larval stage, and together with the scutum-free cuticle this identifies it as a soft tick adult (or late nymph) rather than any hard-tick stage or a soft-tick larva.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Antennae are small paired sensory appendages on the larval head segment, not the cuticular projections running along the body segments where the arrow points -- picked by a student who has recalled a head structure without checking the arrow's actual location.

## answer_b
Bristle.

## explanation_b
Correct. Fly larvae carry hair-like cuticular projections called bristles (setae) along their body segments, which are sensory structures used partly for species-level larval identification alongside the posterior spiracle pattern. In the photograph, the arrow indicates one of these projecting hairs along a body segment, distinguishing it from a mouthpart structure at the head end or an internal feeding structure. Bristle number, length and arrangement are recorded systematically when identifying fly larvae to species in forensic and medical entomology.

Labium.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The labium is a mouthpart structure at the head end of the larva, not a projecting cuticular hair along the body -- picked by a student naming a real larval structure but the wrong one for the arrowed location.

## answer_d
Maxillary palp.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Maxillary palps are sensory mouthpart appendages near the head, not projecting bristles along the body segments -- picked by a student who has correctly recalled a larval anatomy term without matching it to where the arrow points.
