## title
What is the expected sensory and motor finding below a complete spinal cord transection?

## question
A patient sustains a traumatic injury with complete transection of the spinal cord at the T8 level. Which of the following is expected below the level of the lesion?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Total loss of all sensory modalities with bilateral paraplegia

## explanation_a
Correct. Complete transection severs every ascending and descending pathway bilaterally at that level, so below the lesion there is total loss of all sensory modalities (touch, pain, temperature, proprioception, vibration) together with bilateral paraplegia from loss of all descending motor control. This is unlike partial cord syndromes, which selectively spare specific tracts because only part of the cord's cross-section is damaged. Recognising this all-or-nothing pattern is the first step in distinguishing complete transection from Brown-Séquard or anterior cord syndrome.

## answer_b
Ipsilateral loss of proprioception only, with normal power

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Isolated ipsilateral proprioceptive loss with normal power describes a partial dorsal-column lesion, not complete transection, which abolishes power and every sensory modality bilaterally.

## answer_c
Contralateral loss of pain and temperature only

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Isolated contralateral pain/temperature loss describes anterior cord syndrome (dorsal columns spared), not complete transection, which spares nothing.

## answer_d
Preserved sensation with isolated bilateral leg weakness

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Complete transection abolishes sensation entirely below the lesion; it does not preserve sensation while producing isolated motor weakness.

## title
The fourth ventricle develops as the cavity of which embryonic brain vesicle?

## question
The fourth ventricle is the cerebrospinal fluid-filled cavity of which primary embryonic brain vesicle?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Rhombencephalon (hindbrain)

## explanation_a
Correct. The fourth ventricle is the cavity of the rhombencephalon (hindbrain). The third ventricle is the cavity of the diencephalon, and the cerebral aqueduct is the cavity of the mesencephalon. Each major ventricular cavity maps to a specific embryonic brain vesicle in this way.

## answer_b
Prosencephalon (forebrain)

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The prosencephalon (forebrain) gives rise to the telencephalon and diencephalon, whose cavities are the lateral ventricles and third ventricle, not the fourth ventricle.

## answer_c
Mesencephalon (midbrain)

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The mesencephalon (midbrain) gives rise to the narrow cerebral aqueduct of Sylvius, not the fourth ventricle.

## answer_d
Telencephalon

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The telencephalon's cavities are the paired lateral ventricles, not the fourth ventricle.

## title
Which fissure divides the cerebellum into anterior and posterior lobes?

## question
Which fissure of the cerebellum separates the anterior lobe from the posterior lobe?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Primary fissure

## explanation_a
Correct. The primary fissure is the deepest and most prominent fissure on the superior surface of the cerebellum. It divides the cerebellum into the anterior lobe and the posterior lobe. The anterior/posterior lobe distinction it defines underlies the spinocerebellum's functional territory.

## answer_b
Posterolateral fissure

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The posterolateral fissure separates the posterior lobe from the flocculonodular lobe, not the anterior lobe from the posterior lobe.

## answer_c
Horizontal fissure

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The horizontal fissure runs along the lateral cerebellar surface and is not the landmark dividing anterior from posterior lobe.

## answer_d
Uvulonodular fissure

## explanation_d
Incorrect. There is no uvulonodular fissure marking the anterior/posterior lobe boundary; the uvula and nodule are components of the vermis and flocculonodular lobe respectively.

## title
Into which pathway does the spinal lemniscus continue?

## question
The lateral spinothalamic pathway, ascending through the spinal cord as the 'spinal lemniscus', continues rostrally into the brainstem as which tract?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Neospinothalamic tract

## explanation_a
Correct. The spinal lemniscus, formed by second-order lateral spinothalamic fibres, continues rostrally as the neospinothalamic tract. This is a fast, few-synapse pathway carrying sharp, well-localised pain directly to the ventral posterolateral thalamic nucleus. It contrasts with the slower, multisynaptic paleospinothalamic pathway, which carries dull, poorly localised pain via the reticular formation.

## answer_b
Medial lemniscus

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The medial lemniscus carries dorsal column-mediated fine touch/proprioception, formed by internal arcuate fibres from the gracile and cuneate nuclei, a separate pathway from the spinal lemniscus.

## answer_c
Trigeminothalamic tract

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The trigeminothalamic tract carries facial (not body) pain/temperature and touch sensation via the trigeminal system, a separate pathway from the spinal lemniscus.

## answer_d
Dorsal column pathway

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The dorsal column pathway is a distinct, uncrossed system for fine touch/proprioception, unrelated to the spinothalamic spinal lemniscus.

## title
Into which vein does the common facial vein drain?

## question
The common facial vein, formed by union of the facial and retromandibular veins, drains directly into which vessel?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Internal jugular vein

## explanation_a
Correct. The common facial vein is formed in the upper neck by union of the facial vein and the anterior division of the retromandibular vein. It then crosses superficial to the carotid sheath and drains directly into the internal jugular vein. This gives the internal jugular vein several individually named tributaries along its course.

## answer_b
External jugular vein

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The external jugular vein is formed chiefly by the posterior division of the retromandibular vein together with the posterior auricular vein, a separate drainage route from the common facial vein.

## answer_c
Subclavian vein

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The subclavian vein receives the external jugular vein at the neck's root, not the common facial vein directly.

## answer_d
Cavernous sinus

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The cavernous sinus receives blood via the superior ophthalmic vein and emissary veins, not directly from the common facial vein, though facial vein infection can spread intracranially by this valveless route.
