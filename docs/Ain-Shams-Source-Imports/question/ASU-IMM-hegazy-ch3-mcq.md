## title
The smallest unit of an antigen that is capable of binding with antibodies is:

## question
The smallest unit of an antigen that is capable of binding with antibodies is:

## subject
imm

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Adjuvant

## explanation_a
Incorrect. An adjuvant is a substance that boosts an immune response to an accompanying antigen; it is not itself the antibody-binding unit of an antigen.

## answer_b
Hapten

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A hapten is a small molecule that cannot provoke an antibody response alone unless bound to a carrier protein — a separate concept from the antigen-binding unit itself.

## answer_c
Paratope

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The paratope is the antigen-binding site on the antibody, the complementary partner to the epitope, not a unit of the antigen.

## answer_d
Epitope

## explanation_d
Correct. The epitope (also called the antigenic determinant) is the smallest structural unit of an antigen that is specifically recognised and bound by an antibody or a T-cell receptor. A single antigen molecule, especially a large protein, typically carries multiple epitopes, and it is the epitope — not the whole antigen molecule — that defines antibody specificity.

## answer_e
Tolerogen

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A tolerogen is a form of antigen that induces immunological tolerance rather than a response; it is not the antibody-binding unit.

## question
Which of the following criteria should be fulfilled for a substance to be antigenic?

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Low molecular weight actually works against immunogenicity — small molecules (haptens) generally cannot provoke a response alone.

## answer_b
Simple in structure

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Structural simplicity works against, not for, antigenicity; a repetitive, simple structure is poorly immunogenic.

Have one antigenic determinant

Chemical complexity

## explanation_e
Correct. Chemical complexity — a varied, non-repetitive composition of amino acids or sugars — is a genuine criterion for antigenicity, working alongside sufficient size and foreignness to the host. A structurally simple, uniform molecule is generally a poor antigen even if it is otherwise large.

## title
Class I MHC proteins:

## question
Class I MHC proteins:

## subject
imm

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Present Ag to CD4 helper T cell

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Class I MHC presents to CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, not CD4+ helper T cells — that pairing belongs to class II.

## answer_b
Encoded by HLA DP, DQ, DR loci

## explanation_b
Incorrect. HLA-DP, -DQ and -DR are the class II loci; class I is encoded by HLA-A, -B and -C.

## answer_c
Present on RBCs

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Mature red blood cells lack MHC molecules entirely, including class I, since they have no nucleus and shed their surface MHC during maturation.

## answer_d
Heterodimers composed of an alpha chain & β2 microglobulin

## explanation_d
Correct. Class I MHC proteins are heterodimers composed of a polymorphic alpha (heavy) chain, encoded by HLA-A, -B or -C, non-covalently associated with the invariant beta-2 microglobulin light chain. Beta-2 microglobulin does not itself carry the peptide-binding groove, but it is required for the alpha chain to fold correctly and reach the cell surface. Without it, class I expression is lost, which is exactly why beta-2 microglobulin deficiency causes a class-I-deficient immunodeficiency.

## question
Sites on the Ag which will elicit immune response & react with antibodies:

Incorrect. Agglutinins are the antibodies that cause clumping of particulate antigen, not the antigen's own binding sites.

## answer_b

## title
What is the principle function of class I MHC molecules?

## question
What is the principle function of class I MHC molecules?

## subject
imm

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
They display peptide Ags for B cells

## explanation_a
Incorrect. B cells recognise native, unprocessed antigen through their B-cell receptor, not processed peptide displayed on MHC class I.

## answer_b
They display peptide Ags for CD4 molecules on T helper cells

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Displaying peptide for CD4 T helper cells is the function of MHC class II, not class I.

## answer_c
They display peptide Ags for CD8 molecules on T cytotoxic cells

## explanation_c
Correct. The principal function of class I MHC molecules is to display processed peptide antigen, typically derived from intracellular or cytosolic proteins, in the groove between its alpha-1 and alpha-2 domains. This peptide-class-I complex is what CD8 molecules on cytotoxic T cells recognise. That recognition event is exactly the mechanism that lets cytotoxic T cells identify and kill virally infected or tumour cells from the inside out.

## answer_d
They help endocytosis by phagocytic cells

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Assisting phagocytic endocytosis is not an MHC function; MHC molecules present already-processed peptide, they do not drive the uptake step.

## answer_e
They recognize microbes by Pathogen associated molecular patterns

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Recognising PAMPs is the role of pattern recognition receptors (e.g. Toll-like receptors) in innate immunity, not of MHC class I.

## title
What is the principle function of class II MHC molecules?

## question
What is the principle function of class II MHC molecules?

## subject
imm

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
They display peptide Ags for B cells

## explanation_a
Incorrect. B cells recognise native antigen through their own receptor, not peptide displayed to them by MHC class II.

## answer_b
They display peptide Ags for CD4 molecules on T helper cells

## explanation_b
Correct. The principal function of class II MHC molecules is to display processed peptide antigen, typically derived from extracellular or endocytosed proteins, for recognition by CD4 molecules on T helper cells. This is the mechanism underlying CD4+ T-cell activation. Downstream of that activation, helper T cells go on to license B cells for antibody production and macrophages for enhanced microbial killing.

## answer_c
They display peptide Ags for CD8 molecules on T cytotoxic cells

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Displaying peptide for CD8 cytotoxic T cells is the function of class I MHC, not class II.

## answer_d
Their Ag binding site lies between al & a2 domains

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The class I (not class II) antigen-binding groove is formed between the alpha-1 and alpha-2 domains; class II's groove instead forms between one alpha and one beta domain.

## answer_e
They recognize microbes by Pathogen associated molecular patterns

## explanation_e
Incorrect. PAMP recognition is an innate pattern-recognition-receptor function, not a role of MHC class II.

## title
Linkage of a disease to an HLA allele means that:

## question
Linkage of a disease to an HLA allele means that:

## subject
imm

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Everyone with that allele will eventually get the disease

## explanation_a
Incorrect. An HLA-disease association is a risk modifier, not a deterministic guarantee — most carriers of a disease-linked allele never develop the disease.

## answer_b
People with that allele have a higher risk for the disease

## explanation_b
Correct. Linkage of a disease to a particular HLA allele means that people carrying that allele have a statistically higher risk of developing the disease compared with the general population. It is an association derived from population studies, not a deterministic guarantee. Most carriers of even a strongly disease-linked allele, such as HLA-B27 and ankylosing spondylitis, never actually develop the disease.

## answer_c
MHC protein encoded by that allele is defective

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The MHC protein encoded by a disease-linked allele is not inherently defective; the allele's structure may influence immune recognition patterns in ways that raise disease risk without the protein being non-functional.

## answer_d
The allele will eventually disappear from the population

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A disease-linked allele is not expected to simply disappear from the population; many disease-associated HLA alleles persist because they also confer other advantages (e.g. resistance to certain infections).

## answer_e
Non of the above is true

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Option B is a true and complete description of what an HLA-disease linkage means, so 'none of the above' is wrong.
