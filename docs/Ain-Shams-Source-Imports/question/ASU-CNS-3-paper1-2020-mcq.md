## title
Which drug is first-line for essential tremor?

## question
An elderly patient with benign prostatic hyperplasia is found to have a fine, bilateral action tremor of the hands, diagnosed as essential tremor. Which drug is first-line pharmacological treatment?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Propranolol

## explanation_a
Correct. Propranolol, a non-selective beta-adrenergic antagonist, is first-line pharmacological therapy for essential tremor: blocking peripheral beta-2 receptors in skeletal muscle reduces tremor amplitude, and it is used across age groups including elderly patients (with appropriate caution for comorbidities). Recognising this first-line choice is clinically important, since essential tremor is often mistaken for the resting tremor of Parkinson disease, which responds to different drugs.

## answer_b
Levodopa

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Levodopa is the mainstay treatment for the resting tremor of Parkinson disease, not the action/postural tremor of essential tremor, and is not first-line here.

## answer_c
Amantadine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Amantadine is an antiparkinsonian agent, not a first-line treatment for essential tremor.

## answer_d
Diazepam

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Diazepam is not a first-line pharmacological treatment for essential tremor; propranolol is preferred.

## title
What is the classic presentation of anterior spinal artery occlusion at the spinal cord level?

## question
Occlusion of the anterior spinal artery at the level of the spinal cord classically produces which pattern of deficit below the lesion?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Bilateral flaccid paraplegia with bilateral loss of pain and temperature, dorsal columns spared

## explanation_a
Correct. The anterior spinal artery supplies the anterior two-thirds of the spinal cord; its occlusion at the spinal cord level causes bilateral paraplegia below the lesion with bilateral loss of pain and temperature sensation, while the dorsal columns (proprioception, vibration), supplied by the posterior spinal arteries, are classically spared. Recognising this pattern clinically distinguishes a spinal cord-level vascular syndrome from the different combination of deficits seen with a brainstem-level anterior spinal artery territory lesion.

## answer_b
Ipsilateral tongue weakness with contralateral limb weakness

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Ipsilateral tongue weakness with contralateral limb weakness describes medial medullary syndrome, from anterior spinal artery territory occlusion at the medulla, a different level with a different, more localised picture.

## answer_c
Ipsilateral loss of proprioception with contralateral loss of pain and temperature

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This pattern describes Brown-Sequard syndrome (spinal cord hemisection), not bilateral anterior spinal artery occlusion.

## answer_d
Complete bilateral sensory and motor loss including proprioception

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Anterior spinal artery occlusion classically spares the dorsal columns (posterior spinal artery territory), so proprioception is not lost.

## title
Which drug reverses fentanyl intoxication?

## question
A patient presents with respiratory depression and pinpoint pupils after suspected fentanyl overdose. Which drug is given to acutely reverse the intoxication?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Naloxone

## explanation_a
Correct. Naloxone, a short-acting competitive mu-opioid receptor antagonist, is given intravenously or intramuscularly to acutely reverse opioid (including fentanyl) intoxication, restoring respiratory drive and consciousness within minutes; repeated dosing may be needed since fentanyl can outlast naloxone's effect. Because fentanyl is a potent, long-acting opioid, patients reversed with naloxone must be monitored closely for re-sedation as the antagonist wears off before the opioid does.

## answer_b
Naltrexone

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Naltrexone is a long-acting oral opioid antagonist used for relapse prevention after detoxification, not for acute overdose reversal.

## answer_c
Flumazenil

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Flumazenil is a benzodiazepine receptor antagonist, used to reverse benzodiazepine (not opioid) overdose.

## answer_d
Buprenorphine

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Buprenorphine is a partial mu-opioid agonist used in analgesia and substitution therapy; it is not the acute reversal agent for opioid overdose and could itself precipitate withdrawal if given during acute intoxication.

## title
Which nucleus is the origin of the trapezoid body?

## question
The trapezoid body, a bundle of decussating auditory fibres in the ventral pons, arises from which nucleus?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## source
past-paper

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The cochlear nucleus

## explanation_a
Correct. The trapezoid body arises from the (ventral) cochlear nucleus, the first central relay for auditory information; many of its fibres cross the midline within the trapezoid body to reach the contralateral superior olivary nucleus. This crossing at the trapezoid body is what gives the central auditory pathway its bilateral, partially crossed representation needed for sound localisation.

## answer_b
The superior olivary nucleus

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The superior olivary nucleus is a destination the trapezoid body's fibres synapse upon after crossing, not its nucleus of origin.

## answer_c
The inferior colliculus

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The inferior colliculus is further along the auditory pathway, receiving input after the superior olivary complex, not the origin of the trapezoid body.

## answer_d
The medial geniculate body

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The medial geniculate body is the thalamic auditory relay, much further along the pathway, not the origin of the trapezoid body.
