## title
Which basic tissue uniquely shows polarity?

## question
The only basic tissue showing polarity is:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## format
single best answer

## derived_from
Question 1 of the ASU HCB Dr. Hegazy epithelium MCQ bank ("MCQs - epith MCQ.pdf"), PDF page 1.

## correct_answer
b

## answer_a
Connective tissue

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Connective tissue is built around an extracellular matrix and does not have a free surface versus an attached surface in the way epithelium does, so it is not described as having apical–basal polarity. This is a plausible-sounding distractor because connective tissue does have directional properties (e.g. fibre orientation), but that is not the same feature the question is asking about.

## answer_b
Epithelium

## explanation_b
Correct. Epithelium is the one basic tissue that shows polarity: its apical (free) surface faces a lumen or the exterior, and its basal surface attaches to the underlying basement membrane, with the cell's organelles and membrane specialisations often distributed asymmetrically between the two. This apical–basal difference underlies epithelial functions such as directional secretion and absorption, and it is the defining feature this MCQ tests.

## answer_c
Muscular tissue

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Muscular tissue is organised around contractile filaments and force generation along the length of the fibre, not around an apical-versus-basal surface distinction, so it does not show the polarity being asked about here.

## answer_d
Nervous tissue

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Nervous tissue has a functional directionality (dendrite to axon terminal) but this is not the apical–basal epithelial polarity the bank is testing; the question is specifically about the four basic tissues and picks the one built around a free versus attached surface.

## title
What term names an abnormal increase in the growth of cells?

## question
Abnormal increase in the growth of cells is called:

## subject

## format

## derived_from

## correct_answer
c

## answer_a
Dysplasia

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Dysplasia describes disordered, atypical cell growth and maturation that falls short of true tumour formation; it is a step on the way to neoplasia in some contexts, but the term for "abnormal increase in the growth of cells" itself is neoplasia, not dysplasia.

## answer_b
Metaplasia

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Metaplasia is the reversible change of one differentiated epithelial type into another differentiated type (for example, ciliated columnar epithelium becoming stratified squamous under chronic irritation); it describes a change in cell type, not an increase in the amount of cell growth, so it does not match the question's wording.

## answer_c
Neoplasia = tumor

## explanation_c
Correct. Neoplasia literally means "new growth" and is defined here as the abnormal increase in the growth of cells — the process that produces a tumour (the bank explicitly glosses it as "= tumor"). This distinguishes it from metaplasia (a change in epithelial type) and dysplasia (disordered but non-neoplastic growth).

## answer_d
Elasticity

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Elasticity is a mechanical property of tissue (its ability to stretch and recoil), unrelated to abnormal cell-growth increase, and is included here as an implausible distractor.

## answer_e
Plasticity

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Plasticity refers to a tissue's or cell's capacity to change or adapt, not specifically to an abnormal increase in cell growth, and is a further implausible distractor alongside elasticity.

## title
Which is a genuine character of epithelial tissue?

## question
Which of the following is considered a character of epithelial tissue?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## format
single best answer

## derived_from
Question 4 of the ASU HCB Dr. Hegazy epithelium MCQ bank ("MCQs - epith MCQ.pdf"), PDF page 1.

## correct_answer
a

## answer_a
Cell polarity

## explanation_a
Correct. Apical–basal cell polarity is a defining character of epithelial tissue: the apical surface (facing a lumen or the exterior) differs structurally and functionally from the basal surface (attached to the basement membrane), unlike the other basic tissues. This polarity is what lets an epithelial sheet do directional work — absorbing or secreting from one surface while anchoring to connective tissue at the other — and it is the same feature tested in Question 1 of this bank.

## answer_b
Rarely modified

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Epithelium is, if anything, frequently modified at its free surface — for example into microvilli, cilia or stereocilia for specialised functions — so "rarely modified" misstates a tissue that is known for surface specialisation.

## answer_c
Well vascularized

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Epithelium is avascular: no blood vessels penetrate the epithelial layer itself, and it is nourished by diffusion from the underlying connective tissue, so "well vascularized" is the opposite of a true epithelial character.

## answer_d
Fully differentiated

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Epithelium maintains populations of less-differentiated basal/stem cells that undergo continuous turnover and replace surface cells; describing the tissue as uniformly "fully differentiated" misses this renewal capacity.

## answer_e
Their cells are widely separated

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Epithelial cells are characterised by minimal intercellular substance and are held tightly together by junctional complexes, the opposite of being "widely separated," which better describes loosely arranged connective tissue cells.

## title
Which epithelium lines the esophagus in a patient with oesophagitis?

## question
Which type of epithelia lines the oesophagus?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner

## vignette

## format
single best answer

## derived_from
Question 19 of the ASU HCB Dr. Hegazy epithelium MCQ bank ("MCQs - epith MCQ.pdf"), PDF page 4.

## correct_answer
a

## answer_a
Stratified squamous non-keratinized

## explanation_a
Correct. The esophagus is lined by stratified squamous non-keratinized epithelium: multiple layers of cells for abrasion resistance as swallowed food passes, but non-keratinized because the surface stays moist, unlike the keratinized epithelium of the skin. Inflammation of this lining, as in the vignette's oesophagitis, is exactly the setting in which recognising the underlying epithelial type matters clinically.

## answer_b
Stratified squamous keratinized

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Keratinized stratified squamous epithelium lines the skin, which is exposed to a dry external environment; the esophagus is a moist internal tube and does not keratinize its surface.

## answer_c
Stratified columnar

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Stratified columnar epithelium is a rare epithelial type (seen in short stretches such as parts of the conjunctiva or large excretory ducts) and does not line the esophagus, which needs abrasion resistance, not the secretory/absorptive role columnar cells provide.

## answer_d
Simple cuboidal

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Simple cuboidal epithelium is a single layer of cube-shaped cells suited to secretion and absorption (e.g. in small ducts and kidney tubules), not to withstanding the mechanical abrasion of swallowed food, so it does not fit the esophagus.

## answer_e
Simple columnar

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Simple columnar epithelium is a single layer suited to secretion/absorption (e.g. the stomach and intestine), not to the abrasion resistance the multilayered esophageal lining requires.

## title
What are taste buds an example of?

## question
Taste buds are example of:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## format
single best answer

## derived_from
Question 32 of the ASU HCB Dr. Hegazy epithelium MCQ bank ("MCQs - epith MCQ.pdf"), PDF page 6.

## correct_answer
b

## answer_a
Myoepithelium

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Myoepithelial cells are specialised contractile cells around gland acini and ducts (e.g. salivary, mammary, sweat glands), not sensory structures like taste buds.

## answer_b
Neuroepithelium

## explanation_b
Correct. Taste buds are classified as neuroepithelium: a specialised sensory epithelium whose receptor cells synapse with afferent nerve endings, transducing chemical stimuli into nerve impulses. The olfactory epithelium of the nasal cavity is classified the same way, for the same reason: sensory receptor cells wired directly to afferent nerve fibres.

## answer_c
Stratified epithelium

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Although taste buds sit within the stratified squamous epithelium of the tongue's surface, the taste bud itself is not classified simply as "stratified epithelium" — its defining feature is its sensory, nerve-associated function, which is what the term neuroepithelium captures.

## answer_d
Glandular epithelium

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Glandular epithelium is specialised for secretion, not sensory transduction, and does not describe the function of a taste bud.

## answer_e
Absorptive epithelium

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Absorptive epithelium is specialised for uptake of substances (e.g. intestinal epithelium), not for sensory detection, so it does not describe a taste bud.

## title
How does the mammary gland secrete its lipid droplets?

## question
Droplets of lipid are secreted in the mammary gland by which of following pathway?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Dr. Omar

## vignette

## format
single best answer

## derived_from
Question 33 of the ASU HCB Dr. Hegazy epithelium MCQ bank ("MCQs - epith MCQ.pdf"), PDF page 6.

## correct_answer
e

## answer_a
Endocrine

## explanation_a
Incorrect. "Endocrine" describes secretion directly into the bloodstream (hormones), not the local exocrine release of lipid droplets from mammary epithelial cells into the duct system.

## answer_b
Paracrine

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Paracrine signalling describes a secreted substance acting on nearby cells, a form of cell-to-cell communication, not the mechanism by which a lipid droplet physically leaves a mammary secretory cell.

## answer_c
Merocrine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The mammary gland's protein component is released by the merocrine (exocytotic) route, but its lipid-droplet component is not — the lipid droplet is too large to be packaged in a small exocytotic vesicle and is instead released by the apocrine route.

## answer_d
Holocrine

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Holocrine secretion (as in the sebaceous gland) destroys the whole secreting cell; the mammary gland's lipid-secreting cells survive and regenerate the pinched-off apical cytoplasm, which is the apocrine, not holocrine, pattern.

## answer_e
Apocrine

## explanation_e
Correct. Mammary gland lipid droplets are secreted by the apocrine pathway: the lipid droplet, surrounded by a thin rim of apical cytoplasm and plasma membrane, buds off and pinches away from the cell, while the rest of the cell survives and regenerates the lost portion. This closes out the bank's three-way secretion-mechanism contrast: merocrine keeps the whole cell, apocrine loses only apical cytoplasm, and holocrine loses the whole cell.
