# Item

## id

## title
The one step in protein synthesis that spends no extra high-energy bond

## question
During protein synthesis high energy phosphate bonds are required for the following EXCEPT:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Peptidyl transferase formation of peptide bond

## explanation_a
Correct — this is the exception. Peptide bond formation itself needs no additional energy input at that step: the energy was already stored earlier in the high-energy ester bond linking the amino acid to its tRNA when it was first activated. Peptidyl transferase simply catalyses the transfer using that pre-existing energy, without hydrolysing any further ATP or GTP.

## answer_b
Binding of aminoacyl tRNA to the ribosome

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Delivering each charged aminoacyl-tRNA to the ribosome's A site is driven by an elongation factor (EF-Tu in bacteria, eEF-1 in eukaryotes) together with GTP hydrolysis — this step does spend energy.

## answer_c
Translocation

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Translocation, the step that shifts the ribosome one codon along the mRNA, is driven by a second elongation factor (EF-G in bacteria, eEF-2 in eukaryotes) together with GTP hydrolysis — this step also spends energy.

## answer_d
Activation of amino acids

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Activating an amino acid onto its tRNA consumes two high-energy phosphate bonds: aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase hydrolyses ATP all the way to AMP and pyrophosphate, and the cell's subsequent hydrolysis of that pyrophosphate makes the reaction irreversible.

# Item

## id

## title
tRNA as the adaptor molecule of translation

## question
Adaptor molecule, translating the code into amino acid sequence is:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
tRNA

## explanation_a
Correct. tRNA is the adaptor molecule: one end (the anticodon) reads the mRNA codon by base pairing, and the other end (the 3' acceptor arm) carries the specific amino acid that codon calls for. It is this physical link between a nucleotide triplet and an amino acid that lets the ribosome translate a nucleic-acid sequence into a protein sequence. Francis Crick first proposed this adaptor role before tRNA was even isolated, precisely because nothing about a codon's chemistry can directly recognise an amino acid — something has to physically bridge the two, and tRNA is that bridge.

## answer_b
mRNA

## explanation_b
Incorrect. mRNA carries the coded message (the sequence of codons) but does not itself carry an amino acid — it has no adaptor function of its own.

## answer_c
DNA

## explanation_c
Incorrect. DNA stores the genetic information and is transcribed into mRNA, but DNA never participates directly in translation or carries amino acids.

## answer_d
rRNA

## explanation_d
Incorrect. rRNA is a structural and catalytic component of the ribosome (including the peptidyl transferase ribozyme), but it does not itself read codons or carry amino acids the way tRNA does.

## title
Where a charged tRNA enters the ribosome during elongation

## question
When a charged tRNA molecule approaches the ribosomal during the elongation stage, it enters the large ribosomal subunit at the:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
P site

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The P (peptidyl) site holds the tRNA already carrying the growing polypeptide chain — a new incoming charged tRNA does not enter here first; it arrives at the A site.

## answer_b
A site

## explanation_b
Correct. Every new aminoacyl-tRNA delivered during elongation enters the ribosome's A (aminoacyl) site, where its anticodon is checked against the mRNA codon positioned there before peptidyl transferase forms the peptide bond between it and the chain held in the P site. Only after that bond forms does translocation shift the newly extended peptidyl-tRNA out of the A site and into the P site, freeing the A site to receive the next charged tRNA and repeat the cycle.

## answer_c
R site

## explanation_c
Incorrect. There is no "R site" on the ribosome — this is a distractor with no counterpart in the elongation cycle.

## answer_d
E site

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The E (exit) site is where the spent, deacylated tRNA sits immediately before leaving the ribosome — it is where a tRNA exits, not where an incoming charged tRNA first enters.

# Item

## id

## title
Directionality of mRNA translation in the ribosome

## question
Translation of mRNA in a ribosome proceeds as follow:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
From the 5' end to the 3' end

## explanation_a
Correct. The ribosome always reads mRNA in the 5' to 3' direction, moving one codon (three nucleotides) at a time toward the message's 3' end, which is also why the amino acid sequence of the finished polypeptide corresponds directly to the order of codons encountered along that direction. This fixed directionality is also why bacterial ribosomes can begin translating an mRNA while RNA polymerase is still transcribing further downstream sequence — translation always trails transcription in the same 5'-to-3' direction, never the reverse.

## answer_b
From either 5' or 3' end, depending on the enzyme present

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Translation direction is fixed, not enzyme-dependent — every ribosome, prokaryotic or eukaryotic, reads mRNA 5' to 3' without exception.

## answer_c
From the 3' end to the 5' end

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This reverses the true direction. Reading 3' to 5' would misread every codon out of frame and is not how any ribosome operates.

## answer_d
From an origin in the middle simultaneously towards the 3' & 5' ends

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This describes something closer to bidirectional DNA replication from an origin, not translation. A single ribosome reads unidirectionally, 5' to 3', starting at the AUG codon.

# Item

## id

## title
The growing peptide chain stays attached to tRNA

## question
During the course of protein synthesis:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Amino acids are attached to the tRNA randomly

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Attachment is highly specific, not random — each of the 20 aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases charges only its own one amino acid onto its own matching tRNA.

## answer_b
mRNA binds large ribosomal subunit

## explanation_b
Incorrect. mRNA binds the small ribosomal subunit first (30S in prokaryotes, 40S in eukaryotes); the large subunit joins afterward to complete the initiation complex.

## answer_c
The growing peptide chain is attached to the tRNA

## explanation_c
Correct. Throughout elongation, the nascent polypeptide chain remains covalently attached to the tRNA occupying the P site (peptidyl-tRNA) — it is only released from that last tRNA at termination, when peptidyl transferase hydrolyses the bond. This attachment is what keeps the growing chain positioned correctly at the ribosome's active site for every successive peptide bond, rather than diffusing away after each addition.

## answer_d
The rRNA acts as a template for tRNA

## explanation_d
Incorrect. mRNA, not rRNA, is the template that tRNA anticodons read; rRNA's role is structural and catalytic (forming the ribosome and its peptidyl transferase activity), not templating.

## answer_e
The growing peptide chain is synthesized from its carboxyl end

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Polypeptide synthesis proceeds from the amino (N) terminus toward the carboxyl (C) terminus — each new amino acid is added onto the chain's growing C-terminal end, not synthesized starting from the carboxyl end.

# Item

## id

## title
Which ribosome in a polysome has the longest peptide

## question
In a polysome, which ribosomes will have synthesized the longest peptide chains at any one time:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The ones nearest the 3' end of the mRNA molecule

## explanation_a
Correct. Because every ribosome reads the mRNA 5' to 3', the ribosome that initiated earliest has travelled furthest along the message by any given moment — placing it nearest the 3' end — and therefore carries the longest, most nearly complete peptide chain. Viewed under an electron microscope, this is exactly why a polysome looks like beads of increasing peptide length strung along the mRNA, tapering from short chains near the start codon to long, almost-finished chains near the far end.

## answer_b
The ones nearest the middle of the mRNA molecule

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Position in the middle does not by itself indicate chain length; it is proximity to the 3' end specifically, reflecting how much of the message has already been read, that correlates with chain length.

## answer_c
The ones nearest the AUG codon in the mRNA

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Ribosomes nearest the AUG start codon are typically the newest arrivals, at or near the 5' end of the coding region — they have the shortest chains, not the longest.

## answer_d
The ones nearest the 5' end of the mRNA molecule

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This is the reverse of the true relationship. Ribosomes nearest the 5' end initiated most recently and have made the shortest progress along the message.

## answer_e
The ones nearest both 3' & 5' ends of the mRNA molecule

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Chain length correlates with distance travelled from the start, which is a one-directional (5'-to-3') relationship — it is not shared by ribosomes at both ends simultaneously.

# Item

## id

## title
The first amino acid of a bacterial polypeptide

## question
The 1st amino acid in initiation of protein synthesis in E-coli is:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
N-formyl methionine

## explanation_a

## title
What eukaryotic translation initiation begins with

## question
Initiation of protein synthesis in Eukaryotes begins with binding of

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
30S ribosomal unit on mRNA

## explanation_a
Incorrect. 30S is the prokaryotic small-subunit designation; eukaryotic ribosomes use 40S (small) and 60S (large) subunits instead.

## answer_b
40S ribosomal unit on mRNA

## explanation_b
Correct. Eukaryotic initiation begins when the 40S ribosomal subunit, guided by initiation factors bound to the mRNA's 5' cap, binds the mRNA and scans toward the AUG start codon, where methionyl-tRNA (Met-tRNAi) binds before the 60S subunit joins to complete the 80S initiation complex. This cap-dependent scanning mechanism has no Shine-Dalgarno sequence involved at all, which is exactly what distinguishes eukaryotic initiation from the bacterial mechanism tested in the paired question on this same page.

## answer_c
60S ribosomal unit

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The 60S (large) subunit joins only after the 40S subunit, initiation factors and Met-tRNAi are already positioned at the start codon — it is not the step initiation begins with.

## answer_d
Charging of tRNA with specific amino acid

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Charging Met-tRNAi with methionine is a prerequisite step that happens independently, before or alongside ribosome assembly — it is not itself the mRNA-binding step initiation is defined as beginning with.

## answer_e
Attachment of aminoacyl tRNA on mRNA

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Aminoacyl-tRNA is delivered to the ribosome's A site during elongation, where its anticodon pairs with the mRNA codon presented there — it does not attach to mRNA directly or independently of the ribosome.

## title
Which step needs GTP during protein synthesis

## question
Guanosine triphosphate (GTP) is required by which of the following steps in protein synthesis?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Admin team

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
The activation of amino acids with aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Amino acid activation is ATP-dependent, not GTP-dependent — aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase hydrolyses ATP to AMP and pyrophosphate to charge the tRNA.

## answer_b
Attachment of ribosomes to endoplasmic reticulum

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Docking a translating ribosome onto the rough endoplasmic reticulum is mediated by the signal recognition particle and its receptor — a GTP-using process in its own right, but not the step this question is testing, and not part of the core elongation cycle this concept covers.

## answer_c
Binding of aminoacyl tRNA to A site

## explanation_c
Correct. Delivery of each charged aminoacyl-tRNA to the ribosome's A site is driven by an elongation factor (EF-Tu in bacteria, eEF-1 in eukaryotes) together with GTP hydrolysis — one of the two distinct GTP-dependent steps in every elongation cycle, the other being translocation. GTP hydrolysis here is also thought to contribute to accuracy, since the elongation factor only releases the aminoacyl-tRNA into the A site once correct codon-anticodon pairing has been sensed, adding a kinetic proofreading step on top of the small subunit's own decoding check.

## answer_d
Attachment of the small ribosomal subunit binds to the mRNA

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Small-subunit binding to mRNA (at the Shine-Dalgarno sequence in prokaryotes, or via cap-dependent scanning in eukaryotes) does not itself require GTP hydrolysis at that specific step.

## answer_e
Attachment of the large ribosomal subunit to the small subunit

## explanation_e
Incorrect. While GTP hydrolysis (via initiation factor IF-2/eIF-2) accompanies the overall initiation pathway, the specific coupling of large-to-small subunit joining is not the GTP-dependent step this question is testing — aminoacyl-tRNA delivery to the A site is the clearer, elongation-cycle example.
