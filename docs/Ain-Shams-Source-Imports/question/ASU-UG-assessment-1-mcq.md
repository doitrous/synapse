## title
Which of the following changes increases the glomerular filtration rate (GFR)?

## question
Which of the following changes increases the glomerular filtration rate (GFR)?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
A decrease in plasma (glomerular capillary) oncotic pressure

## explanation_a
Correct. GFR is driven by the net filtration pressure across the glomerular capillary, which equals the glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure minus Bowman's capsule hydrostatic pressure minus the glomerular capillary oncotic pressure (the main opposing force to filtration). Lowering plasma oncotic pressure — as happens with hypoalbuminemia — reduces this opposing force, so more of the hydrostatic pressure is available to drive fluid out of the capillary, increasing GFR.

## answer_b
An increase in Bowman's capsule hydrostatic pressure

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Bowman's capsule hydrostatic pressure opposes filtration, so an increase in it (e.g. from obstruction) would decrease, not increase, GFR.

## answer_c
Afferent arteriolar constriction

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Afferent arteriolar constriction reduces glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure and renal plasma flow, decreasing GFR.

## answer_d
An increase in plasma oncotic pressure

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Increased plasma oncotic pressure increases the opposing force to filtration, decreasing GFR — the opposite direction from what the question asks.

## title
Which of the following is used to measure the maximal secretory capacity (secretory power) of the kidney?

## question
Which of the following is used to measure the maximal secretory capacity (secretory power) of the kidney?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Transport maximum for para-aminohippurate (Tm PAH)

## explanation_a
Correct. PAH is both filtered and, once plasma PAH concentration is raised high enough to saturate the proximal tubular organic anion transporters, actively secreted into the tubule at its maximal rate. The transport maximum for PAH (Tm PAH) therefore measures the kidney's maximal tubular secretory capacity, just as Tm glucose measures maximal tubular reabsorptive capacity.

## answer_b
Inulin clearance

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Inulin is freely filtered and neither reabsorbed nor secreted, which is what makes its clearance a measure of GFR, not of tubular secretory capacity.

## answer_c
Creatinine clearance

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Creatinine clearance approximates GFR (with a small secretory component that makes it a slight overestimate), not a measure of maximal secretory power.

## answer_d
Transport maximum for glucose (Tm glucose)

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Tm glucose measures the maximal rate of tubular reabsorption, the opposite direction of transport from secretion.

## title
Bartter syndrome is characteristically associated with which of the following electrolyte abnormalities?

## question
Bartter syndrome is characteristically associated with which of the following electrolyte abnormalities?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Hypokalemia

## explanation_a
Correct. Bartter syndrome is an inherited tubulopathy caused by defective salt reabsorption in the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle, functionally mimicking chronic loop diuretic use. This produces volume depletion that activates the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, and the resulting hyperaldosteronism drives renal potassium wasting, so patients characteristically present with hypokalemia together with metabolic alkalosis.

## answer_b
Hyperkalemia

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Bartter syndrome causes potassium wasting (via secondary hyperaldosteronism), producing hypokalemia rather than hyperkalemia.

## answer_c
Hypernatremia

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Bartter syndrome causes salt wasting with generally normal-to-low blood pressure, not hypernatremia.

## answer_d
Hypercalcemia

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Bartter syndrome is associated with hypercalciuria (urinary calcium loss) but typically normal or low, not high, serum calcium.

## title
According to the principle of glomerulotubular balance, what happens to peritubular capillary oncotic pressure when GFR increases (at constant renal plasma flow)?

## question
According to the principle of glomerulotubular balance, what happens to peritubular capillary oncotic pressure when GFR increases (at constant renal plasma flow)?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Peritubular capillary oncotic pressure increases

## explanation_a
Correct. When GFR rises relative to renal plasma flow, the filtration fraction (GFR/RPF) increases, meaning a larger share of plasma water leaves the glomerular capillaries as filtrate while plasma proteins stay behind in the blood that continues on to the peritubular capillaries. This concentrates protein in the post-glomerular blood, raising peritubular capillary oncotic pressure, which in turn enhances proximal tubular fluid reabsorption — the physiological basis of glomerulotubular balance, which keeps the fraction of filtered load reabsorbed roughly constant despite changes in GFR.

## answer_b
Peritubular capillary oncotic pressure decreases

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Peritubular capillary oncotic pressure rises, not falls, when filtration fraction increases, since protein is left more concentrated in the blood that did not filter.

## answer_c
Peritubular capillary oncotic pressure is unaffected by GFR

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Peritubular capillary oncotic pressure is directly linked to filtration fraction and therefore does change with GFR at a given renal plasma flow.

## answer_d
Peritubular capillary hydrostatic pressure increases while oncotic pressure falls

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Peritubular capillary hydrostatic pressure is set mainly by efferent arteriolar resistance, not directly driven up by GFR in this way, and it is the rise (not a fall) in oncotic pressure that mediates glomerulotubular balance.

## title
Which of the following removes water from the renal medullary interstitium as part of the countercurrent exchange mechanism?

## question
Which of the following removes water from the renal medullary interstitium as part of the countercurrent exchange mechanism?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The ascending limb of the vasa recta

## explanation_a
Correct. The vasa recta act as passive countercurrent exchangers that preserve, rather than dissipate, the medullary osmotic gradient. As blood ascends back toward the cortex in the ascending limb of the vasa recta, it re-enters progressively less hyperosmotic interstitium, so water that had entered the descending limb (and solute that diffused in along the way) moves back out of the vessel into the blood, effectively removing the excess water that entered the medulla and carrying it away.

## answer_b
The descending limb of the vasa recta

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The descending limb of the vasa recta is where water leaves the blood and solute enters as the blood descends into the increasingly hyperosmotic medulla, the opposite direction of net water movement from what the question asks.

## answer_c
The thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle is impermeable to water; it actively transports NaCl out of the tubule to establish the medullary gradient but does not itself remove interstitial water via blood flow.

## answer_d
The descending limb of the loop of Henle

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The descending limb of the loop of Henle is where tubular fluid loses water into the hyperosmotic interstitium, adding water to it rather than removing it.

## title
Ammonia (NH3/NH4+) is an especially effective urinary buffer in which of the following locations?

## question
Ammonia (NH3/NH4+) is an especially effective urinary buffer in which of the following locations?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The kidney

## explanation_a
Correct. The renal tubular epithelium (chiefly the proximal tubule) synthesizes ammonia from glutamine and secretes it into the tubular lumen, where it combines with secreted H+ to form ammonium (NH4+), which is then trapped in the lumen (since the epithelium is far less permeable to the charged NH4+ than to neutral NH3) and excreted in the urine. Because the kidney can increase ammonia synthesis and secretion on demand — unlike the fixed buffering capacity of phosphate — the ammonia buffer system gives the kidney a large, adaptable capacity to excrete the daily acid load and regenerate bicarbonate, making it especially effective specifically in this organ.

## answer_b
The lungs

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The lungs regulate acid-base balance by adjusting CO2 (respiratory) excretion, not by generating and excreting an ammonia buffer.

## answer_c
The plasma alone

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Ammonia buffering is a renal tubular secretory process, not simply a plasma buffer reaction.

## answer_d
The gastrointestinal tract

## explanation_d
Incorrect. While gut bacteria produce ammonia, it is the kidney's regulated synthesis and urinary trapping of ammonia that makes it a titratable, adaptable acid-base buffer system.

## title
Which of the following conditions is associated with a decrease in urinary titratable acid excretion?

## question
Which of the following conditions is associated with a decrease in urinary titratable acid excretion?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Psychogenic (voluntary) hyperventilation

## explanation_a
Correct. Titratable acid excretion depends on renal H+ secretion, which is stimulated by systemic acidosis and suppressed by alkalosis. Psychogenic hyperventilation blows off CO2 and produces a respiratory alkalosis, which reduces the tubular drive to secrete H+ and therefore decreases urinary titratable acid excretion, the opposite of what occurs in an acidotic state.

## answer_b
Chronic kidney disease with metabolic acidosis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Chronic kidney disease with metabolic acidosis reflects reduced net acid excretion capacity overall, but the acidemic stimulus that is present still tends to drive what H+ secretory capacity remains, unlike the alkalosis of hyperventilation which actively suppresses it.

## answer_c
Diabetic ketoacidosis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Diabetic ketoacidosis is a severe acidemic state that maximally stimulates renal H+ secretion and titratable acid excretion (along with ammoniagenesis), not a state that decreases it.

## answer_d
Chronic respiratory acidosis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Chronic respiratory acidosis (CO2 retention) stimulates, rather than decreases, renal H+ secretion and titratable acid excretion as a compensatory response.

## title
Which of the following acid-base disturbances is characteristically found in chronic kidney disease?

## question
Which of the following acid-base disturbances is characteristically found in chronic kidney disease?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Metabolic acidosis

## explanation_a
Correct. As nephron mass and function decline in chronic kidney disease, the kidneys' capacity to excrete the daily fixed acid load (via ammoniagenesis and titratable acid excretion) and to regenerate bicarbonate progressively falls short of dietary acid production, leading to a chronic, typically normal-anion-gap (early) transitioning to increased-anion-gap (advanced) metabolic acidosis. This retained-acid, reduced-excretion state is one of the classic systemic complications of chronic kidney disease.

## answer_b
Metabolic alkalosis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Metabolic alkalosis is not a characteristic feature of CKD; it more typically arises from causes such as vomiting, diuretic use, or mineralocorticoid excess.

## answer_c
Respiratory acidosis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Respiratory acidosis arises from impaired ventilation (a pulmonary/respiratory problem), not from failing renal acid excretion.

## answer_d
Respiratory alkalosis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Respiratory alkalosis arises from hyperventilation, unrelated to the renal acid-excretion defect of CKD.

## title
Which of the following is a recognized cause of hyperkalemia through cellular potassium release?

## question
Which of the following is a recognized cause of hyperkalemia through cellular potassium release?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Crush (rhabdomyolysis) injury

## explanation_a
Correct. Crush injury causes rhabdomyolysis, in which damaged skeletal muscle cells release their large intracellular potassium stores directly into the extracellular fluid, producing a rapid rise in serum potassium that can be severe and life-threatening, especially once reperfusion of the injured tissue occurs. This is a classic cause of hyperkalemia through cell lysis/release rather than through impaired renal excretion alone.

## answer_b
Insulin administration

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Insulin drives potassium into cells (via stimulating the Na+/K+-ATPase), lowering, not raising, serum potassium — it is used therapeutically to treat hyperkalemia.

## answer_c
Beta-2 adrenergic agonist administration (e.g. salbutamol)

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Beta-2 agonists also drive potassium into cells and lower serum potassium, and are likewise used as an emergency treatment for hyperkalemia.

## answer_d
Metabolic alkalosis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Alkalosis promotes potassium shift into cells (in exchange for H+ leaving cells to buffer the alkalosis), tending to lower, not raise, serum potassium; acidosis is the acid-base disturbance associated with hyperkalemia.

Hydrogen ion secretion occurs exclusively in the proximal tubule, not the distal nephron

Incorrect. While the proximal tubule reabsorbs the bulk of filtered bicarbonate via H+ secretion, the distal nephron (intercalated cells) also secretes H+ and is critical for final urinary acidification.

Reabsorption of filtered bicarbonate in the proximal convoluted tubule requires the activity of which of the following?

## question
Reabsorption of filtered bicarbonate in the proximal convoluted tubule requires the activity of which of the following?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Carbonic anhydrase

## explanation_a
Correct. Proximal tubular bicarbonate reabsorption depends on carbonic anhydrase acting at two sites: within the tubular lumen (brush border isoform), it catalyzes the dehydration of secreted-H+-derived carbonic acid into CO2 and water so CO2 can diffuse into the cell, and within the cytoplasm, it catalyzes the reverse reaction, regenerating carbonic acid that dissociates into H+ (resecreted into the lumen) and bicarbonate (reabsorbed across the basolateral membrane into blood). Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (e.g. acetazolamide) block this process and produce a bicarbonaturic diuresis, which is direct pharmacological evidence for the enzyme's essential role.

## answer_b
Aldosterone

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Aldosterone acts mainly on the distal nephron to promote Na+ reabsorption and K+/H+ secretion, not on proximal tubular bicarbonate reabsorption via carbonic anhydrase.

## answer_c
Antidiuretic hormone (ADH)

## explanation_c
Incorrect. ADH regulates collecting duct water permeability and is not required for proximal bicarbonate reabsorption.

## answer_d
Parathyroid hormone

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Parathyroid hormone acts on calcium and phosphate handling in the kidney, not on the carbonic-anhydrase-dependent bicarbonate reabsorption mechanism.

## title
Juxtaglomerular cells release renin primarily in response to which of the following?

## question
Juxtaglomerular cells release renin primarily in response to which of the following?

## subject
renal

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
A drop in afferent arteriolar perfusion pressure

## explanation_a
Correct. Juxtaglomerular cells in the afferent arteriolar wall act as intrarenal baroreceptors, sensing a fall in perfusion pressure (as occurs with volume depletion, hemorrhage, or renal artery stenosis) as reduced stretch of the arteriolar wall; this reduced stretch is a direct stimulus for renin release. Renin then initiates the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone cascade, ultimately promoting sodium and water retention and vasoconstriction to help restore blood pressure and perfusion.

## answer_b
A rise in afferent arteriolar perfusion pressure

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A rise in perfusion pressure increases arteriolar wall stretch, which suppresses, rather than stimulates, renin release.

## answer_c
An increase in NaCl delivery to the macula densa

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Renin release is instead stimulated by decreased (not increased) NaCl delivery to the macula densa, which signals reduced GFR/perfusion via tubuloglomerular feedback.

## answer_d
Parasympathetic stimulation

## explanation_d
Incorrect. It is sympathetic (beta-1 adrenergic), not parasympathetic, stimulation that promotes renin release from juxtaglomerular cells.
