## title
Which lesion produces a kinetic (intention) tremor that worsens as the target is approached?

## question
Which lesion produces a kinetic (intention) tremor that worsens as the target is approached?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Spinocerebellar lesion

## explanation_a
Correct. A spinocerebellar (cerebellar) lesion produces a kinetic, or intention, tremor: absent at rest, it appears and worsens during a targeted voluntary movement, classically demonstrated as past-pointing on the finger-nose-finger test as the finger nears its target. This reflects loss of the cerebellum's real-time error-correction of ongoing movement, which normally damps oscillation as a target is approached.

## answer_b
Substantia nigra lesion

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Substantia nigra degeneration causes the dopamine deficiency of Parkinson disease, whose tremor is a resting (static) tremor that improves, not worsens, with voluntary movement — the opposite pattern.

## answer_c
Basal ganglia lesion

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Basal ganglia lesions more typically produce resting tremor, chorea, or rigidity, not the movement-worsening kinetic tremor of cerebellar disease.

## answer_d
Peripheral nerve lesion

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Peripheral nerve lesions cause sensory loss and/or weakness in the nerve's distribution, not a movement-triggered central tremor.

## title
Hemisection of the spinal cord (Brown-Séquard syndrome) causes which pattern of sensory loss below the lesion?

## question
Hemisection of the spinal cord (Brown-Séquard syndrome) causes which pattern of sensory loss below the lesion?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Ipsilateral dorsal column loss with contralateral pain and temperature loss

## explanation_a
Correct. Because the dorsal columns ascend uncrossed until the medulla, hemisection produces ipsilateral loss of fine touch, vibration and proprioception below the lesion. Because the lateral spinothalamic tract carries fibres that have already crossed near their level of entry, hemisection instead produces contralateral loss of pain and temperature below the lesion — the two losses lie on opposite sides, the hallmark of Brown-Séquard syndrome. The full syndrome also includes ipsilateral upper motor neuron weakness below the lesion, from corticospinal tract involvement.

## answer_b
Contralateral dorsal column loss with ipsilateral pain and temperature loss

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This reverses the pattern: the dorsal columns are uncrossed (so their loss is ipsilateral, not contralateral) while the spinothalamic tract is crossed (so its loss is contralateral, not ipsilateral).

## answer_c
Bilateral loss of all sensory modalities

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Hemisection affects only one half of the cord, so the sensory loss is split between the two sides by modality, not bilateral and total.

## answer_d
Ipsilateral loss of all sensory modalities

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Only the dorsal-column modalities are lost ipsilaterally; pain and temperature are lost on the opposite side, not the same side.

## title
What is the function of the primary motor cortex (area 4)?

## question
What is the function of the primary motor cortex (Brodmann area 4)?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
It converts a planned movement into the specific motor orders that execute it

## explanation_a
Correct. The primary motor cortex, area 4, occupies the precentral gyrus and contains the large pyramidal (Betz) cells that give rise to a substantial share of corticospinal fibres. It receives the planned movement from the premotor and supplementary motor areas and converts that plan into the specific, discrete motor orders — organised somatotopically as the motor homunculus — that execute the movement.

## answer_b
It generates the initial plan for a voluntary movement before any motor order exists

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Generating the initial movement plan, before it is converted into an executable motor order, is chiefly the role of the premotor and supplementary motor areas (and, further upstream, the posterior parietal cortex), not area 4 itself.

## answer_c
It relays proprioceptive feedback from the periphery to the cerebellum

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Proprioceptive feedback to the cerebellum travels via the spinocerebellar tracts, not through the primary motor cortex.

## answer_d
It stores previously learned motor programmes for automatic release

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Storage of learned, well-rehearsed motor programmes for smooth automatic release is a basal ganglia (putamen circuit) function, not area 4's.

## title
A destructive lesion of area 4 produces which clinical picture?

## question
A destructive lesion of the primary motor cortex (area 4) on one side produces which clinical picture?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Contralateral (crossed) hemiplegia with the most severe loss of fine skilled movement

## explanation_a
Correct. Because the corticospinal tract, arising chiefly from area 4, decussates at the pyramidal decussation in the lower medulla, a unilateral area 4 lesion produces hemiplegia of the contralateral (crossed) side of the body. Fine, discrete, skilled voluntary movements — most severely affecting the distal limb and hand — are lost first and most completely, because they depend most heavily on direct corticospinal (corticomotoneuronal) control.

## answer_b
Ipsilateral hemiplegia sparing fine skilled movement

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Because the corticospinal tract crosses at the medullary pyramids, an area 4 lesion produces contralateral, not ipsilateral, weakness, and fine skilled movement is precisely what is lost, not spared.

## answer_c
Bilateral flaccid paralysis of all four limbs

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A unilateral cortical lesion affects the contralateral side of the body, not all four limbs, and produces spastic (upper motor neuron) rather than flaccid weakness once spinal shock resolves.

## answer_d
Contralateral hemiplegia with fine skilled movement fully preserved

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Fine skilled movement is the most severely affected function after an area 4 lesion, not the one preserved — it depends most on the corticospinal pathway the lesion destroys.

## question
Deficient central transmission of which neurotransmitter is classically associated with mood disorders such as depression?

Glycine

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Glycine is chiefly an inhibitory neurotransmitter of the spinal cord and brainstem, without the mood-regulation role attributed to serotonin.

## title
Which theory explains why rubbing a painful area relieves pain?

## question
Which theory explains why rubbing a painful area provides pain relief?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Gate control theory

## explanation_a
Correct. The gate control theory (Melzack and Wall) proposes that inhibitory interneurons in the spinal cord dorsal horn form a 'gate' regulating nociceptive transmission. Large-diameter, fast-conducting A-beta touch/pressure afferents activate these interneurons, closing the gate and suppressing the slower A-delta/C-fibre pain signals arriving from the same segment — which is why rubbing or massaging a painful area, activating A-beta afferents, provides real pain relief.

## answer_b
Specificity theory

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Specificity theory holds that dedicated, labelled-line pain fibres and receptors independently transmit pain without modulation by other sensory input, which does not explain how competing touch input can reduce pain — the opposite of the observed rubbing effect.

## answer_c
Pattern theory

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Pattern theory proposes that pain arises from a pattern of intense stimulation across non-specific receptors rather than through a specific inhibitory dorsal-horn 'gate', and does not account for the touch-mediated relief seen with rubbing.

## answer_d
Referred pain theory

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Referred pain theory concerns why pain from one location is perceived at a different (typically visceral-to-somatic) site via converging afferents; it does not explain relief obtained by rubbing the painful area itself.

Which receptor is chiefly responsible for regulating resting muscle tone?

## question
Regarding muscle tone, which receptor is chiefly responsible for regulating it?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The muscle spindle

## explanation_a
Correct. Resting muscle tone is set primarily by the muscle spindle's stretch (myotatic) reflex: spindle afferents signal muscle length and rate of stretch, driving reflex alpha-motor-neuron activity that resists over-lengthening and maintains tone. This is distinct from the Golgi tendon organ's tension-sensing, protective role.

## answer_b
The Golgi tendon organ

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The Golgi tendon organ is a tension receptor at the musculotendinous junction whose role is autogenic inhibition (protecting against excess tension), not the length-sensing role that sets resting tone.

## answer_c
The Pacinian corpuscle

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The Pacinian corpuscle is a rapidly adapting cutaneous receptor for vibration/pressure change, unrelated to muscle tone regulation.

## answer_d
The free nerve ending

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Free nerve endings are chiefly nociceptors and thermoreceptors, not the specialised proprioceptors responsible for the stretch reflex that sets muscle tone.

## question
A migraine attack classically begins with which vascular phase?

Venous thrombosis

## title
How do cutaneous touch receptors such as Meissner's corpuscles classically adapt to a sustained stimulus?

## question
How do cutaneous touch receptors such as Meissner's corpuscles classically adapt to a sustained, unchanging stimulus?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Rapidly adapting

## explanation_a
Correct. Touch receptors such as Meissner's and Pacinian corpuscles are classically rapidly adapting: firing falls off quickly despite an unchanging stimulus, so they signal onset, change and removal of a stimulus (velocity/vibration) rather than its constant presence. Slowly adapting receptors do the opposite, continuing to fire for as long as the stimulus is present.

## answer_b
Slowly adapting

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Slowly adapting receptors — Merkel discs and Ruffini endings — continue firing throughout a sustained stimulus to signal steady pressure or stretch; this is the opposite behaviour to Meissner's/Pacinian corpuscles.

## answer_c
Non-adapting (tonic)

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A truly non-adapting (tonic) receptor would maintain a constant firing rate for the stimulus's whole duration, which does not describe any classic cutaneous mechanoreceptor.

## answer_d
Adaptation depends only on stimulus intensity, not receptor type

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Adaptation rate is a defining structural/functional property of the receptor type itself, not something that depends only on stimulus intensity.

Striatal activation of the indirect pathway has what net effect on the subthalamic nucleus?

## status

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Disinhibition (release from tonic GPe inhibition)

## explanation_a
Correct. In the indirect pathway, striatal (D2) neurons inhibit the external globus pallidus (GPe), which normally exerts tonic inhibitory control over the subthalamic nucleus (STN). Direct excitation via a monosynaptic glutamatergic projection

Further inhibition, adding to the GPe's tonic restraint

Incorrect. This is the opposite of the correct double-negative logic — striatal inhibition of the GPe removes (rather than adds to) the GPe's restraint on the STN.

## answer_d
No effect — the subthalamic nucleus is not part of the indirect pathway

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The subthalamic nucleus is a core, defining node of the indirect pathway, receiving GPe inhibition and, once disinhibited, driving increased output to the internal pallidal segment.

A sudden decrease in temperature only

Mechanical pressure regardless of temperature

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Thermal nociceptors are activated specifically by noxious temperature, not by mechanical pressure — that stimulus modality is signalled by a separate class of mechanoreceptors/mechanonociceptors.
