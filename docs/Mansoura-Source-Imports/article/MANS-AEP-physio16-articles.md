## summary
A chemical synapse's postsynaptic receptor, not the transmitter itself, decides whether the synapse excites (Na+ entry, EPSP) or inhibits (Cl- entry, IPSP). Transmission is modulated by pH and oxygenation (alkalosis facilitates, acidosis and hypoxia depress), weakens with repetitive use (synaptic fatigue, mainly from presynaptic transmitter depletion), and — unlike the far more common chemical synapse — a small number of CNS sites such as the hippocampus instead use electrical (gap-junction) synapses.

## sections
### Definition
Synaptic transmission is the passing of a signal from one neuron to the next across a synapse. At the far more common chemical synapse, a presynaptic neurotransmitter crosses the synaptic cleft and binds a postsynaptic receptor, producing either an excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP, a depolarisation) or an inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP, a hyperpolarisation). At an electrical synapse, a gap junction instead lets ionic current pass directly between two neurons' cytoplasm, with no chemical intermediary.

### Mechanism
Whether a synapse excites or inhibits is set by the postsynaptic receptor's ion selectivity, not by the transmitter itself: an excitatory synapse opens ligand-gated Na+ channels (EPSP), an inhibitory synapse opens ligand-gated Cl- channels (IPSP), and the same transmitter can do either depending on which receptor it binds. With repetitive or prolonged stimulation, a synapse's response progressively declines — synaptic fatigue — mainly because the rate of presynaptic transmitter release outpaces the rate of resynthesis, exhausting the terminal's transmitter store, with a smaller contribution from progressive desensitisation of postsynaptic receptors. An electrical synapse's gap junction lets current flow directly between neurons without the delay a chemical synapse's release-and-binding sequence imposes.

### Key determinants
Synaptic transmission is sensitive to the blood's acid-base state and oxygenation: alkalosis raises neuronal excitability and facilitates transmission, while acidosis and oxygen lack both depress it. Repetition itself is a determinant through synaptic fatigue, since sustained high-frequency use outpaces transmitter resynthesis. Which synapse type is present — chemical (the great majority) or the uncommon electrical gap-junction type — also determines whether transmission is receptor-mediated and delayed or direct and essentially instantaneous.

### Clinical significance
Hypoventilation depresses neuronal activity by retaining CO2 and driving the blood toward acidosis, while a sufficiently severe alkalosis (as from hyperventilation) can raise excitability enough to provoke seizures — the pH sensitivity of synaptic transmission is the mechanism behind both. Electrical synapses are uncommon in the mammalian CNS; the hippocampus is the classic example of where they occur.

## hold_these
Receptor (not transmitter) sets excitatory (Na+, EPSP) vs inhibitory (Cl-, IPSP) action. Alkalosis facilitates, acidosis/hypoxia depress transmission. Synaptic fatigue is mainly presynaptic transmitter depletion. Electrical synapses are gap junctions, uncommon in the CNS, exemplified by the hippocampus.

## lose_the_mark
Treating a transmitter's excitatory/inhibitory action as fixed rather than receptor-dependent. Reversing the alkalosis-facilitates/acidosis-depresses pairing. Locating synaptic fatigue's main cause postsynaptically. Confusing an electrical (gap-junction) synapse with the far more common chemical synapse.
