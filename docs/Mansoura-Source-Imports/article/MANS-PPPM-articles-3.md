## summary
Chronic urinary bilharziasis predisposes to squamous cell carcinoma of the bladder, unlike the transitional-cell carcinoma typical of bladder cancer overall.

## sections
### Definition
Chronic infestation of the urinary bladder by Schistosoma haematobium (urinary bilharziasis, or schistosomiasis) is a well-recognised predisposing factor for cancer of the urinary bladder. Unlike bladder cancer overall, which is most often transitional-cell (urothelial) carcinoma, bilharzial bladder cancer is characteristically squamous cell carcinoma.

### Mechanism
The eggs deposited by the parasite in the bladder wall provoke chronic inflammation, fibrosis and squamous metaplasia of the normally transitional (urothelial) lining. It is against this background of long-standing irritation and metaplastic change, itself a recognised precancerous process, that malignant transformation occurs, giving rise to a squamous rather than a urothelial cancer because it arises from the squamous-metaplastic epithelium.

### Key determinants
The strength of this association rests on the chronicity of the infestation: repeated egg deposition sustains the inflammatory and metaplastic cycle over years, which is what distinguishes bilharzial bladder cancer's mechanism from the chemical-carcinogen or smoking-driven pathways that more often produce transitional-cell bladder cancer elsewhere in the world.

### Clinical significance
Recognising bilharziasis as a bladder-cancer risk factor matters for screening and index-of-suspicion in endemic regions, and for correctly anticipating a squamous rather than urothelial histology when bladder cancer is suspected in a patient with a history of schistosomiasis, since the two histological types carry different behaviour and treatment implications.

## hold_these
Chronic urinary bilharziasis (Schistosoma haematobium) predisposes to squamous cell carcinoma of the bladder.
This differs from most bladder cancer worldwide, which is transitional-cell (urothelial) carcinoma.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming bilharzial bladder cancer is transitional-cell rather than squamous, or naming an unrelated cancer as bilharziasis's classic association.

## summary
Fibroblastic proliferation is the single most reliable histological evidence that an inflammatory process is chronic rather than acute.

## sections
### Definition
Several histological features can accompany a prolonged inflammatory process, but they differ in how specifically they point to chronicity. Fibroblastic proliferation, the active laying-down of new connective-tissue cells and matrix, is the single most reliable piece of evidence that an inflammatory process is chronic rather than acute.

### Mechanism
Fibroblastic proliferation reflects the repair-and-matrix-deposition component that develops only once an inflammatory process has persisted long enough for fibrosis to begin. It requires sustained cytokine signalling, chiefly from macrophages that have themselves accumulated over a prolonged course, so its presence implies that the inflammatory process has already been running long enough to recruit and activate that repair machinery.

### Key determinants
Congested blood vessels, exudate, and even macrophages and necrosis can all be seen in acute or subacute inflammation as well as in chronic inflammation, so none of them reliably distinguishes the two states. Fibroblastic proliferation is what sets chronic inflammation's histology apart, because it marks the point at which ongoing tissue destruction is being matched by attempted healing through fibrosis, a combination unique to chronicity.

### Clinical significance
Recognising fibroblastic proliferation on a biopsy helps a pathologist correctly stage an inflammatory process as chronic, which in turn shapes clinical management, since chronic inflammatory conditions typically call for a different diagnostic work-up and treatment approach from an acute, self-limiting process.

## hold_these
Fibroblastic proliferation is the most reliable histological evidence of chronic inflammation.
Congested vessels, exudate, macrophages and necrosis can all occur in acute inflammation too, so none of them is specific to chronicity.

## lose_the_mark
Choosing a feature that can also occur in acute inflammation (congestion, exudate, macrophages, necrosis) instead of fibroblastic proliferation.

## summary
Immune-mediated granuloma formation is a type IV (delayed) hypersensitivity reaction that begins with a macrophage presenting antigen to T lymphocytes.

## sections
### Definition
Immune-mediated granulomatous inflammation is driven by a type IV, delayed-type, cell-mediated hypersensitivity reaction, distinct from the antibody-mediated type I, II and III reactions. Its pathogenesis follows a defined sequence, with macrophage antigen presentation as the first step.

### Mechanism
A macrophage processes a persistent, poorly degradable antigen and presents it to CD4+ T helper lymphocytes, which is the first step in granuloma pathogenesis. The sensitised T cells then release cytokines, chiefly interferon-gamma, that recruit and activate further macrophages, driving their transformation into epithelioid cells and, with continued stimulation, their fusion into multinucleated giant cells around the persistent antigen.

### Key determinants
What makes this a type IV rather than an antibody-mediated hypersensitivity reaction is that the whole process is driven by sensitised T cells and macrophage activation rather than by circulating antibody binding antigen, complement, or immune complexes, the mechanisms behind types I, II and III respectively.

### Clinical significance
Because the sequence always begins with antigen presentation, a persistent, poorly clearable antigen, whether an organism such as the tubercle bacillus or an inert foreign material, is a prerequisite for granuloma formation, which is why granulomas are the hallmark tissue response to infections and materials the immune system cannot fully eliminate.

## hold_these
Granuloma formation is mediated by type IV, delayed-type, hypersensitivity.
Macrophage antigen presentation to T lymphocytes is the first step of granuloma pathogenesis.

## lose_the_mark
Selecting an antibody-mediated hypersensitivity type (I, II or III) for granuloma pathogenesis, or naming a later event (giant-cell formation, fibrosis, mediator release) as the first step.

## summary
Russell bodies, plasma cells distended with stored immunoglobulin, are a classic histological feature of rhinoscleroma alongside Mikulicz cells.

## sections
### Definition
Rhinoscleroma is a chronic granulomatous infection of the nose and upper respiratory tract caused by Klebsiella rhinoscleromatis. Russell bodies, rounded, eosinophilic, hyaline-change inclusions formed by plasma cells overloaded with stored immunoglobulin, are a classic histological feature of this disease.

### Mechanism
Rhinoscleroma's histology shows sheets of large, vacuolated macrophages, Mikulicz cells, containing the causative bacilli, accompanied by numerous plasma cells reacting to the chronic bacterial stimulus. A proportion of these plasma cells become so distended with stored immunoglobulin that their cytoplasm forms the rounded, hyaline-change inclusions recognised as Russell bodies.

### Key determinants
While Russell bodies can occur wherever plasma cells accumulate in chronic inflammation, their combination with Mikulicz cells specifically is what makes the pairing a classic, exam-favourite marker of rhinoscleroma rather than of chronic inflammation generally.

### Clinical significance
Recognising Mikulicz cells with Russell bodies on a nasal or upper-respiratory biopsy points a pathologist toward rhinoscleroma over other chronic granulomatous conditions of the region, guiding the correct antimicrobial management for this specific bacterial infection.

## hold_these
Russell bodies are plasma cells distended with stored immunoglobulin, forming rounded hyaline-change inclusions.
Russell bodies are a classic histological feature of rhinoscleroma, alongside Mikulicz cells.

## lose_the_mark
Choosing an unrelated acute or viral condition that does not feature the chronic, plasma-cell-rich granulomatous infiltrate in which Russell bodies form.

## summary
Surgical wounds heal fastest in highly vascular regions such as the face, because the local blood supply drives every stage of early repair.

## sections
### Definition
The speed of wound healing is strongly influenced by the local blood supply of the region in which a wound lies. Highly vascular regions such as the face and scalp heal comparatively quickly and with minimal scarring.

### Mechanism
The vascular response of early repair, angiogenesis, delivery of inflammatory and repair cells, and oxygen and nutrient supply to proliferating fibroblasts, depends directly on the region's blood supply. A well-vascularised region can mount this response faster and more completely than a poorly vascularised one.

### Key determinants
Poorly vascularised regions such as the distal leg or foot heal more slowly for the same reason in reverse, and are more prone to complications such as infection or wound dehiscence, since a sluggish vascular response delivers fewer of the cells and nutrients repair depends on.

### Clinical significance
Surgeons anticipate this regional difference when planning wound care, incision placement and follow-up, expecting a facial or scalp wound to heal faster than one on the distal limb, and adjusting monitoring for complications accordingly in poorly vascularised sites.

## hold_these
Wound healing speed depends strongly on the local blood supply of the region.
Highly vascular regions such as the face heal fastest; poorly vascularised regions such as the foot heal slowest.

## lose_the_mark
Choosing a poorly vascularised distal region as healing fastest, or attributing healing-speed differences to a factor other than local vascularity.

## summary
Plasma proteins escaping into inflamed tissue raise interstitial osmotic pressure, which draws in additional fluid and produces the protein-rich exudate of acute inflammation.

## sections
### Definition
Acute inflammation increases vascular permeability, allowing protein-rich plasma to escape from vessels into the interstitial tissue. This escape of plasma proteins is what raises interstitial (tissue) osmotic pressure during acute inflammation.

### Mechanism
Mediators such as histamine and leukotrienes act on venular endothelium to widen the gaps between endothelial cells, letting first the smaller albumin molecules and then, as permeability increases further, larger proteins leak into the interstitial space. As these proteins accumulate in the tissue, they raise the interstitial colloid osmotic pressure above that of the plasma.

### Key determinants
This reversed osmotic gradient then draws additional fluid out of the vessels and into the tissue by osmosis, compounding the initial vascular leakage. This is what distinguishes an inflammatory exudate, protein-rich, from a transudate, which forms from hydrostatic imbalance alone without this protein-driven osmotic component.

### Clinical significance
Recognising the protein-driven osmotic mechanism explains why inflammatory swelling can outlast the initial permeability increase, and why draining or aspirating an inflammatory effusion typically yields a protein-rich, often turbid, fluid rather than the clear, low-protein fluid of a transudate.

## hold_these
Escape of plasma proteins into inflamed tissue raises interstitial osmotic pressure.
The reversed osmotic gradient draws more fluid into the tissue, producing a protein-rich exudate rather than a transudate.

## lose_the_mark
Attributing increased tissue osmotic pressure to electrolyte concentration, particle size, intracellular protein, or salt-and-water retention rather than to escaped plasma proteins.

## summary
Acute inflammation is protective overall, but it can itself harm the host, including through tissue swelling from oedema.

## sections
### Definition
Acute inflammation is fundamentally a protective response: it dilutes and neutralises toxins, delivers phagocytes and antibodies to the site of injury, and helps limit the spread of infection. The same processes that make it protective can, however, also cause harm to the host.

### Mechanism
Digestive enzymes released from activated leukocytes and dead cells can damage normal adjacent tissue as an unwanted bystander effect of the very response meant to clear the injurious agent. The oedema fluid of the inflammatory exudate also produces tissue swelling as a direct physical consequence of increased vascular permeability.

### Key determinants
Whether that swelling is merely uncomfortable or genuinely dangerous depends on its location: swelling in a confined or vital space, such as laryngeal oedema narrowing the airway, or cerebral oedema raising intracranial pressure, can be directly harmful, while swelling in more compliant soft tissue is usually only painful and limits mobility.

### Clinical significance
Clinicians weigh this harmful potential when managing inflammation in anatomically confined regions, sometimes treating the inflammatory swelling itself, for example with corticosteroids in threatened airway oedema, rather than relying only on treating the underlying cause.

## hold_these

## summary
Pathological pigments are classified as endogenous, made by the body itself, such as melanin and lipofuscin, or exogenous, introduced from outside, such as carbon dust and tattoo ink.

## sections
### Definition
Pathological pigments are classified by their origin into two groups. Endogenous pigments are synthesised within the body itself; exogenous pigments are introduced into the body from an outside source.

### Mechanism
Endogenous pigments include melanin, made by melanocytes, lipofuscin, a wear-and-tear lipid-peroxidation product, haemosiderin, stored iron derived from haemoglobin breakdown, bilirubin, and hemozoin, a malarial-parasite pigment derived from haemoglobin. Exogenous pigments include inhaled carbon or coal dust producing anthracosis and other pneumoconioses, tattoo ink, and heavy metals such as lead.

### Key determinants
Because exogenous pigments are not produced by the body's own metabolism, they are cleared, if at all, only very slowly; inhaled particulate pigments such as carbon are typically taken up and retained by macrophages rather than broken down, which is why they persist in tissue for decades.

### Clinical significance
Correctly classifying a pigment by origin helps explain its clinical behaviour, for example why a coal miner's anthracotic lungs remain pigmented for life while jaundice from bilirubin, an endogenous pigment, clears once the underlying cause resolves, and it is a recurring exam distinction across pathology.

## hold_these
Endogenous pigments are made within the body: melanin, lipofuscin, haemosiderin, bilirubin, hemozoin.
Exogenous pigments come from outside the body: carbon dust/pneumoconiosis, tattoo ink, lead.

## lose_the_mark
Classifying an inhaled or externally introduced pigment (anthracosis, tattoo ink) as endogenous because it sits within body tissue, or a body-made pigment as exogenous.

## summary
Rheumatoid arthritis is classified as an immune-mediated (autoimmune) chronic inflammatory disease of the synovium.

## sections
### Definition
Chronic inflammatory diseases can be classified by their underlying cause into persistent-infective, immune-mediated (autoimmune), and prolonged-toxic-exposure categories. Rheumatoid arthritis is classified as an immune-mediated chronic inflammatory disease.

### Mechanism
Rheumatoid arthritis is an autoimmune condition in which the synovium of joints is chronically infiltrated by lymphocytes, plasma cells and macrophages. This infiltrate is driven by autoantibodies, such as rheumatoid factor and anti-citrullinated peptide antibody, and by self-perpetuating cytokine-mediated immune activity, rather than by a persistent infectious organism or a toxic exposure.

### Key determinants
What separates rheumatoid arthritis from an infective or toxic-exposure chronic inflammation is precisely this autoimmune driver: the immune system is attacking the joint's own tissue rather than responding to a persisting external agent, which is why it belongs in the immune-mediated category rather than the infective or metabolic categories.

### Clinical significance
This immune-mediated classification explains rheumatoid arthritis's systemic features beyond the joints and its response to immunosuppressive and biological, cytokine-targeted, therapy, guiding treatment choices that would not apply to an infective or metabolic arthritis.

## hold_these
Rheumatoid arthritis is classified as an immune-mediated (autoimmune) chronic inflammatory disease.
Its synovial infiltrate of lymphocytes, plasma cells and macrophages is driven by autoantibodies and cytokines, not by a persistent infection or toxin.

## lose_the_mark
Misclassifying rheumatoid arthritis as acute inflammation, a foreign-body reaction, a metabolic disease, or a vascular disease.
