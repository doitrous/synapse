## summary
When two drugs with the same effect are combined, the outcome is classified by comparing the net effect against the two individual effects: exactly the sum is additive, more than the sum is synergism, one weak agent boosting a strong one is potentiation, and a reduced combined effect is antagonism.

## sections
### Definition
An additive effect is exactly the arithmetic sum of two same-effect drugs' individual effects — for example, two mild analgesics together giving pain relief equal to the sum of each drug's own effect. This is the baseline case against which synergism and antagonism are both defined.

### Mechanism
Synergism is a combined effect greater than the simple sum of the two individual effects, with both drugs already active on their own. Potentiation is the specific case where one agent, with little or no effect of its own, increases the effect of a second, active drug. Antagonism is a combined effect smaller than expected, one drug reducing the action of the other.

### Key determinants
Whether both combined drugs are independently active (synergism) or one is essentially inactive on its own (potentiation) is what separates those two terms, and whether the net effect is larger than, equal to, or smaller than the simple sum determines synergism, an additive effect, or antagonism respectively.

### Clinical significance
Recognising which combination category applies guides safe co-prescribing: an additive or synergistic combination may allow lower individual doses for the same effect, while an unrecognised antagonistic combination can silently undertreat a patient.

## hold_these
An additive effect is exactly the sum of the two individual effects; synergism exceeds that sum.
Potentiation specifically involves an agent with little or no effect of its own boosting an active drug, not two already-active drugs.

## lose_the_mark
Treating "synergism" and "potentiation" as interchangeable terms for any enhanced combined effect, instead of checking whether both drugs are independently active (synergism) or one is essentially inactive on its own (potentiation).

## summary
Salbutamol is a short-acting beta-2 selective adrenergic agonist that relaxes bronchial smooth muscle with relatively little cardiac stimulation, making it the standard bronchodilator for both acute asthma attacks and chronic asthma management.

## sections
### Definition
Salbutamol (albuterol) is a short-acting adrenergic agonist selective for beta-2 receptors over beta-1.

### Mechanism
Selective beta-2 stimulation relaxes bronchial smooth muscle, producing bronchodilation, with relatively little of the beta-1-mediated cardiac stimulation seen with non-selective agonists such as isoprenaline or ephedrine.

### Key determinants
Receptor selectivity is what separates salbutamol from non-selective sympathomimetics (ephedrine, isoprenaline) and from beta blockers (propranolol, timolol), which act on the same receptor family in the opposite direction.

### Clinical significance
Salbutamol is used both for quick relief of acute asthma attacks and, alongside longer-acting agents, in chronic asthma management. Non-selective beta blockers such as propranolol or timolol are avoided in asthma because they can precipitate bronchospasm — the opposite pharmacological direction from a beta-2 agonist.

## hold_these
Salbutamol is beta-2 selective, giving bronchodilation with comparatively little cardiac (beta-1) stimulation.
Non-selective beta blockers (propranolol, timolol) can precipitate bronchospasm and are avoided in asthma.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing a beta-2 selective agonist (bronchodilator, used in asthma) with a beta blocker (contraindicated in asthma) purely because both names mention "beta".

## summary
Neostigmine, an indirect-acting cholinesterase inhibitor, raises acetylcholine at gut muscarinic receptors, stimulating smooth muscle contraction and increasing GIT motility — the opposite clinical direction from antimuscarinic drugs, which reduce it.

## sections
### Definition
Neostigmine is an indirect-acting, reversible cholinesterase inhibitor.

### Mechanism
Neostigmine blocks acetylcholinesterase, raising acetylcholine levels at muscarinic receptors in the gut wall, which stimulates smooth muscle contraction and increases gastrointestinal motility.

### Key determinants
This is the opposite clinical direction from antimuscarinic drugs (atropine, scopolamine, ipratropium, benztropine), which instead reduce GIT motility and secretions — the receptor action (agonist-like accumulation of acetylcholine versus receptor blockade) is what separates the two directions.

### Clinical significance
Neostigmine is used clinically for postoperative paralytic ileus and for urinary retention from bladder atony, distinct from its more commonly tested neuromuscular-junction reversal-of-blockade use at nicotinic receptors.

## hold_these
Neostigmine increases GIT motility by raising acetylcholine at muscarinic receptors in the gut wall.
Antimuscarinic drugs (atropine, scopolamine, ipratropium) reduce, rather than increase, GIT motility.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing neostigmine's GIT-motility use with its more commonly tested neuromuscular-junction reversal-of-blockade use — same drug class mechanism, different receptor site and clinical purpose.

## summary
Local anaesthetics block voltage-gated sodium channels in the neuronal membrane, preventing the nerve fibre from generating or propagating an action potential, producing local, reversible loss of sensation.

## sections
### Definition
Local anaesthetics are drugs that produce local, reversible loss of sensation by preventing nerve impulse conduction.

### Mechanism
Local anaesthetic drugs bind voltage-gated sodium channels in the neuronal cell membrane, blocking the rapid sodium influx needed for the rising (depolarising) phase of the action potential. Decreasing sodium permeability this way prevents the nerve fibre from conducting an impulse past the blocked segment.

### Key determinants
This axonal-conduction mechanism is distinct from neuromuscular blockers (acting on nicotinic receptors), cholinesterase inhibitors (raising synaptic acetylcholine) or drugs blocking transmitter release from nerve terminals — local anaesthetics act upstream of any synapse, on the axon itself.

### Clinical significance
Blocking sodium-channel-dependent conduction is what makes a local anaesthetic effective for regional pain control during minor procedures and surgery, with the block localised to wherever the drug is applied or infiltrated.

## hold_these
Local anaesthetics block voltage-gated sodium channels, decreasing membrane sodium permeability.
This prevents action potential generation/propagation along the nerve fibre, distinct from synaptic-receptor mechanisms.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing the sodium-channel mechanism of local anaesthetics with a synaptic-receptor mechanism (nicotinic blockade, acetylcholinesterase inhibition), when local anaesthetics act on axonal conduction itself.

## summary
Phenoxybenzamine is a non-selective alpha blocker that binds the receptor covalently and irreversibly, a property exploited for preoperative preparation in pheochromocytoma and a textbook example of covalent drug-receptor interaction.

## sections
### Definition
Phenoxybenzamine is a non-selective alpha-adrenergic receptor blocker that binds covalently to the receptor.

### Mechanism
Phenoxybenzamine binds covalently to the alpha-adrenergic receptor, forming an essentially irreversible bond rather than the reversible, competitive binding of drugs such as prazosin or atenolol. Its blockade does not depend on maintaining a plasma drug concentration and persists until new receptors are synthesised.

### Key determinants
Covalent, irreversible binding versus reversible, competitive binding is what separates phenoxybenzamine from the subtype-selective alpha blockers (prazosin, yohimbine, tamsulosin), and this same covalent bond is a textbook example of covalent drug-receptor interaction.

### Clinical significance
Because a pheochromocytoma can release large, unpredictable catecholamine surges, this irreversible, non-selective alpha blockade is specifically valuable for preoperative preparation, controlling the severe hypertensive episodes those surges would otherwise cause. A beta blocker is added only after adequate alpha blockade, to avoid unopposed alpha-mediated hypertensive crisis.

## hold_these
Phenoxybenzamine's alpha blockade is covalent and irreversible, unlike the reversible, competitive alpha blockers.
In pheochromocytoma, alpha blockade must be established before any beta blocker is added.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing phenoxybenzamine's non-selective, irreversible mechanism with the reversible, subtype-selective alpha blockers (prazosin, yohimbine, tamsulosin) used for other indications.

## summary
Yohimbine selectively blocks alpha-2 receptors, while tamsulosin selectively blocks the alpha-1A and alpha-1D subtypes concentrated in prostatic tissue, sparing vascular alpha-1B — which is why tamsulosin relieves BPH symptoms with comparatively little effect on blood pressure.

## sections
### Definition
Alpha-adrenergic antagonists differ in receptor-subtype selectivity, and yohimbine and tamsulosin are worked examples of alpha-2 and alpha-1A/alpha-1D selectivity respectively.

### Mechanism
Yohimbine is a selective alpha-2 antagonist. Because alpha-2 receptors are presynaptic autoreceptors that normally inhibit further noradrenaline release, blocking them increases sympathetic outflow. Tamsulosin is selective for the alpha-1A and alpha-1D subtypes that predominate in prostatic and bladder-neck smooth muscle, relative to the alpha-1B subtype that predominates in vascular smooth muscle.

### Key determinants
This subtype selectivity is why tamsulosin relieves the smooth-muscle-mediated urinary symptoms of benign prostatic hyperplasia with comparatively little effect on blood pressure, unlike the broader alpha-1 blockade of prazosin or the non-selective, irreversible blockade of phenoxybenzamine.

### Clinical significance
Tamsulosin's uroselectivity makes it a first-line drug for benign prostatic hyperplasia symptoms with a favourable blood-pressure side-effect profile, while yohimbine's alpha-2 antagonism increases sympathetic outflow rather than treating urinary symptoms.

## hold_these
Yohimbine is selective for alpha-2 receptors.
Tamsulosin is selective for alpha-1A/alpha-1D (prostatic) over alpha-1B (vascular), giving it little blood-pressure effect compared with less-selective alpha-1 blockers.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming every alpha blocker lowers blood pressure to the same degree, instead of checking receptor-subtype selectivity.

## summary
Noradrenaline's combined alpha-1 vasoconstrictor and beta-1 inotropic action makes it a standard vasopressor, given as a slow, titrated intravenous infusion to raise blood pressure in shock.

## sections
### Definition
Noradrenaline (norepinephrine) is an endogenous catecholamine used clinically as an intravenous vasopressor.

### Mechanism
Noradrenaline is a potent agonist at alpha-1, alpha-2 and beta-1 adrenergic receptors, with little beta-2 activity. Its alpha-1-mediated vasoconstriction and beta-1-mediated increase in cardiac contractility together raise blood pressure.

### Key determinants
This alpha-1/beta-1-dominant receptor profile, with little beta-2 activity, is what distinguishes noradrenaline's pressor action from agents with significant beta-2 vasodilator activity (isoprenaline) or from agents used to lower blood pressure (clonidine, phenoxybenzamine).

### Clinical significance
Given as a slow, carefully titrated intravenous infusion, noradrenaline is a standard vasopressor used clinically to raise blood pressure in states such as septic or other forms of shock.

## hold_these

## summary
A prodrug is administered in an inactive or much less active form and is converted in the body, usually by hepatic metabolism, into an active metabolite that produces the therapeutic effect.

## sections
### Definition
A prodrug is a compound administered in an inactive, or much less active, form that is converted in the body — usually by hepatic metabolism — into its active metabolite, which then produces the therapeutic effect.

### Mechanism
The inactive parent compound undergoes metabolic conversion, usually hepatic, into an active metabolite; only after this conversion does the therapeutic effect appear.

### Key determinants
Being inactive (or weakly active) until metabolic conversion is what separates a prodrug from a prototype (first-in-class reference) drug, a depot formulation releasing already-active drug gradually from tissue storage, or a drug undergoing no metabolism at all.

### Clinical significance
Prodrugs are designed to improve properties such as absorption, stability or reduced local irritation compared with giving the active form directly, which matters when choosing or explaining a drug's formulation.

## hold_these
A prodrug is inactive (or weakly active) until converted in the body to an active metabolite.
This is distinct from a prototype drug and from a depot/sustained-release formulation.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing "prodrug" with "prototype drug" on name similarity, or with a depot formulation that releases the already-active drug gradually rather than requiring metabolic activation.
