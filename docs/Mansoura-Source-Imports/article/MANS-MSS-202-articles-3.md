## summary
Non-narcotic analgesics lower fever by inhibiting central prostaglandin synthesis, and, lacking any opioid receptor action, produce none of the euphoria or dependence risk that defines narcotic analgesics.

## sections
### Definition
Non-narcotic (non-opioid) analgesics are the NSAID and paracetamol family: drugs that relieve pain and fever through cyclooxygenase inhibition rather than through opioid receptor binding.

### Mechanism
Fever is driven by hypothalamic prostaglandin E2, raising the thermoregulatory set point; COX inhibition lowers PGE2 synthesis there, allowing the set point (and body temperature) to fall back toward normal, which is the antipyretic effect.

### Key determinants
Because non-narcotic analgesics never engage opioid receptors, they cannot produce euphoria, antitussive suppression, respiratory depression or physical dependence — the entire cluster of narcotic-specific effects is mechanistically unavailable to them.

### Clinical significance
This is why a 'non-narcotic agents cause ....' exam stem is answered by antipyretic effect, and why non-narcotic analgesics are the safe first-line choice when dependence risk must be avoided.

## summary
Aspirin raises bleeding risk when combined with warfarin, through added protein-binding displacement and its own independent antiplatelet action.

## sections
### Definition
A clinically important aspirin drug interaction is potentiation of warfarin's anticoagulant effect, increasing bleeding risk when the two are combined.

### Mechanism
Aspirin displaces warfarin from plasma albumin, transiently increasing free warfarin, and separately inhibits platelet cyclooxygenase-1 irreversibly, blocking thromboxane A2 and impairing platelet aggregation independently of warfarin's own anticoagulant action.

### Key determinants
The two mechanisms are additive rather than the same pathway: one raises warfarin's own activity, the other blunts haemostasis by a separate platelet route, and both push in the same direction toward more bleeding.

### Clinical significance
This combination is avoided or closely monitored clinically, and is the standard correct answer whenever an exam stem asks which drug interaction occurs with concomitant aspirin.

## summary
At anti-inflammatory doses, aspirin's sustained COX inhibition gives it a genuine anti-rheumatic effect, historically used to arrest joint destruction in acute rheumatic fever.

## sections
### Definition
Aspirin's pharmacologic effects are dose-dependent: low doses give antiplatelet action, moderate doses give analgesia and antipyresis, and high, repeated doses give genuine anti-inflammatory and anti-rheumatic activity.

### Mechanism
Sustained cyclooxygenase inhibition at anti-inflammatory doses reduces prostaglandin-driven inflammation in joint tissue, which is what underlies the anti-rheumatic effect distinct from aspirin's low-dose antiplatelet action.

### Key determinants
The dose that gives anti-inflammatory benefit is far higher than the dose that gives antiplatelet benefit, because platelet COX-1 inhibition is irreversible and saturates easily, while inflamed tissue needs sustained coverage across the whole dosing interval.

### Clinical significance
This is the pharmacological basis for high-dose aspirin's historical use to prevent progressive joint destruction in acute rheumatic fever, a therapeutic use distinct from its cardioprotective or analgesic roles.

## summary
Ibuprofen blocks both COX isoforms without touching the separate lipoxygenase pathway, so it reduces cyclooxygenase products like thromboxane and prostaglandins but not leukotriene B4.

## sections
### Definition
Ibuprofen's mechanism of action is non-selective inhibition of cyclooxygenase, blocking both COX-1 and COX-2 without meaningful preference between them.

### Mechanism
By inhibiting cyclooxygenase, ibuprofen reduces synthesis of thromboxane A2, PGE2, PGF2a and PGI2, the cyclooxygenase-pathway eicosanoids, while leaving the parallel lipoxygenase pathway (which produces leukotrienes such as LTB4) entirely unaffected.

### Key determinants
The cyclooxygenase and lipoxygenase pathways both start from arachidonic acid but diverge immediately; an NSAID acting on cyclooxygenase has no mechanistic reach into the lipoxygenase branch at all.

### Clinical significance
This selectivity distinction explains why ibuprofen reduces fever, pain, platelet aggregation and inflammation (cyclooxygenase products) without altering leukotriene-driven processes such as bronchoconstriction, which instead respond to leukotriene-pathway-specific drugs.

## summary
A proposed COX-1 splice variant, COX-3, expressed mainly in the CNS, has been offered as the molecular explanation for paracetamol's centrally selective antipyretic-analgesic action.

## sections
### Definition
The COX-3 hypothesis proposes that paracetamol's centrally selective analgesic-antipyretic action, without meaningful peripheral anti-inflammatory effect, works through inhibition of a CNS-expressed COX-1 splice variant called COX-3.

### Mechanism
COX-3 inhibition would explain why paracetamol reduces central prostaglandin synthesis (fever, pain) while sparing peripheral COX-1/COX-2 activity, avoiding the gastric and platelet effects that non-selective peripheral NSAIDs produce.

### Key determinants
The hypothesis is specifically attached to paracetamol among the listed options; nimesulide, ketorolac, rofecoxib and aspirin are all understood through conventional peripheral COX-1/COX-2 pharmacology, not the COX-3 mechanism.

### Clinical significance
This mechanism is the textbook explanation offered for paracetamol's distinctive profile: analgesic and antipyretic like an NSAID, but essentially free of NSAID-typical gastric irritation and antiplatelet effects.

## summary
Aspirin covalently and irreversibly acetylates cyclooxygenase, which permanently disables thromboxane A2 synthesis in anucleate platelets and underlies its use in myocardial infarction prophylaxis.

## sections
### Definition
Aspirin is the one NSAID that inhibits cyclooxygenase irreversibly and non-selectively, by covalent acetylation of the enzyme's active site, rather than the reversible competitive inhibition typical of other NSAIDs.

### Mechanism
In platelets, which cannot resynthesise cyclooxygenase because they have no nucleus, this irreversible acetylation permanently blocks thromboxane A2 production for that platelet's remaining lifespan, reducing thromboxane-driven platelet aggregation.

### Key determinants
The uniqueness lies in the word irreversible: a reversible inhibitor's effect fades as drug levels fall, but aspirin's platelet effect persists until new platelets are made, which is why a single daily low dose is enough for durable antiplatelet cover.

### Clinical significance
This mechanism, inhibition of cyclooxygenase in platelets specifically, is the pharmacological basis for aspirin's use in the prophylaxis of myocardial infarction and other thrombotic events.

## summary
By inhibiting renal and vascular prostaglandin (prostacyclin) synthesis, NSAIDs blunt both antihypertensive drug efficacy and furosemide's diuretic action through loss of prostaglandin-mediated renal hemodynamic support.

## sections
### Definition
NSAIDs interact with both antihypertensive drugs and loop diuretics by inhibiting the renal and vascular prostaglandins (chiefly prostacyclin) that normally support blood-pressure-lowering and natriuretic drug action.

### Mechanism
Vascular prostacyclin normally opposes vasoconstriction, so its loss under NSAID treatment blunts antihypertensive efficacy; renal prostaglandins normally support renal blood flow and tubular salt excretion that furosemide's action partly depends on, so their loss blunts furosemide's diuretic effect too.

### Key determinants
Both interactions share the same root cause, loss of prostaglandin-mediated internal renal/vascular hemodynamic support, rather than a direct action on the ascending limb of the loop of Henle, the distal tubule, aldosterone, or a distal sodium channel.

### Clinical significance
Because of this shared mechanism, NSAIDs are used cautiously in patients on antihypertensives or diuretics, since either drug class's therapeutic effect can be measurably reduced by concomitant NSAID use.

## summary
Acetaminophen overdose overwhelms hepatic glutathione, letting the reactive metabolite NAPQI accumulate and cause centrilobular hepatocyte necrosis, seen as rising aminotransferases and jaundice.

## sections
### Definition
Acetaminophen (paracetamol) hepatotoxicity is liver injury from its reactive metabolite NAPQI accumulating once hepatic glutathione, which normally detoxifies it, is depleted by an overdose.

### Mechanism
At normal doses acetaminophen is cleared mainly by glucuronidation and sulfation, with only a small CYP2E1-mediated fraction becoming NAPQI, safely conjugated by glutathione; overdose (single large dose or cumulative escalation) exceeds glutathione's capacity, letting NAPQI accumulate and cause centrilobular hepatocyte necrosis.

### Key determinants
The injury pattern, rising aminotransferases with jaundice in a patient escalating an acetaminophen-containing product, points specifically to the acetaminophen component, not to any opioid co-ingredient, which has no comparable hepatotoxic mechanism.

### Clinical significance
Recognising acetaminophen as the responsible agent in a combination-product overdose is essential, since N-acetylcysteine (a glutathione precursor) is the specific antidote that must be given promptly to prevent progression to liver failure.

## summary
Morphine taken orally suffers extensive hepatic first-pass glucuronidation, giving only about 25% bioavailability and requiring much higher oral than parenteral doses.

## sections
### Definition
Morphine's oral bioavailability is only about 25%, far lower than its bioavailability by parenteral routes.

### Mechanism
After gut absorption, morphine passes through the portal circulation to the liver, where extensive first-pass glucuronidation converts much of the dose to morphine-3- and morphine-6-glucuronide before it ever reaches the systemic circulation.

### Key determinants
The bottleneck is hepatic, not intestinal: morphine itself is well absorbed from the gut, so the loss in bioavailability happens after absorption, during hepatic first-pass processing, not because of poor gut uptake or digestive breakdown.

### Clinical significance
This is why oral morphine dosing regimens use substantially higher doses than the equivalent parenteral (IV/IM) dose, a conversion clinicians must apply correctly when switching a patient between routes.

## summary
Morphine increases GI circular muscle tone and anal sphincter tone while reducing propulsive peristalsis, the mechanism behind both opioid-induced constipation and its use in controlling diarrhoea.

## sections
### Definition
Morphine slows gastrointestinal transit through mu-opioid receptor action on the enteric nervous system, the mechanism behind opioid-induced constipation.

### Mechanism
Mu-receptor activation in the gut wall increases circular smooth muscle tone and segmenting contractions, increases anal sphincter tone, and reduces coordinated propulsive peristalsis, so contents move forward more slowly even though local muscle tone is higher.

### Clinical significance
This is why a chronic pain patient on a morphine pump can report improved (reduced) diarrhoea as an incidental effect, and why loperamide, a peripherally restricted opioid agonist, is used specifically to treat diarrhoea through this same GI mechanism.

## summary
Tolerance develops to most opioid effects, but constipation and miosis are classic exceptions that persist unchanged even with long-term opioid use.

## sections
### Definition
Opioid tolerance is the need for escalating doses to reproduce an effect with repeated use; it develops for most opioid actions but not uniformly across all of them.

### Mechanism
Analgesia, euphoria, sedation and nausea/vomiting all show clear tolerance with chronic dosing, reflecting receptor and downstream signalling adaptation in the CNS, while the GI (constipation) and pupillary (miosis) effects use pathways that do not adapt the same way.

Constipation and miosis are the two effects singled out as persisting despite tolerance; this pairing is the standard 'except' answer whenever an exam question asks which opioid effect tolerance does not develop to.

### Clinical significance
This is why chronic opioid patients are routinely co-prescribed laxatives indefinitely, and why pinpoint pupils remain a dependable clinical sign of opioid use or overdose even in patients who have become tolerant to the drug's analgesic and euphoric effects.

## summary
Opioid receptor activation constricts the pupil via the Edinger-Westphal nucleus, making pinpoint pupils, not dilated ones, the reliable sign of opioid effect.

## sections
### Definition
Opioids characteristically produce miosis, pupillary constriction, rather than mydriasis, pupillary dilation.

### Mechanism
Opioid receptor activation stimulates the Edinger-Westphal nucleus, driving parasympathetic outflow to the pupillary constrictor muscle and producing the classic pinpoint pupil.

Pinpoint pupils, together with respiratory depression and decreased bowel sounds, form the classic opioid overdose triad, and their presence (rather than dilated, mydriatic pupils) is what points a clinician toward an opioid rather than a sympathomimetic or anticholinergic cause.

## sections
