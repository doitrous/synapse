## summary
Prophase I of meiosis unfolds in five named substages — leptotene, zygotene, pachytene, diplotene, diakinesis — each defined by one event: condensation, pairing, exchange, separation, or breakdown of the nuclear envelope.

## sections
### Definition
Prophase I is the longest phase of meiosis and the only one divided into named substages.

### Mechanism
Leptotene condenses the chromosomes into visible threads; zygotene pairs the homologs (synapsis, via the synaptonemal complex); pachytene lets the paired, non-sister chromatids exchange material (crossing over); diplotene lets the homologs begin to separate, held only at the crossover points (chiasmata); diakinesis finishes condensation and dissolves the nuclear envelope.

### Key determinants
Synapsis (zygotene) has to precede crossing over (pachytene) — pairing makes the physical exchange between homologous strands possible.

### Clinical significance
Diplotene is the stage at which a primary oocyte arrests, from fetal life until the cycle in which that follicle is selected for ovulation — sometimes for four decades.

## hold_these
Prophase I runs leptotene, zygotene (synapsis), pachytene (crossing over), diplotene, diakinesis, in that order.

## lose_the_mark
Treating synapsis and crossing over as the same event, or as happening in the same substage.

## summary
A normal somatic cell has 46 chromosomes — 22 autosome pairs plus one sex-chromosome pair — and the chromatid count per chromosome doubles from 46-s to 46-d across S-phase, without the chromosome count itself changing.

## sections
### Definition
46 chromosomes = 44 autosomes (22 pairs) + one pair of sex chromosomes (XX or XY).

### Mechanism
Before S-phase (G1): 46 chromosomes, each a single chromatid (46-s). After S-phase (G2, mitotic prophase/metaphase): the same 46 chromosomes, each now two sister chromatids (46-d, 92 chromatids total). At anaphase the sisters separate, restoring 46-s in each daughter cell.

### Key determinants
The chromosome count (46) does not change across the cell cycle; only the chromatid count per chromosome does.

### Clinical significance
This notation is the basis for describing meiotic products (e.g. a secondary spermatocyte's 23 duplicated chromosomes, 23-d) later in the same genetics sequence.

## hold_these
46 chromosomes = 22 autosome pairs + 1 sex-chromosome pair; 46-s in G1, 46-d after S-phase.

## lose_the_mark
Reading a doubled chromatid count (46-d) as a doubled chromosome count (92 chromosomes) — replication doubles the DNA content, not the chromosome number.

## summary
An exocrine gland keeps a duct to a surface; an endocrine gland loses its duct and secretes hormone straight into the surrounding capillaries — the pituitary is the standard example of the latter.

## sections
### Definition
Exocrine = duct present, secretes to a surface. Endocrine = ductless, secretes into blood.

### Mechanism
An endocrine gland develops from surface epithelium like any exocrine gland, but loses its connecting duct during development; its cells then release hormone directly into adjacent capillaries.

### Key determinants
The duct, not the tissue location, is what separates the two classes — an endocrine gland can sit among exocrine glands (e.g. pituitary near other head-and-neck glands) despite having no duct of its own.

### Clinical significance
Recognising a named gland as endocrine or exocrine predicts how its secretion reaches its target — locally via a duct, or systemically via the bloodstream.

## hold_these
Exocrine = has a duct; endocrine = ductless, secretes into blood; pituitary is the standard endocrine example.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming a gland is exocrine because it sits in a location (like skin) dominated by exocrine glands, rather than checking for a duct.

## summary
The thick filament (~15 nm) is built of myosin, the thin filament (~5-7 nm) of actin — a diameter-and-protein pairing that separates the two from tubulin (microtubules) and the intermediate filament proteins (desmin, cytokeratin).

## sections
### Definition
Thick filament = myosin. Thin filament = actin.

### Mechanism
Myosin's globular heads bind actin and hydrolyse ATP to generate the sliding force that underlies contraction; the thick/thin diameter difference (~15 nm vs ~5-7 nm) is the visual cue.

### Key determinants
Diameter sorts the distractors: myosin (thick, ~15 nm) is roughly double actin's diameter (thin, ~5-7 nm); tubulin builds microtubules, not filaments of this system at all; desmin and cytokeratin are intermediate filament proteins.

### Clinical significance
This pairing underlies both smooth-muscle/skeletal-muscle contraction and the actin-myosin-based contractility of non-muscle cells (e.g. the pericyte, the macrophage).

## hold_these
Thick filament = myosin; thin filament = actin.

## lose_the_mark
Picking actin for the thick filament by familiarity rather than checking which protein pairs with which diameter.

## summary
Elastic fibers do not take up H&E, Masson's trichrome, or silver reliably, so a dedicated special stain — Verhoeff's (blue-black), orcein or resorcin-fuchsin (brown) — is used to demonstrate them.

## sections
### Definition
Elastic fibers need a special stain distinct from the ones used for collagen and reticular fibers.

### Mechanism
Verhoeff's stain (iron-haematoxylin-based, usually counterstained with van Gieson) colours elastic fibers blue-black to purple-black; orcein and resorcin-fuchsin colour them brown to purple-brown.

### Key determinants
Masson's trichrome is the standard collagen stain and silver impregnation the standard reticular-fiber stain — neither reliably shows elastic fibers, which is why they need their own method.

### Clinical significance
Special elastic stains are used wherever elastic fiber content or distribution is diagnostically relevant (e.g. elastic laminae of arteries, elastic cartilage, the dermis).

## hold_these
Elastic fibers need a dedicated special stain — Verhoeff's, orcein, or resorcin-fuchsin — because H&E, Masson's trichrome and silver do not show them reliably.

## lose_the_mark
Reaching for Masson's trichrome (collagen) or a silver stain (reticular fibers) when the question specifically asks for the elastic-fiber stain.
