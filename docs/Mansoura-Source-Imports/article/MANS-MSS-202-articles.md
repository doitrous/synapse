## summary
The rotator cuff is four muscles — SITS — whose tendons blend with the shoulder capsule; teres major shares an action and a neighbouring origin with teres minor but is not part of the cuff.

## sections
### Definition
The rotator cuff is supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor and subscapularis (SITS), whose tendons fuse with the glenohumeral joint capsule to hold the humeral head centred in the glenoid through the full arc of shoulder movement.

### Mechanism
Supraspinatus initiates the first 15 degrees of abduction and resists superior humeral head migration; infraspinatus and teres minor laterally rotate; subscapularis medially rotates. Teres major, arising from the scapula's inferior angle, adducts and medially rotates the arm alongside latissimus dorsi, but its tendon does not blend with the capsule.

### Key determinants
Capsular blending is the defining feature of a rotator cuff muscle, not proximity to the shoulder or a shared action — teres major fails this test despite sitting right next to teres minor.

### Clinical significance
In a torn rotator cuff, teres major's tendon remains intact and functionally normal, which is why it is the correct "which muscle is unaffected" answer in a rotator cuff tear vignette.

## summary
The posterior cord gives five branches — upper/lower subscapular, thoracodorsal, axillary, radial — while the long thoracic nerve arises earlier, directly from the roots.

## sections
### Definition
The posterior cord of the brachial plexus forms from the posterior divisions of all three trunks and gives five named branches: upper subscapular, lower subscapular, thoracodorsal, axillary and radial nerves.

### Mechanism
Each branch supplies a muscle posterior to the axillary artery's line — subscapularis, teres major, latissimus dorsi, deltoid/teres minor, and the extensor compartment of the arm/forearm respectively. The long thoracic nerve instead arises directly from the anterior rami of C5-C7, before the trunks or cords have even formed, and supplies serratus anterior.

### Key determinants
Timing of origin along the plexus (root vs cord level), not the muscle's location or its relevance to scapular movement, is what separates the long thoracic nerve from the posterior cord's true branches.

### Clinical significance
"Which of the following is NOT a branch of the posterior cord" questions use the long thoracic nerve as the distractor precisely because it is easy to mistake for a cord branch given its shared relevance to shoulder/scapular stability.

## summary
Pectoralis minor's entire distal attachment is the coracoid process, so a fracture there — not a nerve injury — is the classic cause of losing the scapula's forward-and-downward pull.

## sections
### Definition
Pectoralis minor arises from ribs 3-5 and inserts on the coracoid process of the scapula, drawing the scapula forward and downward (protraction and depression).

### Mechanism
Because the coracoid process is pectoralis minor's only distal attachment, a fracture through it removes the muscle's mechanical leverage even though the muscle and its nerve supply (medial and lateral pectoral nerves) remain intact.

### Key determinants
This is a structural (bony) cause of lost function, not a neural one — the distinguishing detail a vignette naming "fracture of the coracoid process" is testing.

### Clinical significance
A patient unable to draw the scapula forward and downward after trauma near the coracoid process should raise suspicion of a coracoid process fracture affecting pectoralis minor's attachment, separate from a brachial plexus injury.

## summary
Latissimus dorsi extends, adducts and medially rotates the arm via the thoracodorsal nerve; its paralysis is tested by isolated loss of arm adduction.

## sections
### Definition
Latissimus dorsi arises broadly from the lower thoracic/lumbar spine, iliac crest and lower ribs and inserts into the floor of the intertubercular groove, acting to extend, adduct and medially rotate the arm, supplied by the thoracodorsal nerve.

### Mechanism
Because adduction is a movement latissimus dorsi contributes to more powerfully than its synergists (pectoralis major, teres major), its paralysis is isolated in a vignette by describing loss specifically of arm adduction after a fall or sporting injury.

### Key determinants
The thoracodorsal nerve, not the axillary nerve, is latissimus dorsi's nerve supply — a frequent distractor pairing since both nerves arise from the posterior cord.

### Clinical significance
Weakness climbing (a "latissimus dorsi cough" or difficulty pushing up from a chair using the arms) can point to thoracodorsal nerve or latissimus dorsi dysfunction, distinct from the abduction weakness of an axillary nerve lesion.

## summary
The ulnar nerve's deep (motor) branch supplies the hypothenar muscles, medial two lumbricals, all interossei, and adductor pollicis — the one "thumb" muscle the ulnar, not median, nerve supplies.

## sections
### Definition
After Guyon's canal, the ulnar nerve divides into a superficial (mainly sensory) and a deep (motor) branch, the latter curving around the hook of hamate to supply the hypothenar group, medial two lumbricals, all interossei and adductor pollicis.

### Mechanism
Adductor pollicis sits near the thenar eminence but is functionally grouped with the ulnar-supplied intrinsic muscles rather than the true thenar group (median-supplied abductor pollicis brevis, flexor pollicis brevis, opponens pollicis).

### Key determinants
Testing the deep branch clinically uses postures the interossei/lumbricals or adductor pollicis specifically produce — the "L-position" (MCP flexion with IP extension) or resisted thumb adduction against a card (Froment's sign if compensated by flexor pollicis longus).

### Clinical significance
An ulnar nerve lesion (e.g. at the elbow or wrist) weakens fine hand movements and thumb adduction even though the thenar muscles proper remain median-innervated and unaffected.

## summary
The median nerve's recurrent branch is purely motor to the thenar muscles; its isolated (superficial) injury weakens thumb opposition/abduction with no sensory loss.

## sections
### Definition
Just distal to the carpal tunnel, the median nerve gives a small recurrent (thenar, motor) branch that curls back to supply abductor pollicis brevis, flexor pollicis brevis and opponens pollicis.

### Mechanism
Because this branch is purely motor and runs superficially at the base of the thenar eminence, a laceration there can sever it in isolation without touching the sensory fibres that travel more centrally in the palm.

### Key determinants
Presence or absence of sensory loss is the discriminating clinical finding: full median nerve or carpal tunnel injury carries sensory loss over the lateral three and a half digits, while an isolated recurrent branch injury does not.

### Clinical significance
A vignette describing weak thumb opposition/abduction "with no sensory deficits reported" after a superficial cut at the base of the thumb points specifically to the recurrent motor branch, not a more proximal median nerve lesion.

## summary
Piriformis syndrome compresses the sciatic nerve, producing posterior thigh/lateral foot symptoms while sparing the medial leg, which the femoral nerve's saphenous branch supplies instead.

## sections
### Definition
Piriformis syndrome is compression or irritation of the sciatic nerve as it passes beneath (or, in a common variant, through) piriformis in the gluteal region.

### Mechanism
Compression produces pain and sensory change along the sciatic nerve's own distribution — buttock, posterior thigh, and down to the lateral foot.

The medial leg is supplied by the saphenous nerve, a cutaneous branch of the femoral nerve that never joins the sciatic nerve, so it remains the one area of intact sensation — the discriminating physical-exam finding.

## summary
Superior gluteal nerve injury weakens gluteus medius/minimus, the pelvis-stabilisers, producing a waddling (Trendelenburg) gait — distinct from inferior gluteal nerve injury, which weakens hip extension.

## sections
### Definition
Gluteus medius and minimus, both supplied by the superior gluteal nerve, stabilise the pelvis during single-leg stance by pulling the ipsilateral hip down relative to the unsupported side.

### Mechanism
Superior gluteal nerve injury weakens this stabilisation, so during the injured side's stance phase the contralateral pelvis drops instead, producing the characteristic waddling, side-to-side gait.

### Key determinants
Inferior gluteal nerve injury weakens gluteus maximus (hip extension) instead, and does not produce this pelvis-stabilisation failure — the two nerves are frequently confused in exam distractors.

### Clinical significance
Trendelenburg gait after hip surgery or a deep gluteal wound should raise suspicion of superior gluteal nerve injury during a surgical approach through the gluteal region.

## summary
The iliofemoral (Y) ligament is the body's strongest ligament, checking hip hyperextension and enabling relaxed standing.

## sections
### Definition
The iliofemoral ligament (ligament of Bigelow) runs from the anterior inferior iliac spine to the intertrochanteric line in an inverted-Y shape and is the strongest ligament in the human body.

### Mechanism
It becomes taut on hip extension, checking hyperextension and allowing the pelvis to lean backward over the femoral heads without sustained muscular effort.

### Key determinants
This distinguishes it from the pubofemoral and ischiofemoral ligaments, which reinforce the hip capsule inferiorly and posteriorly respectively but are weaker and not credited with this specific hyperextension-limiting, standing-support role.

### Clinical significance
The iliofemoral ligament's strength is part of why anterior hip dislocation is far less common than posterior dislocation, and why standing posture is metabolically efficient rather than requiring constant muscular hip-extensor effort.

## summary
The cruciate ligaments are intracapsular; the collateral and patellar ligaments are extracapsular — a recurring "which one is different" exam pattern.

## sections
### Definition
The knee joint capsule encloses the anterior and posterior cruciate ligaments, which cross between the femoral and tibial condyles (though even they are extrasynovial, wrapped in a synovial fold).

### Mechanism
The medial and lateral collateral ligaments lie outside the capsule proper, and the patellar ligament (the quadriceps tendon's continuation below the patella) also runs extracapsularly, anterior to the joint.

### Key determinants
Intracapsular vs extracapsular is the classification axis tested, cutting across the more general category "knee ligament."

### Clinical significance

5-ASA has local anti-inflammatory effects; sulphapyridine is well absorbed and is thought to carry most of the systemic, disease-modifying effect in rheumatoid arthritis.

### Key determinants
This prodrug/bacterial-cleavage mechanism is distinct from a calcineurin inhibitor, a biologic DMARD, or a folate antagonist — the other mechanism classes options in this question test against.

### Clinical significance
Sulfasalazine's prodrug design is shared conceptually with other 5-ASA compounds used in inflammatory bowel disease, though its RA indication relies more on sulphapyridine.

## summary
Rituximab is a chimeric anti-CD20 antibody that depletes B lymphocytes, used as a biologic DMARD in RA refractory to other agents.

## sections
### Definition
Rituximab is a chimeric monoclonal antibody — part mouse-, part human-derived — directed against CD20, a surface marker on B lymphocytes from the pre-B stage to mature B cells.

### Mechanism
Binding CD20 triggers B-cell depletion through complement- and antibody-dependent cytotoxicity.

### Key determinants
Rituximab's target (CD20, a B-cell marker) is distinct from TNF-alpha (infliximab/etanercept/adalimumab) or IL-6 receptor (tocilizumab) — other biologic DMARDs tested alongside it as distractors.

### Clinical significance
Rituximab is used in rheumatoid arthritis refractory to other DMARDs, alongside its original oncology indication in B-cell lymphomas.
