## summary
The plasma cell is an antibody-secreting cell recognised by its deeply basophilic cytoplasm, eccentric clock-face nucleus and a pale juxtanuclear “negative Golgi image”.

## sections
### Definition
The plasma cell is a differentiated B lymphocyte specialised to synthesise and secrete antibody. It is a large, oval connective-tissue cell numerous in loose connective tissue and lymphoid organs.

### Mechanism
Its cytoplasm is packed with rough endoplasmic reticulum for immunoglobulin synthesis, which makes it strongly basophilic. The large Golgi apparatus, which packages the antibody, sits to one side of the nucleus and excludes the basophilic ribosomes, so it appears in H&E as a pale, unstained area — the “negative Golgi image”.

### Key determinants
The nucleus is round and eccentric with coarse heterochromatin arranged in a radial “clock-face” (cartwheel) pattern. Together the basophilic cytoplasm, negative Golgi image and clock-face nucleus identify the cell.

### Clinical significance
Plasma-cell numbers rise in chronic inflammation; their neoplastic proliferation is multiple myeloma.

## hold_these
Plasma cell = eccentric clock-face nucleus + basophilic cytoplasm + pale negative Golgi image.

## lose_the_mark
Calling the pale juxtanuclear zone an artefact rather than the Golgi, or confusing the plasma cell with the metachromatic-granuled mast cell or kidney-nucleus monocyte.

## summary
The Langerhans cell is the bone-marrow-derived dendritic (antigen-presenting) cell scattered in the epidermis, the sentinel of cutaneous immunity.

## sections
### Definition
The Langerhans cell is a dendritic, antigen-presenting cell of the epidermis, one of the non-keratinocyte cells of the stratum spinosum, derived from bone-marrow precursors of the mononuclear phagocyte / dendritic-cell lineage.

### Mechanism
It captures antigen that penetrates the skin, processes it, and then migrates to the regional lymph node, where it presents the antigen to T lymphocytes to initiate the immune response. Its characteristic tennis-racket Birbeck granules are seen on electron microscopy.

### Key determinants
It is distinct from the pigment-producing melanocyte and from the sensory Merkel cell, the other non-keratinocytes of the epidermis.

### Clinical significance
Langerhans cells drive contact dermatitis and are the proliferating cell in Langerhans cell histiocytosis.

## hold_these
Langerhans cell = epidermal dendritic antigen-presenting cell (APC).

## lose_the_mark
Attributing skin colour (melanocyte) or antibody secretion (plasma cell) to the Langerhans cell.

## summary
The dermis has a thin, loose, cellular, vascular papillary layer that raises the dermal papillae and houses Meissner's corpuscles, over a thick, dense reticular layer.

## sections
### Definition
The dermis is the connective-tissue layer beneath the epidermis, formed of a superficial papillary layer and a deep reticular layer.

### Mechanism
The papillary layer is thin loose connective tissue that projects as dermal papillae into the epidermis; it is more cellular and more vascular and carries capillary loops and the encapsulated Meissner's (tactile) corpuscles for fine touch. The reticular layer is thick dense irregular connective tissue, less cellular and less vascular, carrying the deeper Pacinian and Ruffini receptors.

### Key determinants
Meissner's corpuscles sit in the papillary dermis close to the surface; Pacinian corpuscles lie deep in the reticular dermis and hypodermis.

### Clinical significance
The dermo-epidermal papillary interface flattens with ageing, weakening epidermal anchorage.

## hold_these
Papillary layer = thin, loose, cellular, vascular, holds Meissner's corpuscles; reticular = thick, dense, less vascular.

## lose_the_mark
Placing Pacinian or Ruffini corpuscles, or calling the papillary layer cell-poor, in the papillary layer.

## summary
Thin (hairy) skin covers most of the body and lacks the stratum lucidum (clear layer), which is present only in the thick, hairless skin of palms and soles.

## sections
### Definition
Skin is classified as thin or thick by the thickness of its epidermis, especially its keratin layer. Thick skin is limited to the palms and soles; thin skin covers the rest of the body.

### Mechanism
Thick skin shows all five epidermal strata — basale, spinosum, granulosum, lucidum and a very thick corneum — and bears no hair. Thin skin has a thinner epidermis with a reduced granular and horny layer and, crucially, no stratum lucidum (clear layer); it does carry hair follicles.

### Key determinants
The stratum lucidum (clear layer) is the discriminating feature: present in thick skin, absent in thin skin.

### Clinical significance
The thick keratin of palms and soles resists the friction those surfaces meet.

## hold_these
Thin skin lacks the clear layer (stratum lucidum); thick skin has all five layers and no hair.

## lose_the_mark
Thinking thin skin lacks a basal, spinous or horny layer — it lacks only the clear layer.

## summary
Mitosis runs prophase (condensation, spindle formation, nuclear-envelope breakdown), metaphase (equatorial alignment), anaphase (chromatid separation) and telophase (nuclei re-form).

## sections
### Definition
Mitosis is the division of the nucleus that distributes an identical, diploid chromosome set to each of two daughter cells, in four stages, followed by cytokinesis.

### Mechanism
Prophase condenses the chromosomes, forms the spindle from the centrosomes and ends with breakdown of the nuclear envelope and disappearance of the nucleolus. Metaphase aligns the chromosomes on the equatorial (metaphase) plate. Anaphase splits the sister chromatids and pulls them to opposite poles. Telophase re-forms a nuclear envelope around each set and the chromosomes decondense; cytokinesis then divides the cytoplasm.

### Key determinants
Nuclear-envelope breakdown ends prophase; equatorial alignment defines metaphase; envelope re-formation defines telophase.

### Clinical significance
Arresting cells in metaphase with colchicine is how karyotypes are prepared.

## hold_these
Prophase (envelope breaks down) → metaphase (align) → anaphase (separate) → telophase (envelope re-forms).

## lose_the_mark
Assigning envelope breakdown to metaphase, or central alignment to prophase.

## summary
The Golgi apparatus is invisible in routine H&E and is demonstrated by silver or osmium impregnation; it modifies, sorts and packages secretory products.

## sections
### Definition
The Golgi apparatus is a stack of membrane-bounded cisternae, polarised into a cis (forming, immature) face and a trans (maturing) face, that processes and packages the cell's secretory and lysosomal products.

### Mechanism
Because it is not stained by H&E, the Golgi was first shown by Camillo Golgi's silver impregnation and is also demonstrated by osmium; in H&E it may appear only as a pale, negative juxtanuclear zone. Vesicles enter at the cis face, are modified through the stack, and leave from the trans face as secretory vesicles or primary lysosomes.

### Key determinants
Silver/osmium impregnation is required to see it; its faces are cis-immature and trans-mature (not cis-mature).

### Clinical significance
A prominent Golgi marks actively secreting cells such as goblet and plasma cells.

## hold_these
Golgi = invisible in H&E, shown by silver (or osmium), cis-immature/trans-mature faces.

## lose_the_mark
Calling the Golgi H&E-visible, or reversing its faces to a “cis mature face”.

## summary
The desmosome (macula adherens) is a spot junction that anchors intermediate filaments between adjacent cells, the strongest junction, resisting mechanical separation.

## sections
### Definition
The desmosome, or macula adherens, is a spot-like adhering junction between adjacent epithelial cells that links their intermediate-filament cytoskeletons across the intercellular space.

### Mechanism
Transmembrane cadherins (desmogleins/desmocollins) bind cell-to-cell in a dense plaque to which keratin intermediate filaments attach on each side. By tying the tough intermediate-filament networks of neighbouring cells together, the desmosome distributes shearing forces and holds cells firmly, making it the strongest cell junction.

### Key determinants
Distinguish from the tight junction (seals the lumen), the gap junction (communication) and the hemidesmosome (anchors the cell base to the basement membrane).

### Clinical significance
Autoantibodies against desmosomal cadherins cause the blistering disease pemphigus vulgaris.

## hold_these
Desmosome = macula adherens = strong spot junction anchoring intermediate filaments; prevents cell separation.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing the desmosome's adhesion role with sealing (tight junction), communication (gap) or basal anchorage (hemidesmosome).

## summary
Reticular fibres are fine, branching type III collagen fibres that are argyrophilic (blackened by silver) and PAS-positive, forming supporting meshworks.

## sections
### Definition
Reticular fibres are a class of connective-tissue fibre made of type III collagen, forming delicate branching networks in glands, lymphoid and haemopoietic organs and around muscle and nerve.

### Mechanism
Their surface carries carbohydrate, which lets them bind silver salts (argyrophilia) and react with PAS, so they are demonstrated by silver impregnation rather than by H&E, which barely shows them. They form a fine three-dimensional supporting scaffold.

### Key determinants
Argyrophilia distinguishes reticular fibres from thick collagen (H&E/trichrome) and from elastic fibres (orcein, Verhoeff's).

### Clinical significance
The reticular framework of lymph nodes and bone marrow supports the free cells that migrate through them.

## hold_these
Reticular fibre = type III collagen, argyrophilic (silver-black), PAS-positive, branching support meshwork.

## lose_the_mark
Calling collagen or elastic fibres argyrophilic, or forgetting reticular fibres are chemically collagen (type III).

## summary

## sections

Organelles are the living, permanent, essential subunits of the cytoplasm; they are divided by whether they are bounded by a membrane.

## summary

## sections

## summary
A secondary lysosome is a primary lysosome fused with material for digestion; when digestion is complete the indigestible remnant persists as a residual body (accumulating as lipofuscin).

## sections
### Definition
Lysosomes are membrane-bounded vesicles of acid hydrolases. A primary lysosome is the fresh, enzyme-only vesicle; a secondary lysosome is one that has entered a digestive event.

### Mechanism
A primary lysosome fusing with a phagosome forms a heterolysosome (digesting external material); fusing with worn organelles it forms an autolysosome. When digestion finishes, the undigested residue remains within a residual body. In long-lived cells such as neurons and cardiac muscle, residual bodies accumulate as lipofuscin, the age (“wear-and-tear”) pigment.

### Key determinants
Primary lysosome = homogeneous dense enzyme store; secondary lysosome = heterogeneous, ends as a residual body; all are membranous. Marker enzyme is acid phosphatase (D-amino acid oxidase is the peroxisome's).

### Clinical significance
Failure of lysosomal digestion causes the lysosomal storage diseases.

## hold_these
Secondary lysosome = digesting lysosome ending as a residual body of undigested material (→ lipofuscin).

## lose_the_mark
Calling secondary lysosomes non-membranous, budding from peroxisomes, or containing D-amino acid oxidase.
