## summary
Fluid movement across the capillary wall is set by four Starling forces: capillary hydrostatic pressure (about 30 mmHg arterial end, 10 mmHg venous end, mean 17.3 mmHg) and interstitial colloid osmotic pressure push fluid outward, while plasma colloid osmotic pressure (about 28 mmHg, mainly from albumin) and interstitial hydrostatic pressure (normally slightly negative, about -3 mmHg in loose tissue) pull it inward. Filtration (about 20 L/day) predominates at the arterial end, absorption (about 18 L/day) at the venous end, and about 90% of filtered fluid is reabsorbed before the venous end — the small remainder returned by the lymphatics. The classic whole-capillary balance sums to about 28.3 mmHg outward against 28 mmHg inward, a net filtration pressure of about 0.3 mmHg.

## sections
### Definition
Capillary dynamics describes how fluid moves between plasma and the interstitial space across the capillary wall, governed by the balance of four Starling forces: two hydrostatic (capillary and interstitial) and two colloid osmotic (plasma/capillary and interstitial).

### Mechanism
Capillary hydrostatic pressure and interstitial colloid osmotic pressure both push fluid OUTWARD, out of the capillary; plasma (capillary) colloid osmotic pressure and interstitial hydrostatic pressure both pull fluid INWARD, into the capillary — except that interstitial hydrostatic pressure is normally slightly negative in loose tissue, which flips its own contribution to also favour filtration rather than opposing it. Because capillary hydrostatic pressure falls steeply from about 30 mmHg at the arteriolar end to about 10 mmHg at the venular end while plasma colloid osmotic pressure (about 28 mmHg) stays essentially constant, the net balance itself shifts along the vessel: net filtration dominates near the arterial end, net absorption dominates near the venous end.

### Key determinants
Averaged over the whole capillary, the classic (Guyton) table gives outward forces of capillary hydrostatic pressure (mean 17.3 mmHg) + the outward pull of a negative interstitial hydrostatic pressure (3 mmHg) + interstitial colloid osmotic pressure (8 mmHg) = 28.3 mmHg total outward, against a single inward force of plasma colloid osmotic pressure (28 mmHg) — a small net outward (filtration) pressure of about 0.3 mmHg. Daily filtration (~20 L) slightly exceeds absorption (~18 L); about 90% of filtered fluid is reabsorbed before the venous end, and the remaining ~10% (2 to 4 L/day) is drained by the lymphatics rather than lost. Interstitial fluid pressure itself varies regionally: about -3 mmHg in loose tissue such as muscle, but positive in tightly encapsulated organs (kidney about +6 mmHg, brain about +4 mmHg), because a rigid capsule resists the tissue swelling a negative pressure elsewhere depends on.

### Clinical significance
Any shift that raises capillary hydrostatic pressure (e.g. venous obstruction, heart failure), lowers plasma colloid osmotic pressure (e.g. hypoalbuminaemia, hypoproteinaemia), or blocks lymphatic drainage tips this normally near-balanced system toward net filtration exceeding absorption and lymphatic capacity — producing oedema. The small physiological filtration surplus that is normally cleared by the lymphatics illustrates why lymphatic failure, not just a Starling-force imbalance, is itself a distinct oedema mechanism.

## hold_these
Outward forces: capillary hydrostatic pressure (~30 arterial/~10 venous/17.3 mean mmHg) and interstitial colloid osmotic pressure (~8 mmHg); a negative interstitial hydrostatic pressure also acts outward. Inward force: plasma colloid osmotic pressure (~28 mmHg, mainly albumin via concentration). Classic table: 28.3 outward vs 28 inward, net ~0.3 mmHg outward. Filtration (~20 L/day) at the arterial end; absorption (~18 L/day) at the venous end; ~90% of filtered fluid is reabsorbed, ~10% (2-4 L/day) drained by lymphatics. Interstitial pressure: ~-3 mmHg in loose tissue, positive in encapsulated organs (kidney ~+6, brain ~+4 mmHg).

## lose_the_mark
Reversing which end filtration/absorption predominates at (filtration = arterial end, absorption = venous end). Assuming interstitial fluid pressure is uniformly negative everywhere (encapsulated organs run positive). Confusing the whole-capillary mean net filtration pressure (~0.3 mmHg outward) with the venous end's own local net force. Attributing plasma colloid osmotic pressure's dominance to albumin's individual osmotic strength rather than its plasma concentration. Assuming all filtered fluid returns directly to the capillary rather than a ~10% lymphatic fraction.
