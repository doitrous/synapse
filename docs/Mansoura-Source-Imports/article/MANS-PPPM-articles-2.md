## summary
Active transport is primary when the pump hydrolyses ATP itself, and secondary when it instead spends an existing ion gradient another pump already built.

## sections
### Definition
Active transport moves a solute against its concentration gradient and is classed as primary or secondary by where the energy comes from. Primary active transport (the Na-K ATPase, H-K ATPase, H+ ATPase and Ca ATPase) hydrolyses ATP directly to move its ion. Secondary active transport, such as the Na-Ca exchanger, spends no ATP of its own; instead it borrows the electrochemical gradient a primary pump already built (here, the sodium gradient from the Na-K ATPase) to move a second ion (calcium) against its own gradient.

### Mechanism
The Na-Ca exchanger couples the inward, downhill movement of sodium (following the gradient the Na-K ATPase maintains) to the outward, uphill movement of calcium. No ATP is consumed by the exchanger itself — the energy for calcium's uphill movement comes entirely from the potential energy stored in the sodium gradient. This is why blocking the Na-K ATPase (with ouabain, for example) indirectly stalls the Na-Ca exchanger too, even though ouabain never touches the exchanger directly.

### Key determinants
The discriminating question for any transporter is not whether it moves a solute uphill (both primary and secondary active transport do) but where the immediate energy comes from: direct ATP hydrolysis marks primary active transport, while dependence on a pre-existing ion gradient built by a separate pump marks secondary active transport. A transporter that is truly passive (simple or facilitated diffusion) needs neither ATP nor a borrowed gradient.

### Clinical significance
This classification underlies how drugs and toxins that inhibit the Na-K ATPase (cardiac glycosides such as digoxin) have knock-on effects on secondary active transporters that depend on the sodium gradient it maintains, including cardiac Na-Ca exchange, a mechanism relevant to digoxin's inotropic effect.

## hold_these
Primary active transport hydrolyses ATP directly (Na-K ATPase, H-K ATPase, H+ ATPase, Ca ATPase).
Secondary active transport (the Na-Ca exchanger) spends no ATP of its own, borrowing an existing ion gradient instead.

## lose_the_mark
Treating any carrier-mediated, uphill transporter as automatically primary active transport without checking whether it hydrolyses ATP itself or merely rides another pump's gradient.

## summary
Donepezil is the reversible cholinesterase inhibitor used for Alzheimer disease because, unlike neostigmine or pyridostigmine, it is lipophilic enough to cross into the CNS.

## sections
### Definition
Donepezil is a reversible acetylcholinesterase inhibitor whose lipophilicity lets it cross the blood-brain barrier and raise acetylcholine levels within the CNS. Because Alzheimer disease is marked by loss of central cholinergic neurons, this central action is what makes donepezil (along with rivastigmine and galantamine) useful for its cognitive symptoms, unlike quaternary cholinesterase inhibitors such as neostigmine and pyridostigmine, which stay largely outside the CNS and are used instead for peripheral neuromuscular disease.

### Mechanism
By inhibiting acetylcholinesterase, donepezil slows the breakdown of acetylcholine released by the surviving central cholinergic neurons, partially compensating for the neuronal loss that underlies Alzheimer's cognitive decline. Its non-quaternary, lipophilic structure is the key property that lets it cross the blood-brain barrier at all — the same enzyme-inhibiting mechanism in a quaternary (charged) molecule would stay confined to the periphery.

### Key determinants
The distinguishing property among reversible cholinesterase inhibitors is CNS penetration, not the shared mechanism of enzyme inhibition. Donepezil, rivastigmine and galantamine cross into the brain and are used for Alzheimer disease; neostigmine and pyridostigmine do not cross and are used instead for myasthenia gravis and reversing neuromuscular blockade.

### Clinical significance
This blood-brain-barrier distinction explains a recurring exam pattern: a cholinesterase-inhibitor question about a CNS indication (Alzheimer disease) has a different correct answer from a cholinesterase-inhibitor question about a peripheral indication (myasthenia gravis), even though both drugs share the same enzyme target.

## hold_these
Donepezil is the reversible cholinesterase inhibitor used for Alzheimer disease.
It works because it crosses the blood-brain barrier, unlike the peripherally-restricted neostigmine and pyridostigmine.

## lose_the_mark
Picking any reversible cholinesterase inhibitor for a CNS indication without checking whether that specific agent actually crosses the blood-brain barrier.

## summary
Continuous, unbroken agonist exposure causes the cell to internalise and degrade its own receptors, a pharmacodynamic (receptor-level) cause of tolerance.

## sections
### Definition
Receptor down-regulation is a cellular adaptation to continuous, unbroken agonist exposure: the cell internalises and degrades surface receptors, reducing the number available to respond to further agonist at the same dose. This is a receptor-level (pharmacodynamic) mechanism of tolerance, distinct from pharmacokinetic mechanisms such as faster drug clearance from enzyme induction, and it specifically follows sustained occupancy rather than merely prolonged, intermittent dosing.

### Mechanism
Sustained agonist binding drives receptor internalisation via endocytosis, followed by lysosomal degradation of a portion of the internalised receptor pool, so fewer receptors sit on the cell surface for the same total agonist exposure. Because this depends on the receptor staying continuously occupied, a dosing schedule with breaks (allowing receptor numbers to recover between doses) produces far less down-regulation than truly continuous exposure at the same total dose.

### Key determinants
Down-regulation is the cause (fewer receptors); tolerance is the resulting effect (a smaller response to the same dose), and tolerance can also arise from purely pharmacokinetic causes such as faster metabolism. The specific trigger for down-regulation is continuous, unbroken agonist occupancy, not simply a prolonged treatment course with intermittent dosing.

### Clinical significance
Recognising down-regulation as pharmacodynamic tolerance explains why continuous infusions or sustained-release formulations of some agonists lose efficacy over time even when drug clearance is unchanged, and why an intermittent dosing schedule can sometimes preserve responsiveness that a continuous one would blunt.

## hold_these
Continuous agonist exposure drives receptor down-regulation, a pharmacodynamic mechanism of tolerance.
This is distinct from pharmacokinetic tolerance mechanisms such as enzyme induction speeding drug clearance.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming any prolonged agonist course, even with dosing breaks, produces the same down-regulation as truly continuous, unbroken receptor occupancy.

## summary
Clopidogrel needs CYP2C19 to bioactivate it into its antiplatelet metabolite, so CYP2C19 genotype predicts how well the drug will work.

## sections
### Definition
Clopidogrel is a prodrug that requires hepatic bioactivation, mainly by CYP2C19, into an active thiol metabolite before it can irreversibly block the platelet P2Y12 ADP receptor. Patients carrying loss-of-function CYP2C19 alleles (poor metabolisers) generate less active metabolite and get a smaller antiplatelet effect at a standard dose, which is why CYP2C19 genotype is the gene tested to predict clopidogrel effectiveness.

### Mechanism
Clopidogrel itself has no antiplatelet activity; a two-step hepatic oxidation, disproportionately dependent on CYP2C19, converts it into the active thiol metabolite that irreversibly binds and blocks the platelet P2Y12 receptor for the platelet's lifespan. A patient's CYP2C19 genotype therefore governs how much active metabolite is generated from a given oral dose, and by extension how much platelet inhibition that dose achieves.

### Key determinants
Not every major cytochrome P450 enzyme predicts every prodrug's activation equally — CYP3A4 metabolises a far broader range of drugs overall, but clopidogrel's bioactivation is disproportionately CYP2C19-dependent, which is why CYP2C19 (not CYP3A4 or the other CYP isoforms) is the genotype tested clinically.

### Clinical significance
CYP2C19 poor metabolisers on standard-dose clopidogrel have a documented higher risk of stent thrombosis and recurrent ischaemic events after percutaneous coronary intervention, which is why genotype-guided antiplatelet selection (or dose adjustment) is a live clinical practice in some settings.

## hold_these
CYP2C19 bioactivates clopidogrel into its active, platelet-blocking metabolite.
CYP2C19 loss-of-function alleles (poor metabolisers) blunt clopidogrel's antiplatelet effect at a standard dose.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming CYP3A4, the broadest-acting CYP enzyme overall, must be the one that activates any given prodrug, rather than checking which CYP isoform actually dominates that specific drug's bioactivation.

## summary
Both simple and facilitated diffusion are passive and downhill, but only facilitated diffusion needs a carrier protein and is therefore saturable.

## sections
### Definition
Simple diffusion is the passive movement of a solute directly across the lipid bilayer (or through a channel), down its concentration gradient, without a carrier protein; because it depends on no fixed number of binding sites, its rate keeps rising with concentration and it is not saturable. Facilitated diffusion also moves a solute passively downhill, but it depends on a specific carrier protein, which makes it, unlike simple diffusion, saturable (rate-limited) once all carriers are occupied. Neither requires metabolic energy or moves solute uphill; those properties belong to active transport instead.

### Mechanism
Simple diffusion's rate is set by the solute's lipid solubility and the size of the concentration gradient alone, so plotting rate against concentration gives a straight line with no ceiling. Facilitated diffusion's rate instead depends on how many carrier proteins are available: as concentration rises, an increasing fraction of carriers are occupied at any instant, and the rate curve flattens toward a maximum (Vmax) once every carrier is saturated, the same kinetic signature carrier-mediated processes share with enzyme kinetics.

### Key determinants
The property that separates the two passive mechanisms is carrier-dependence and its consequence, saturability — not whether the movement is passive (both are) or whether it is downhill (both are). A transporter that plateaus at high concentration is carrier-mediated (facilitated diffusion); one whose rate keeps climbing is not (simple diffusion).

### Clinical significance
Recognising a transporter's saturability matters clinically whenever a drug or nutrient relies on facilitated diffusion for absorption or cellular uptake (glucose transport via GLUT carriers, for example), since transport capacity — and therefore uptake rate — has a ceiling that simple lipid-soluble diffusion does not.

## hold_these
Simple diffusion needs no carrier and is not saturable; facilitated diffusion needs a carrier and is saturable.
Both are passive and move solute downhill; neither requires ATP.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming that because both mechanisms are passive, they behave identically at high concentration — only the carrier-mediated one (facilitated diffusion) has a ceiling.

## summary
Naloxone shifts morphine's dose-response curve right without lowering the achievable maximum and has no effect on its own — the signature of competitive (surmountable) antagonism.

## sections
### Definition
A surmountable rightward shift of an agonist's dose-response curve, one that can still be overcome by giving enough agonist to reach the same maximal effect, combined with the second drug producing no effect alone, is the signature of competitive (surmountable) antagonism. Naloxone shows this pattern with morphine: it competes for the same opioid receptor, has no efficacy of its own, and enough added morphine displaces it and restores full analgesia. A noncompetitive antagonist would instead lower the achievable maximum no matter how much agonist is given.

### Mechanism
Naloxone binds the same opioid receptor site as morphine, reversibly and without producing a receptor-activating effect itself. Because the binding is reversible and the two drugs compete for the identical site, raising morphine's concentration enough shifts the receptor-occupancy equilibrium back in morphine's favour, letting the same maximal analgesic effect be reached at a higher morphine dose — the operational definition of a surmountable, competitive interaction.

### Key determinants
The diagnostic test for competitive versus noncompetitive antagonism is whether the maximal effect is still reachable with enough agonist. A rightward-shifted but ultimately reachable maximum marks competitive antagonism; a permanently lowered maximum, regardless of how much agonist is given, marks noncompetitive antagonism instead.

### Clinical significance
This is exactly why naloxone reversal of opioid overdose can be overcome by re-dosing with a long-acting opioid once naloxone (itself short-acting) wears off, and why repeat or continuous naloxone dosing is sometimes needed clinically — the antagonism is surmountable, and receptor occupancy shifts back toward the agonist once naloxone's plasma concentration falls.

## hold_these
Naloxone shifts morphine's dose-response curve right but does not lower the achievable maximum — competitive (surmountable) antagonism.
Naloxone alone produces no effect; it must compete with an agonist already present.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming any antagonist that reduces potency (shifts the curve right) is automatically competitive, without checking whether the maximum is still reachable (competitive) or permanently lowered (noncompetitive).

## summary
A bactericidal drug kills bacteria outright; a bacteriostatic drug only stops them growing, leaving the host immune system to finish clearing the infection.

## sections
### Definition
A bactericidal effect is the actual killing (destruction) of bacterial cells. A bacteriostatic effect instead only inhibits bacterial growth and reproduction without killing the organism, relying on the host's immune system to clear the still-viable bacteria. This distinction matters clinically because bactericidal drugs are generally preferred in immunocompromised patients or in infections such as endocarditis, where a fully functioning immune system cannot be relied on to finish the job.

### Mechanism
Bactericidal drugs act on structures or processes essential to bacterial survival — such as cell wall synthesis (beta-lactams, vancomycin) — so that the bacterium cannot remain viable once the target is blocked. Bacteriostatic drugs instead act on processes needed for growth and reproduction, such as protein synthesis at sub-lethal levels (many tetracyclines and macrolides), halting bacterial multiplication without directly killing the existing population, which then depends on host defences (phagocytosis, antibody-mediated clearance) to eliminate.

### Key determinants
The discriminator is the fate of the bacterium once exposed to the drug — dead (bactericidal) or merely non-dividing but still viable (bacteriostatic) — not simply whether the drug 'works' against the organism in a culture plate inhibition sense, since both classes can produce a zone of inhibition.

### Clinical significance
Choosing a bactericidal agent is a recognised priority in patients with impaired host defences (neutropenia, immunosuppression) or in infections at sites the immune system reaches poorly or must sterilise completely, such as infective endocarditis and meningitis, where a merely bacteriostatic drug that halts growth is not considered sufficient on its own.

## hold_these

## lose_the_mark
Treating 'inhibits bacterial growth' and 'kills bacteria' as interchangeable descriptions of any antimicrobial action — only the killing description is bactericidal.

## summary

## sections
### Definition
Beta-2 receptors on bronchial smooth muscle mediate bronchodilation. Blocking them, whether with a non-selective beta blocker or a beta-2-specific antagonist, removes that relaxing tone and can precipitate severe, potentially life-threatening bronchospasm in a susceptible airway. Normal bronchial smooth muscle tone is kept relaxed in part by tonic sympathetic beta-2 stimulation. ## summary
At low dose, aspirin permanently disables platelet cyclooxygenase-1 for that platelet's lifespan, which is why it is used prophylactically against arterial thrombotic disorders.

## sections
### Definition
At low dose, aspirin irreversibly inhibits platelet cyclooxygenase-1, blocking thromboxane A2 synthesis for the entire remaining lifespan of that platelet (platelets cannot resynthesise the enzyme, having no nucleus). This antiplatelet effect reduces platelet aggregation, which is why low-dose aspirin is used prophylactically to reduce the risk of arterial thrombotic events such as myocardial infarction and ischaemic stroke in at-risk patients.

### Mechanism
Aspirin acetylates a serine residue in the active site of cyclooxygenase-1, a covalent, irreversible modification that permanently blocks the enzyme's ability to convert arachidonic acid into thromboxane A2, the mediator platelets use to promote their own aggregation and further activate nearby platelets. Because platelets are anucleate and cannot synthesise new enzyme, the inhibition of any individual platelet's cyclooxygenase-1 persists for that platelet's roughly ten-day lifespan, even though aspirin itself is cleared from plasma within hours.

### Key determinants
Low-dose, prophylactic use targets platelet cyclooxygenase-1 selectively enough to suppress thromboxane A2 without meaningfully affecting the higher, less platelet-selective doses used for aspirin's separate anti-inflammatory and analgesic actions — the dose, not a different drug, is what distinguishes the antiplatelet indication from aspirin's other uses.

### Clinical significance
This mechanism is the pharmacological basis for aspirin's role in secondary prevention after myocardial infarction or ischaemic stroke, and it is also why aspirin is stopped before elective surgery for a period covering roughly one platelet lifespan when bleeding risk needs to be minimised, rather than the much shorter period that would suffice for a reversible antiplatelet agent.

## summary
Metabolism generally converts a lipophilic drug into a more polar metabolite, which speeds its elimination in the urine rather than slowing it.

## sections
### Definition
Biotransformation (drug metabolism) generally converts a lipophilic drug into a more polar, water-soluble metabolite, most often through phase I oxidation followed by phase II conjugation. This increased polarity makes the metabolite harder to reabsorb across the lipid renal tubular membrane, so it is excreted in the urine faster than the more lipophilic parent drug would have been.

### Mechanism
A lipophilic parent drug filtered at the glomerulus can be passively reabsorbed back into the blood across the lipid tubular epithelium as urine becomes concentrated along the nephron. Phase I reactions (chiefly cytochrome P450-mediated oxidation) introduce or unmask a polar functional group, and phase II reactions (glucuronidation, sulfation, and similar conjugations) attach an even more polar moiety; the resulting metabolite is too polar to be efficiently reabsorbed, so it stays in the tubular fluid and is excreted rather than recycled back into the circulation.

### Key determinants
The general direction of biotransformation is toward greater polarity and faster elimination — the opposite of what a learner might assume if picturing 'metabolism' as simply activating or building up a compound. Specific reactions can vary (some metabolites are even less polar, or more pharmacologically active, than the parent), but the textbook default relationship is metabolism-to-polarity-to-excretion.

### Clinical significance
This general rule explains why drugs with extensive first-pass hepatic metabolism (which converts them to more polar, more readily excreted metabolites) often have shorter durations of action orally than would be predicted from the parent drug's own lipophilicity alone, and why renal impairment can prolong the action of drugs whose active or toxic metabolites depend on renal clearance.

## summary

## sections
### Definition

With severe hypoalbuminemia, less albumin is available to bind a highly protein-bound drug, so a larger fraction of the same therapeutic dose stays free and active, producing a greater-than-normal, potentially toxic response even though total (bound plus free) drug concentration may look unchanged on a standard assay. ### Mechanism

### Key determinants
The clinically relevant variable is the free (active) drug concentration, not the total concentration a routine assay reports — a normal-looking total level in a hypoalbuminemic patient can mask a dangerously elevated free fraction, which is why highly protein-bound, narrow-therapeutic-index drugs (phenytoin is the classic teaching example) need special interpretation, or free-level monitoring, in hypoalbuminemic patients.

### Clinical significance
This is a recurring source of drug toxicity in malnourished, cirrhotic or nephrotic-syndrome patients, whose low albumin can produce exaggerated or toxic responses to standard doses of highly protein-bound drugs, and it is a specific teaching trap for drugs where 'normal' total plasma levels are misread as reassuring.

## summary
Prazosin's characteristic side effect, first-dose hypotension, is why therapy is classically started at a low bedtime dose.

## sections
### Definition
Prazosin is a selective, competitive alpha-1 adrenergic blocker. Its characteristic side effect is first-dose hypotension: with the first dose or a dose increase, sudden marked alpha-1 blockade can produce profound orthostatic hypotension and syncope, which is why therapy is classically started at a low bedtime dose. Because prazosin spares presynaptic alpha-2 receptors (which continue providing negative feedback on noradrenaline release), it causes comparatively little reflex tachycardia compared with non-selective alpha blockers such as phenoxybenzamine.

### Mechanism
A previously unblocked vascular bed accustomed to baseline alpha-1-mediated vasoconstrictor tone loses that tone abruptly with the first exposure to prazosin, before any compensatory mechanism has adjusted, producing an exaggerated fall in peripheral resistance and blood pressure relative to the smaller falls seen with subsequent, steady-state dosing. Sparing presynaptic alpha-2 autoreceptors preserves their negative-feedback brake on noradrenaline release, which is why prazosin causes noticeably less reflex tachycardia than a non-selective alpha blocker that also removes that brake.

### Key determinants
The exam-relevant discriminator among alpha blockers is selectivity: a selective alpha-1 blocker (prazosin) spares the alpha-2 autoreceptor feedback loop and shows less reflex tachycardia, while a non-selective alpha blocker (phenoxybenzamine) blocks both subtypes and shows more reflex tachycardia — first-dose hypotension itself is a property of the abrupt vasodilation rather than of selectivity specifically.

### Clinical significance
The low-bedtime-starting-dose rule is a direct clinical response to first-dose hypotension risk, minimising the chance of syncope and fall injury with the drug's first exposure; the same caution applies whenever a prazosin dose is increased, not just at initiation.

### Definition

## summary

## sections
### Definition
Clonidine is a centrally-acting alpha-2 adrenergic agonist. By stimulating alpha-2 autoreceptors in the brainstem's vasomotor centre, it reduces sympathetic outflow from the CNS, lowering peripheral vascular resistance, heart rate and blood pressure. This central sympatholytic action is why clonidine is used as an antihypertensive despite being an adrenergic agonist rather than a blocker, the opposite mechanism class from peripherally-acting antihypertensives such as alpha or beta blockers.

### Mechanism
Alpha-2 autoreceptors normally provide negative feedback on noradrenergic neurons — their stimulation inhibits further noradrenaline release. Clonidine exploits this same feedback loop at the level of the brainstem vasomotor centre: stimulating central alpha-2 receptors there reduces the centre's own sympathetic outflow to the periphery, so the net effect of an agonist acting centrally is a sympatholytic, blood-pressure-lowering one, even though at a peripheral vascular alpha-2 receptor the same agonist class would tend toward vasoconstriction.

### Key determinants
The key distinguishing feature is site of action, not receptor-agonist-versus-blocker status: a centrally-acting alpha-2 agonist (clonidine) reduces sympathetic outflow at its source, producing a net antihypertensive effect, while a peripherally-acting alpha agonist would instead tend to raise blood pressure through direct vasoconstriction — the same broad receptor class can push blood pressure in opposite directions depending on where it acts.

### Clinical significance
Clonidine's central mechanism is also why abrupt discontinuation after chronic use can cause rebound hypertension (a surge of sympathetic outflow once the central inhibition is suddenly withdrawn), a recognised clinical hazard that does not occur with peripherally-acting antihypertensives working by a different mechanism.

## sections
### Definition

### Mechanism

## summary

## sections
### Definition
Oxidising drugs such as nitrates place oxidative stress on the red blood cell, which normally neutralises reactive oxygen species using NADPH generated by the glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) pathway. In G6PD deficiency, red cells cannot regenerate enough reduced glutathione to withstand this oxidative stress, so oxidant drugs, including nitrates as well as certain antimalarials and sulfonamides, can precipitate acute hemolysis.

### Mechanism
G6PD is the rate-limiting enzyme of the pentose phosphate pathway, generating the NADPH the red cell needs to keep glutathione in its reduced (protective) form. Without adequate NADPH, oxidised glutathione cannot be regenerated fast enough to neutralise the reactive oxygen species an oxidant drug generates, so haemoglobin and membrane proteins are oxidatively damaged, haemoglobin denatures into Heinz bodies, and the red cell is prematurely destroyed, both by direct membrane injury and by splenic removal of the damaged cells.

### Key determinants
G6PD-deficiency hemolysis is specifically triggered by oxidant drugs (nitrates, certain antimalarials, sulfonamides, and others), not by drugs in general — the enzyme deficiency is a vulnerability to a specific class of oxidative challenge, not a general drug-toxicity susceptibility, which is why identifying an oxidant trigger is the key step in linking a hemolytic episode to G6PD deficiency.

### Clinical significance
Screening for G6PD deficiency (or eliciting a history of it) before prescribing known oxidant triggers is standard practice in populations where the deficiency is common, since an acute hemolytic episode from an avoidable drug exposure is a preventable adverse event.

The vagus nerve supplies parasympathetic secretomotor fibres to the stomach and pancreas: vagal stimulation increases gastric acid and pepsinogen secretion (partly directly, partly via gastrin release) and stimulates pancreatic exocrine (enzyme and bicarbonate) secretion, part of the cephalic and gastric phases of digestion. The same vagal (parasympathetic) stimulation also increases gastrointestinal smooth muscle motility, while slowing the heart (bradycardia via muscarinic M2 receptors) and constricting the bronchi, the opposite effector pattern from sympathetic stimulation at each site.

### Mechanism
Vagal preganglionic fibres synapse in the enteric plexus and postganglionic cholinergic fibres act on muscarinic receptors on parietal cells, chief cells, pancreatic acinar cells and gut smooth muscle, all of which respond to muscarinic stimulation by increasing secretion or contraction. This is the same neurotransmitter (acetylcholine) acting through the same receptor family (muscarinic) as vagal input to the heart, but the downstream effect at the gut is stimulatory (increased secretion, increased motility) while the effect at the sinoatrial node is inhibitory (slowed rate), because the receptor's downstream signalling differs by effector tissue.

### Key determinants
Parasympathetic effects are not uniformly 'quiet' or 'active' across every organ; the direction of the effect (increase or decrease in activity) depends on the specific effector organ, not a single blanket rule about what parasympathetic stimulation does — the gut responds with increased secretion and motility, while the heart and bronchi respond with slowing and constriction respectively.

### Clinical significance
This organ-specific pattern is why anticholinergic (antimuscarinic) drugs produce a mixed clinical picture — reduced gastric and salivary secretion and reduced gut motility alongside tachycardia and bronchodilation — all from blocking the same receptor class at different organs with opposite baseline parasympathetic effects.

## lose_the_mark

## summary
Acetylcholine's synaptic action ends mainly by rapid enzymatic hydrolysis, the classic exception to the reuptake mechanism most other neurotransmitters use.

## sections
### Definition
Unlike noradrenaline, whose action is mainly terminated by presynaptic reuptake, acetylcholine's action at cholinergic synapses is terminated mainly by rapid enzymatic hydrolysis. Acetylcholinesterase, present in the synaptic cleft, splits acetylcholine into choline and acetate within milliseconds, ending its receptor binding almost as fast as it began. Simple diffusion away from the cleft is far too slow to account for the very brief duration of acetylcholine's action on its own.

### Mechanism
Acetylcholinesterase is anchored in the synaptic cleft itself (unlike a reuptake transporter, which sits on the presynaptic membrane and requires the transmitter to travel back to it), so hydrolysis can begin the instant acetylcholine is released, extremely rapidly ending receptor occupancy. The choline product is then taken back up presynaptically by a separate high-affinity transporter to be resynthesised into new acetylcholine, but this reuptake step recycles a metabolite rather than terminating the original synaptic signal, which enzymatic hydrolysis has already done.

### Key determinants
The teaching trap is assuming every neurotransmitter is terminated by presynaptic reuptake of the intact transmitter (true for noradrenaline, dopamine and serotonin); acetylcholine is the classic exception, terminated by enzymatic breakdown in the cleft, with only its choline breakdown product, not intact acetylcholine, being taken back up.

### Clinical significance
This mechanism is exactly why cholinesterase inhibitors (neostigmine, donepezil, organophosphate poisons) work by blocking the termination step itself, prolonging acetylcholine's action — a pharmacological strategy with no equivalent 'reuptake inhibitor' class for acetylcholine, unlike the reuptake-inhibitor drug classes that exist for noradrenaline and serotonin.

## summary

## sections
### Definition
Neostigmine (prostigmine) is a reversible cholinesterase inhibitor whose quaternary structure keeps it largely outside the CNS, acting mainly at the peripheral neuromuscular junction, where it raises acetylcholine levels and improves muscle strength. This makes it, along with pyridostigmine, a mainstay of chronic symptomatic myasthenia gravis treatment, distinct from edrophonium's brief use purely to diagnose the condition and from neostigmine's own separate uses reversing neuromuscular blockade and increasing GIT motility.

### Mechanism

## summary
Epinephrine's beta-1 action on cardiac myocytes increases the strength of myocardial contraction, distinct from its alpha-1-mediated effects elsewhere.

## sections
### Definition
Cardiac beta-1 adrenergic receptors mediate a positive inotropic effect. Epinephrine binding beta-1 receptors on cardiac myocytes raises intracellular cAMP, which increases calcium influx and increases the strength (and rate) of myocardial contraction. This is a classic beta-receptor-mediated action of epinephrine, distinct from its alpha-1-mediated actions such as piloerection, pupillary dilatation and skin/splanchnic vasoconstriction.

### Mechanism
Beta-1 receptor stimulation activates Gs-protein-coupled adenylyl cyclase, raising intracellular cAMP, which activates protein kinase A and increases calcium influx through L-type calcium channels during each action potential; more intracellular calcium means more cross-bridge cycling and a stronger contraction for the same fibre length, the direct cellular basis of positive inotropy. This Gs-cAMP-calcium pathway is entirely separate from the Gq-coupled alpha-1 pathway epinephrine activates at vascular and other alpha-1-bearing tissues.

### Key determinants
Sorting epinephrine's many physiological effects by receptor subtype, not by organ, is the correct organising principle — a single organ (the eye, for instance) can show both alpha-1-mediated effects (mydriasis) and beta-mediated effects (ciliary relaxation) side by side, so 'which organ' does not reliably predict 'which receptor'.

### Clinical significance
This beta-1-mediated positive inotropy is part of why epinephrine is used in cardiac arrest and severe anaphylaxis, where increasing myocardial contractile force (alongside its alpha-1 vasopressor action) supports circulation, and it is the mechanistic basis for beta-1 agonists being used clinically whenever increased contractility, rather than increased vascular tone, is the therapeutic goal.

## summary
A genetic pseudocholinesterase deficiency slows succinylcholine's normal rapid breakdown, prolonging its neuromuscular block into post-operative apnea.

## sections
### Definition
Succinylcholine, a depolarising neuromuscular blocker used for rapid intubation, is normally broken down quickly by plasma pseudocholinesterase (butyrylcholinesterase). In patients with a genetic pseudocholinesterase deficiency, succinylcholine is metabolised much more slowly, so its neuromuscular blockade, and the resulting inability to breathe, persists for a prolonged period after surgery instead of wearing off within minutes.

### Mechanism
Succinylcholine's typically brief action (a few minutes) depends entirely on rapid plasma hydrolysis by pseudocholinesterase before it can diffuse away from the neuromuscular junction and re-equilibrate. With reduced or genetically abnormal (low-activity) pseudocholinesterase, the drug persists in the circulation and continues occupying nicotinic receptors at the motor endplate far longer than normal, so the depolarising block, and the resulting flaccid paralysis of respiratory muscles, does not wear off on the expected timescale and the patient cannot resume spontaneous breathing.

### Key determinants
Pseudocholinesterase deficiency and G6PD deficiency are both inherited enzyme deficiencies that alter a drug response, but by unrelated mechanisms and with unrelated triggers — pseudocholinesterase deficiency specifically prolongs succinylcholine's neuromuscular block, while G6PD deficiency specifically predisposes to oxidant drug-induced hemolysis; neither predicts the other.

### Clinical significance
Unexpected, prolonged post-operative apnea after succinylcholine should prompt suspicion of pseudocholinesterase deficiency, managed with continued mechanical ventilation and sedation until the block wears off (which can take hours in severe deficiency) rather than repeated attempts to reverse a depolarising block pharmacologically, since standard anticholinesterase reversal agents do not reliably reverse depolarising blockade the way they do non-depolarising blockade.

## summary

## sections
### Definition
The adrenal medulla's chromaffin cells synthesise and secrete both adrenaline (epinephrine) and noradrenaline (norepinephrine). In humans, roughly 80% of the secretion is adrenaline, making it the most abundant signalling molecule released from the adrenal medulla, with noradrenaline as the smaller remaining fraction. Dopamine is a biosynthetic precursor within the chromaffin cell but is not the predominant molecule ultimately secreted.

### Mechanism
Chromaffin cells synthesise catecholamines along the pathway tyrosine to dopa to dopamine to noradrenaline to adrenaline; the final step, methylation of noradrenaline to adrenaline by phenylethanolamine N-methyltransferase, is highly expressed in adrenal medullary chromaffin cells (an expression pattern induced by locally high cortisol from the adjacent adrenal cortex), which is why the adrenal medulla preferentially converts most of its noradrenaline onward to adrenaline before secretion, unlike sympathetic postganglionic neurons, which secrete noradrenaline as their primary neurotransmitter without this final conversion step.

### Key determinants
The distinction to hold onto is between what stimulates the chromaffin cell (acetylcholine, released from the preganglionic sympathetic fibre synapsing directly on it, since the adrenal medulla is functionally a modified sympathetic ganglion) and what the chromaffin cell itself secretes into the bloodstream in response (mainly adrenaline) — these are two different molecules at two different points in the same pathway.

### Clinical significance
This 80:20 adrenaline-to-noradrenaline secretion ratio underlies the physiological 'fight or flight' response's characteristic mix of effects (marked beta-mediated cardiac and metabolic effects alongside alpha-mediated vasoconstriction), and it is the biological basis for why an adrenal medullary tumour (pheochromocytoma) typically produces a clinical picture dominated by excess adrenaline (and noradrenaline) effects.

## lose_the_mark

## summary

## sections
### Definition
Sympathetic stimulation of the eye dilates the pupil (mydriasis, via the alpha-1-mediated iris dilator muscle) and relaxes the ciliary muscle, flattening the lens to focus for distance vision. The combined result of cervical sympathetic chain stimulation is that the eye is optically set up to see far objects, the opposite of the parasympathetic near-vision accommodation reflex (ciliary muscle contraction, pupillary constriction). ### Mechanism
Sympathetic postganglionic fibres travelling in the cervical sympathetic chain reach the iris dilator muscle (alpha-1 mediated contraction, widening the pupil) and the ciliary muscle (beta-mediated relaxation, flattening the lens by increasing tension on the suspensory ligaments). ### Key determinants

### Clinical significance
Recognising Horner syndrome as the loss (not the gain) of cervical sympathetic tone, and distinguishing it from a stimulated sympathetic state, is a classic localising clinical sign for lesions anywhere along the three-neuron sympathetic pathway from the hypothalamus to the eye.

## summary
Thinner nerve fibres conduct action potentials more slowly than thick ones because their higher internal resistance slows the spread of depolarisation.

## sections
### Definition
Conduction velocity along a nerve fibre depends on axon diameter and myelination: thinner fibres have higher internal (axoplasmic) resistance to current flow, which slows the passive spread of depolarisation ahead of the active zone, so the action potential is conducted more slowly in thin nerve fibres than in thick ones. The action potential itself is all-or-none (not graded), triggered only by a threshold or supra-threshold stimulus, and its upstroke is a rapid inward sodium current rather than repolarisation.

### Mechanism
Cable properties of the axon govern how far and how fast local depolarising current spreads ahead of an active region before triggering the next patch of membrane to threshold; a thinner axon presents a smaller cross-sectional area for current flow, meaning higher axoplasmic resistance per unit length, which slows this passive spread and therefore slows conduction, independent of any change in the active sodium- and potassium-channel kinetics that generate the action potential's shape.

### Key determinants
An action potential must be kept conceptually separate from a graded (local) potential: it is triggered only by a threshold or supra-threshold stimulus and then propagates all-or-none at fixed amplitude, unlike a graded potential, whose amplitude varies with stimulus strength and which does not by itself propagate over distance without decrement.

### Clinical significance
Fibre-diameter-dependent conduction velocity underlies the clinically recognised order in which local anaesthetics and nerve blocks affect different fibre types (small, thinly or unmyelinated pain fibres are typically blocked before large, myelinated motor fibres), and it is why demyelinating disease, which effectively reduces the fibre's functional conduction properties, slows nerve conduction velocity on electrophysiological testing.

## hold_these

## lose_the_mark
Assuming the action potential is graded like a local potential, or requires no energy at all — restoring ionic gradients afterward does require ATP, even though depolarisation/repolarisation themselves are passive ion flow.

## summary
Active transport needs ongoing cellular ATP and stops when the cell dies, unlike passive transport mechanisms, which need no living cellular machinery.

## sections
### Definition
Active transport requires continuous expenditure of metabolic energy (ATP) by the cell's own pumps to move solute against its concentration gradient. Because it depends on the cell's living metabolic machinery, it stops as soon as the cell dies or its ATP supply is blocked, unlike passive mechanisms (simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis), which need no living cellular machinery and can, at least transiently, continue to act on an existing gradient even in a dead cell.

### Mechanism
Active transport pumps are enzymes (ATPases) that couple ATP hydrolysis to conformational changes moving a solute against its gradient; once ATP production stops (cell death, metabolic poisons such as cyanide, or severe hypoxia), the pump can no longer cycle and active transport ceases essentially immediately. Passive mechanisms instead rely purely on the physical properties of the membrane and any existing concentration gradient, which persist, at least transiently, independent of the cell's metabolic state.

### Key determinants
Carrier-mediated transport is not automatically active transport — facilitated diffusion uses a carrier protein but is passive (no ATP spent, movement downhill only); the discriminator for active transport specifically is ATP-dependence and the ability to move solute uphill, not merely the presence of a carrier.

### Clinical significance
This energy dependence is the physiological basis for cellular swelling and ionic derangement in ischaemia and cell death — once ATP-dependent pumps such as the Na-K ATPase fail, the concentration gradients they maintained collapse, sodium and water enter the cell along their now-unopposed gradients, and cellular oedema and dysfunction follow.

## lose_the_mark

## summary

## sections
### Definition
The flexor withdrawal reflex, rapidly flexing a limb away from a painful stimulus, is a somatic reflex: its efferent limb runs through somatic motor neurons directly to skeletal (voluntary) muscle. Autonomic (visceral) reflexes such as salivary secretion, gastric juice secretion, and cardiac rate changes instead run their efferent limb through autonomic ganglia to smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, or glands, a structurally distinct reflex arc from the somatic one.

### Mechanism

Autonomic reflexes instead route their efferent output through a two-neuron chain (preganglionic to postganglionic, synapsing in an autonomic ganglion) before reaching smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, or a gland, a structurally distinct efferent pathway even though both reflex types share the same basic afferent-centre-efferent architecture.

### Key determinants
A reflex should be classified by its efferent limb and effector organ (skeletal muscle for somatic; smooth muscle, cardiac muscle or gland for autonomic), not by its trigger — a painful stimulus can provoke either a somatic response (withdrawal) or an autonomic response (a change in heart rate or sweating) depending on which reflex arc is engaged.

### Clinical significance
Distinguishing somatic from autonomic reflex arcs matters clinically when localising a neurological lesion, since damage to a somatic reflex arc (loss of the withdrawal or deep tendon reflexes) points to a different anatomical level and pathway than damage affecting autonomic reflex function (orthostatic hypotension, bladder or bowel dysfunction).

## summary

## sections
### Definition
Four cranial nerves carry parasympathetic fibres: III (oculomotor, to the pupil and ciliary muscle), VII (facial, to the lacrimal, submandibular and sublingual glands), IX (glossopharyngeal, to the parotid gland) and X (vagus, to thoracic and abdominal viscera). ### Mechanism
Each of the four parasympathetic cranial nerves arises from its own brainstem nucleus (Edinger-Westphal for III, superior salivatory for VII, inferior salivatory for IX, dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus for X) and synapses in a dedicated peripheral ganglion (ciliary for III, pterygopalatine/submandibular for VII, otic for IX, and diffuse enteric/visceral ganglia for X) before reaching its target organ — this consistent preganglionic-to-named-ganglion-to-target architecture is what makes each of the four traceable and separately testable.

### Key determinants
Only III, VII, IX and X originate parasympathetic fibres of their own; other cranial nerves that travel alongside or share a foramen with one of these four (for instance, branches of the trigeminal nerve carrying parasympathetic fibres that actually originated in VII) do not themselves originate parasympathetic output, a distinction worth holding onto separately from which nerve merely transports fibres that started elsewhere.

### Clinical significance
Localising a lesion to a specific parasympathetic cranial nerve pathway (a fixed, dilated pupil suggesting oculomotor nerve compression; dry eye and impaired lacrimation suggesting facial nerve involvement; loss of parotid secretion suggesting glossopharyngeal or otic ganglion pathology) is a recurring clinical localisation exercise built directly on this four-nerve anatomy.

## summary
A long plasma half-life is a genuinely desired antibiotic property, supporting convenient, adherence-friendly dosing, unlike resistance or hypersensitivity, which are drawbacks.

## sections
### Definition
Among the properties a clinician weighs when selecting an antibiotic, a long plasma half-life is a genuinely desirable pharmacokinetic property: it allows less frequent dosing, which improves patient adherence and maintains more stable plasma concentrations between doses. This is distinct from properties that are drawbacks to be minimised, such as a strong tendency to develop bacterial resistance or a propensity to cause hypersensitivity reactions, and distinct from properties (spectrum width) whose desirability is situation-dependent rather than universal.

### Mechanism
A longer half-life means plasma concentration falls more slowly between doses, keeping the drug above the minimum inhibitory concentration for longer and allowing a longer interval before the next dose is needed while still maintaining adequate exposure; fewer daily doses is directly associated with better patient adherence in clinical practice, which in turn matters for treatment success and for limiting the development of resistance from under-dosed, inconsistently-taken courses.

### Key determinants
Not every listed antibiotic property sorts neatly into 'always good' or 'always bad': spectrum width is situational (broad-spectrum favoured for empirical therapy before an organism is identified, narrow-spectrum favoured once it is, to limit collateral damage to normal flora and resistance pressure), while a long half-life is unconditionally favourable and resistance-proneness or hypersensitivity risk are unconditionally unfavourable.

### Clinical significance
This is the pharmacokinetic reasoning behind preferring once-daily or extended-interval antibiotic regimens where efficacy is equivalent, since simpler dosing schedules are a recognised, evidence-based lever for improving real-world antibiotic adherence and treatment completion.

## lose_the_mark
Treating every listed antibiotic property as uniformly desirable or undesirable, instead of recognising that spectrum width is situational while half-life, resistance and hypersensitivity are not.

## summary
Cefepime, like all beta-lactams, inhibits bacterial cell wall synthesis by binding penicillin-binding proteins and blocking peptidoglycan cross-linking.

## sections
### Definition
Cefepime is a fourth-generation cephalosporin, a beta-lactam antibiotic. Like other beta-lactams, it inhibits bacterial cell wall synthesis by binding penicillin-binding proteins and blocking the transpeptidation (cross-linking) step of peptidoglycan synthesis, weakening the cell wall and causing bacterial lysis, a mechanism distinct from the protein-synthesis-inhibiting aminoglycosides, macrolides and tetracyclines, or the folate-synthesis-inhibiting sulfonamides/trimethoprim.

### Mechanism
Penicillin-binding proteins are the transpeptidase enzymes that cross-link adjacent peptidoglycan strands, giving the bacterial cell wall its mechanical strength; cefepime's beta-lactam ring structurally mimics the enzyme's natural substrate and binds covalently to the active site, irreversibly inactivating the transpeptidase. Without ongoing cross-linking, the cell wall cannot withstand the bacterium's high internal osmotic pressure, and the cell lyses, particularly during active growth and division when new wall synthesis is most needed.

### Key determinants
Antibiotics are more reliably sorted by mechanism class than by generation or spectrum label alone — cefepime's fourth-generation status describes its expanded spectrum (including Pseudomonas coverage) relative to earlier cephalosporins, but its mechanism (cell wall synthesis inhibition via penicillin-binding proteins) is the same beta-lactam mechanism shared with every generation of cephalosporin and with penicillins.

### Clinical significance
Because cell-wall-synthesis inhibition only kills actively dividing bacteria (a bacterium not actively building new wall is comparatively less vulnerable), beta-lactams like cefepime are generally most effective against actively multiplying organisms, a pharmacodynamic property relevant to dosing strategy and to why beta-lactams are often described as time-dependent rather than concentration-dependent killers.

## lose_the_mark

## summary
Vancomycin remains active against MRSA because its cell-wall mechanism bypasses the altered penicillin-binding protein that confers methicillin resistance.

## sections
### Definition
Vancomycin, a glycopeptide, inhibits bacterial cell wall synthesis by binding the D-Ala-D-Ala terminus of peptidoglycan precursors. This mechanism does not rely on the penicillin-binding proteins altered in methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), so vancomycin remains effective where beta-lactams such as cloxacillin and ampicillin, which MRSA is by definition resistant to, no longer work, making it the classic drug of choice for serious MRSA infection.

### Mechanism
MRSA's defining resistance mechanism is an altered penicillin-binding protein (PBP2a, encoded by the mecA gene) with low affinity for beta-lactam antibiotics, so beta-lactams can no longer bind and inhibit it effectively. Vancomycin sidesteps this resistance entirely because it does not target the penicillin-binding protein at all — instead it binds directly to the D-Ala-D-Ala terminal residues of the peptidoglycan precursor itself, physically blocking the transpeptidase (and transglycosylase) reaction from a different point in the pathway, a target PBP2a's altered structure does not protect.

### Key determinants
Resistance to one member of a mechanism class does not imply resistance to every drug that shares the same broad mechanism category (cell wall synthesis inhibition) — what matters is whether the specific molecular target has been altered by the resistance mechanism, and vancomycin's different binding target (the precursor, not the enzyme) is exactly why it escapes an enzyme-altering resistance mechanism like MRSA's.

### Clinical significance
Vancomycin's continued reliability against MRSA (though vancomycin-resistant strains do exist and are a distinct, separately-tested concern) is why it remains a first-line agent for serious MRSA infections such as bacteraemia and endocarditis, and understanding its distinct binding target explains both its continued utility and the separate resistance mechanisms (altered D-Ala-D-Ala termini) that can eventually defeat it.

## hold_these

## lose_the_mark
Assuming resistance to one cell-wall-synthesis inhibitor (a beta-lactam) implies resistance to all cell-wall-synthesis inhibitors, rather than checking the specific molecular target each drug binds.

## summary
Clavulanic acid inactivates bacterial beta-lactamase, protecting a co-administered beta-lactam from degradation and restoring its activity against resistant organisms.

## sections
### Definition
Clavulanic acid has weak antibacterial activity of its own but binds and irreversibly inactivates many bacterial beta-lactamase enzymes, protecting a co-administered beta-lactam from being hydrolysed. The amoxicillin-clavulanic acid combination is therefore active against many beta-lactamase-producing organisms that amoxicillin alone could not treat, unlike a combination of two plain beta-lactams (with no inhibitor), which offers no such protection against beta-lactamase degradation.

### Mechanism
Bacterial beta-lactamase enzymes hydrolyse the beta-lactam ring of susceptible antibiotics, destroying their activity before they can reach and bind their penicillin-binding-protein target. Clavulanic acid, structurally itself a beta-lactam, acts as a 'suicide substrate': it binds the beta-lactamase active site and forms a stable, essentially irreversible complex with the enzyme, permanently inactivating it, which spares the co-administered partner beta-lactam (amoxicillin) from hydrolysis and lets it reach and inhibit its penicillin-binding-protein target intact.

### Key determinants
Combining two beta-lactams does not confer beta-lactamase protection on its own — only a genuine beta-lactamase inhibitor (clavulanic acid, sulbactam, tazobactam) provides that protection, since the inhibitor's specific job is inactivating the resistance enzyme itself, a distinct pharmacological role from simply adding a second antibacterial agent.

### Clinical significance
Beta-lactam/beta-lactamase-inhibitor combinations (amoxicillin-clavulanate, piperacillin-tazobactam) are a standard clinical strategy for extending a beta-lactam's usefulness against organisms that would otherwise degrade it, and recognising which drug in a combination is the actual inhibitor (versus which is simply a second antibiotic) is central to understanding why some combinations restore activity against resistant organisms and others do not.

## lose_the_mark

## summary

## sections
### Definition
Linezolid is an oxazolidinone that inhibits bacterial protein synthesis at the 50S ribosomal subunit, at the initiation step. Its clinical niche is serious Gram-positive infections resistant to other agents, most notably vancomycin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (VRSA) and vancomycin-resistant enterococci, where it is a key remaining oral or IV option; it has no useful activity against the Gram-negative organisms (Pseudomonas, Neisseria, E. coli) that other antimicrobial classes target instead.

### Mechanism
Linezolid binds the 23S ribosomal RNA of the bacterial 50S ribosomal subunit and blocks formation of the initiation complex needed to begin translation, a mechanism and binding site distinct enough from other protein-synthesis inhibitors (macrolides, aminoglycosides, tetracyclines) that cross-resistance between linezolid and those other classes is uncommon, which is exactly why it remains active against organisms already resistant to vancomycin and to many other agents through unrelated mechanisms.

### Key determinants
A drug reserved for 'resistant' infections is not automatically broad-spectrum — linezolid's resistant-organism niche is specifically Gram-positive; it has essentially no useful Gram-negative coverage, unlike some other last-resort agents whose reserved status comes precisely from broad-spectrum activity against multidrug-resistant Gram-negative organisms instead.

### Clinical significance
Linezolid's availability as both an oral and intravenous formulation with essentially complete bioavailability makes it clinically useful for stepping down from IV to oral therapy for serious Gram-positive infections without losing systemic exposure, a practical advantage in prolonged courses for conditions such as VRSA bacteraemia or endocarditis.

## summary

## sections
### Definition
Azole antifungals, such as fluconazole, itraconazole and ketoconazole, inhibit the fungal cytochrome P450 enzyme lanosterol 14-alpha-demethylase, blocking a key step in the synthesis of ergosterol, the fungal membrane's principal sterol. The resulting ergosterol depletion, and accumulation of toxic sterol intermediates, disrupts fungal cell membrane structure and function, a different target from the cell-wall-glucan-synthesis inhibitors (echinocandins) or the microtubule-disrupting agents (griseofulvin).

### Mechanism
Ergosterol occupies in the fungal membrane the structural role cholesterol occupies in the human cell membrane, maintaining fluidity and integrity; blocking its synthesis at the lanosterol demethylase step both depletes functional ergosterol and causes toxic methylated sterol precursors to accumulate in the membrane instead, together disrupting membrane permeability and the function of membrane-embedded enzymes, ultimately inhibiting fungal growth (fungistatic at typical concentrations for most azoles against most organisms).

### Key determinants
Antifungal drug classes should not be lumped together as simply 'attacking the fungal membrane' — only azoles (via ergosterol synthesis inhibition) and the polyenes (via direct ergosterol binding, a different mechanism again) actually target ergosterol or the membrane; echinocandins instead target the fungal cell wall's beta-glucan synthesis, and griseofulvin targets fungal mitotic spindle (microtubule) function, unrelated targets entirely.

### Clinical significance
Because the azole target enzyme (a cytochrome P450) is structurally related to human hepatic cytochrome P450 enzymes, azoles are well known for causing clinically significant drug-drug interactions by inhibiting human CYP enzymes as well, a practical prescribing consideration that follows directly from the drug class's target enzyme family.

## summary
During a constant-rate infusion, plasma concentration reaches 50% of the eventual steady-state level after one half-life, so that value predicts the eventual steady state.

## sections
### Definition
During a constant-rate IV infusion, plasma concentration rises toward steady state along a predictable exponential accumulation curve: about 50% of the eventual steady-state concentration is reached after one half-life, about 75% after two half-lives, and effectively 100% (steady state) after four to five half-lives. A concentration measured at a known number of half-lives into the infusion can therefore be used to calculate the eventual steady-state concentration, for example doubling a concentration measured at exactly one half-life.

### Mechanism
A constant-rate infusion adds drug at a fixed rate while first-order elimination removes a constant fraction of the total drug present per unit time; these two processes reach equilibrium (steady state) only asymptotically, following the same 50%-per-half-life pattern that governs first-order elimination in reverse — each additional half-life closes half of the remaining gap between the current concentration and the eventual steady-state plateau, which is why the accumulation curve and the elimination curve are mirror images of the same exponential process.

### Key determinants
The concentration measured partway through an infusion is not itself the steady-state concentration and must not be read as such; only after roughly four to five half-lives does the measured concentration closely approximate steady state, and before that point the accumulation-fraction arithmetic (50% at one half-life, 75% at two, and so on) must be applied to project the eventual value.

### Clinical significance
This accumulation rule is the basis for the clinical practice of checking a drug level partway through a maintenance infusion (rather than only at presumed steady state) to catch an unexpectedly high or low trajectory early, and for understanding why a loading dose is used clinically to reach a therapeutic concentration immediately rather than waiting the four to five half-lives a maintenance infusion alone would take.

## hold_these
Plasma concentration reaches about 50% of steady state after one half-life during a constant-rate infusion.
Full steady state is reached only after about four to five half-lives.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing the concentration measured partway through an infusion with the eventual steady-state concentration itself, rather than applying the accumulation-fraction rule.

## summary
Frozen section freezes and cuts fresh, unfixed tissue for a report within minutes, which is what makes it suited to intraoperative consultation.

## sections
### Definition
Frozen section is a rapid tissue-preparation technique in which fresh, unfixed tissue is snap-frozen and cut on a cryostat, allowing a pathologist to examine and report on it within minutes while the patient is still under anaesthesia. This speed is what makes it suited to intraoperative consultation, such as confirming a mass is malignant or checking that resection margins are clear, so the surgeon can decide on further surgery before closing, unlike routine fixed (paraffin) processing, which takes far longer and is used for the definitive, non-time-pressured permanent diagnosis.

### Mechanism
Snap-freezing tissue in a cryostat hardens it enough to be cut into thin sections without the lengthy chemical fixation and paraffin-embedding steps routine histology requires, trading some of the crisp cellular detail a well-fixed, paraffin-embedded section provides for the speed needed to report back to the operating room within the time a patient can safely remain under anaesthesia; the sacrificed detail is why a frozen-section diagnosis is typically followed by definitive confirmation on routinely processed permanent sections once time pressure is no longer a factor.

### Key determinants
Frozen section's defining advantage is time-to-report, not diagnostic superiority over routine processing — it exists specifically to serve the narrow window of intraoperative decision-making, and its use is confined to that time-pressured context rather than any postoperative or postmortem setting where routine fixed processing, with its better tissue detail, is used instead.

### Clinical significance
A classic intraoperative use is assessing surgical margins during tumour resection, where a frozen-section report of a positive (tumour-involved) margin lets the surgeon extend the resection in the same operative sitting, avoiding a second surgery that would be needed if margin status were only known after the patient had already left the operating room.

## summary
An ulcer is a local discontinuity of surface epithelium from sloughing of inflamed, necrotic tissue, distinct from a fistula or a sinus.

## sections
### Definition
An ulcer is defined as a local defect, or discontinuity, of the surface epithelium of skin or mucosa, produced by the shedding (sloughing) of inflamed, necrotic tissue and exposing the underlying connective tissue. This break in surface continuity is the defining structural feature, distinguishing an ulcer from a fistula (a tract connecting two epithelial surfaces) and from a sinus (a blind-ended tract from defective healing), both different lesions built around a tract rather than a surface breach.

### Mechanism
Ulceration begins with an inflammatory or ischaemic insult severe enough to cause necrosis of surface epithelium and adjacent tissue; as the necrotic material is shed (sloughed) off, whether by the inflammatory process itself, mechanical trauma, or both, the underlying connective tissue is left exposed at the surface, and this exposed base, with its surrounding inflamed, often heaped-up or undermined margin, is the structural entity an ulcer describes, distinct from an intact surface with an underlying tract running beneath it.

### Key determinants
The structural family of related surface-continuity lesions (ulcer, fistula, sinus) is distinguished by geometry, not by cause or by inflammation alone: an ulcer is a breach open at a single surface, a fistula is a tract connecting two separate epithelial surfaces (such as skin and bowel), and a sinus is a tract that is blind-ended at one end rather than connecting two surfaces.

### Clinical significance
Correctly classifying a lesion as an ulcer versus a fistula or sinus guides both description in clinical documentation and management planning, since a fistula or sinus often implies a deeper tract requiring tracing and different surgical management from a surface ulcer, even when both may arise from a similar underlying inflammatory process.

## hold_these
An ulcer is a local discontinuity of surface epithelium, from sloughing of inflamed, necrotic tissue.
This is distinct from a fistula (a tract connecting two epithelial surfaces) and a sinus (a blind-ended tract).

## lose_the_mark
Confusing an ulcer (a surface breach) with a fistula (a tract between two surfaces) or a sinus (a blind tract) — related but structurally distinct lesions.
