## sections
### Definition
The Gram reaction (Gram-positive vs Gram-negative) is a stain of the bacterial cell WALL: Gram-positive organisms retain crystal violet-iodine because their wall is a thick, multilayered peptidoglycan sheet, while Gram-negative organisms decolorise and counterstain because their wall is a thin peptidoglycan layer covered by an outer lipopolysaccharide membrane. No other bacterial structure -- the cytoplasmic membrane, the (absent) nucleus, mesosomes or ribosomes -- accounts for the staining difference.
### Mechanism
Crystal violet-iodine complex forms inside the peptidoglycan meshwork during staining; a thick multilayer traps the complex against alcohol decolorisation (Gram-positive), while a thin layer covered by an outer membrane releases it easily (Gram-negative), which is then replaced by the pink/red safranin counterstain.
### Key determinants
The determining factor is peptidoglycan thickness plus the presence or absence of an outer membrane -- not membrane composition, nuclear structure, mesosome number or ribosome type, none of which differ meaningfully between the two groups.
### Clinical significance
Because the stain reads wall structure, Gram reaction correlates with antibiotic susceptibility patterns (for example beta-lactam penetration through the outer membrane) and guides empirical antibiotic choice before culture results are available.
## hold_these
The Gram reaction (Gram-positive vs Gram-negative) is a stain of the bacterial cell WALL: Gram-positive organisms retain crystal violet-iodine because their wall is a thick, multilayered peptidoglycan sheet, while Gram-negative organisms decolorise and counterstain because their wall is a thin peptidoglycan layer covered by an outer lipopolysaccharide membrane.
## lose_the_mark
Assuming the cytoplasmic membrane (rather than the wall external to it) is what the Gram stain reads, or forgetting bacteria have no true nucleus at all.

## summary

## sections
### Definition
Standard microbiology teaching describes the bacterial genome as a single haploid (one copy) circular double-stranded DNA molecule, lying free in the cytoplasm as the nucleoid, with no surrounding nuclear envelope and no associated histone proteins (histone packaging is a eukaryotic feature). A plasmid, when present, is a separate, smaller, dispensable circular DNA molecule, not part of the chromosome itself.
### Mechanism
A single circular chromosome replicates bidirectionally from one origin and segregates into daughter cells at division; because there is only one copy (haploid) and no histone scaffold, the DNA is compacted instead by supercoiling and DNA-binding proteins (e.g. HU, H-NS) into the nucleoid.
### Key determinants
Ploidy (haploid), topology (circular), protein association (no histones) and compartmentalisation (no nuclear envelope) are the four features that together define standard bacterial genome architecture, distinguishing it from both eukaryotic chromosomes and from any individual plasmid the cell may also carry.
### Clinical significance
Because the genome is haploid, a single mutation (for example in a drug target or efflux gene) can immediately alter the bacterium's phenotype without a second wild-type allele to mask it, which is part of why antibiotic resistance mutations can spread and manifest so readily in bacterial populations.
## hold_these
Standard microbiology teaching describes the bacterial genome as a single haploid (one copy) circular double-stranded DNA molecule, lying free in the cytoplasm as the nucleoid, with no surrounding nuclear envelope and no associated histone proteins (histone packaging is a eukaryotic feature).
## lose_the_mark
Accepting a printed source's claim that the bacterial genome is diploid without flagging the inconsistency with standard haploid-genome teaching.

## summary
A commonly taught teaching correlation maps the bacterial growth curve onto the clinical course of an infection: lag phase to the incubation period, log (exponential) phase to the prodromal period, stationary phase to the period of overt illness/symptoms (fastigium), and decline phase to convalescence.
## sections
### Definition
A commonly taught teaching correlation maps the bacterial growth curve onto the clinical course of an infection: lag phase to the incubation period, log (exponential) phase to the prodromal period, stationary phase to the period of overt illness/symptoms (fastigium), and decline phase to convalescence. Because this is a teaching mnemonic rather than a strict biological rule, different sources sometimes assign the 'period of symptoms' step to either the log or the stationary phase.
### Mechanism
As bacterial numbers and their released toxins/antigens rise through lag, log and stationary phases, the corresponding host inflammatory and immune response is taught to rise and then fall in step, giving the four-phase growth curve its parallel four-stage clinical correlate.
### Key determinants
Because this is a teaching simplification rather than a measured biological correspondence, which single phase is labelled 'period of symptoms' can differ between sources -- the determining factor for exam purposes is which specific source and cohort a given question is drawn from, not a single universally fixed mapping.
### Clinical significance
Understanding the growth-curve/illness-stage analogy helps explain why antibiotics are most effective when given during the actively multiplying (log) phase, and why symptoms can persist or worsen even as antibiotic therapy begins to reduce bacterial numbers.
## hold_these
A commonly taught teaching correlation maps the bacterial growth curve onto the clinical course of an infection: lag phase to the incubation period, log (exponential) phase to the prodromal period, stationary phase to the period of overt illness/symptoms (fastigium), and decline phase to convalescence.
## lose_the_mark
Treating this mnemonic as a fixed, universally agreed mapping rather than a teaching simplification that different sources render differently.

## summary

## sections
### Definition
Most bacterial plasmids exist as covalently closed circular (CCC) DNA, a topology with no free ends and therefore relative resistance to exonuclease degradation. A linear plasmid, seen in some genera such as Borrelia and Streptomyces, has two exposed free DNA ends, making it a much easier substrate for exonucleases and comparatively unstable compared with the closed-circular form. The most common and typical physical form of a bacterial plasmid is covalently closed circular (CCC) double-stranded DNA: a closed loop with no free ends, usually maintained in a supercoiled state, which both protects it from exonuclease degradation and is the standard state in which plasmids are purified and studied in the laboratory (for example by alkaline lysis and gel electrophoresis).
### Mechanism
Exonucleases processively degrade DNA starting from a free 3' or 5' end; a linear plasmid presents two such ends per molecule, giving exonucleases a ready entry point, whereas a covalently closed circular plasmid has no free end for the enzyme to begin at.
### Key determinants
Topology (linear vs closed-circular) is the determining structural factor for exonuclease vulnerability, independent of the plasmid's size, host range or copy number.
### Clinical significance
Some naturally linear plasmids (e.g. in Borrelia, the Lyme disease agent) carry important virulence or antigenic-variation genes despite this instability, so their maintenance mechanisms are of direct clinical interest in those organisms.
## hold_these
Most bacterial plasmids exist as covalently closed circular (CCC) DNA, a topology with no free ends and therefore relative resistance to exonuclease degradation.
## lose_the_mark
Assuming all plasmids share the same closed-circular stability, or confusing structural (linear vs circular) instability with functional properties such as host range or copy number.

## summary
Of the three classic forms of bacterial horizontal gene transfer -- conjugation (direct cell-to-cell contact via a sex pilus), transformation (direct uptake of free DNA by a competent cell) and transduction -- transduction is the one mediated by a bacteriophage: during its lytic cycle the phage accidentally packages donor bacterial DNA into a phage particle, which then injects that DNA into a new recipient cell on subsequent infection.
## sections
### Definition
Of the three classic forms of bacterial horizontal gene transfer -- conjugation (direct cell-to-cell contact via a sex pilus), transformation (direct uptake of free DNA by a competent cell) and transduction -- transduction is the one mediated by a bacteriophage: during its lytic cycle the phage accidentally packages donor bacterial DNA into a phage particle, which then injects that DNA into a new recipient cell on subsequent infection.
### Mechanism
During a phage's lytic cycle, host DNA fragments are occasionally packaged by mistake into an assembling phage head instead of (generalized transduction) or alongside (specialized transduction) the phage's own genome; the resulting particle then injects that donor DNA into the next bacterium it infects.
### Key determinants
The presence of an intermediary bacteriophage particle carrying the donor DNA is what defines transduction and separates it from conjugation (direct cell contact) and transformation (free DNA uptake); no phage is involved in either of those other two routes.
### Clinical significance
Transduction is a recognised route by which virulence factors and antibiotic-resistance genes spread between bacterial strains (for example some Staphylococcus aureus toxin genes), making it clinically relevant to the emergence of new pathogenic or resistant strains.
## hold_these
Of the three classic forms of bacterial horizontal gene transfer -- conjugation (direct cell-to-cell contact via a sex pilus), transformation (direct uptake of free DNA by a competent cell) and transduction -- transduction is the one mediated by a bacteriophage: during its lytic cycle the phage accidentally packages donor bacterial DNA into a phage particle, which then injects that DNA into a new recipient cell on subsequent infection.
## lose_the_mark
Confusing transduction (needs a phage vector) with conjugation (needs direct cell contact) or transformation (needs free DNA uptake by a competent cell).

## summary
Phoresis is a host-parasite (or, more broadly, organism-organism) relationship in which one organism is carried by another purely for transport, without deriving nourishment from the carrier and without harming or benefiting it -- a mechanical, transport-only association distinct from true parasitism (harm to the host), commensalism (one-sided benefit without harm) and mutualism (benefit to both).
## sections
### Definition
Phoresis is a host-parasite (or, more broadly, organism-organism) relationship in which one organism is carried by another purely for transport, without deriving nourishment from the carrier and without harming or benefiting it -- a mechanical, transport-only association distinct from true parasitism (harm to the host), commensalism (one-sided benefit without harm) and mutualism (benefit to both).
### Mechanism
In a phoretic relationship the carried organism simply attaches to or is transported on the surface (or within a body cavity) of the carrier organism for the duration of the journey, then detaches once transport is complete, without feeding on or otherwise interacting metabolically with the carrier.
### Key determinants
The absence of any nutritional exchange or harm/benefit in either direction is what separates phoresis from parasitism, commensalism and mutualism, all of which involve some form of nutritional or physiological dependency.
### Clinical significance
Phoretic transport is medically relevant chiefly in vector biology, where it can explain how a non-parasitic organism is nonetheless moved into proximity with a human or animal host, an indirect route distinct from a true parasitic infection.
## hold_these
Phoresis is a host-parasite (or, more broadly, organism-organism) relationship in which one organism is carried by another purely for transport, without deriving nourishment from the carrier and without harming or benefiting it -- a mechanical, transport-only association distinct from true parasitism (harm to the host), commensalism (one-sided benefit without harm) and mutualism (benefit to both).
## lose_the_mark
Confusing phoresis with commensalism (which involves a nutritional or habitat benefit) or assuming the host benefits, which would instead describe mutualism.

## summary

## sections
### Definition
Pili (fimbriae) are fine, hair-like proteinaceous appendages that arise from basal bodies anchored in the cytoplasmic membrane of many, chiefly Gram-negative, bacteria. Structurally they are thinner, shorter, straighter and far more numerous per cell than flagella; ordinary pili mediate adhesion to host cells and surfaces, while specialised sex pili mediate conjugative DNA transfer. Compared with fimbriae/pili, bacterial flagella are typically few in number per cell (one to a modest tuft, depending on arrangement, versus several hundred pili), thicker in diameter, longer, and helical/wavy (rotating to produce movement) rather than straight and rigid -- differences that track their different jobs, motility for flagella versus adhesion for pili.
### Mechanism
A basal body anchored in the cytoplasmic membrane nucleates polymerisation of pilin protein subunits into a long, thin, rigid filament that projects from the bacterial cell surface, with adhesin proteins typically at the pilus tip mediating attachment to specific host-cell receptors.
### Key determinants
Pilus number (usually hundreds per cell), diameter (thinner than flagella) and rigidity (straighter, non-rotating) are the structural determinants that distinguish pili from the far less numerous, thicker, helical, rotating flagella.
### Clinical significance
Pili are frequently targeted by vaccine and anti-adhesion drug development strategies, since blocking initial adhesion via pili can prevent colonisation before infection is established.

## lose_the_mark

## sections
### Definition
An insertion sequence (IS) element is the simplest bacterial transposable element: a central gene encoding transposase (the enzyme needed for the element's own movement), flanked at both ends by short direct or inverted repeat sequences that serve as the recognition sites the transposase acts on. Unlike composite or complex transposons (named Tn, e.g. Tn5, Tn7), an IS element carries no accessory genes such as antibiotic resistance.
### Mechanism
The transposase enzyme encoded by the IS element's central gene recognises the flanking repeat sequences at the element's own ends, excises or copies the element, and inserts it (with its flanking repeats) into a new target DNA site, often generating a short target-site duplication in the process.
### Key determinants
The presence of flanking direct or inverted repeats bracketing a single transposase gene, with no additional accessory genes, is what defines the 'simplest' category of transposable element and distinguishes an IS element from a composite or complex transposon.
### Clinical significance
Insertion sequences are a recognised cause of gene inactivation (by inserting into a coding sequence) and of genome rearrangement, and when two copies flank a resistance gene they can form a composite transposon that mobilises that resistance gene between replicons.
## hold_these
An insertion sequence (IS) element is the simplest bacterial transposable element: a central gene encoding transposase (the enzyme needed for the element's own movement), flanked at both ends by short direct or inverted repeat sequences that serve as the recognition sites the transposase acts on.

Confusing an insertion sequence with a composite transposon (which carries extra genes, such as resistance determinants, between two flanking insertion sequences) or naming Tn5/Tn7 as insertion sequences rather than transposons.

## sections
### Definition
Because bacteria have no mitochondria, the electron transport chain and oxidative phosphorylation take place across the bacterial cytoplasmic membrane instead, generating the proton-motive force used for ATP synthesis, active transport and (in motile species) flagellar rotation -- making the cytoplasmic membrane functionally analogous to the eukaryotic inner mitochondrial membrane.
### Mechanism

### Key determinants

### Clinical significance
Because electron transport and ATP synthesis occur at the bacterial cytoplasmic membrane rather than in a mitochondrion, agents that selectively disrupt this bacterial membrane function (for example certain antibiotics) can kill bacteria with comparatively less effect on human mitochondrial function.

### Definition
The viral nucleic acid (the DNA or RNA genome) is the truly infectious component of a virus: it alone carries the genetic information needed to direct a host cell's machinery to produce new virions, and for some non-enveloped viruses, experimentally purified nucleic acid stripped of its capsid can still initiate infection when introduced directly into a susceptible cell, even though far less efficiently than a complete virion.
### Mechanism

### Clinical significance
This principle underlies both the biology of some plant and animal viral infectivity assays using purified nucleic acid and the rationale behind certain nucleic-acid-based vaccine and antiviral strategies that target the genome directly.

## sections
### Definition
Fungi characteristically reproduce by spore formation. Spores may be produced sexually, following fusion of compatible mating types with genetic recombination, or asexually, by mitotic division producing clonal spores -- this dual sexual-or-asexual spore-forming capacity, rather than fission, fragmentation or simple mitosis, is the defining fungal reproductive mechanism.
### Mechanism
In sexual reproduction, hyphae of compatible mating types fuse (plasmogamy), nuclei may fuse (karyogamy), and meiosis follows to generate genetically recombined sexual spores; in asexual reproduction, spores are produced directly by mitosis from a single parent, yielding genetically identical (clonal) spores.
### Key determinants
Whether nuclear fusion and meiotic recombination occur is the determining factor separating sexual from asexual fungal spore formation; both routes nonetheless converge on producing a resistant, dispersible spore as the reproductive unit.
### Clinical significance
Asexual spores (conidia) are typically the infectious propagule inhaled or inoculated in most fungal infections, making the asexual reproductive pathway the one of most direct relevance to how fungal disease is usually acquired.

### Definition
A specific parasite is one restricted to a single host species, able to complete its life cycle only in that host -- a narrow host range that distinguishes it from non-specific (euryxenous) parasites able to infect multiple host species, and from unrelated classifications such as accidental parasitism or zoonotic transmission direction.

### Clinical significance
Host specificity has direct public-health relevance: a strictly human-specific parasite has no animal reservoir, which shapes control strategies (eradication is theoretically possible), unlike a non-specific parasite that can persist in animal reservoirs despite human-directed control measures.
