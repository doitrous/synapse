## summary

Whether flow is laminar or turbulent is predicted by the Reynolds number (turbulent above roughly 3000), flow velocity is highest at a vessel's centre and lowest in the high-cross-sectional-area capillary bed, and blood viscosity — chiefly set by haematocrit — rises with dehydration and falls with anaemia or hypoproteinaemia.

## sections
### Definition
Hemodynamics is the physics of blood flow through the circulation. Poiseuille's equation is its central relationship: the resistance a vessel offers to flow varies directly with blood viscosity and vessel length, and inversely with the radius raised to the fourth power. Blood flow itself follows F = ΔP / R — the pressure gradient divided by resistance.

### Mechanism
Because vessel length is essentially fixed and viscosity changes only slowly, radius is the one Poiseuille determinant the body actively varies — arteriolar dilatation sharply lowers resistance (by the fourth-power relationship) and so raises flow, both locally and into the downstream capillary bed; constriction does the reverse. Within a vessel, laminar flow forms a parabolic velocity profile — near zero at the wall, greatest at the centre — while across vessel types, velocity is set by the total cross-sectional area available to flow at that level, making the capillary bed, despite each capillary's narrow individual radius, the site of the lowest flow velocity because its combined cross-sectional area is largest. Whether flow stays laminar or becomes turbulent is predicted by the Reynolds number, which rises with velocity, diameter and blood density and falls with viscosity; flow is reliably laminar below roughly 2000 and reliably turbulent above roughly 3000.

### Key determinants
Vascular resistance and peripheral resistance are dominated by arteriolar radius, with viscosity and vessel length as secondary, more slowly changing contributors. Blood viscosity is set chiefly by haematocrit: dehydration (haemoconcentration) raises it, while anaemia and hypoproteinaemia lower it, and a lower viscosity in turn lowers resistance. Turbulence is favoured by a high Reynolds number — high velocity, wide diameter, low viscosity — and by partial vessel narrowing, which locally raises velocity.

### Clinical significance
Turbulent flow generates vibrations audible as murmurs and bruits, the clinical signature of the Reynolds-number physics underlying this topic. Anaemia and hypoproteinaemia lower blood viscosity and so lower peripheral resistance, while dehydration raises viscosity — both routes by which a patient's fluid and blood-composition state shifts resistance and flow through the same Poiseuille relationship taught here.

## hold_these
Resistance: direct with viscosity and length, inverse with radius^4 (Poiseuille). Radius is the physiologically variable determinant. F = ΔP/R; arteriolar dilatation raises flow; flow is directly proportional to diameter. Reynolds number predicts turbulence: laminar below ~2000, turbulent above ~3000. Velocity is near zero at the vessel wall, highest at the centre, and lowest in the capillaries (largest total cross-sectional area). Dehydration raises blood viscosity; anaemia and hypoproteinaemia lower it.

## lose_the_mark
Reversing the viscosity/length relationship to resistance (both are direct, not inverse). Treating length or viscosity, rather than radius, as the actively-controlled determinant. Assuming flow falls with arteriolar dilatation or wider diameter. Citing 200 or 2000 as the reliably-turbulent Reynolds threshold instead of ~3000. Assuming capillary flow velocity is high because capillaries are individually narrow. Assuming any compositional change raises viscosity, rather than specifically dehydration (raises) versus anaemia/hypoproteinaemia (lower).
