## summary
Interphase (G1, S, G2) takes up the great majority of the cell cycle, commonly 90% or more of total cycle time, while the four mitotic stages together occupy only a small remaining fraction.

## sections
### Definition
The cell cycle is interphase (G1, S, G2) followed by the four mitotic stages (prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase).

### Mechanism
Interphase is when the cell grows, does its ordinary metabolic work, and replicates its DNA in S-phase; mitosis proper — condensing and segregating the chromosomes — is comparatively brief.

### Key determinants
A "resting" cell seen between divisions under the microscope is in interphase, its longest and most metabolically active stage, not a dormant one.

### Clinical significance
Anticancer drugs that target rapidly dividing cells are most effective against tumours whose cells cycle quickly through interphase into repeated rounds of mitosis.

## hold_these
Interphase, not any single mitotic stage, is the longest phase of the cell cycle.

## lose_the_mark
Picking prophase (the longest of the four mitotic stages) as the cycle's longest phase overall — it is still far shorter than interphase as a whole.

## summary
S-phase doubles the cell's DNA content before its cytoplasm has grown further, so the DNA-to-cytoplasm ratio peaks right at the end of S-phase and falls gradually as the cytoplasm keeps growing through G2 and beyond.

## sections
### Definition
S-phase replicates DNA content from a single (46-s) to a doubled (46-d) chromatid state, without yet growing the cytoplasm further.

### Mechanism
The instant S-phase completes, the cell holds its full doubled DNA content within the smallest cytoplasmic volume it will have for the rest of the cycle — the highest DNA:cytoplasm ratio of the cycle.

### Key determinants
G2 and mitosis keep the same doubled DNA content, but cytoplasmic volume keeps growing through G2, so the ratio falls after its S-phase peak rather than rising further.

### Clinical significance
Flow cytometry measuring cellular DNA content exploits this same doubling to distinguish a cell's cycle phase (G1 vs S vs G2/M) in diagnostic and research settings.

## hold_these
The DNA-to-cytoplasm ratio is highest at the end of S-phase, once DNA content has doubled but before further cytoplasmic growth in G2 dilutes it again.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming the ratio keeps rising through G2 and mitosis because DNA content is still doubled there — cytoplasmic growth across that same span means the ratio itself falls after its S-phase peak.

## summary
The thyroid follicle, a colloid-filled sphere where thyroglobulin is made and stored, is lined by simple cuboidal epithelium at its normal resting activity state — flattening toward squamous when under-stimulated and rising toward columnar when strongly stimulated.

## sections
### Definition
A thyroid follicle is lined by a single epithelial layer enclosing a central colloid store.

### Mechanism
At normal activity the lining is simple cuboidal; the same cells flatten (squamous) when hypoactive and rise (columnar) when hyperactive.

### Key determinants
Follicular cell height is a marker of the gland's own stimulation level, not a fixed, unchanging feature.

### Clinical significance

## summary
Not every vesicle budded from the Golgi's mature (trans) face is exported: some are instead retained inside the cell as primary lysosomes, so the same trans-Golgi network feeds both the secretory and the lysosomal pathway.

## sections
### Definition
The Golgi apparatus has an immature (cis) entry face and a mature (trans) exit face, from which vesicles bud.

### Mechanism
Some trans-face vesicles become secretory vesicles, exported from the cell; others are instead retained internally as primary lysosomes, sacs of newly packaged hydrolytic enzymes.

### Key determinants
The Golgi is therefore central to both the secretory pathway and the lysosomal (digestive) pathway, not to secretion alone.

### Clinical significance
Defects in this Golgi sorting/packaging machinery underlie several inherited lysosomal storage and trafficking disorders.

## hold_these
Some vesicles budded from the Golgi's trans face are retained inside the cell as primary lysosomes rather than being exported.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming every vesicle that buds from the Golgi's exit face leaves the cell — some are retained internally as primary lysosomes instead.

## summary
Chromosomal abnormalities are numerical (an extra or missing whole chromosome, as in Down or Turner syndrome) or structural (a change to a chromosome's own architecture, as in Wolf syndrome's chromosome 4p deletion) — two different mechanisms, not one spectrum.

## sections
### Definition
Numerical abnormalities change the total chromosome count; structural abnormalities change a chromosome's own internal architecture through breakage and rejoining.

### Mechanism
Down (trisomy 21), Turner (45,XO), triple-X (47,XXX) and XYY are numerical; Wolf syndrome (4p deletion) and cri-du-chat (5p deletion) are structural.

### Key determinants
A structural abnormality can leave the total chromosome count normal (46), while a numerical one always changes it (45 or 47) — this count is the fastest way to tell the two categories apart on a karyotype.

### Clinical significance
Distinguishing structural from numerical abnormalities matters for genetic counselling: structural deletions/translocations carry different recurrence-risk and inheritance implications than numerical trisomies/monosomies, which mostly arise sporadically from meiotic nondisjunction.

## hold_these
Wolf (Wolf-Hirschhorn) syndrome, a partial deletion of chromosome 4's short arm, is a structural chromosomal abnormality, distinct from the numerical abnormalities Down, Turner, triple-X and XYY syndrome.

## lose_the_mark
Grouping every named chromosomal syndrome together without separating structural (architecture-altering) causes from numerical (count-altering) ones.

## summary
The small and large intestine are lined by simple columnar absorptive epithelium, whose brush-bordered surface cells maximise nutrient, water and electrolyte absorption — distinct from the stomach's secretory columnar lining and the oesophagus's protective squamous one.

## sections
### Definition
Simple columnar absorptive epithelium lines the intestine.

### Mechanism
Its surface (absorptive) cells carry an apical microvillous brush border that vastly increases absorptive surface area; goblet cells interspersed among them secrete protective mucus.

### Key determinants
The stomach's own simple columnar lining is secretory, not absorptive — "simple columnar" alone does not imply absorption; the intestine's brush border does.

### Clinical significance
Disorders that flatten or destroy this brush border, such as coeliac disease, directly impair nutrient absorption because they damage the very structure responsible for it.

## hold_these
Simple columnar absorptive epithelium lines the intestine.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming any simple columnar epithelium in the gastrointestinal tract must be absorptive — the stomach's is secretory, not absorptive.

Each taste cell within a taste bud extends short apical microvilli ("taste hairs") into the taste pore, the receptive surface that first contacts dissolved tastant molecules — not true cilia and not stereocilia.

## sections
### Definition
A taste bud's gustatory cells reach the epithelial surface at a small opening, the taste pore.

Each taste cell's apical surface carries short microvilli extending into the pore, maximising the receptive area exposed to dissolved tastants in saliva.

### Key determinants
Microvilli, not motile cilia and not stereocilia, are the taste cell's apical feature — a distinction the exam books test directly against those two look-alike surface specialisations.

## summary
The epidermis's fully keratinised horny layer (stratum corneum) is filled with soft keratin — pliable and continuously shed and replaced — distinct from the harder, more durable keratin of hair and nail.

## sections
### Definition
The horny layer (stratum corneum) is the anucleate, fully keratinised surface layer of the epidermis.

### Mechanism
Its flattened dead cells are filled with soft keratin, chemically and structurally distinct from the hard keratin of the hair shaft and nail plate.

### Key determinants
Soft keratin suits the epidermis's job — a flexible barrier constantly worn away and replaced from below — while hard keratin suits hair and nail's job of persisting and growing without piecemeal shedding.

### Clinical significance
Disorders of keratinisation (such as ichthyosis) alter this soft-keratin barrier's structure and function, producing abnormal scaling of the skin surface.

## hold_these
The epidermal horny layer contains soft keratin, distinct from the hard keratin of hair and nail.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming all keratin in the integument is the same substance, rather than distinguishing the epidermis's soft keratin from hair/nail's hard keratin.

## summary
The macrophage can sit as a fixed, resident histiocyte in a given organ's connective tissue, or actively wander to a site of injury as a free, chemotactically recruited phagocyte — a dual capacity that sets it apart from connective tissue cells that are always fixed or always free.

## sections
### Definition
Connective tissue cells are sometimes sorted as fixed (resident) or free (mobile).

### Mechanism
The macrophage uniquely can be either: a quiet, resident histiocyte, or an actively wandering phagocyte recruited by chemotactic signals to an injury or infection site.

### Key determinants
Cells such as the fibroblast and adipose cell are always fixed; the mast cell and plasma cell are always free; the macrophage alone moves between the two states.

### Clinical significance
This mobility is exactly what lets circulating monocytes convert into tissue macrophages and be recruited in large numbers to any site of injury or infection, central to the inflammatory and repair response.

## hold_these
The macrophage may exist as either a fixed (resident histiocyte) or a free (wandering) connective tissue cell.

## lose_the_mark
Sorting the macrophage into a single fixed-or-free category rather than recognising it can be either, depending on whether it is resident or actively wandering.

## summary
Melanin transferred from matrix melanocytes is deposited in the hair cortex, the thick middle layer of the hair shaft, which is what gives hair its colour — not the medulla, the cuticle, or the follicle's own sheaths.

## sections
### Definition
The hair shaft is built of medulla (thin central core), cortex (thick middle bulk) and cuticle (thin outer scale layer).

### Mechanism
Melanin granules, supplied by matrix melanocytes as the hair grows, are deposited within the cortex's keratin-filled cortical cells.

### Key determinants
The cuticle, the most visually obvious layer, carries no pigment of its own; colour comes specifically from the cortex beneath it.

### Clinical significance
Greying hair reflects declining melanocyte activity at the hair matrix, reducing the melanin deposited into each new segment of cortex as the hair grows.

## hold_these
Hair colour pigment is deposited in the hair cortex.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming the outer cuticle, the most visible layer, is where hair pigment sits — the cuticle has no pigment of its own.

## summary
The sperm tail is the human body's flagellum, a single motile process built on the same 9+2 axoneme as a cilium but present singly per cell and specialised to propel the whole cell forward, unlike the many shorter cilia of a ciliated epithelium.

## sections
### Definition
A flagellum and a cilium share the same internal 9+2 microtubule axoneme.

### Mechanism
A flagellum is a single long process that propels the whole cell (the sperm tail); cilia are numerous, shorter processes on a ciliated epithelium that move fluid or particles across a stationary surface.

### Key determinants
Number and job, not internal structure, separate a flagellum from cilia — the sperm tail is the human body's only flagellum, appearing singly, while ciliated cells carry many cilia each.

### Clinical significance
Primary ciliary dyskinesia, a defect of the dynein arms shared by cilia and flagella, causes both chronic sinopulmonary disease (immotile cilia) and male infertility (immotile sperm tails) in the same patient.

## hold_these
The sperm tail is the human body's flagellum, a single motile process built on the same axoneme as a cilium.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing a flagellum with cilia because both share the same 9+2 axoneme — the distinguishing feature is number and job, not internal structure.
