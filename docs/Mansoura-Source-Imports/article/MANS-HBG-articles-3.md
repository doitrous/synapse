## summary
A peripheral nerve is wrapped by three nested connective-tissue layers — endoneurium around each fibre, perineurium around each fascicle, epineurium around the whole trunk — and the endoneurium is the loose, innermost one.

## sections
### Definition
The endoneurium is the innermost of the three named connective-tissue coverings of a peripheral nerve, a thin sleeve of loose connective tissue that surrounds each individual nerve fibre and its Schwann cell sheath.

### Mechanism
The perineurium, a denser layer with its own epithelial-like cell layer, groups many endoneurium-wrapped fibres into a fascicle and forms the blood-nerve barrier; the epineurium, dense irregular connective tissue carrying the nerve's own blood vessels, binds the fascicles into the whole nerve trunk and attaches it to surrounding structures.

### Key determinants
Looseness (endoneurium) versus density (perineurium, epineurium), and the level wrapped (single fibre versus fascicle versus trunk) are the two features that separate the three layers on a section.

### Clinical significance
Nerve injury classification (Seddon/Sunderland) is built directly on which of these layers stays intact — an injury that spares the endoneurial tubes allows the best regrowth, while loss of the perineurium or epineurium's continuity worsens the prognosis for functional recovery.

## hold_these
Endoneurium wraps one fibre (loose CT); perineurium bundles fibres into a fascicle and is the blood-nerve barrier; epineurium binds fascicles into the trunk.

## lose_the_mark
Picking the perineurium when a question asks specifically about the covering of one single nerve fibre.

## summary
The eccrine sweat gland's excretory duct opens directly onto the free surface of the skin, unlike the apocrine sweat gland, whose duct opens into the upper hair follicle.

## sections
### Definition
The eccrine (merocrine) sweat gland is a simple coiled tubular gland, distributed over almost the whole body surface, whose straight duct pierces the epidermis and opens onto the skin's own surface.

### Mechanism
The duct's epidermal segment has no wall of its own, spiralling between keratinocytes to reach the surface, while its dermal segment is lined by a two-layered cuboidal duct epithelium that reabsorbs sodium from the primary secretion on the way out.

### Key determinants
Gland type (eccrine versus apocrine) fixes the opening site: eccrine ducts always reach the surface directly, apocrine ducts always open into a hair follicle above the sebaceous duct.

### Clinical significance
Because eccrine ducts open independently onto the surface across almost the whole body, they are the main route for thermoregulatory sweating; disorders that block this duct (miliaria) trap sweat at whichever epidermal level the blockage sits.

## hold_these
Eccrine duct opens onto the skin surface; apocrine and sebaceous ducts both open into the hair follicle instead.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming every sweat gland duct behaves the same way as the sebaceous duct and opens into the hair follicle.

## summary
The epidermis is stratified squamous keratinized epithelium, layered basal-spinous-granular-horny, and its spinous layer is built of polyhedral cells joined by desmosomal spines.

## sections
### Definition
The epidermis is classified as stratified squamous keratinized epithelium: many cell layers thick, flattening toward the surface, and capped by a dead, keratin-filled horny layer.

### Mechanism
From base to surface the epidermis is the basal (stratum basale, mitotically active), spinous (stratum spinosum, polyhedral cells with desmosomal spines), granular (stratum granulosum, keratohyalin granules) and horny (stratum corneum, anucleate keratin squames) layers, with a stratum lucidum added in thick skin.

### Key determinants
Keratinization (versus the non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium of wet surfaces) and the polyhedral, desmosome-spined shape of the spinous layer's cells are the two identifying features tested here.

### Clinical significance
Diseases that attack the desmosomes joining spinous-layer cells (pemphigus vulgaris) cause the cells to separate, acantholysis, producing intraepidermal blisters at exactly the level these spines hold together.

## hold_these
Epidermis = stratified squamous keratinized; spinous layer cells = polyhedral, joined by desmosomal spines.

## lose_the_mark
Describing the epidermis as non-keratinized, or the spinous layer's cells as flat or columnar rather than polyhedral.

## summary
A neuron's axon is the single process that conducts the impulse away from the cell body, the opposite direction from the multiple, tapering dendrites.

## sections
### Definition
The axon is a neuron's process, at most one per cell, of uniform diameter, that conducts the nerve impulse away from the cell body toward a synapse, muscle or gland.

### Mechanism
Dendrites receive signals at synapses on their branching, tapering surface and conduct them toward the cell body (decrementally, without an action potential in most cases); the axon then fires and propagates the action potential in the opposite direction, away from the body, often over a long, unbranching, myelin-wrapped length.

### Key determinants
Direction of conduction, not process count, length or branching, is the constant that identifies an axon across every neuron shape (multipolar, bipolar, pseudounipolar).

### Clinical significance
This one-way conduction is what lets a peripheral nerve be functionally, not just anatomically, mixed: sensory (afferent) axons carry impulses toward the CNS from a dendrite-like peripheral receptor process, while motor (efferent) axons carry impulses away from CNS cell bodies to muscle.

## hold_these
Axon = away from the cell body; dendrite = toward the cell body.

## lose_the_mark
Defining the axon by length or branching pattern instead of by the direction it conducts.

## summary
The lysosome is the membrane-bound, acid-hydrolase-filled organelle whose job is intracellular digestion of engulfed or worn-out material.

## sections
### Definition
The lysosome is a Golgi-derived, membrane-bound organelle packed with acid hydrolase enzymes that digest material inside the cell.

### Mechanism
A primary lysosome fuses with a phagosome (engulfed extracellular material) or an autophagosome (the cell's own aged organelles) to form a secondary lysosome, in which the acid hydrolases, active at the organelle's own low internal pH, break the contents down into reusable subunits.

### Key determinants
Acid phosphatase histochemistry visualises the lysosome by its own enzyme content, the feature that distinguishes it from the Golgi apparatus (packaging), the endoplasmic reticulum (synthesis) and ribosomes (translation).

### Clinical significance
Inherited lysosomal storage diseases (Tay-Sachs, Gaucher, Pompe) each result from a missing single lysosomal enzyme, so the undigested substrate that enzyme should have broken down instead accumulates inside the cell's lysosomes.

## hold_these
Lysosome = intracellular digestion, via acid hydrolase enzymes, demonstrated by acid phosphatase stain.

## lose_the_mark
Picking the residual body, the leftover product of incomplete lysosomal digestion, instead of the lysosome itself as the digesting organelle.

## summary
Every blood vessel, from the aorta down to a capillary, is lined by endothelium, a single layer of simple squamous epithelial cells.

## sections
### Definition
The endothelium is the simple squamous epithelial lining of the tunica intima, present in every blood vessel and continuous with the endocardium.

### Mechanism
The flat, thin endothelial cell minimises the diffusion distance for gases and solutes crossing between blood and tissue, and its smooth, non-thrombogenic apical surface keeps flowing blood from clotting against the vessel wall.

### Key determinants
Vessel size does not change the endothelium's classification: an artery, a vein and a capillary are all lined by the same simple squamous epithelium, differing instead in the wall layers outside it.

### Clinical significance
Endothelial injury or dysfunction is the first step of atherosclerosis, exposing the subendothelial connective tissue and triggering the inflammatory cascade that builds an atheromatous plaque.

## hold_these
Blood vascular endothelium = simple squamous epithelium, in every vessel regardless of size.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming a large, thick-walled vessel like the aorta must have a thicker epithelial lining than a capillary.

## summary
The Schwann cell wraps and myelinates one internode of one peripheral axon, the PNS counterpart of the CNS's oligodendrocyte.

## sections
### Definition
The Schwann cell is a neural-crest-derived glial cell that synthesises the myelin sheath of peripheral nervous system axons.

### Mechanism
A Schwann cell wraps its own plasma membrane repeatedly around a single segment (internode) of one axon; the compacted, lipid-rich wrapped layers form that internode's myelin, with a node of Ranvier, where the axon is exposed, between one Schwann cell's territory and the next.

### Key determinants
One Schwann cell myelinates one internode of one axon (a one-to-one PNS relationship), against one oligodendrocyte myelinating segments of several different axons at once in the CNS.

### Clinical significance
Guillain-Barré syndrome targets the Schwann cell's peripheral myelin (or the axon itself in its axonal variants), producing the ascending weakness of an acute peripheral demyelinating polyneuropathy, distinct from a central demyelinating disease like multiple sclerosis, which targets oligodendrocytes instead.

## hold_these
Schwann cell myelinates PNS axons, one cell per internode; oligodendrocyte myelinates CNS axons, one cell for several internodes across different axons.

## lose_the_mark
Naming the oligodendrocyte for a peripheral nervous system myelination question.

The centriole, too fine to resolve reliably with routine H&E, is demonstrated as a dense basophilic dot near the nucleus by iron hematoxylin.

## sections
### Definition
Iron hematoxylin is the special stain used to demonstrate the centriole, a small non-membranous organelle of nine microtubule triplets.

## summary
Every epithelial tissue is closely packed with little intercellular substance, rests on a basement membrane, is avascular, and regenerates continuously by mitosis.

## sections
### Definition
Epithelial tissue's defining general characters are closely packed cells with minimal intercellular substance, a basement membrane, avascularity, and continuous mitotic regeneration.

### Mechanism
The basement membrane separates epithelium from the underlying vascular connective tissue, so epithelial cells are nourished by diffusion rather than by their own blood supply; because epithelial surfaces face wear from the exterior, a cavity or a lumen, basal (or otherwise proliferative) cells divide throughout life to replace what is lost.

### Key determinants
Cell packing, the basement membrane, avascularity and continuous turnover together, not any one alone, are what mark a tissue as epithelial rather than connective.

### Clinical significance
This continuous regenerative capacity is exactly what most epithelial cancers exploit and what radiotherapy and chemotherapy toxicity targets, since rapidly dividing epithelial linings (gut, skin, mucosae) are collateral damage alongside the tumour.

Epithelium: closely packed cells, little intercellular substance, rests on basement membrane, avascular, continuous mitotic regeneration.

## summary
A dorsal root ganglion holds large, uniform pseudounipolar cell bodies fully capsuled by satellite cells with a poor blood supply; an autonomic ganglion holds smaller, size-varied multipolar cell bodies with a richer blood supply.

## sections
### Definition
A ganglion is a cluster of neuron cell bodies outside the CNS, classed as sensory (dorsal root) or autonomic by neuron type and structural features.

### Mechanism
Dorsal root ganglion neurons are pseudounipolar, uniform and large, each fully capsuled by flattened satellite cells, and relay sensory input through without synapsing on it; autonomic ganglion neurons are multipolar, smaller and size-varied, less completely capsuled, and do synapse, receiving a preganglionic fibre and sending out a postganglionic one.

### Key determinants
Neuron shape (pseudounipolar vs multipolar), cell body size uniformity, satellite-cell capsule completeness, and blood supply richness together identify which ganglion type is on a section.

### Clinical significance
Herpes zoster (shingles) reactivates from latency specifically in a dorsal root ganglion's sensory neuron cell bodies, producing a dermatomal rash that maps to the one ganglion's territory, a pattern that follows directly from the ganglion's sensory, segmentally-organised structure.

## hold_these
Dorsal root ganglion: pseudounipolar, uniform large cell bodies, complete satellite capsule, poor blood supply. Autonomic ganglion: multipolar, smaller varied cell bodies, richer blood supply, synapses.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming every peripheral ganglion looks alike regardless of whether it is sensory or autonomic.

## sections
### Definition
Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) is the routine, general-purpose stain combination used to examine essentially every histological section.

### Mechanism
Haematoxylin, a basic dye, binds acidic (basophilic) structures — chromatin-rich nuclei, RNA-rich rough ER — and colours them blue to purple; eosin, an acidic dye, binds basic (eosinophilic) structures — most cytoplasmic protein, collagen — and colours them pink to red.

## hold_these

Naming a special stain (Masson's trichrome, silver, Verhoeff's) when the question asks for the routine, general-purpose stain.

## summary
The aorta's tunica media is dominated by concentric elastic laminae, not collagen, letting the wall stretch and recoil with each heartbeat.

## sections
### Definition
Elastic fibres are the connective tissue fibre that dominates the wall of the aorta and other large elastic (conducting) arteries.

### Mechanism
Fenestrated elastic laminae, arranged concentrically and interleaved with smooth muscle, collagen and ground substance, build the elastic artery's tunica media, letting the wall distend during systole and recoil during diastole.

### Key determinants
Artery classification (elastic vs muscular) follows the dominant wall fibre: elastic arteries (aorta and major branches) are fibre-dominated by elastin, muscular arteries (most named arteries) by smooth muscle with proportionally less elastin.

### Clinical significance
Loss of elastic fibre integrity, as in Marfan syndrome's fibrillin defect or age-related elastin degradation, weakens the aortic wall's recoil and predisposes to aneurysm and dissection at exactly the layer this concept describes.

## hold_these
Aorta and large elastic arteries: elastic fibres dominate the tunica media, giving stretch and recoil (Windkessel effect).

## lose_the_mark
Naming collagen as the dominant fibre of a large elastic artery's wall.

## summary
The fibroblast is the connective tissue cell activated at a healing wound, proliferating and synthesising the new collagen that builds granulation tissue and the eventual scar.

## sections
### Definition
The fibroblast is the connective tissue cell specifically activated at a wound site during healing, synthesising new collagen and ground substance.

### Mechanism
Inflammatory and growth-factor signals from the injury activate resting, spindle-shaped fibroblasts, which proliferate, migrate into the wound, enlarge, and lay down the collagen and ground substance of granulation tissue, later remodelled into the mature scar.

### Key determinants
The fibroblast's synthetic role (new matrix) is distinct from the macrophage's phagocytic role (debris clearance) at the same wound, and from the mast cell, plasma cell and adipocyte's own unrelated functions among connective tissue's other cells.

### Clinical significance
Excess or dysregulated fibroblast activity produces a hypertrophic scar or keloid, while impaired fibroblast function (as in some connective tissue disorders or with corticosteroid use) delays wound healing and weakens the resulting scar.

## hold_these
Fibroblast = the CT cell activated at a wound during healing, synthesising new collagen and matrix.

## lose_the_mark

## summary
Meiosis I separates homologous pairs to leave 23 double (23-d) chromosomes; meiosis II separates sister chromatids to leave the final gamete's 23 single (23-s) chromosomes.

## sections
### Definition
Meiosis reduces a primary spermatocyte/oocyte's 46-d chromosome complement to the final gamete's haploid 23-s complement across two successive divisions.

### Mechanism
Meiosis I (reductional) separates the two homologous chromosomes of each pair, producing a secondary spermatocyte/oocyte with 23 chromosomes still made of two sister chromatids each (23-d); meiosis II (equational, like mitosis) then separates those sister chromatids, producing a final gamete with 23 single-chromatid chromosomes (23-s).

### Key determinants
Which division a cell has completed, first (23-d) or second (23-s), is what a question's specific wording ("daughter cell of the first meiotic division" versus "daughter cell of meiosis"/the final gamete) is asking to distinguish.

### Clinical significance
Non-disjunction, chromosomes or chromatids failing to separate properly, can occur at either meiotic division and is the mechanism behind most numerical chromosomal abnormalities, including the maternal meiosis I non-disjunction that most often produces Down syndrome.

## hold_these
Meiosis I product = 23-d (still doubled); meiosis II product = 23-s (final haploid gamete).

## lose_the_mark
Reading any daughter cell of meiosis as automatically 23-s, without checking whether the first or the second division is the one described.
