## summary
Meiosis, the reduction division, is restricted to the germ (sex) cells undergoing gametogenesis in the gonads; every somatic tissue divides by mitosis instead.

## sections
### Definition
Meiosis halves the chromosome number from diploid (46) to haploid (23) across two successive divisions. It occurs only in the germ-cell lineage of the gonads — oogonia/oocytes in the ovary, spermatogonia/spermatocytes in the testis — as part of gametogenesis.

### Mechanism
Somatic tissues (liver, intestinal epithelium, neurons, skin) either do not divide at all (as with mature neurons) or renew themselves by mitosis, producing two diploid daughter cells genetically identical to the parent. None of them undergo meiosis; only the specialised germ-cell lineage does, because only a gamete needs a halved chromosome number to restore the diploid count at fertilisation.

### Key determinants
The tissue asked about: gonadal germ cells (meiosis) versus any other actively renewing somatic tissue (mitosis only).

### Clinical significance
Errors during meiosis (nondisjunction) in gametogenesis are the origin of numerical chromosomal aneuploidies such as Down, Turner and Klinefelter syndromes — the reason meiosis's restriction to germ cells matters clinically, not just as a classification fact.

## hold_these
Meiosis is confined to the sex cells (gametogenesis) of the gonads; every other tissue listed as a distractor divides by mitosis, if it divides at all.

## lose_the_mark
Treating meiosis as one of two possible division types available to any actively dividing tissue, rather than a specialisation unique to the germ-cell lineage.

## summary
A motor (efferent) nerve fibre carries the impulse from the CNS out to a muscle or gland; a sensory (afferent) fibre carries it the opposite way, from a receptor to the CNS; a mixed nerve carries both together.

## sections
### Definition
Peripheral nerve fibres are classified by the direction in which they conduct the nerve impulse relative to the central nervous system.

### Mechanism
A motor (efferent) fibre conducts away from the brain or spinal cord towards an effector organ (skeletal, smooth or cardiac muscle, or a gland), driving contraction or secretion. A sensory (afferent) fibre conducts the opposite way, from a peripheral receptor towards the CNS. A mixed nerve, such as most named peripheral nerves, bundles both motor and sensory fibres together in the same trunk.

### Key determinants
The direction of conduction relative to the CNS — towards it (sensory/afferent) or away from it (motor/efferent) — is what a "mixed nerve" answer option does not itself specify, since it names a bundle of both.

### Clinical significance
This afferent/efferent distinction underlies the reflex arc and the classification of peripheral neuropathies as predominantly motor, sensory or mixed.

## hold_these
Motor (efferent) fibre: brain/spinal cord to muscle. Sensory (afferent) fibre: receptor to brain/spinal cord. Mixed nerve: both fibre types together, not a single direction.

## lose_the_mark
Choosing "mixed nerve" when the question specifies one single, defined direction of conduction (brain to muscle); a mixed nerve is a bundle of both directions, not the direction itself.

## summary
The neuron's cell body has one large, rounded, central, pale (vesicular) nucleus; Nissl substance (rough ER) fills the cell body and dendrites but is absent from the axon, and centrioles play no role in protein synthesis.

## sections
### Definition
The multipolar neuron's perikaryon (cell body) contains one nucleus that is large, rounded, centrally placed, with finely dispersed euchromatin (a "vesicular" or pale, open appearance) and a single prominent nucleolus, reflecting the cell's ongoing high transcriptional activity.

### Mechanism
Nissl substance — clumps of rough endoplasmic reticulum and free ribosomes responsible for the cell body's basophilia — fills the cell body and extends into the dendrites, but is absent from the axon hillock and the axon itself, which therefore relies on the cell body for its proteins, transported down the axon. Mitochondria are present and required throughout the cell body to meet its high metabolic demand. Centrioles, present in the immature, still-dividing neuroblast, are retained in the mature neuron mainly as basal bodies for structures such as cilia, not as machinery for protein synthesis, a function that belongs to ribosomes and rough ER.

### Key determinants
Location within the neuron — cell body and dendrites (Nissl-positive) versus axon (Nissl-negative) — is the discriminator most often tested against these four listed statements.

### Clinical significance
Chromatolysis, the dispersal and loss of Nissl substance from the cell body after axonal injury, is a histological marker of nerve cell reaction to axotomy, and depends on Nissl substance normally being a cell-body/dendrite feature to begin with.

## hold_these
Neuron nucleus: large, rounded, central, vesicular. Nissl substance: cell body and dendrites, not the axon. Mitochondria: present in the cell body. Centrioles: not involved in protein synthesis.

## lose_the_mark
Extending Nissl granules into the axon, or assigning centrioles a protein-synthesis role — both misassign features that belong to different neuronal compartments or organelles.
