## summary
The definitive (final) host is the host in which a parasite reaches sexual maturity -- either by sexual reproduction or, for asexually-reproducing parasites, by completing adult development.
## sections
### Definition
The definitive (final) host is the host in which a parasite reaches sexual maturity: either sexual reproduction takes place there, or -- for parasites that reproduce asexually -- the parasite completes its adult development there. This is the single defining criterion of the term.
### Mechanism
An intermediate host carries a larval or asexual developmental stage on the way to the infective stage, but the parasite does not become sexually mature there. A paratenic (transport) host merely carries the infective stage without any further development. A dead-end host is one in which the parasite survives but cannot complete its cycle or transmit onward -- humans are the dead-end host for many zoonotic parasites whose true definitive host is an animal.
### Key determinants
Identifying the definitive host is the first step in mapping any parasite life cycle, because it is where egg, oocyst or larval output for onward transmission actually originates. The dog is the definitive host for Toxocara canis and Echinococcus granulosus (both reach sexual maturity in the dog intestine); humans are the definitive host for the four species of human Plasmodium in the sense that the sexual (sporogonic) cycle actually completes in the Anopheles mosquito, illustrating that 'definitive host' always tracks the site of sexual-stage completion, whichever organism that turns out to be.
### Clinical significance
Public health measures aimed at breaking transmission (e.g. treating the definitive host, or preventing contact between definitive and intermediate hosts) depend on this identification being correct.
## hold_these
The definitive host is the host in which a parasite reaches sexual maturity (its adult stage), as distinct from the intermediate host (larval/asexual development), the paratenic host (transport only) and the dead-end host (cannot complete the cycle).
## lose_the_mark
Confusing the definitive host with the intermediate host, or assuming a host that merely carries the parasite (paratenic, dead-end) is where it matures.

## summary
Wire screening (mesh over windows, doors and eaves) is a mechanical mosquito-control measure that excludes the flying adult from a building; it does not act on the aquatic egg, larval or pupal stages.
## sections
### Definition
Wire screening is a mechanical control measure: mesh fitted over windows, doors and other openings physically prevents the winged, host-seeking adult mosquito from entering a building.
### Mechanism
Because the adult is the only mobile, flying stage, screening acts specifically on it. The egg, larval and pupal stages are all aquatic and immobile relative to a building opening, so they are addressed instead by source reduction (draining or removing breeding water), larvicides, larvivorous fish, or surface oiling -- measures that would have no effect on an already-emerged flying adult.
### Key determinants
Mosquito control is commonly taught by category: mechanical (screening, bed nets), chemical (larvicide, residual adulticide spraying), biological (larvivorous fish, Bacillus thuringiensis) and environmental/source reduction (drainage). Screening is the classic mechanical, adult-stage example within this scheme.
### Clinical significance
Adult-exclusion measures like screening reduce human-vector contact directly and are especially valuable where breeding-site elimination is impractical (e.g. widespread small water collections), complementing rather than replacing larval-stage control.
## hold_these
Wire screening is a mechanical control measure that excludes the flying adult mosquito from a building; it does not act on the aquatic egg, larval or pupal stages, which are controlled by different methods.
## lose_the_mark
Assuming any mosquito-control measure by default targets the larval/pupal stages; screening specifically targets the adult.

## summary
Trematodes characteristically require a freshwater snail as their first intermediate host, in which the miracidium develops through sporocyst and redia stages into cercariae -- a defining, snail-obligate step in fluke biology.
## sections
### Definition
Trematode (fluke) life cycles characteristically require a freshwater snail as the first intermediate host. The miracidium, hatching from an egg passed by the definitive host, penetrates the snail and develops through sporocyst and redia stages into free-swimming cercariae, the stage that goes on to infect the next host (directly, via a second intermediate host, or via aquatic vegetation, depending on the species).
### Mechanism
The miracidium, hatching from an egg passed by the definitive host, penetrates the snail and develops through sporocyst and redia stages into free-swimming cercariae, the stage that goes on to infect the next host (directly, via a second intermediate host, or via aquatic vegetation, depending on the species).
### Key determinants
Cestodes typically use arthropod (fleas, beetles) or vertebrate (cattle, pigs, fish) intermediate hosts rather than a snail. Nematodes either have no intermediate host (the soil-transmitted group) or use an arthropod/paratenic vertebrate host when one is needed. Intestinal and urogenital protozoa use no intermediate host at all, transmitting directly. The snail requirement is therefore specific to trematodes among these groups.
### Clinical significance
Because the snail step is obligatory, the geographic distribution of trematode infections (schistosomiasis, fascioliasis, clonorchiasis) tracks the distribution of the specific snail species each fluke needs -- a key reason snail-control (molluscicides, habitat modification) features in trematode-disease control programmes.
## hold_these
Trematodes require a freshwater snail as the first intermediate host to complete their life cycle, developing through sporocyst and redia stages into cercariae -- unlike cestodes (arthropod/vertebrate intermediate hosts) or protozoa (no intermediate host).
## lose_the_mark
Assuming any helminth with an intermediate host uses a snail; the snail requirement is specific to trematodes.

## summary
Flagella (Mastigophora) and pseudopodia (Sarcodina) are both locomotor organelles: the flagellum propels by a whip-like beat, and pseudopodia produce amoeboid movement by cytoplasmic flow.
## sections
### Definition
The flagellum is a long, whip-like organelle whose rhythmic beat propels a flagellate protozoan (Mastigophora, e.g. Giardia, Trypanosoma, Trichomonas) through its fluid environment. Pseudopodia ('false feet') are temporary cytoplasmic extensions that a Sarcodina protozoan (e.g. Entamoeba) projects and flows into, producing amoeboid movement. Both are locomotor organelles, and each is the group-defining feature of its respective classical protozoan group.
### Mechanism
The flagellum's whip-like beat and the pseudopodium's cytoplasmic flow are both direct mechanical drivers of cell displacement, in contrast to the coordinated beating of many short cilia over the cell surface used by Ciliophora (ciliates, e.g. Balantidium coli) -- the third locomotor mode in the classical scheme.
### Key determinants
Apicomplexa/Sporozoa (e.g. Plasmodium) have no distinct locomotor organelle at any stage and move, when motile, by gliding, which is why they form the fourth classical group rather than sharing one of the three named locomotor modes.
### Clinical significance
Locomotor organelle is the organising feature of the classical (pre-molecular) protozoan classification scheme still taught at this level: naming the organelle a protozoan uses to move identifies which of the four classical groups it belongs to, which in turn frames how each group's medically important genera and diseases are taught.
## hold_these
Flagella (Mastigophora) and pseudopodia (Sarcodina) both function as locomotor organelles -- the flagellum by a whip-like beat, pseudopodia by cytoplasmic flow -- parallel to ciliary movement in Ciliophora.
## lose_the_mark
Assigning flagella or pseudopodia a non-locomotor function (nutrition, secretion, excretion, respiration), or confusing which classical group each organelle defines.

## summary
Ivermectin (oral or topical), alongside topical permethrin, is a first-line therapy for scabies, killing Sarcoptes scabiei by opening invertebrate-specific glutamate-gated chloride channels.
## sections
### Definition
Ivermectin is a first-line therapy for scabies. Given orally or as a topical preparation, it kills the causative mite, Sarcoptes scabiei, by opening glutamate-gated chloride channels that are specific to invertebrate nerve and muscle cells (mammalian cells lack this channel type, giving the drug its favourable safety profile).
### Mechanism
Given orally or as a topical preparation, ivermectin kills the causative mite, Sarcoptes scabiei, by opening glutamate-gated chloride channels that are specific to invertebrate nerve and muscle cells (mammalian cells lack this channel type, giving the drug its favourable safety profile).
### Key determinants
Ivermectin's scabicide action (an acaricide effect via invertebrate chloride channels) is distinct from antiprotozoal drugs (metronidazole), antimalarials (chloroquine) and anthelminthics (mebendazole), none of which act on Sarcoptes scabiei.
### Clinical significance
Ivermectin's ease of single/repeat oral dosing makes it especially useful in outbreak settings -- institutions, crowded households, or where topical application to every affected person is impractical -- and it is used alongside topical permethrin as first-line options in current practice.
## hold_these
Ivermectin (oral or topical), along with topical permethrin, is a first-line scabies therapy, killing Sarcoptes scabiei via invertebrate-specific glutamate-gated chloride channels.
## lose_the_mark
Selecting an antiprotozoal, antimalarial or anthelminthic drug for scabies therapy by pattern-matching a 'drug question' rather than the specific mite target.

## summary
Myiasis is fly-larva invasion of tissue; its specific (obligatory) form requires living tissue, unlike semi-specific (dead-tissue) or accidental forms, and it is diagnosed by directly finding the larvae.
## sections
### Definition
Myiasis is the invasion of human (or other vertebrate) tissue, organs or body cavities by the larvae (maggots) of flies (order Diptera).
### Mechanism
Specific (obligatory) myiasis is caused by larvae -- such as Dermatobia hominis, Cordylobia anthropophaga and Hypoderma -- that require living tissue to complete development and actively invade it. Semi-specific (facultative) myiasis flies normally breed in decaying organic matter but can opportunistically invade already-dead or necrotic wound tissue. Accidental (pseudo-) myiasis follows incidental ingestion or inhalation of eggs/larvae, with no active tissue invasion required for the larva's development.
### Key determinants
Myiasis is diagnosed directly by finding, and where possible extracting and identifying, the causative larvae within the affected tissue -- a direct visual/parasitological identification rather than an indirect laboratory test such as serology, skin testing or blood culture, which are unnecessary given the organism is macroscopic and visible. Species identification of the recovered larva can further guide management, since specific-myiasis species may need complete extraction while semi-specific species are often managed with wound care alone.
### Clinical significance
Recognising which category a case falls into (specific vs semi-specific vs accidental) affects both prognosis and management, since specific/obligatory myiasis larvae will continue to damage living tissue if not removed, while semi-specific and accidental forms are generally self-limited once the underlying wound or exposure is addressed.
## hold_these
Myiasis is fly-larva invasion of tissue; its specific (obligatory) form requires and invades only living tissue (unlike semi-specific/dead-tissue or accidental forms), and it is diagnosed by directly finding the larvae.
## lose_the_mark
Confusing specific (living-tissue, obligatory) myiasis with semi-specific (dead-tissue, facultative) or accidental (incidental ingestion/inhalation) myiasis, or reaching for an indirect test (serology, skin test) instead of direct larval identification.

## summary
Parasitism is a host-organism relationship in which the parasite benefits while the host is harmed, distinguishing it from commensalism (host unaffected) and mutualism (both benefit).
## sections
### Definition
Parasitism is defined as a relationship in which the parasite benefits -- gaining nutrition, shelter, or both -- while the host is harmed to some degree by the association.
### Mechanism
Commensalism describes a relationship in which one organism benefits while the host is unaffected (neither harmed nor helped). Mutualism describes a relationship in which both organisms benefit. Parasitism sits at the harm-with-benefit end of this spectrum, distinguished from both by the host actually being harmed.
### Key determinants
Whether an organism is classed as a parasite, commensal or mutualist turns on whether the host is measurably harmed by the association, not merely on where the organism lives or what it consumes -- the same body site can host organisms in any of the three relationships depending on this criterion.
### Clinical significance
Classifying a host-organism relationship correctly (parasitism vs commensalism vs mutualism) underlies how a given organism's clinical significance is taught -- a commensal organism found on/in a host is not itself a target for treatment the way a true parasite causing harm is.
## hold_these
Parasitism is a relationship in which the parasite benefits at the expense of the host, who is harmed -- distinct from commensalism (host unaffected) and mutualism (both benefit).
## lose_the_mark
Confusing parasitism with commensalism (no harm) or mutualism (mutual benefit), or with a simple carrier state where the host merely carries the organism.

## summary

## sections
### Definition
House flies (Musca domestica) transmit bacterial pathogens mechanically: contaminated mouthparts, legs and body surface pick up organisms from faeces, refuse or wound discharge and physically deposit them on food or open wounds.
### Mechanism
In mechanical transmission, the pathogen undergoes no development or multiplication inside the vector -- it is simply carried and deposited, as with the house fly and bacteria. ### Key determinants

### Clinical significance
Mechanical transmission by house flies is implicated in the spread of enteric bacterial diseases such as typhoid fever, cholera and bacillary dysentery, particularly where sanitation is poor and flies have ready access to both faecal material and food.
## hold_these
House flies transmit bacterial pathogens mechanically -- by physical carriage on contaminated mouthparts, legs and body surface, with no development of the pathogen inside the fly -- unlike biological transmission (e.g. mosquito-malaria).

## summary
The protozoan nucleus directs reproduction, the cytostome handles food intake, the cytopyge handles egestion of waste, and the ectoplasm gives rise to surface locomotor structures such as cilia.
## sections
### Definition
As in other cells, the protozoan nucleus houses the genetic material and directs cell division (binary fission, or the sexual/asexual processes some protozoa undergo). The cytostome ('cell mouth') is a fixed or semi-permanent opening in the pellicle of some protozoa (notably ciliates such as Balantidium coli) through which food particles are ingested by phagocytosis, forming a food vacuole.
### Mechanism
The cytopyge ('cell anus') is a fixed point on the pellicle, also seen classically in ciliates, through which undigested food-vacuole residue is expelled -- the counterpart, at the 'output' end, to the cytostome's role in intake. The ectoplasm, the clear, gel-like outer layer of protozoan cytoplasm immediately beneath the cell membrane/pellicle, gives rise to surface locomotor and protective organelles, including cilia (as in Ciliophora), flagella and pseudopodia, and the pellicle itself.
### Key determinants
Not all protozoa have a cytostome -- amoebae ingest food by engulfing it with pseudopodia anywhere on the cell surface -- so its presence itself helps identify ciliates and some flagellates. Together, a fixed cytostome and cytopyge give ciliates a directional, almost tract-like processing of food, unlike the more diffuse ingestion and egestion of amoebae. Nuclear number, size and chromatin arrangement (vesicular vs compact) are also used taxonomically, helping distinguish protozoan genera and pathogenic from non-pathogenic amoebae.
### Clinical significance
Recognising which organelle a described structure or function belongs to (nucleus, cytostome, cytopyge, or an ectoplasm-derived surface structure) underlies both taxonomic identification of protozoa under the microscope and basic-science exam questions that test organelle function in isolation.
## hold_these

## sections
### Definition
House dust mites (Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus and D. farinae) do not bite or infest humans directly.
### Mechanism
Proteins in mite faecal pellets and body fragments are potent inhaled allergens. In sensitised individuals, these allergens trigger an IgE-mediated hypersensitivity response, producing allergic rhinitis, and contributing more broadly to allergic asthma and atopic dermatitis -- an allergenic mechanism rather than direct parasitism or infestation.

### Clinical significance
Dust-mite avoidance measures (vacuuming, allergen-impermeable covers, humidity reduction) and, where needed, antihistamines or intranasal corticosteroids target this allergenic mechanism directly, rather than treating an infestation that is not actually present.

House dust mites do not infest humans directly; inhaled allergen from their faecal pellets and body fragments causes IgE-mediated allergic rhinitis in sensitised individuals.
## lose_the_mark
Attributing a direct-infestation disease (scabies, pediculosis) or a toxin-mediated disease (tick paralysis) to house dust mites instead of their true allergenic mechanism.

## summary
Tick paralysis is an ascending flaccid paralysis caused by a salivary neurotoxin from certain feeding female ticks, characteristically resolving rapidly once the tick is removed.
## sections
### Definition
Tick paralysis is an ascending flaccid paralysis caused by a neurotoxin secreted in the saliva of certain feeding female ticks (e.g. Dermacentor species), which blocks neuromuscular transmission.
### Mechanism
The paralysis characteristically resolves rapidly once the feeding tick is found and removed -- a distinctive feature that helps differentiate tick paralysis from other, non-reversible or infection-mediated causes of acute paralysis.
### Key determinants
Tick paralysis (a direct toxin effect) is a different mechanism from tick-transmitted infectious diseases (e.g. Lyme disease from Ixodes ticks, or Texas cattle fever from Boophilus ticks in cattle), which require antimicrobial treatment rather than simple tick removal. Paralysis is not a typical feature of mosquito, flea, house-dust-mite or louse exposure; among common arthropods encountered in this context, the tick is specifically associated with this salivary-neurotoxin paralytic syndrome.
### Clinical significance
A careful skin examination for an embedded, feeding tick is a key step in any unexplained acute ascending paralysis, since tick removal alone is often curative and avoids unnecessary investigation for other neurological causes.
## hold_these
Tick paralysis is an ascending flaccid paralysis from a salivary neurotoxin of certain feeding female ticks, resolving rapidly once the tick is removed.
## lose_the_mark
Confusing tick paralysis (direct toxin effect, reversible on removal) with a tick-transmitted infectious disease requiring treatment, or attributing paralysis to a different arthropod.

## sections
### Definition
Sarcoptes scabiei is an obligate ectoparasite of human skin. The fertilised female burrows into the epidermis (the stratum corneum), creating linear burrows in which she lays eggs.
### Mechanism
Burrows are most often found at the finger webs, wrists, elbows and other skin-fold sites, producing the intense itching characteristic of scabies (worse at night, classically), which reflects a delayed-type hypersensitivity response to the mite, its eggs and its faecal pellets within the burrow.
### Key determinants
Because Sarcoptes scabiei's habitat is confined strictly to the epidermis, no other body site (conjunctiva, nasal passages, gut) is relevant to this particular mite's life cycle, distinguishing it from arthropods with different habitats or feeding sites.
### Clinical significance
Diagnosis and treatment target the skin directly (e.g. skin scraping for mite/egg/faecal-pellet identification, topical or oral scabicide therapy), consistent with the mite's epidermis-confined habitat.
## hold_these
Sarcoptes scabiei is an obligate ectoparasite of human skin, burrowing into the epidermis (stratum corneum) to lay eggs, most often at finger webs, wrists and elbows.
## lose_the_mark
