## summary
Lymphatic vessels, like veins, have one-way valves; a small set of tissues (teeth, cartilage, CNS) have none at all. Lymph capillaries are the first step in the collecting chain, uniting to form lymph vessels.

## sections
### Valves and the avascular exceptions
Lymphatic vessels rely on one-way valves to keep flow moving toward the venous system, the same way veins do; arteries and capillaries have none. A small set of tissues have no lymphatic vessels at all: the teeth, cartilage, the eye, bone marrow and the CNS.

### The collecting chain's first step
Lymph begins in blind-ended capillaries, which unite to form lymph vessels, which pass through nodes, unite into trunks, and finally drain via the thoracic or right lymphatic duct.

## hold_these
Veins and lymphatics have valves; arteries/capillaries do not. Teeth, cartilage and the CNS lack lymphatic vessels. Capillaries unite to form vessels, the collecting chain's first step.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming every tissue has lymphatic vessels, or skipping straight from capillaries to trunks/ducts.

## summary
The tonsils are unencapsulated lymphoid tissue, not a true (encapsulated) lymphoid organ; the thymus, by contrast, is a true lymphoid organ that involutes from puberty onward.

## sections
### Organ vs tissue
True lymphoid organs (lymph nodes, spleen, thymus) have a capsule; the tonsils and MALT lack one and are therefore classed as lymphoid tissue, not organs.

### Thymic involution
The thymus is largest and most active in fetal life and childhood, then progressively involutes from puberty onward, replaced gradually by fat; this puberty-linked shrinkage is distinctive to the thymus among lymphoid organs.

## hold_these
Tonsils lack a capsule (tissue, not organ). Thymus involutes from puberty onward.

## lose_the_mark
Treating any lymphocyte collection as a "lymphoid organ" regardless of capsule.

## summary
The right upper limb (and right head/neck/thorax) drains via the right lymph trunk to the small right lymphatic duct; everywhere else, including the pelvis via the cisterna chyli, drains via the much larger thoracic duct.

## sections
### The right-sided exception
Lymph from the right side of the head/neck, right upper limb and right thorax drains via the right lymphatic duct into the right venous angle, a territory of roughly one-quarter of the body.

### The thoracic duct's wider reach
The thoracic duct, the body's largest lymphatic vessel, begins as the cisterna chyli (both lower limbs and pelvis via the lumbar trunks), then collects the left thorax and the left side of the head, neck and upper limb, emptying at the left venous angle.

## hold_these
Right upper limb/head/neck/thorax = right lymphatic duct. Everywhere else, including the pelvis, = thoracic duct.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming a symmetrical "left lymphatic duct" exists; the left side drains via the thoracic duct itself.

## summary
The breast drains predominantly to the axillary lymph nodes, so the axillary group is the first examined clinically and staged surgically in suspected breast cancer.

## sections
### Why axillary nodes come first
Around three-quarters of breast lymphatic drainage goes to the axillary nodes, the rest to the parasternal nodes; because breast cancer spreads first via this lymphatic route, the axillary group is examined first and staged surgically (sentinel node biopsy or clearance).

## hold_these
Breast cancer: examine the axillary lymph nodes first.

## summary
The external carotid artery's first branch is the superior thyroid artery; its two terminal branches, arising behind the neck of the mandible, are the superficial temporal and maxillary arteries.

## sections

## summary
The internal jugular vein begins at the sigmoid sinus and terminates by joining the subclavian vein to form the brachiocephalic vein; the external jugular vein, a separate superficial route, terminates directly in the subclavian vein.

## sections
### Internal jugular vein
Begins at the jugular foramen as the continuation of the sigmoid sinus, descends in the carotid sheath, and terminates behind the clavicle by uniting with the subclavian vein to form the brachiocephalic vein (the venous angle).

### External jugular vein
Runs superficially down the neck and pierces the deep fascia above the clavicle to drain directly into the subclavian vein, a separate, more superficial route from the internal jugular vein.

## hold_these
IJV: sigmoid sinus in, brachiocephalic vein out. EJV: drains into the subclavian vein.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing the IJV's cranial origin (sigmoid sinus) with its lower termination (brachiocephalic vein), or confusing the IJV's and EJV's separate termination points.

## sections

The retromandibular vein forms in the parotid gland from the superficial temporal and maxillary veins, mirroring the external carotid's own arterial terminal division; the maxillary artery itself originates at that same division, behind the neck of the mandible.

Ascending pharyngeal = medial-origin exception. Retromandibular vein = superficial temporal + maxillary vein union. Maxillary artery origin = neck of mandible.

## lose_the_mark
