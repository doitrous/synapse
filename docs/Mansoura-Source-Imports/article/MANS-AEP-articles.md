## summary
Flexion decreases the angle at a joint and extension increases it, each measured from the neutral anatomical position. The AEP question bank tests the pair directly on a worked knee example, so the definition — a change in joint angle, not a fixed direction in space — is what a question is really asking for.

## sections
### Definition
Flexion is the movement at a joint that decreases the angle between the two bones on either side of it, typically bending the joint away from the anatomical position. Extension is the opposite movement, increasing that angle, typically straightening the joint back towards — or past — the anatomical position.

### Worked example
Increasing the angle between the thigh and the leg at the knee is extension; decreasing that angle is flexion. The same logic applies at other hinge joints, such as the elbow.

### What flexion/extension is not
The pair is not the same as abduction/adduction (movement towards or away from a reference line) or supination/pronation and inversion/eversion (rotations or tilts of the forearm and foot). Each of those is a separate named movement.

## hold_these
Flexion decreases the joint angle and extension increases it — direction in space is not the defining feature.
Flexion/extension is distinct from abduction/adduction, rotation, and supination/pronation.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming flexion always means bending forward. At the shoulder, flexion swings the arm forward, but at the knee, flexion bends the leg backward — the angle change is what defines it, not the direction.

## summary
Abduction moves a part of the body away from its reference line and adduction brings it back towards that line. The AEP bank tests both directions of the pair, on the limb as a whole (reference: the median plane) rather than on the fingers or toes (reference: the axis of the hand or foot).

## sections
### Definition
Abduction is the movement of a part of the body away from a reference line: for a whole limb this is the median plane of the body, and for the fingers or toes it is instead the axis of the hand (the middle finger) or the foot (the second toe). Adduction is the opposite movement, bringing the part back towards that same reference line.

### What the pair is not
Abduction/adduction is distinct from medial/lateral rotation, which turns a limb about its own long axis without changing its distance from the reference line, and from flexion/extension, which changes the angle at a joint rather than distance from a reference line.

## hold_these
Abduction moves away from the reference line, adduction moves towards it.
The reference line is the median plane for a whole limb, but the axis of the hand/foot for the fingers/toes.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing abduction/adduction with medial/lateral rotation — rotation turns the limb about its own axis, it does not move the limb towards or away from the reference line.

## summary
Supination turns the forearm so the palm faces forwards (or up); pronation turns it so the palm faces backwards (or down). The AEP bank ties this directly to the anatomical position, which is defined with the forearm fully supinated.

## sections
### Definition
Supination is the rotation of the forearm at the radioulnar joints that turns the palm to face forwards (or, with the elbow flexed, upwards). Pronation is the opposite rotation, turning the palm to face backwards (or downwards).

### Why the anatomical position is supinated
The anatomical position is defined with the forearm fully supinated, which is why the palm faces forwards in that reference posture — the one deliberate part of the position, since every other element is simply how a person naturally stands.

### What the pair is not
Supination/pronation is a rotation of the forearm about its long axis, distinct from flexion/extension of the elbow, which changes the joint angle rather than which way the palm faces.

## hold_these
Supination turns the palm forwards/up; pronation turns it backwards/down.
The anatomical position requires a fully supinated forearm.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing supination/pronation with flexion/extension of the elbow — a forearm can be fully extended and either supinated or pronated; the elbow angle and the palm direction are independent.

## summary
Dorsiflexion lifts the foot towards the shin at the ankle; plantarflexion points it away, as in standing on tiptoe. The AEP bank's own worked example is exactly this tiptoe scenario, so the two must be told apart from inversion/eversion, a separate tilting movement.

## sections
### Definition
Dorsiflexion is the movement at the ankle joint that lifts the dorsum (top) of the foot up towards the shin, decreasing the angle between them, as when walking on the heels. Plantarflexion is the opposite movement, pointing the foot downwards away from the shin, as when pressing a pedal or rising onto the toes.

### Worked example
Standing on tiptoe (lowering the sole and heel while the forefoot stays down) requires plantarflexion at the ankle.

### What the pair is not
Dorsiflexion/plantarflexion moves the whole foot up or down at the ankle, distinct from inversion/eversion, which instead tilts the sole medially or laterally without lifting or lowering it.

## hold_these
Dorsiflexion lifts the foot towards the shin; plantarflexion points it away.
Standing on tiptoe is plantarflexion, not inversion.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing dorsiflexion/plantarflexion with inversion/eversion — both are foot movements, but one raises/lowers the whole foot and the other tilts the sole.

Ipsilateral means on the same side of the body as a stated reference point, structure or lesion. Contralateral means on the opposite side of the body from that reference.

## summary
Compact bone dominates the shaft (diaphysis) of a long bone; cancellous (spongy) bone, under a thin compact shell, dominates each end (epiphysis). The AEP bank tests both halves of this distribution, so neither region should be assumed to be built of just one bone tissue.

## sections
### Definition
Within a long bone, the diaphysis (shaft) is built as a thick tube of compact bone surrounding the central medullary cavity, making compact bone the dominant tissue there. The epiphysis (each expanded end) is, by contrast, chiefly cancellous (spongy) bone internally, wrapped in only a thin outer shell of compact bone.

### Why this distribution recurs
This reversal — compact-dominant shaft, spongy-dominant ends — echoes the short and flat bone shape classes, which are likewise built as spongy bone under a thin compact shell (short bones) or as spongy diploe between two compact plates (flat bones).

### What this is not
This is a statement about where each bone tissue sits within a single long bone, distinct from the six whole-bone shape classes (long, short, flat, pneumatic, irregular, sesamoid), which classify an entire bone rather than its internal regions.

## hold_these
The diaphysis is mainly compact bone; the epiphysis is mainly cancellous (spongy) bone under a thin compact shell.
This shaft-versus-end pattern is not the same question as which of the six shape classes a whole bone belongs to.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming a long bone is uniformly compact bone throughout — only the shaft is predominantly compact, and each end is predominantly spongy.

## summary
Synovial joints are grouped into six types by the shape of their articulating surfaces, and each shape predicts the movement the joint allows. The AEP question bank tests this scheme directly on the knee, which by shape is a condylar joint.

## sections
### The six shapes
Plane joints have flat, gliding surfaces (e.g. the intertarsal joints). Hinge joints are uniaxial, allowing flexion/extension only (e.g. the elbow, the ankle). Pivot joints are uniaxial for rotation (e.g. the radio-ulnar joints). Condylar joints have rounded condyles on one bone against flatter surfaces on the other, giving mainly flexion/extension with a little added rotation (e.g. the knee). Saddle joints have reciprocally concave-convex surfaces and are biaxial (e.g. the carpometacarpal joint of the thumb). Ball-and-socket joints are multiaxial, a rounded head in a cup-shaped socket (e.g. the shoulder, the hip).

### The knee as a condylar joint
The knee's femoral condyles articulate with the flatter tibial condyles, which classifies it as condylar rather than a simple hinge — a distinction that matters because it is what allows the knee its small extra rotation once flexed. A related, more detailed corpus record instead calls the knee a "modified-hinge" joint; both descriptions are used in different texts for the same joint, and this article follows the printed exam key (condylar).

## hold_these
Six shape-based types: plane, hinge, pivot, condylar, saddle, ball-and-socket.
The knee is condylar; a pure hinge such as the elbow moves in one plane only.

## lose_the_mark
Treating "condylar" and "hinge" as interchangeable. A condylar joint like the knee adds a small rotation a true hinge cannot perform.

### Simple, compound, complex
A simple joint has two articulating bones (e.g. the hip). A compound joint has more than two bones meeting at one joint (e.g. the elbow: humerus, radius and ulna). A complex joint additionally contains an intra-articular disc or meniscus dividing the cavity, whatever its bone count — the knee is the standard example, its two menisci dividing the femorotibial compartments.

### Why the knee, not the elbow
The elbow has three bones but no meniscus, so it is compound, not complex. The knee's menisci are what earn it the "complex" label, on top of — not instead of — its bone count.

## hold_these
Simple = 2 bones; compound = more than 2 bones; complex = has an intra-articular disc/meniscus.
The knee is complex because of its menisci, not just because of how many bones meet there.

## lose_the_mark
Calling any multi-bone joint "complex". The elbow is compound (3 bones, no meniscus); only a disc or meniscus makes a joint complex.

## summary
A joint is the point of union between two or more bones, permitting or restricting movement between them. Every finer classification of joints — by tissue, by shape, by bone count — describes this same basic union in more detail.

## sections
### Definition
A joint, or articulation, is the point of union between two or more bones, or between bone and cartilage, and this union permits or restricts the movement possible between the parts it connects.

### Why this matters
Naming a component found within some joints, such as a cushion or meniscus, is not the same as defining a joint. The definition is about the union between bones itself, not any one structure inside it.

## hold_these
A joint = the point of union between two or more bones, permitting or restricting movement between them.

## lose_the_mark
Describing a joint by a component (a cushion, a disc) rather than stating the union between bones.

## summary
The zona pellucida is shed before the blastocyst implants, so it is not one of the structures implantation depends on. Implantation instead needs a secretory-phase endometrium and both trophoblast layers.

## sections
### Where the zona pellucida's job ends
The zona pellucida blocks polyspermy at fertilisation and holds the dividing blastomeres together through cleavage. By the time the blastocyst reaches the endometrium it has already shed this coat (hatching), so an intact zona pellucida is not present at implantation.

### What implantation actually needs
A receptive, secretory-phase (progestational) endometrium, and a trophoblast already differentiated into an inner cytotrophoblast and an outer, invasive syncytiotrophoblast — the layer that erodes the endometrium and opens the implantation site.

## hold_these
The zona pellucida is shed before implantation; it is not a requirement for implantation.
Implantation requires a secretory-phase endometrium and both trophoblast layers.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming a structure present at fertilisation is still present at implantation. The zona pellucida's role ends at cleavage, well before implantation.

## summary
The syncytiotrophoblast secretes HCG from very early in implantation, maintaining the corpus luteum and giving every standard pregnancy test its target hormone.

## sections
### Source and action
The syncytiotrophoblast, the outer, invasive trophoblast layer, secretes human chorionic gonadotropin (HCG) from very early in implantation onward. HCG signals the maternal corpus luteum to keep producing progesterone, maintaining the decidua of pregnancy instead of allowing it to break down.

### Why it matters clinically
Because HCG rises early and is detectable in maternal blood and urine, it is the hormone every standard pregnancy test detects, well before the mature placenta has formed.

## hold_these
The syncytiotrophoblast secretes HCG.
HCG maintains the corpus luteum and is what a pregnancy test detects.

## lose_the_mark
Attributing HCG to the mature placenta as a whole, or to the cytotrophoblast, instead of naming the syncytiotrophoblast specifically.
