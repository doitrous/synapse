## summary
The Gell and Coombs scheme sorts every hypersensitivity reaction into one of four types by mechanism, not by trigger. The department's own L1 microbiology revision questions test this classification directly on worked examples — pollen allergy, penicillin allergy and Rh(D) haemolytic disease of the newborn — so the mechanism, not the substance, is what a question is really asking for.

## sections
### Definition
Hypersensitivity is an exaggerated or inappropriate immune response that damages the host's own tissue. The Gell and Coombs scheme classifies every such reaction into one of four types by the mechanism responsible, independent of what triggered it.

### Type I - immediate (IgE-mediated)
Antigen-specific IgE binds to high-affinity receptors on mast cells and basophils. When antigen cross-links two adjacent IgE molecules, the cell degranulates within minutes, releasing histamine and other preformed mediators. Pollen allergy and immediate drug allergy (for example to penicillin) are both type I by this mechanism, whatever the antigen.

### Type II - cytotoxic (antibody against a fixed antigen)
IgG or IgM antibody binds directly to an antigen that is fixed to a cell surface or tissue. Complement activation or antibody-dependent phagocytosis then destroys that cell. Rh(D) haemolytic disease of the newborn is the department's worked type II example: maternal anti-D antibody crosses the placenta and binds directly to the D antigen on the fetal red cell surface.

### Type III - immune complex
Antibody binds soluble antigen in the circulation rather than a fixed cell-surface target, forming immune complexes that deposit in vessel walls and tissue. Complement activation at the deposition site drives the inflammation, not destruction of a single antigen-bearing cell.

### Type IV - delayed (cell-mediated)
No antibody is involved. Sensitised T lymphocytes react directly against antigen, and the reaction develops over 24-72 hours rather than minutes. Contact dermatitis and the tuberculin skin test are classic type IV reactions.

### Reading a worked example
The type is read off the mechanism, not the trigger: penicillin hypersensitivity and pollen hypersensitivity are stated together as "both IgE-mediated" in the department's own revision set precisely because the substance differs but the mechanism (type I) does not.

## hold_these
Type is decided by mechanism (antibody class, fixed antigen vs immune complex, antibody vs T-cell), never by which substance triggered the reaction.
Rh(D) haemolytic disease of the newborn is the type II worked example in this module, not type III.

## lose_the_mark
Calling any drug or food reaction "type I" by reflex instead of checking whether IgE-mediated immediate degranulation is actually the stated mechanism.

## summary
Visceral leishmaniasis (kala-azar) is transmitted by the sand fly's promastigote form, lives inside human macrophages as the amastigote, and is diagnosed by finding that amastigote on a stained tissue smear. The department's own Parasitology-HIS question set tests this vector/form/diagnosis triad repeatedly, alongside the clinical picture (hepatosplenomegaly, pancytopenia) and first-line drugs.

## sections
### Definition
Visceral leishmaniasis (kala-azar) is a systemic protozoal infection, in the Mediterranean basin (including Egypt) usually caused by Leishmania infantum, that infects the reticuloendothelial system throughout the body.

### Mechanism
The female sand fly (genus Phlebotomus in the Old World) transmits Leishmania by injecting the promastigote form — flagellated, found in the vector's gut and in NNN culture medium. Once phagocytosed by a human macrophage, the promastigote transforms into the amastigote — non-flagellated, obligate intracellular, found throughout the reticuloendothelial system (spleen, liver, bone marrow, lymph nodes).

### Key determinants
The amastigote in a Leishman- or Giemsa-stained tissue smear (bone marrow, spleen or liver aspirate) is the diagnostic stage. The Leishmanin (Montenegro) skin test — injecting killed promastigotes intradermally — detects past infection (positive 6-8 weeks after cure) rather than active disease, and is typically negative during active infection because of the marked depression of cell-mediated immunity that visceral leishmaniasis itself causes.

### Clinical significance
Untreated visceral leishmaniasis causes hepatosplenomegaly, generalised lymphadenopathy, and pancytopenia (normocytic normochromic anaemia + leucopenia + thrombocytopenia) from marrow hyperplasia replacing normal haemopoietic tissue. Skin changes include early hyperpigmentation (giving the disease its "black fever" name) and, after apparent cure, post-kala-azar dermal leishmaniasis (PKDL). Systemic parenteral therapy — sodium stibogluconate (Pentostam) or amphotericin B — is first-line; miltefosine is the drug given orally.

## hold_these
Promastigote = sand fly/culture form (flagellated); amastigote = human macrophage/diagnostic form (non-flagellated).
The Leishmanin skin test detects past infection, not active disease, and is negative during active infection.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing this vector (female sand fly) with malaria's (Anopheles mosquito) or filariasis's (Culex mosquito) — a repeated distractor pattern in this department's own bank.

## summary
Bancroftian filariasis (Wuchereria bancrofti) is transmitted by the Culex mosquito; adult worms live in the lymphatics, and the acute-vs-chronic split in its clinical picture follows directly from whether the living/dying worm is irritating a vessel (acute) or years of obstruction and fibrosis have set in (chronic, elephantiasis). The department's own Parasitology-HIS question set tests this vector/site/mechanism triad repeatedly, along with the nocturnal-periodicity diagnosis rule.

## sections
### Definition
Bancroftian filariasis is a chronic lymphatic infection with the nematode Wuchereria bancrofti, whose adult worms live in the lymphatic vessels and nodes, mainly of the lower limbs and genital organs.

### Mechanism
The Culex mosquito transmits Wuchereria bancrofti by injecting infective filariform larvae. Adult worms establish themselves in the lymphatics, where mating produces circulating microfilariae that show nocturnal periodicity (more numerous in peripheral blood 10 PM-2 AM).

### Key determinants
Acute manifestations — lymphangitis, lymphadenitis, filarial fever — come from mechanical irritation, worm metabolites/toxins and secondary bacterial infection while the worm is alive. Chronic manifestations — lymphoedema, hydrocele, elephantiasis — follow years of lymphatic obstruction and fibrosis, driven by worms blocking the lumen, endothelial proliferation, and fibrosis of the lymphatics and nodes themselves, not by circulating microfilariae directly. Tropical pulmonary eosinophilia (TPE) is a distinct, hypersensitivity-driven (type I) manifestation from exaggerated immune response to microfilarial antigens, causing dyspnoea, cough and asthmatic attacks.

### Clinical significance
Knott's concentration method recovers microfilariae in low-density infections; ultrasound can show the live adult worm's movement in the scrotal lymphatics (the "filarial dance sign"); and calcified dead worms may be visible on plain X-ray. Diethylcarbamazine (Hetrazan) is the drug of choice for microfilariae and TPE, unlike for elephantiasis, which does not respond to it once fibrosis has set in.

## hold_these
Adult W. bancrofti worms live in the lymphatics; chronic elephantiasis comes from obstruction/fibrosis, not directly from circulating microfilariae.
Diagnostic blood collection is timed to 10 PM-2 AM for nocturnal periodicity.

## summary
The mature erythrocyte is a biconcave disc about 7.5 micrometres across with a mean corpuscular volume of about 87-95 cubic micrometres, whose major job is carrying oxygen. It has no mitochondria, uses carbonic anhydrase for CO2 handling, and keeps potassium — not sodium — as its chief intracellular cation. This module's own physiology bank and its Priority-4 scanned exam bank both test this same bundle of "characteristics of the RBC" facts repeatedly.

## sections
### Definition
The mature erythrocyte is a biconcave, anucleate disc with an average diameter of about 7.5-7.8 micrometres and a mean corpuscular volume (MCV) of about 87-95 cubic micrometres (femtolitres).

### Mechanism
Its major function is the carriage of oxygen from the lungs to the tissues, bound to haemoglobin. It also contributes to CO2 carriage (partly as carbamino-haemoglobin) and to blood's acid-base buffering and viscosity, but these are secondary to its oxygen-carrying role. The biconcave shape gives a larger surface area than an equivalent sphere would, which is part of why it aids rather than limits gas exchange, and lets the cell fold and deform through capillaries narrower than its own resting diameter without rupturing.

### Key determinants
The mature erythrocyte has no nucleus and no mitochondria — its energy comes from anaerobic glycolysis, not oxidative phosphorylation. It contains carbonic anhydrase, which speeds the interconversion of CO2 and bicarbonate. Potassium is its chief intracellular cation, maintained against the plasma gradient by the membrane's sodium-potassium pump.

### Clinical significance
These size and biochemistry facts are the baseline a student compares against when a clinical stem describes an abnormal red cell — a smaller-than-normal MCV points toward microcytic anaemia (iron deficiency, thalassaemia), and impaired carbonic anhydrase or membrane pump function underlies several of the red cell enzymopathies and membranopathies covered later in the curriculum.

## hold_these
Diameter ~7.5 μm, MCV ~87-95 fL; major function is oxygen carriage.
No mitochondria (anaerobic glycolysis); carbonic anhydrase present; potassium is the chief intracellular cation.

## lose_the_mark
Treating biconcavity as reducing surface area — it increases it relative to a sphere of the same volume, which is why it aids gas exchange.

## summary
ALA synthase is the mitochondrial enzyme that condenses glycine with succinyl-CoA to start haem synthesis, and it is the pathway's rate-limiting step. The department's own Biochemistry Notes and Important MCQ sections test this enzyme's identity directly and repeatedly, distinct from ALA dehydratase (the next, cytosolic step) and ferrochelatase (the final, mitochondrial step).

## sections
### Definition
ALA synthase (delta-aminolevulinic acid synthase) is the mitochondrial enzyme that catalyses the first, rate-limiting step of haem synthesis.

### Mechanism
ALA synthase (delta-aminolevulinic acid synthase) condenses glycine with succinyl-CoA to form delta-aminolevulinic acid (ALA) — the first and rate-limiting step of the pathway. ALA then moves to the cytosol, where ALA dehydratase converts it to porphobilinogen, and further cytosolic steps build up through uroporphyrinogen and coproporphyrinogen. The pathway returns to the mitochondrion for its final steps, ending with ferrochelatase inserting iron into protoporphyrin IX to complete haem. The main site of haem synthesis is the bone marrow (the erythroid series), with the liver as a secondary site.

### Key determinants
Because ALA synthase sets the pace of the whole pathway, it is the enzyme haem itself feeds back on to switch production down when haem is abundant — this is what makes it "rate-limiting" rather than simply "first."

### Clinical significance
Lead poisoning blocks the pathway at both ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase — not ALA synthase itself — which is why lead poisoning, like iron deficiency, produces a microcytic anaemia, though by enzyme inhibition rather than substrate deficiency.

## hold_these
ALA synthase = mitochondrial, first step, rate-limiting.
ALA dehydratase (cytosolic, second step) and ferrochelatase (mitochondrial, final step) are the two enzymes lead blocks — do not confuse either with ALA synthase.

## lose_the_mark
Naming ALA dehydratase when asked for the rate-limiting/regulatory enzyme — that is ALA synthase, the first step, not the second.

Iron deficiency has its own clinical signature (koilonychia, angular stomatitis, pica) distinct from the neurological picture of B12 deficiency, and their absence in an anaemic patient should redirect a workup toward B12. The department's Pharmacology Important MCQ section, marked "Power Qs" (likely drawn from an external question bank), tests this differentiation and the routes of parenteral iron preparations.

## sections
### Definition
Iron deficiency and vitamin B12 deficiency both cause anaemia and pallor, but each carries its own additional clinical signature that lets a clinician tell them apart without waiting for laboratory confirmation.

### Mechanism
Iron deficiency anaemia's own clinical signs go beyond pallor: koilonychia (spoon-shaped, brittle nails), angular stomatitis (cracking at the mouth corners), and pica (craving non-food substances, including ice — "pagophagia"). Vitamin B12 deficiency instead produces neurological and cognitive symptoms (progressive weakness, fatigue, confusion, and in more advanced disease subacute combined degeneration of the cord) that iron deficiency does not.

### Key determinants
Their absence in an anaemic patient, especially an elderly one with progressive weakness, fatigue and confusion, should raise vitamin B12 deficiency instead of iron deficiency as the higher-priority differential.

### Clinical significance
Oral iron is first-line for iron-deficiency anaemia. When parenteral therapy is needed, the preparations differ in route: iron sucrose is given intravenously only, while iron dextran can be given either intramuscularly or intravenously.

## hold_these
No koilonychia/angular stomatitis in an anaemic, confused elderly patient points away from iron deficiency and toward B12 deficiency.
Iron sucrose = IV only; iron dextran can be IM or IV.
