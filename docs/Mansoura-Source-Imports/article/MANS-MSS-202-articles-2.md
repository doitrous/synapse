## summary
The brachial artery ends in the cubital fossa, medial to the biceps tendon, which is exactly where a stethoscope is placed to auscultate blood pressure.

## sections
### Definition
The brachial artery continues the axillary artery from the lower border of teres major and terminates in the cubital fossa, opposite the neck of the radius, by dividing into the radial and ulnar arteries.

### Mechanism
In the cubital fossa the brachial artery lies medial to the biceps tendon, deep to the bicipital aponeurosis, which separates it from the superficial median cubital vein used for venepuncture. This superficial, palpable position at the elbow is where a blood pressure cuff is applied around the arm and a stethoscope diaphragm is placed to listen for the Korotkoff sounds as the cuff deflates.

### Key determinants
Position relative to the biceps tendon (medial) and the bicipital aponeurosis (deep to it) fix the brachial artery's auscultation point; the radial artery, by contrast, is used only for the palpatory pulse check, not the auscultatory reading itself.

### Clinical significance
Correct placement over the brachial artery in the cubital fossa is what makes a manual blood pressure reading reliable; the cuff's lower edge is set a few centimetres proximal to this point to leave room for the stethoscope.

## summary
Unlike quadriceps, sartorius and pectineus, psoas major is innervated directly by lumbar plexus root branches high in the abdomen, not by the femoral nerve in the thigh.

## sections
### Definition
Psoas major arises from the T12-L5 vertebral bodies and discs and the lumbar transverse processes, joins iliacus to form iliopsoas, and inserts on the lesser trochanter to flex the hip.

### Mechanism
Small branches from the ventral rami of L1-L3 supply psoas major directly while they are still high in the abdomen, before the femoral nerve has formed as a distinct trunk; the other anterior-thigh muscles instead receive their supply from the femoral nerve once it has descended into the thigh.

### Key determinants
The distinguishing fact is timing and location of innervation, not muscle group membership — psoas major is anatomically and developmentally grouped with the lumbar plexus roots rather than with the femoral nerve's thigh-level branches.

### Clinical significance
A lesion high in the lumbar plexus (rather than a distal femoral nerve injury) can selectively weaken psoas major's contribution to hip flexion while sparing femoral-nerve-supplied muscles, or vice versa.

## summary
A young child pulled by the hand with the arm extended and pronated can sublux the radial head through the annular ligament — a soft-tissue injury with a normal X-ray.

## sections
### Definition
Nursemaid's elbow (pulled elbow) is subluxation of the radial head out from under the annular ligament, typically in children under five years old.

### Mechanism
A longitudinal traction force on the extended, pronated arm — a caregiver pulling or swinging the child by the hand — pulls the radial head partly out through the distal part of the thin, loosely-attached annular ligament, which becomes trapped between the radial head and the capitulum.

### Key determinants
Age (the annular ligament is thinner and looser in young children) and mechanism (traction on an extended, pronated arm) are the determining features; the injury is a soft-tissue subluxation, not a fracture.

### Clinical significance
The child refuses to use the arm, holding it slightly flexed and pronated, with no swelling and a normal X-ray; diagnosis is clinical, and treatment is a reduction manoeuvre (supination-flexion or hyperpronation), not casting.

## summary
Tennis elbow is tendinopathy at the common extensor origin on the lateral epicondyle; standard teaching implicates extensor carpi radialis brevis, though this source's printed key names longus.

## sections
### Definition
Lateral epicondylitis (tennis elbow) is overuse tendinopathy at the common extensor origin on the lateral epicondyle of the humerus.

### Mechanism
Repetitive wrist extension and grip, as in a backhand stroke, stresses the common extensor origin, which gives rise to extensor carpi radialis brevis, extensor digitorum, extensor digiti minimi and extensor carpi ulnaris. Extensor carpi radialis longus arises separately, higher up on the lateral supracondylar ridge, above the common extensor origin.

### Key determinants
Origin site is the distinguishing anatomical fact: brevis shares the common extensor origin implicated in tennis elbow, while longus's higher origin places it outside that structure — the reason standard teaching names brevis specifically.

### Clinical significance
Pain is reproduced by resisted wrist extension with the elbow extended; this record notes that the source question's printed key names extensor carpi radialis longus, which the printed-keys-stand rule preserves even though it differs from the textbook-standard answer.

## summary
The ulnar nerve crosses the wrist superficial to the flexor retinaculum through Guyon's canal, unlike the median nerve and flexor tendons, which pass deep to it.

## sections
### Definition
Guyon's canal is the superficial passage at the wrist through which the ulnar nerve and ulnar artery reach the hand, bounded by the pisiform medially and the hook of hamate laterally.

### Mechanism
The flexor retinaculum roofs the deep carpal tunnel, through which the median nerve and the long flexor tendons pass; the ulnar nerve and artery instead cross superficial to the retinaculum through Guyon's canal, a separate and more superficial route.

### Key determinants
Depth relative to the flexor retinaculum is the distinguishing feature: superficial (ulnar nerve/artery, Guyon's canal) versus deep (median nerve and flexor tendons, carpal tunnel).

### Clinical significance
A wrist laceration confined to the superficial layer can injure the ulnar nerve alone, sparing the median nerve and flexor tendons beneath the retinaculum; deep compression at Guyon's canal produces ulnar, not median, sensorimotor loss.

## summary
Gripping a piece of paper between adducted fingers tests the palmar interossei, which adduct the fingers and are supplied by the ulnar nerve's deep branch.

## sections
### Definition
The palmar interossei adduct the fingers toward the middle finger's axis (PAD), while the dorsal interossei abduct them (DAB); both are supplied by the deep branch of the ulnar nerve.

### Mechanism
The paper test asks the patient to grip paper between adjacent, adducted fingers while the examiner tries to pull it free; resisting that pull is a finger-adduction action, isolating palmar interosseous function.

### Key determinants
The action tested (adduction versus abduction) is what distinguishes the paper test (palmar interossei) from a test of finger spreading (dorsal interossei) or of thumb adduction (Froment's sign, adductor pollicis) — all three are ulnar-nerve-deep-branch muscles but test different, specific actions.

### Clinical significance
Weakness on the paper test points to ulnar nerve deep branch dysfunction, part of the broader intrinsic-hand-muscle weakness pattern seen in ulnar nerve lesions.

## summary
Skeletal muscle is supplied by A-alpha fibres, the largest and fastest-conducting class of peripheral nerve fibre.

## sections
### Definition
Peripheral nerve fibres are classified by diameter, myelination and conduction velocity into A (alpha, beta, gamma, delta), B and C groups; A-alpha fibres are the largest and most heavily myelinated, conducting fastest.

### Mechanism
A-alpha fibres carry both the efferent output of alpha motor neurons to extrafusal skeletal muscle fibres and the afferent input of the primary (Ia) muscle spindle and Golgi tendon organ endings, matching a fast, reliable signal to the job of triggering coordinated motor unit contraction.

### Key determinants
Fibre size and myelination set conduction speed and functional role: A-alpha for motor/proprioceptive signalling, smaller A-gamma for intrafusal spindle motor fibres, and the still-smaller A-delta and C fibres for pain and temperature.

### Clinical significance
Selective loss of large, fast fibres (as in some neuropathies) impairs motor strength and proprioception disproportionately to pain and temperature sensation, which depend on the smaller fibre classes.

## summary
Lambert-Eaton syndrome is an autoimmune, often paraneoplastic disorder caused by antibodies against presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channels, distinct from myasthenia gravis.

## sections
### Definition
Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome (LEMS) is caused by autoantibodies against P/Q-type voltage-gated calcium channels on the presynaptic nerve terminal at the neuromuscular junction.

### Mechanism
Reduced presynaptic calcium influx lowers acetylcholine vesicle release with each impulse; because successive stimuli let residual calcium accumulate and partly overcome the antibody's effect, repetitive nerve stimulation shows an incremental (facilitating) compound muscle action potential, opposite to myasthenia gravis's decremental pattern.

### Key determinants
Over half of LEMS cases are paraneoplastic, most often to small cell lung cancer, whose tumour cells express the same channel and are thought to trigger the antibody response.

### Clinical significance
Proximal weakness with autonomic features (dry mouth, constipation) and depressed reflexes that transiently return after exercise distinguish LEMS from myasthenia gravis, which worsens rather than improves with sustained activity.

## summary
Calmodulin and troponin C are structurally related calcium-binding proteins, but they trigger contraction through different mechanisms in smooth versus striated muscle.

## sections
### Definition
Troponin C (thin-filament calcium sensor in skeletal and cardiac muscle) and calmodulin (cytoplasmic calcium sensor activating myosin light-chain kinase in smooth muscle) both belong to the EF-hand family of calcium-binding proteins.

### Mechanism
Calcium-bound troponin C moves tropomyosin off the actin-myosin binding site directly, a thin-filament switch; calcium-bound calmodulin instead activates a kinase that phosphorylates the myosin head, a thick-filament switch — different downstream mechanisms despite the shared calcium-binding fold.

### Key determinants
Structural homology (shared EF-hand ancestry) is distinct from mechanistic identity — the two proteins are related in structure but act on opposite sides of the cross-bridge cycle.

### Clinical significance
Understanding this distinction explains why smooth muscle has no troponin regulating its thin filament at all, while striated muscle has no calmodulin-MLCK regulation of its myosin heads under normal conditions.

## summary
Malignant hyperthermia is a RyR1 mutation, triggered by volatile anaesthetics and succinylcholine, that causes uncontrolled sarcoplasmic calcium release and a metabolic crisis.

## sections
### Definition
Malignant hyperthermia is a rare, autosomal-dominant pharmacogenetic disorder, most often caused by a mutation in the ryanodine receptor 1 (RyR1) gene on the sarcoplasmic reticulum.

### Mechanism
The mutated channel is triggered abnormally by volatile inhalational anaesthetics and succinylcholine, and stays open uncontrollably, flooding the cytoplasm with calcium; the muscle responds with sustained contracture and a massive rise in metabolic rate as fibres try to pump the excess calcium back out.

### Key determinants
The trigger (anaesthetic/succinylcholine exposure) and the genetic defect (RyR1 mutation) together determine onset; without the specific pharmacologic trigger, carriers are usually asymptomatic.

### Clinical significance
Muscle rigidity, tachycardia, hypercapnia and a rapid severe rise in temperature are the hallmark features; treatment is immediate dantrolene, which blocks the ryanodine receptor directly, alongside stopping the trigger and cooling.
