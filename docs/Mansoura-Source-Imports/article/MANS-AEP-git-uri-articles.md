## summary
The three major salivary glands drain through ducts that open at different sites inside the mouth: the parotid duct opens into the vestibule, while the submandibular and sublingual ducts open onto the floor of the mouth.

## sections
### Two different openings
The parotid gland's duct (Stensen's duct) pierces buccinator and opens into the vestibule of the mouth, typically opposite the upper second molar tooth. The submandibular gland's duct (Wharton's duct) and the sublingual gland's ducts instead open onto the floor of the mouth proper, on either side of the lingual frenulum.

### Why the distinction matters
A question naming "vestibule of the mouth" as a duct's termination is describing the parotid duct; one naming the floor of the mouth or oral cavity proper is describing the submandibular or sublingual duct.

## hold_these
Parotid duct: opens into the vestibule (opposite the upper 2nd molar). Submandibular and sublingual ducts: open onto the floor of the mouth.

## lose_the_mark
Swapping the parotid duct's vestibule termination with the submandibular duct's floor-of-mouth termination.

## summary
The large intestine runs cecum (lower right) to ascending colon, right colic flexure, transverse colon, left colic (splenic) flexure (upper left), descending colon (left side), and sigmoid colon (lower left) — a quadrant map the AEP bank uses to localise cancer vignettes.

## sections
### The route around the abdomen
The large intestine begins at the cecum in the right iliac fossa, ascends as the ascending colon, turns at the right colic (hepatic) flexure into the transverse colon, turns again at the left colic (splenic) flexure — in the upper left abdomen — into the descending colon, and ends as the sigmoid colon in the lower left.

### Reading a quadrant vignette
A tumour in the upper left abdominal cavity points to the left colic flexure or the start of the descending colon; one confined to the left side generally points to the descending colon; one in the lower right points to the cecum.

## hold_these
Cecum: lower right. Left colic (splenic) flexure: upper left, transition to descending colon. Descending colon: left side generally. Sigmoid colon: lower left.

## lose_the_mark
Treating the left colic flexure's specific upper-left position as identical to the descending colon's more general left-sided course, or misplacing the cecum outside the lower right.

## summary
Sacculations (haustra), produced by the three taeniae coli, are a gross feature of the large intestine and are absent from the small intestine, appendix and rectum.

## sections
### How haustra form
The colon's outer longitudinal muscle is gathered into three bands, the taeniae coli, shorter than the gut tube itself, which pucker the wall into a series of pouches called sacculations or haustra.

### What lacks them
The small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, ileum), the vermiform appendix and the rectum have no taeniae coli and so lack the sacculated appearance, a feature that helps distinguish large from small bowel.

## hold_these
Sacculations (haustra): produced by the taeniae coli, seen along the colon, absent from the small intestine, appendix and rectum.

## lose_the_mark
Attributing sacculations to a small-intestine segment, the appendix or the rectum instead of the colon.

## summary
The small intestine's three parts are the duodenum (fixed, most proximal), the jejunum (proximal ~2/5 of the mobile bowel) and the ileum (distal ~3/5, both the longest and most distal segment).

## sections
### Three parts, one direction
Chyme leaves the stomach into the duodenum, the fixed first part of the small intestine, then the freely mobile jejunum and finally the ileum before the cecum.

### Jejunum vs ileum
The jejunum forms the proximal roughly 2/5 of the mobile small bowel and the ileum the distal roughly 3/5, making the ileum both the more distal and the longer of the two segments.

## hold_these
Duodenum: fixed, most proximal. Jejunum: proximal ~2/5 of the mobile bowel. Ileum: distal ~3/5, longest and most distal.

## lose_the_mark

## summary
The pancreatic head sits within the duodenum's C-shaped curve, so cancer or disease of the pancreatic head characteristically affects the duodenum.

## sections
### A tight fit
The pancreas's head lies cradled within the concavity of the duodenum's C-loop, with the bile duct passing through or immediately behind it before both drain into the duodenum's second part.

### Clinical consequence
A mass or cancer in the pancreatic head classically compresses or invades the duodenum (and the bile duct running through it), rather than the jejunum or transverse colon.

Pancreatic head: cradled in the duodenal C-loop; its disease characteristically affects the duodenum.

## summary
The liver produces bile, which drains via the bile duct into the duodenum at the hepatopancreatic ampulla, alongside the pancreatic duct.

## sections
### Production and drainage
Hepatocytes in the liver produce bile continuously; it is concentrated and stored in the gallbladder, then carried by the common bile duct, which joins the pancreatic duct and opens into the duodenum's second part at the major duodenal papilla.

### Where it terminates
The bile duct terminates in the duodenum, not the stomach, jejunum or ileum, so the duodenum is the segment that receives both bile and pancreatic secretions together.

## hold_these
Liver: produces bile. Bile duct: terminates in the duodenum.

## lose_the_mark

## summary
The pharynx is the shared pathway for both food and air, connecting the nose and mouth above to the esophagus and larynx below.

The pharynx runs from the skull base to the cricoid cartilage (C6); air travelling from nose or mouth to the larynx and trachea, and food travelling from the mouth to the esophagus, both cross through it, unlike the larynx, trachea, esophagus or bronchi, each dedicated to only one function.

## summary
The trigone is the smooth triangular area on the inner aspect of the bladder base, bounded by the two ureteric openings and the internal urethral orifice.

## sections
### A smooth triangle on the base
The bladder base's inner surface carries a smooth-walled triangular area, the trigone, in contrast to the folded (rugose) mucosa lining the rest of the bladder.

### Its three corners
The trigone's corners are the two ureteric orifices, where the ureters enter obliquely, and the single internal urethral orifice, where the urethra begins — so the trigone both lies on the bladder base and receives the ureteric openings.

## summary
The nephron is the functional unit of the kidney, comprising a renal corpuscle and a system of tubules that together form urine.

## sections
### One unit, one job
Each kidney holds roughly a million nephrons, each a renal corpuscle (glomerulus plus Bowman's capsule) feeding a tubule system (proximal convoluted tubule, loop of Henle, distal convoluted tubule) that reabsorbs and secretes before draining into a collecting duct.
