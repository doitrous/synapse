## summary
The brainstem is built of three parts stacked between the spinal cord and the cerebrum: the medulla oblongata (lowest), the pons (middle) and the midbrain (highest). The AEP bank tests both ends of this stack directly, asking for the "lower" and "upper" parts by name.

## sections
### The three parts, bottom to top
The medulla oblongata is the lowest part of the brainstem, continuous below with the spinal cord at the foramen magnum. The pons lies above the medulla, forming the middle part. The midbrain is the highest part, continuous above with the diencephalon and cerebrum.

### Why the order matters
A question naming "the lower part of the brainstem" is asking for the medulla; one naming "the upper part" is asking for the midbrain. The pons sits between them as the unnamed middle part, and keeping the bottom-to-top order (medulla, pons, midbrain) fixed in memory prevents the two ends being swapped.

## hold_these
Medulla = lowest, continuous with the spinal cord. Midbrain = highest, continuous with the cerebrum. Pons = the middle part between them.

## lose_the_mark
Swapping medulla and midbrain when only "upper" or "lower" is asked, without the pons named as the reference middle point.

## summary
The brain has four CSF-filled ventricles: two lateral ventricles (one per cerebral hemisphere), a third ventricle (diencephalon) and a fourth ventricle (between pons/medulla and cerebellum). The AEP bank tests the lateral ventricle's own location and the four-ventricle set as a whole.

## sections
### The lateral ventricle
Each cerebral hemisphere contains one lateral ventricle, a C-shaped cavity that is the largest of the brain's four ventricles. "The cavity of the cerebral hemisphere" names the lateral ventricle specifically.

### The full chain
The two lateral ventricles drain through the interventricular foramina into the single third ventricle in the diencephalon. The third ventricle continues through the cerebral aqueduct in the midbrain into the fourth ventricle, which lies between the pons/medulla and the cerebellum and opens into the subarachnoid space.

### What "ventricles" is not
Ventricles are distinct from cisterns (subarachnoid CSF pools outside the brain substance) and venous sinuses (which carry blood, not CSF).

## hold_these
Four ventricles total: two lateral (cerebral hemisphere), one third (diencephalon), one fourth (pons/medulla/cerebellum region). The lateral ventricle is the cerebral hemisphere's own cavity.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing ventricles with cisterns or sinuses — only the four named ventricles are the CSF-filled cavities inside the brain substance itself.

## summary
Most cortical folds vary between people, but four sulci are constant enough to serve as landmarks: central, lateral, parieto-occipital and calcarine. The AEP bank tests the calcarine sulcus, on the medial occipital lobe, as an example of this set.

## sections
### The four landmark sulci
The central sulcus separates the frontal and parietal lobes. The lateral sulcus separates the temporal lobe from the frontal/parietal lobes. The parieto-occipital sulcus separates the parietal from the occipital lobe. The calcarine sulcus lies on the medial surface of the occipital lobe and marks the primary visual cortex.

### Why only these four
Most sulci fold the cortex in a pattern that varies from person to person, so only these four are constant enough to be relied on as fixed anatomical landmarks.

## hold_these
Four constant, named sulci: central, lateral, parieto-occipital, calcarine. The calcarine sulcus sits on the medial occipital lobe and marks the visual cortex.

## lose_the_mark
Treating every cortical fold as an equally reliable landmark — only the four named sulci are constant enough to serve that role.

## summary
Nucleus, ganglion, tract and bundle are the four basic terms used to organise nerve cell bodies and fibres. The AEP bank tests both the ganglion/nucleus split (by CNS-versus-PNS location) and the tract/bundle split (by shared-versus-mixed fibre origin).

## sections
### Cell-body groupings: nucleus versus ganglion
A nucleus is an aggregation of nerve cell bodies inside the central nervous system. A ganglion is the equivalent aggregation outside the CNS, in the peripheral nervous system.

### Fibre groupings: tract versus bundle
A tract is a collection of nerve fibres that share the same origin, termination and function. A bundle is a collection of nerve fibres that do not all share the same origin, termination or function.

## hold_these
Nucleus = cell bodies inside the CNS; ganglion = cell bodies outside the CNS. Tract = fibres with one shared origin/termination/function; bundle = fibres without that shared pattern.

## lose_the_mark
Treating "bundle" as a generic synonym for any fibre collection — it specifically names the mixed-origin case, the opposite of a tract.

## summary
A synapse is classified by what the presynaptic axon terminal meets, and that same terminal is where synaptic vesicles cluster before releasing neurotransmitter. The AEP bank tests both the axo-somatic synapse type and the vesicles' location.

## sections
### The three axo- synapse types
An axo-dendritic synapse meets another neuron's dendrites. An axo-somatic synapse meets another neuron's cell body. An axo-axonic synapse meets another neuron's axon. When the terminal meets a skeletal muscle fibre instead, the junction has its own separate name, the motor end plate.

### Where vesicles sit
Synaptic vesicles, which store the presynaptic neuron's neurotransmitter, cluster in the axon terminal itself, immediately next to the synaptic cleft. An arriving action potential opens voltage-gated calcium channels there, and the calcium influx triggers vesicle fusion and neurotransmitter release.

## hold_these
Axo-dendritic/axo-somatic/axo-axonic name what the axon terminal meets. Synaptic vesicles are located in the axon terminal, not the synaptic cleft or the postsynaptic element.

## lose_the_mark
Calling the axon-to-muscle junction an axo-somatic synapse, or placing vesicles in the synaptic cleft rather than the presynaptic terminal.

## summary
The sympathetic and parasympathetic systems are functional opposites everywhere except the heart, and the parasympathetic system leaves the brainstem at three separate levels rather than one. The AEP bank tests a sympathetic effect and the cranial outflow's brainstem span.

## sections
### Opposite effects (heart excepted)
Sympathetic activity is inhibitory to the gut (decreasing secretion/peristalsis), contracts the sphincters, and produces bronchodilatation. Parasympathetic activity is excitatory to the gut, relaxes the sphincters, and produces bronchoconstriction. The heart is the one exception: both divisions excite it, sympathetic speeding it and parasympathetic slowing it.

### Cranial outflow across three levels
The parasympathetic system's cranial outflow is not confined to one brainstem level: it leaves via the oculomotor nerve (III) from the midbrain, the facial nerve (VII) from the pons, and the glossopharyngeal (IX) and vagus (X) nerves from the medulla.

## hold_these
Sympathetic: gut-inhibitory, sphincter-contracting, bronchodilator. Parasympathetic: gut-excitatory, sphincter-relaxing, bronchoconstrictor. Heart: excited by both. Parasympathetic cranial outflow spans midbrain, pons and medulla together.

## lose_the_mark
Treating the sympathetic system as purely inhibitory and the parasympathetic as purely excitatory everywhere, including the heart — or picking only one brainstem level for the cranial outflow.

## summary
A spinal nerve forms from the union of a purely sensory dorsal root and a purely motor ventral root; once united, the nerve — and every peripheral nerve carrying impulses from the brain to a muscle — is functionally motor (efferent) in that direction. The AEP bank tests both halves of this picture.

## sections
### Root union
The dorsal (posterior) root carries sensory (afferent) fibres only, and bears the dorsal root ganglion. The ventral (anterior) root carries motor (efferent) fibres only. Their union, within the vertebral canal, forms the mixed spinal nerve trunk.

### Motor versus sensory direction
Motor (efferent) nerves carry impulses away from the CNS, from the brain or spinal cord out to muscles and glands. Sensory (afferent) nerves carry impulses the opposite way, from peripheral receptors into the CNS. A nerve described as carrying an impulse from the brain to a muscle is being asked about specifically by its motor (efferent) component.

## hold_these
Dorsal root = sensory only; ventral root = motor only; their union = mixed nerve trunk. Motor (efferent) = CNS to muscle; sensory (afferent) = receptor to CNS.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing "root" (purely sensory or purely motor, inside the vertebral canal) with "ramus" (already mixed, just outside it) — or naming "mixed nerve" when a question asks specifically for the CNS-to-muscle direction.

### Two cell types, two jobs
Schwann cells wrap around peripheral axons, forming the myelin sheath along the fibre's length. Satellite cells instead surround the cell bodies of neurons lying within a ganglion (such as a dorsal root or autonomic ganglion), supporting that soma much as astrocytes support central neurons.

### A same-name trap
The ganglion satellite cell is unrelated to the muscle (myosatellite) cell of skeletal-muscle regeneration — the two share only a name, one being neuroglial and the other a muscle stem cell.

## hold_these
Schwann cells = peripheral axon myelination. Satellite cells = peripheral ganglion cell-body support. Both are distinct from the unrelated muscle satellite (myosatellite) cell.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing the ganglion satellite cell with the muscle satellite cell — they share a name but nothing else.

## sections
### Two compartments by location
The central nervous system (CNS) is the brain and the spinal cord, protected within the skull and vertebral column. The autonomic (sympathetic/parasympathetic) system is a functional subdivision running through both the CNS and the PNS, not a third anatomical compartment alongside them.

## summary
The cerebellum coordinates movement, balance and muscle tone without initiating movement itself, so its disease produces loss of balance rather than paralysis, sensory loss or memory loss. This exact pairing is the AEP bank's single most repeated CNS fact.

## sections
### Three core functions
The cerebellum maintains equilibrium (balance), coordinates the timing and force of voluntary muscle movement, and regulates muscle tone.

### What its disease produces, and does not
Because the cerebellum does not itself carry the motor or sensory pathways, cerebellar disease characteristically produces loss of balance and coordination (ataxia). Paralysis points instead to a corticospinal-tract problem, sensory loss to a sensory-pathway problem, and memory loss to a cerebral-cortex problem — none of them a cerebellar sign.

## hold_these
Cerebellum: equilibrium, coordination, muscle tone. Its disease: loss of balance, not paralysis, sensory loss or memory loss.

## lose_the_mark
Attributing paralysis or sensory loss to cerebellar disease instead of to the corticospinal tract or sensory pathways.

## summary
An axillary block anaesthetises the brachial plexus's peripheral (mixed) nerves as they pass through the axilla, not the nerve roots. The AEP bank's own worked example is a wrist operation using exactly this technique.

## sections
### What the injection reaches
The brachial plexus (C5-T1 ventral rami) passes through the axilla alongside the axillary artery. Local anaesthetic deposited there blocks the already-formed, mixed peripheral spinal nerves of the upper limb, producing temporary loss of both sensation and movement in that limb.

### What it cannot reach
The dorsal and ventral nerve roots lie inside the vertebral canal, far proximal to the axilla, so an axillary injection cannot reach them — only the peripheral nerves at the point the plexus has already reached.

## hold_these
An axillary block targets the peripheral (mixed) nerves of the upper limb in the axilla, giving temporary combined sensory-and-motor block, not root-level or permanent effects.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming a peripheral regional block can reach the dorsal or ventral nerve roots, which sit inside the vertebral canal.
