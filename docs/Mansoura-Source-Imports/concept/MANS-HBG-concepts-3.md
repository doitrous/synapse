Endoneurium is the loose connective tissue that surrounds each individual nerve fibre, inside the perineurium

## definition
A peripheral nerve is wrapped by three named connective-tissue layers, nested one inside the other. The endoneurium is the innermost: a thin sleeve of loose (delicate) connective tissue, with reticular fibres, occasional fibroblasts and capillaries, that surrounds each individual nerve fibre and its Schwann cell sheath. It is distinct from the perineurium, a denser connective-tissue layer that bundles many nerve fibres together into a fascicle, and from the epineurium, the outermost dense irregular connective tissue that binds the fascicles into the whole nerve trunk. Because the endoneurium is loose rather than dense, and surrounds one fibre rather than a bundle or the trunk, it is the layer identified whenever a question isolates connective tissue around a single nerve fibre from among the nerve's three coverings.

## explicit_objective
Identify the endoneurium as the loose connective tissue sleeve around a single nerve fibre, and distinguish it by looseness and level (single fibre) from the denser perineurium (fascicle) and epineurium (whole trunk).

## pitfalls
Picking the perineurium for a single-fibre question. The perineurium is defined by what it bundles, many fibres into one fascicle, not by wrapping one fibre; only the endoneurium sits at the single-fibre level, and only it is loose rather than dense connective tissue.

## definition
Eccrine and apocrine sweat glands differ in where their excretory duct opens as well as in their secretion. The eccrine (merocrine) sweat gland is a simple coiled tubular gland, present over almost the entire body surface, whose straight excretory duct pierces the epidermis independently and opens directly onto the free surface of the skin. The apocrine sweat gland, confined to the axilla, groin and a few other sites, instead opens into the upper part of a hair follicle, above the sebaceous gland's own opening, rather than reaching the skin surface on its own. This duct-opening site is the distinguishing landmark used to separate the two gland types once the secretory mechanism has already been asked.

## explicit_objective
State that the eccrine sweat gland duct opens directly onto the skin surface, as distinct from the apocrine sweat gland duct, which opens into the hair follicle.

## pitfalls
Assuming all sweat gland ducts share one opening site. Only the eccrine duct reaches the surface on its own; the apocrine duct empties into the hair follicle, the same site the sebaceous gland duct also uses.

The epidermis is stratified squamous keratinized epithelium, built from base to surface of basal, spinous, granular and horny layers, with the spinous layer's cells polyhedral and joined by desmosomal spines

## id
CON-DER-2A56D2FE509FB3

## canonical_key
skin.epidermis.epithelium-type-and-layer-cell-shapes

Epidermis epithelium type

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases

## definition
The epidermis, the epithelial component of the skin, is classified histologically as stratified squamous keratinized epithelium: many cell layers thick, flattening toward the surface, and capped by a dead, keratin-filled horny layer, which is what separates it from the stratified squamous non-keratinized epithelium lining wet surfaces such as the oesophagus. Between the deep basal layer and the superficial granular/horny layers sits the stratum spinosum, the spinous (prickle-cell) layer: several rows of cells that are polyhedral (many-sided) in shape rather than flat or cuboidal, named for the spine-like intercellular bridges, desmosome-anchored tonofibril bundles, that appear to radiate from each cell when the tissue shrinks during fixation.

## explicit_objective
Classify the epidermis as stratified squamous keratinized epithelium, and describe the spinous layer's cells as polyhedral, joined by desmosomal spines.

## pitfalls
Calling the epidermis simple or non-keratinized. It is always stratified and, everywhere on the body, keratinized; thick and thin skin differ in how thick the horny layer is, not in whether keratin is present at all.

## label
The axon is the single process that carries the nerve impulse away from the neuron's cell body

## id
CON-NEU-870A2390839248

## canonical_key
neuron.axon.single-process-carries-impulse-away-from-cell-body

## aliases
Axon function
Axon vs dendrite direction of conduction

## arabic_label

## definition
A neuron's processes are functionally, not just structurally, distinct. A neuron has at most one axon: a single, usually long process, uniform in diameter, that conducts the nerve impulse away from the cell body toward a synapse, a muscle or a gland. Dendrites, by contrast, are typically multiple, taper, and conduct impulses toward the cell body. Even where a neuron's shape varies, a multipolar cell with many dendrites, a bipolar cell with one dendrite and one axon, a pseudounipolar sensory cell with a single process that splits, the axon is defined by this one-way, away-from-the-body direction of conduction, not by number, branching pattern or length.

## explicit_objective
State that the axon is the process that conducts an impulse away from the neuron's cell body, as distinct from a dendrite's impulse-toward-the-body direction.

## pitfalls
Defining the axon by its length or lack of branching. Length and branching vary by neuron type; the one constant that identifies an axon in any neuron is the direction it conducts, away from the cell body.

The lysosome is the organelle responsible for intracellular digestion

## id

lysosome.function.intracellular-digestion

## aliases
Lysosome function

## arabic_label

## definition
The lysosome is a membrane-bound organelle, budded from the Golgi apparatus, packed with acid hydrolase enzymes (proteases, nucleases, lipases, glycosidases) that work best at the lysosome's own acidic internal pH. Its job is intracellular digestion: it fuses with a phagosome or an aged or damaged organelle enclosed in an autophagosome, and its enzymes break the engulfed material down into reusable subunits, leaving behind a residual body if digestion is incomplete. This digestive role is what the acid phosphatase histochemical stain visualises, and it is what distinguishes the lysosome from the other membranous organelles offered as distractors: the Golgi apparatus packages and modifies, the endoplasmic reticulum synthesises, and ribosomes translate, but only the lysosome digests.

## explicit_objective
Identify the lysosome as the organelle responsible for intracellular digestion, distinct from the Golgi apparatus, endoplasmic reticulum, residual bodies and ribosomes offered as distractors.

## pitfalls
Picking residual bodies for the digestion function. A residual body is what is left behind after incomplete digestion, a product of the lysosome's activity, not the digesting organelle itself.

Blood vascular endothelium is simple squamous epithelium

## id
CON-CVS-A889F238880B02

## canonical_key
blood-vessel.endothelium.simple-squamous-epithelium

## aliases
Vascular endothelium epithelium type
Endothelium classification

## arabic_label

## definition
The innermost lining of every blood vessel, from the largest artery to the smallest capillary, is the endothelium: a single layer of flattened, simple squamous epithelial cells resting on a basal lamina. This classification holds regardless of the vessel's size or wall thickness — the tunica intima's endothelium is always simple squamous, in continuity with the endocardium lining the heart's own chambers. Functionally this thin, flat single layer minimises the diffusion distance for gases and small solutes between blood and the surrounding tissue, and provides the smooth, non-thrombogenic surface blood needs to flow across without clotting.

## explicit_objective
Classify the endothelium lining blood vessels as simple squamous epithelium.

## pitfalls
Confusing endothelium (blood vessels) with mesothelium (body cavities). Both are simple squamous, but the question asks for the epithelium type, not the name, so simple squamous is the answer whichever lining is named.

## definition
Across all its types, epithelial tissue shares a set of defining general characters that distinguish it from connective tissue. ## definition
Among the fixed and wandering cells of connective tissue proper, the fibroblast is the one specifically activated at a site of tissue injury during healing. Resting fibroblasts are relatively flat, spindle-shaped cells with little cytoplasm; on activation by the inflammatory and growth-factor signals a wound generates, they proliferate, migrate into the wound, and become larger and more active, synthesising the new collagen and ground substance that build granulation tissue and, eventually, the scar. This role is specific to the fibroblast among connective tissue's other cells: the mast cell, plasma cell, macrophage and adipocyte each have their own distinct function, but new matrix production for repair is the fibroblast's job.

## explicit_objective
Identify the fibroblast as the connective tissue cell activated during wound healing, and state that its role is new collagen and matrix synthesis.

## pitfalls
Picking the macrophage for a wound-healing question. The macrophage is active early in healing too, but as a phagocyte clearing debris, not as the cell that lays down the new collagen matrix, that synthetic role belongs to the fibroblast.

Meiosis halves the chromosome number in two steps: the first division separates homologous pairs to leave 23 double (23-d) chromosomes, the second separates sister chromatids to leave 23 single (23-s) chromosomes in each gamete

## id

## canonical_key
meiosis.chromosome-chromatid-count-through-both-divisions

## aliases
Meiosis I and II chromosome count
Gamete chromosome number

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases

## definition
A primary spermatocyte or oocyte enters meiosis with the doubled, replicated complement of 46 chromosomes, each of two sister chromatids (46-d). The first meiotic division is reductional: it separates the two homologous chromosomes of each pair from each other, so each resulting secondary spermatocyte/oocyte has only 23 chromosomes, but each of those 23 is still made of two sister chromatids, so the notation is 23-d. The second meiotic division is equational, like mitosis: it separates the sister chromatids of each of those 23 chromosomes, so each final gamete (spermatid or ovum) ends with 23 chromosomes of a single chromatid each, 23-s, the haploid, single-copy complement that fertilisation restores to 46-s by combining with the other gamete's 23-s.

## explicit_objective
Trace the chromosome and chromatid count through both meiotic divisions: 46-d at the start, 23-d after meiosis I, 23-s in the final haploid gamete after meiosis II.

## pitfalls
Reading the daughter cell of meiosis as meaning after the first division only. The first division's product is 23-d, still doubled; only the final gamete, after the second (equational) division, is the single-chromatid 23-s haploid cell that fertilisation expects.
