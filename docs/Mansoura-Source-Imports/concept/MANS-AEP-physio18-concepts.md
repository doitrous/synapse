Interstitial (tissue) fluid pressure is normally slightly negative relative to atmospheric pressure, with regional variation — about -3 mmHg in loose tissues such as skeletal muscle, but positive in tightly encapsulated organs such as the kidney (about +6 mmHg) and the brain (about +4 mmHg)

## definition
The pressure of the free fluid in the interstitial space is normally slightly subatmospheric (negative), not equal to or greater than capillary pressure — in loose subcutaneous and skeletal-muscle tissue it is usually cited as about -3 mmHg, which is generated and maintained by continuous lymphatic pumping of any excess filtered fluid out of the interstitium. This value is not uniform across the body: organs enclosed by a tight, non-distensible capsule or membrane instead run a positive interstitial pressure, because the capsule resists any tissue swelling and so opposes, rather than accommodates, outward capillary filtration — the kidney (encased in a fibrous renal capsule) runs about +6 mmHg, and the brain (enclosed within the rigid cranium) runs about +4 mmHg. A negative interstitial pressure itself is a filtration-favouring (outward-pulling) Starling force, while a positive interstitial pressure opposes filtration.

## explicit_objective
State that interstitial fluid pressure is normally slightly negative in loose tissue (about -3 mmHg) but positive in encapsulated organs (kidney about +6 mmHg, brain about +4 mmHg), and that its sign determines whether it favours or opposes capillary filtration.

## pitfalls
Assuming interstitial pressure is uniformly negative everywhere in the body — encapsulated organs (kidney, brain) run a POSITIVE interstitial pressure because their capsule prevents the tissue swelling that a negative pressure elsewhere depends on; and confusing the sign's effect — a NEGATIVE interstitial pressure favours (adds to) filtration, while a POSITIVE one opposes it.

Capillary hydrostatic pressure is the dominant outward (filtration-favouring) Starling force; it falls along the capillary's length from about 30 mmHg at the arteriolar end to about 10 mmHg at the venular end, giving a functional mean capillary pressure of about 17.3 mmHg

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Capillary hydrostatic pressure (Pc) is the pressure the blood itself exerts against the capillary wall, and it is the principal force pushing fluid OUTWARD, out of the capillary and into the interstitium. It is not uniform along the vessel: it is highest at the arteriolar end (about 30 mmHg, just downstream of the resistance offered by the arterioles) and falls to about 10 mmHg by the venular end (upstream of the low-resistance venous system). Because a simple arithmetic mean would overweight the pressure drop's shape, physiologists instead use the functional mean capillary pressure — calculated from the actual capillary pressure-length profile — which is about 17.3 mmHg; this is the single value used when the capillary's net filtration/absorption balance across its whole length is computed (as in the classic Starling equilibrium calculation).

## explicit_objective
State that capillary hydrostatic pressure is the dominant outward Starling force, falling from about 30 mmHg at the arteriolar end to about 10 mmHg at the venular end, with a functional mean of about 17.3 mmHg.

## pitfalls
Reversing the arterial-end and venous-end values (pressure is HIGH near the arteriolar end and LOW near the venular end, not the other way round), or treating the functional mean (17.3 mmHg) as a simple arithmetic average of the two end values (30 + 10)/2 = 20, which it is not — the functional mean is derived from the actual pressure profile along the capillary, not a linear average of its two ends.

## definition
Plasma colloid osmotic pressure (also called plasma oncotic pressure) is the osmotic pressure exerted by the plasma proteins that cannot cross the capillary wall, and it is normally about 28 mmHg. Although several plasma proteins contribute, albumin is by far the main determinant — not because it is the "strongest" osmotic particle molecule for molecule, but because its small molecular size and high plasma concentration give it far more osmotically active particles per litre than the larger, less concentrated globulins. Plasma colloid osmotic pressure is the principal force pulling fluid INWARD, back into the capillary, and it is this force — opposing capillary hydrostatic pressure — that drives fluid reabsorption at the venous end of the capillary bed.

## explicit_objective
State that plasma colloid osmotic pressure is normally about 28 mmHg, that albumin (via its high concentration, not its individual osmotic strength) is its main determinant, and that it is the principal inward Starling force.

## pitfalls
Attributing plasma colloid osmotic pressure mainly to the globulins (or crediting albumin's dominance to a stronger individual osmotic effect) rather than to albumin's much higher plasma CONCENTRATION relative to the globulins — molecule for molecule the difference is smaller, but albumin greatly outnumbers the globulins in plasma.

## label
Fluid filtration across the capillary wall occurs mainly at the arterial end (about 20 L/day, driven chiefly by capillary hydrostatic pressure, aided by interstitial colloid osmotic pressure and by a negative interstitial hydrostatic pressure, opposed by a positive one), while fluid absorption occurs mainly at the venous end (about 18 L/day, driven chiefly by capillary colloid osmotic pressure, aided by a positive interstitial hydrostatic pressure, opposed by interstitial colloid osmotic pressure)

## arabic_label

## definition
Along the length of a typical capillary, the balance of Starling forces shifts from net outward movement at the arterial (arteriolar) end to net inward movement at the venous (venular) end, because capillary hydrostatic pressure — the dominant outward force — falls steeply along the vessel while capillary colloid osmotic pressure — the dominant inward force — stays essentially constant. Filtration (fluid leaving the capillary) therefore predominates at the arterial end, amounting to about 20 litres per day for the body as a whole; it is aided by interstitial colloid osmotic pressure (which pulls fluid out into the interstitium) and, whenever interstitial hydrostatic pressure is NEGATIVE, by that negative pressure itself (subtracting a negative value adds to the net outward force) — a POSITIVE interstitial hydrostatic pressure instead opposes filtration. Absorption (fluid re-entering the capillary) predominates at the venous end, amounting to about 18 litres per day; its main driving force is the capillary's own colloid osmotic pressure, aided by interstitial hydrostatic pressure whenever that pressure is POSITIVE (now pushing fluid from the interstitium into the capillary), and it is opposed by interstitial fluid colloid osmotic pressure (which keeps pulling fluid the other way, out of the capillary). Because filtration (20 L/day) slightly exceeds absorption (18 L/day), the roughly 2 L/day surplus is returned to the circulation via the lymphatics rather than being reabsorbed directly into the capillary.

## explicit_objective
State that capillary filtration (about 20 L/day) predominates at the arterial end and absorption (about 18 L/day) at the venous end, name the forces driving and aiding each — including that a POSITIVE interstitial hydrostatic pressure aids absorption but OPPOSES filtration — and explain why the small filtration surplus is drained by the lymphatics.

## pitfalls
Reversing which end filtration and absorption predominate at (filtration is arterial-end, absorption is venous-end, not the reverse); and reversing the sign-dependence of interstitial hydrostatic pressure — a POSITIVE interstitial hydrostatic pressure AIDS absorption and OPPOSES filtration, while a NEGATIVE interstitial hydrostatic pressure does the opposite (aids filtration). Interstitial colloid osmotic pressure runs the other way: it aids filtration and opposes absorption.

## label
The classic Starling equilibrium sums to a total outward force of about 28.3 mmHg (capillary hydrostatic pressure 17.3 + the pull of a negative interstitial hydrostatic pressure 3 + interstitial colloid osmotic pressure 8) against a total inward force of about 28 mmHg (plasma colloid osmotic pressure), leaving a small net outward (filtration) pressure of about 0.3 mmHg averaged over the whole capillary — while at the venous end alone, the local balance instead favours a net inward (reabsorptive) force of about 7 mmHg

## id

## arabic_aliases

## definition
The classic (Guyton) summary of the Starling forces, averaged over the whole length of a capillary, lists the forces tending to move fluid OUTWARD — capillary hydrostatic pressure (mean, about 17.3 mmHg), the outward pull contributed by a negative interstitial hydrostatic pressure (about 3 mmHg), and interstitial fluid colloid osmotic pressure (about 8 mmHg) — which sum to a total outward force of about 28.3 mmHg. Opposing this is the single dominant force tending to move fluid INWARD, plasma colloid osmotic pressure, about 28 mmHg. The difference — about 28.3 minus 28, or roughly +0.3 mmHg net outward — is the small mean net filtration pressure that, applied over the enormous combined surface area of the systemic capillaries, filters slightly more fluid than is reabsorbed, with the surplus returned to the circulation by the lymphatics. This whole-capillary mean, however, is not the same figure as the local balance at either end: because capillary hydrostatic pressure itself falls sharply along the vessel, the venous end alone runs a local net INWARD (reabsorptive) force of about 7 mmHg, distinct from the +0.3 mmHg whole-capillary net outward mean.

## explicit_objective
Reproduce the classic Starling forces table (outward: 17.3 + 3 + 8 = 28.3 mmHg; inward: 28 mmHg), state the resulting small net outward (filtration) pressure of about 0.3 mmHg, and distinguish this whole-capillary mean from the venous end's own local net inward (reabsorptive) force of about 7 mmHg.

## pitfalls
Confusing the whole-capillary mean net filtration pressure (about +0.3 mmHg outward) with the venous end's own local net force (about 7 mmHg inward) — they describe different points along the same capillary and are not interchangeable; and misplacing interstitial colloid osmotic pressure or the negative interstitial hydrostatic pressure's own sign among the outward-force terms.

## definition
Of all the fluid that leaves the capillaries by filtration at the arterial end, the great majority — approximately 90% — re-enters the capillaries by absorption before the blood reaches the venous end, driven by the shift in Starling-force balance along the vessel's length (falling capillary hydrostatic pressure against a roughly constant plasma colloid osmotic pressure). The remaining approximately 10% of the filtered fluid does not return directly to the capillary; instead it is picked up by the initial lymphatics as lymph and returned to the systemic circulation indirectly, via the thoracic duct and right lymphatic duct into the great veins — amounting to roughly 2 to 4 litres per day for the body as a whole. This lymphatic route is essential: without it, the small daily filtration surplus would progressively accumulate in the interstitium as oedema.

## explicit_objective
State that about 90% of capillary-filtered fluid is reabsorbed before the venous end, and that the remaining 10% (about 2 to 4 L/day) returns via the lymphatics rather than directly into the capillary.

## pitfalls
Assuming ALL filtered fluid is reabsorbed directly into the capillary — a small but essential fraction (about 10%) is instead collected by the lymphatics, and failure of this lymphatic drainage (not merely a change in Starling forces) is itself a distinct cause of oedema.
