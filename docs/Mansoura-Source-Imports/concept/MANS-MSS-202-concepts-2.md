## label
The brachial artery, palpable in the cubital fossa medial to the biceps tendon, is the standard site for auscultatory blood pressure measurement

## id
CON-MSK-B4C13BE6A17539

## canonical_key
brachial-artery-cubital-fossa-bp-auscultation-site

## aliases
Brachial artery auscultation site
Blood pressure cuff artery

## definition
The brachial artery is the continuation of the axillary artery beyond the lower border of teres major, and it runs down the medial side of the arm to end in the cubital fossa, opposite the neck of the radius, by dividing into the radial and ulnar arteries. In the cubital fossa it lies medial to the biceps tendon and is crossed superficially by the bicipital aponeurosis, which separates it from the median cubital vein used for venepuncture; this superficial, palpable position is exactly why a sphygmomanometer cuff is placed around the arm and a stethoscope diaphragm is set over the brachial artery in the cubital fossa to auscultate the Korotkoff sounds during a manual blood pressure reading.

## explicit_objective
Identify the brachial artery, at its termination in the cubital fossa medial to the biceps tendon, as the vessel auscultated when measuring blood pressure with a cuff and stethoscope.

## pitfalls
Naming the radial or axillary artery as the auscultation site. The radial artery is used for palpating the pulse and for the palpatory pre-estimate of systolic pressure, not for the auscultatory reading itself, and the axillary artery lies proximal to the cuff, under the arm, not in the cubital fossa where the stethoscope is placed.

## definition
In a young child (classically under 5 years), the annular ligament that rings the radial head is thinner and more loosely attached than in an adult, and a longitudinal traction force on the extended, pronated arm — such as a caregiver pulling or swinging the child by the hand — can pull the radial head partly out through the distal part of the annular ligament, which then becomes trapped between the radial head and the capitulum. ## explicit_objective
Recognise the classic pulled-elbow vignette (traction on an extended, pronated arm in a young child) as radial head subluxation through the annular ligament, and distinguish it from a fracture.

## pitfalls
Expecting an X-ray abnormality. Nursemaid's elbow is a soft-tissue subluxation with a radiographically normal joint, which is exactly why the diagnosis is made from the mechanism and the child's guarded posture rather than from imaging, unlike a true fracture.

## definition
The palmar interossei (three or four muscles, one to each finger except the middle) adduct the fingers toward the axis of the middle finger ("PAD" — Palmar ADduct), while the dorsal interossei abduct them ("DAB" — Dorsal ABduct); both groups are supplied by the deep branch of the ulnar nerve. ## explicit_objective
Explain the paper test as a bedside test of finger adduction, isolating palmar interosseous (ulnar nerve deep branch) function.

## pitfalls
Confusing the paper test with tests of finger abduction (which would examine the dorsal interossei instead) or of thumb adduction (Froment's sign, which examines adductor pollicis). All three are ulnar-nerve-deep-branch muscles, but the paper test specifically isolates the palmar interossei's finger-adduction action.

## label
Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome is an autoimmune paraneoplastic disorder, most often linked to small cell lung cancer, caused by antibodies against presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channels at the neuromuscular junction

## id
CON-MSK-A3A8D2C7306987

## canonical_key
lambert-eaton-myasthenic-syndrome-vgcc-antibody-sclc

## aliases
Lambert-Eaton syndrome
Anti-VGCC antibody
Paraneoplastic myasthenic syndrome

## definition
Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome (LEMS) is caused by autoantibodies directed against the P/Q-type voltage-gated calcium channels (VGCC) on the presynaptic nerve terminal at the neuromuscular junction, reducing calcium influx and so reducing acetylcholine vesicle release with each nerve impulse. It is a paraneoplastic disorder in over half of cases, most often associated with small cell lung cancer, whose tumour cells express the same channel and are thought to trigger the antibody response. Clinically it causes proximal muscle weakness with autonomic features (dry mouth, constipation, erectile dysfunction) and depressed or absent deep tendon reflexes that characteristically return, and repetitive nerve stimulation shows an incremental (facilitating) compound muscle action potential, because each successive stimulus lets more residual calcium accumulate in the terminal and overcome the antibody-reduced calcium entry — the opposite electrophysiological pattern from myasthenia gravis, whose postsynaptic receptor loss produces a decremental response.

## explicit_objective
State that Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome is caused by anti-presynaptic-voltage-gated-calcium-channel antibodies, is most often paraneoplastic to small cell lung cancer, and produces improvement (not worsening) of weakness and an incremental CMAP with repeated use, unlike myasthenia gravis.

## pitfalls
Confusing Lambert-Eaton syndrome with myasthenia gravis because both cause fluctuating proximal weakness. Myasthenia gravis is postsynaptic (anti-acetylcholine-receptor antibody), worsens with exercise, and gives a decremental response on repetitive stimulation; Lambert-Eaton is presynaptic (anti-calcium-channel antibody), improves transiently with exercise, and gives an incremental response.

## label
Calmodulin, the calcium sensor that triggers smooth-muscle contraction via myosin light-chain kinase, is structurally homologous to troponin C, the calcium sensor on the thin filament of skeletal and cardiac muscle

## id

## canonical_key
calmodulin-troponin-c-structural-homology-calcium-sensors

## aliases
Calmodulin structure
Troponin C homology
EF-hand calcium-binding proteins

## definition
Troponin C (the calcium-binding subunit of the troponin complex on the thin filament of skeletal and cardiac muscle) and calmodulin (the free cytoplasmic calcium sensor that activates myosin light-chain kinase in smooth muscle, and many other calcium-dependent enzymes throughout the body) are both members of the EF-hand family of calcium-binding proteins, sharing a closely related helix-loop-helix structural motif and a similar four-calcium-binding-site architecture, and calmodulin can itself substitute for troponin C's calcium-binding role in some experimental settings. Despite this structural kinship, the two proteins act through entirely different downstream mechanisms: calcium-bound troponin C moves tropomyosin off the actin-myosin binding site directly (a thin-filament, actin-based switch), while calcium-bound calmodulin activates a kinase that phosphorylates the myosin head itself (a thick-filament, myosin-based switch) — the structural similarity of the sensor is not matched by a similarity in how the signal is used.

## explicit_objective
State that calmodulin and troponin C are structurally related (both EF-hand calcium-binding proteins) despite triggering contraction through different mechanisms — troponin C via the thin filament in striated muscle, calmodulin via myosin light-chain kinase in smooth muscle.

## pitfalls
Assuming structural homology means the two proteins work the same way. They share ancestry and a calcium-binding fold, but troponin C acts on the actin/tropomyosin side of the cross-bridge cycle, while calmodulin acts on the myosin side through a kinase — the mechanisms are genuinely distinct, not a renamed version of one another.

## label
Malignant hyperthermia is a pharmacogenetic reaction, usually triggered by a volatile anaesthetic plus succinylcholine, caused by a ryanodine receptor (RyR1) mutation that produces uncontrolled sarcoplasmic calcium release and a massive rise in skeletal muscle metabolic rate

## id
CON-MSK-756993594C09F8

## canonical_key
malignant-hyperthermia-ryr1-mutation-metabolic-crisis

## aliases
Malignant hyperthermia
Ryanodine receptor mutation
Anaesthesia-triggered hypermetabolism

## definition
Malignant hyperthermia is a rare, autosomal-dominant pharmacogenetic disorder, most often caused by a mutation in the ryanodine receptor 1 (RyR1) gene — the same calcium-release channel on the terminal cisternae of the sarcoplasmic reticulum that normally opens in response to the dihydropyridine receptor's signal during excitation-contraction coupling. The mutated channel is triggered abnormally by volatile inhalational anaesthetics (such as halothane) and by the depolarising muscle relaxant succinylcholine, and it stays open uncontrollably, flooding the cytoplasm with calcium; the muscle responds with sustained, generalised contracture and a massive increase in aerobic and anaerobic metabolic rate as the fibres try to pump the excess calcium back out, producing the syndrome's hallmark features — muscle rigidity, tachycardia, hypercapnia, and a rapid, severe rise in body temperature — which is fatal if not treated promptly with dantrolene, a drug that blocks the ryanodine receptor directly.

## explicit_objective
Explain malignant hyperthermia as an anaesthetic/succinylcholine-triggered RyR1 mutation producing uncontrolled calcium release and a massive increase in skeletal muscle metabolic rate, and name its defining clinical trigger and treatment.

## pitfalls
