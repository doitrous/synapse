## definition
The Gell and Coombs scheme sorts every hypersensitivity reaction into one of four mechanistic types. Type I (immediate) is IgE bound to mast cells and basophils; when antigen cross-links that surface-bound IgE the cells degranulate and release histamine and other mediators within minutes — the mechanism behind pollen and other atopic allergy. Type II (cytotoxic) is IgG or IgM antibody directed against an antigen fixed to a cell surface, which is then destroyed by complement or phagocytosis — the mechanism of Rh(D) haemolytic disease of the newborn. Type III (immune complex) is antibody-antigen complexes that deposit in tissue and trigger complement-mediated inflammation rather than acting at a single fixed target. Type IV (delayed, cell-mediated) is driven by sensitised T lymphocytes rather than antibody, and takes 24-72 hours to develop rather than minutes.

## explicit_objective
Assign a described hypersensitivity reaction to its Gell and Coombs type (I-IV) from its mechanism — antibody class, whether the antigen is a fixed cell-surface target or a circulating immune complex, and antibody-mediated versus T-cell-mediated — rather than from the antigen alone.

## pitfalls
Classifying a reaction by its trigger (a drug, pollen, a transfused antigen) instead of its mechanism. The same trigger class can produce different types depending on the mechanism involved — drug hypersensitivity can be type I, II or III depending on how the immune system responds to that particular drug-antigen — so the type is read off the mechanism described, never assumed from the substance named.

## definition
Leishmania donovani (and, in the Mediterranean basin including Egypt, L. infantum) causes visceral leishmaniasis, transmitted by the bite of the female sand fly (genus Phlebotomus in the Old World). The sand fly injects the promastigote form (flagellated, extracellular), which is taken up by human macrophages and transforms into the amastigote form (non-flagellated, obligate intracellular, found in the reticuloendothelial system throughout the body). The diagnostic stage is the amastigote seen on a stained tissue smear (bone marrow, spleen or liver), classically with Leishman or Giemsa stain. Untreated disease produces hepatosplenomegaly, generalised lymphadenopathy and pancytopenia from marrow hyperplasia replacing normal haemopoietic tissue, with a normocytic normochromic anaemia pattern. First-line systemic therapy is parenteral (sodium stibogluconate/Pentostam, or amphotericin B); miltefosine is the drug given orally.

## explicit_objective
State the vector, the two morphological forms and where each is found (promastigote in the sand fly, amastigote in human macrophages), and the diagnostic stage and stain used to identify visceral leishmaniasis.

## pitfalls
Confusing which form belongs to which host — the promastigote (flagellated) lives in the sand fly vector and in culture (NNN medium), while the amastigote (non-flagellated) is the form found inside human macrophages and is the diagnostic stage on a tissue smear. Confusing kala-azar's vector (sand fly) with malaria's (Anopheles mosquito) or filariasis's (Culex mosquito) is a repeated distractor pattern across this department's own question bank.

## definition
Wuchereria bancrofti causes Bancroftian (lymphatic) filariasis, transmitted by the bite of the Culex mosquito, which injects infective filariform larvae. Adult worms live in the lymphatic vessels and nodes, mainly of the lower limbs and genital organs. Acute manifestations (lymphangitis, lymphadenitis, filarial fever) come from the living or dying adult worm irritating the vessel; chronic manifestations (lymphoedema, hydrocele, elephantiasis) follow years of lymphatic obstruction and fibrosis from repeated inflammation and dead worms blocking the lumen. Circulating microfilariae show nocturnal periodicity (more numerous in peripheral blood 10 PM-2 AM), so diagnostic blood films are timed to that window; Knott's concentration method recovers microfilariae in low-density infections, and ultrasound can show the live adult worm's movement in the scrotal lymphatics ("filarial dance sign"). Diethylcarbamazine (Hetrazan) kills microfilariae and, with repeated courses, adult worms, and is also the drug used for tropical pulmonary eosinophilia (TPE), a hypersensitivity reaction to microfilarial antigens.

## explicit_objective
State the vector, where the adult worm lives, and the mechanism distinguishing acute (worm irritation) from chronic (obstruction/fibrosis) manifestations of lymphatic filariasis, and the timing rule for diagnostic blood collection.

## pitfalls
Confusing filariasis's vector (Culex mosquito) with malaria's (Anopheles) or leishmaniasis's (sand fly) — a repeated distractor pattern in this bank across past-exam cohorts, one of which (see triage) even printed a conflicting "Anopheles" key for this same vector fact, which this concept does not resolve in either direction. Treating elephantiasis as caused directly by circulating microfilariae rather than by chronic obstruction and fibrosis from the adult worm's presence — TPE, not elephantiasis, is the hypersensitivity-driven manifestation.

## definition
The mature human erythrocyte is a biconcave disc with an average diameter of about 7.5-7.8 micrometres and a mean corpuscular volume of about 87-95 cubic micrometres (femtolitres). Its major function is the carriage of oxygen from the lungs to the tissues, bound to haemoglobin. Internally it carries no nucleus and no mitochondria (its energy comes from anaerobic glycolysis), and it contains carbonic anhydrase, the enzyme that speeds the interconversion of carbon dioxide and bicarbonate and so contributes to both CO2 transport and acid-base buffering. Potassium, not sodium, is the erythrocyte's chief intracellular cation, maintained against the plasma gradient by the membrane's sodium-potassium pump.

## explicit_objective
State the mature erythrocyte's normal size (diameter and mean corpuscular volume), its major function, and the basic intracellular facts (no mitochondria, carbonic anhydrase present, potassium as chief intracellular cation) that this department's own question bank tests as a bundled "characteristics of the RBC" theme.

## pitfalls
Treating the biconcave shape's larger surface-area-to-volume ratio as reducing gas exchange rather than increasing it. Assuming the erythrocyte's main function is anything other than oxygen carriage (blood viscosity and acid-base buffering are real but secondary contributions of the red cell mass, not its major function).

## definition
Haem synthesis begins in the mitochondrion, where ALA synthase (delta-aminolevulinic acid synthase) condenses glycine with succinyl-CoA to form delta-aminolevulinic acid (ALA) — the first and rate-limiting step of the pathway. The pathway then alternates between cytosol (where ALA dehydratase converts ALA to porphobilinogen, and further steps build up through uroporphyrinogen and coproporphyrinogen) and mitochondrion again (where the final steps, including ferrochelatase's insertion of iron into protoporphyrin IX, complete haem). Because ALA synthase sets the pace of the whole pathway, it is the enzyme haem itself feeds back on to switch production down when haem is abundant.

## explicit_objective
Identify ALA synthase as the mitochondrial, rate-limiting, first-step enzyme of haem synthesis, as distinct from ALA dehydratase (cytosolic, second step) and ferrochelatase (mitochondrial, final step).

## pitfalls
Confusing ALA synthase (first, rate-limiting, mitochondrial) with ALA dehydratase (second step, cytosolic, the enzyme lead poisoning blocks alongside ferrochelatase) — both names sound alike and both are tested as "which enzyme does X" questions in this bank.

## definition
Iron deficiency anaemia has its own characteristic clinical signs beyond pallor — koilonychia (spoon-shaped, brittle nails), angular stomatitis (cracking at the corners of the mouth), and pica (craving for non-food substances such as ice, "pagophagia"). Their absence in an anaemic patient, especially an elderly one presenting with progressive weakness, fatigue and confusion, should raise vitamin B12 deficiency instead, which produces neurological and cognitive symptoms (confusion, subacute combined degeneration) that iron deficiency does not. Oral iron is first-line for iron-deficiency anaemia; when parenteral therapy is needed, the available intramuscular/intravenous preparations differ in route — iron sucrose is given intravenously only, while iron dextran can be given either intramuscularly or intravenously.

## explicit_objective
Use the presence or absence of koilonychia, angular stomatitis and pica to distinguish iron deficiency from vitamin B12 deficiency as the more likely cause of anaemia in a clinical vignette, and identify iron sucrose as an IV-only parenteral iron preparation.

## pitfalls
Assuming any elderly anaemic, confused patient defaults to iron deficiency because it is the most common anaemia overall — the specific absence of iron deficiency's own skin/nail/mucosal signs is what should redirect the diagnosis toward B12 deficiency in this vignette pattern. Assuming all parenteral iron preparations share the same administration route; iron sucrose is IV-only while iron dextran is not.
