# Item

## label
The rotator cuff is SITS — supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis; teres major is not one of them and keeps normal tendon function in a torn cuff

## id
CON-MSK-B3A1C6D2E4F508

## canonical_key
rotator-cuff-sits-muscles-and-teres-major-exception

## aliases
SITS muscles
Rotator cuff muscles

## definition
The rotator cuff is formed by four muscles whose tendons blend with the shoulder joint capsule and hold the humeral head against the glenoid through the whole arc of abduction: supraspinatus (initiates the first 15 degrees and resists superior humeral head migration), infraspinatus and teres minor (lateral rotators), and subscapularis (medial rotator). Teres major is not part of this group — it arises from the inferior angle of the scapula and inserts on the medial lip of the intertubercular groove, acting with latissimus dorsi to adduct and medially rotate the arm, and its tendon stays intact and functionally normal when a rotator cuff tear is present.

## explicit_objective
Name the four rotator cuff muscles (SITS) and explain why teres major, despite its similar name and nearby origin, is excluded from the group and keeps normal function in a rotator cuff tear.

## pitfalls
Assuming any muscle near the scapula that rotates the shoulder is "in the cuff." Teres major shares an action (medial rotation, alongside subscapularis) and a neighbouring origin with teres minor, which is exactly why exam questions pair the two — the distinguishing fact is capsular blending, which teres major's tendon does not do.

## label
The posterior cord of the brachial plexus gives the upper and lower subscapular nerves, thoracodorsal nerve, axillary nerve and radial nerve; the long thoracic nerve arises directly from the roots, not from any cord

## id
CON-MSK-C7F209A15E3D46

## canonical_key
posterior-cord-branches-and-long-thoracic-nerve-exception

## aliases
Posterior cord branches
Brachial plexus cord branches

## definition
The posterior cord of the brachial plexus (formed from the posterior divisions of all three trunks) gives five named branches: the upper and lower subscapular nerves (to subscapularis, and to subscapularis plus teres major), the thoracodorsal nerve (to latissimus dorsi), and its two terminal branches, the axillary and radial nerves. The long thoracic nerve is a frequent distractor in "which nerve is NOT a branch of the posterior cord" questions because it also supplies a muscle relevant to scapular movement (serratus anterior), but it arises directly from the anterior rami of C5-C7 before the trunks or cords form, making it a root-level branch, not a cord branch.

## explicit_objective
List the five branches of the posterior cord and explain why the long thoracic nerve, despite testing alongside them, is a root-level branch rather than a cord branch.

## pitfalls
Grouping every nerve that affects the scapula or shoulder as "posterior cord." The posterior cord's branches all lie posterior to the axillary artery and supply extensor/lateral-rotator-adjacent muscles of the shoulder and arm; the long thoracic nerve's early root-level origin and its serratus anterior target place it in a different, earlier tier of the plexus entirely.

# Item

## label
The deep (motor) branch of the ulnar nerve supplies the hypothenar muscles, the medial two lumbricals, all the interossei and adductor pollicis; testing intrinsic hand function (e.g. the L-position, or forced thumb adduction) evaluates this branch

## id
CON-MSK-F16B834D2C9A57

## canonical_key
ulnar-nerve-deep-branch-and-adductor-pollicis

## aliases
Ulnar nerve deep branch
Adductor pollicis nerve supply
Intrinsic hand muscle testing

## definition
After passing through Guyon's canal at the wrist, the ulnar nerve divides into a superficial (mainly sensory) branch and a deep (motor) branch. The deep branch curves around the hook of hamate and supplies the hypothenar muscles, the third and fourth (medial two) lumbricals, all the palmar and dorsal interossei, and adductor pollicis — despite the "pollicis" muscles usually being grouped with the thenar (median-supplied) muscles, adductor pollicis is the one thumb muscle the ulnar nerve supplies. Clinical tests of this branch include asking the patient to hold a card between the thumb and index finger against resistance (adductor pollicis, Froment's sign if compensating with flexor pollicis longus) or to flex the metacarpophalangeal joints while extending the interphalangeal joints (the "L-position," testing the interossei and lumbricals it innervates).

## explicit_objective
List the muscles supplied by the ulnar nerve's deep branch, and recognise that adductor pollicis is the exception to "thenar = median, hypothenar = ulnar."

## pitfalls
Assuming all thumb muscles are median-supplied because they sit in the thenar region. Adductor pollicis is anatomically near the thenar eminence but is functionally and developmentally grouped with the ulnar-supplied intrinsic hand muscles, not the thenar group proper (abductor pollicis brevis, flexor pollicis brevis, opponens pollicis).

## label
The recurrent (motor) branch of the median nerve, given off just distal to the flexor retinaculum before the nerve enters the palm proper, supplies the thenar muscles alone — its injury (e.g. a laceration at the base of the thumb) weakens opposition and abduction of the thumb with no sensory loss

## id
CON-MSK-A29C475E1D8B63

## canonical_key
median-nerve-recurrent-motor-branch-thenar-injury

## aliases
Recurrent branch of median nerve
Median nerve motor branch to thenar muscles

## definition
Just after the median nerve emerges from the carpal tunnel, it gives off a small recurrent (motor, or "thenar") branch that curls back proximally and laterally to supply the three thenar muscles — abductor pollicis brevis, flexor pollicis brevis and opponens pollicis. Because this branch is purely motor and lies superficially at the base of the thenar eminence, a laceration there (classically from broken glass) can sever it in isolation, producing weak thumb abduction and opposition with no sensory deficit — unlike a carpal tunnel lesion or a more proximal median nerve injury, which also carries sensory loss over the lateral three and a half digits.

## explicit_objective
Localise the recurrent (motor) branch of the median nerve, name the muscles it alone supplies, and explain why its isolated injury produces motor loss without sensory loss.

## pitfalls
Assuming any median nerve injury must include sensory loss. The recurrent branch's superficial, purely-motor course at the base of the thenar eminence is exactly what makes an isolated, sensation-sparing injury possible there — a detail the vignette's "no sensory deficits were reported" line is testing directly.

## definition

## label
A Trendelenburg (waddling) gait, the pelvis falling toward the unsupported side at each step, follows injury to the superior gluteal nerve and the resulting weakness of gluteus medius and minimus

## id
CON-MSK-F384A2617B9C05

## canonical_key
trendelenburg-gait-superior-gluteal-nerve-injury

## aliases
Trendelenburg gait
Superior gluteal nerve injury
Waddling gait

## definition
Gluteus medius and gluteus minimus, both supplied by the superior gluteal nerve, act during single-leg stance to hold the pelvis level by pulling the ipsilateral hip down relative to the contralateral, unsupported side. Injury to the superior gluteal nerve — for example from a deep gluteal wound or iatrogenic damage during hip surgery approaches through this region — weakens this action, so on the injured side's stance phase the contralateral (unsupported) side of the pelvis drops instead, producing the waddling, side-to-side gait named for Trendelenburg. This is distinct from inferior gluteal nerve injury, which weakens gluteus maximus (hip extension) rather than pelvic stabilisation during gait.

## explicit_objective
Explain the mechanism of a Trendelenburg gait as loss of pelvic stabilisation by gluteus medius/minimus following superior gluteal nerve injury, and distinguish it from inferior gluteal nerve injury.

## pitfalls
Attributing a waddling gait to the inferior gluteal nerve because gluteus maximus is the larger, more familiar gluteal muscle. The pelvis-stabilising action tested by gait is specifically gluteus medius/minimus (superior gluteal nerve); gluteus maximus (inferior gluteal nerve) mainly extends the hip and does not stabilise the pelvis during single-leg stance in the same way.

Cathelicidin is an antimicrobial peptide produced by keratinocytes that acts as a broad-spectrum natural antibiotic in the skin

## id
CON-DER-C26E495D3A8F71

## canonical_key
cathelicidin-keratinocyte-antimicrobial-peptide

## aliases
Cathelicidin
Skin antimicrobial peptides

## definition
Keratinocytes produce several antimicrobial peptides as part of the skin's innate chemical barrier, most notably cathelicidin (and, complementing it, the defensins). Cathelicidin disrupts the cell membranes of a broad range of bacteria, some fungi and enveloped viruses, giving the skin surface a constant, non-adaptive "natural antibiotic" defence that operates without needing prior exposure to the pathogen — distinct from the skin's mechanical barrier (the stratum corneum) and its acidic surface pH, which are separate, complementary defences.

## explicit_objective
Identify cathelicidin as a keratinocyte-produced antimicrobial peptide providing broad-spectrum, non-specific chemical defence at the skin surface.

## pitfalls
Confusing cathelicidin with defensins (a related but chemically distinct family) or with the skin's mechanical/physical barrier functions — the question asks specifically for the "natural antibiotic," i.e. the chemical antimicrobial peptide, not the physical barrier.

## id

Core body temperature range
Normal body temperature

## definition
Core body temperature is held within a narrow physiological range, classically cited as about 36.5-37.2°C, by the hypothalamic thermoregulatory centre balancing heat production (metabolism, shivering) against heat loss (radiation, convection, evaporation via sweating, and behavioural adjustment). This narrow range is what makes body temperature a useful vital sign — deviations outside it (fever from a raised hypothalamic set point, or hypothermia from heat loss exceeding production) are clinically significant precisely because the normal range is so tightly held.

## explicit_objective
State the normal physiological range for human core body temperature (approximately 36.5-37.2°C).

## pitfalls
Confusing core temperature with skin/peripheral temperature, which is normally lower and fluctuates more with ambient conditions — the "normal range" tested here is the core (internal) temperature the hypothalamus actively defends.

Lipid-soluble molecules penetrate intact skin most readily, since they can dissolve through the lipid-rich stratum corneum rather than needing an aqueous or active-transport route

## id

Skin permeability lipid-soluble molecules

## definition
The stratum corneum, the skin's outermost barrier layer, is built from corneocytes embedded in a lipid-rich intercellular matrix (ceramides, cholesterol and free fatty acids arranged in lamellar sheets). This structure makes the stratum corneum a formidable barrier to water-soluble substances, ions and large molecules (proteins, polysaccharides, nucleic acids), which cannot easily cross it, while small, lipid-soluble (lipophilic) molecules dissolve directly into and diffuse through the lipid matrix — the physiological basis for transdermal drug delivery systems (patches), which are formulated to be lipid-soluble for exactly this reason.

## pitfalls
Assuming water-soluble or ionic substances cross skin easily because skin is a living, hydrated tissue — the rate-limiting barrier is specifically the lipid-rich stratum corneum, which favours lipophilic over hydrophilic penetration.

The COX inhibition shared by most NSAIDs gives them a common triad of effects — antipyretic, analgesic and anti-inflammatory — distinguishing them from narcotic analgesics, which relieve pain without any of the other two

## definition
Because nearly all NSAIDs work through the same core mechanism — inhibiting cyclooxygenase and thereby reducing prostaglandin synthesis in peripheral tissue — they share a characteristic triad of clinical effects: antipyretic (reduced hypothalamic prostaglandin-mediated fever), analgesic (reduced peripheral nociceptor sensitisation by prostaglandins) and anti-inflammatory (reduced prostaglandin-driven vasodilation, oedema and pain at sites of inflammation). Picking an option combining an NSAID property with an opioid-specific one (e.g. "analgesic, immunosuppressive, anti-inflammatory," or "narcotic, analgesic, anti-inflammatory") — the correct triad is specifically antipyretic + analgesic + anti-inflammatory, all downstream of the one shared COX-inhibition mechanism.

## label
Celecoxib is a selective COX-2 inhibitor, preferred over non-selective NSAIDs when a patient needs anti-inflammatory/analgesic therapy but carries a higher gastrointestinal (or antiplatelet-sensitive) risk, since it spares the COX-1-mediated protective gastric prostaglandins and platelet thromboxane

## id
CON-PHM-E9A6B384F1C275

## canonical_key
celecoxib-selective-cox2-gi-sparing

## aliases
Celecoxib selective COX-2 inhibitor
COX-2 selective NSAID

## definition
COX-1 is constitutively expressed and maintains protective gastric mucus/bicarbonate secretion and platelet thromboxane A2 production, while COX-2 is the inducible isoform mainly responsible for prostaglandin-driven inflammation and pain. Non-selective NSAIDs (aspirin, ibuprofen, diclofenac, indomethacin) inhibit both isoforms, which is why they carry gastric and antiplatelet risk alongside their therapeutic effect. Celecoxib selectively inhibits COX-2 while relatively sparing COX-1, giving comparable anti-inflammatory/analgesic benefit with less gastric mucosal damage — making it a preferred choice for a patient (for example, one with chronic pain who also has significant hyperacidity or peptic ulcer history) who needs NSAID-class therapy but is at elevated GI risk. It is, however, avoided in patients with a history of cardiovascular disease such as myocardial infarction, since COX-2 selective inhibition shifts the vascular prostacyclin/thromboxane balance toward a prothrombotic state.

## explicit_objective
Identify celecoxib as a selective COX-2 inhibitor, explain why that selectivity reduces GI risk relative to non-selective NSAIDs, and recognise cardiovascular disease as a contraindication to its use.

## pitfalls
Assuming "selective" NSAIDs are simply safer NSAIDs with no trade-off. Celecoxib trades reduced GI/antiplatelet risk for an increased cardiovascular risk, which is exactly why a history of myocardial infarction — not merely GI risk — is the more compelling reason to avoid it in a given patient.

## label
Methotrexate's most common adverse effect is hepatotoxicity; methotrexate toxicity (including hepatotoxicity) is reversed with folinic acid (leucovorin), which bypasses its block of dihydrofolate reductase

## id
CON-PHM-F1B7C495A2D386

## canonical_key
methotrexate-hepatotoxicity-and-folinic-acid-rescue

## aliases
Methotrexate hepatotoxicity
Folinic acid rescue
Leucovorin rescue

## definition
Methotrexate, used as a DMARD in rheumatoid arthritis (and at higher doses in oncology), inhibits dihydrofolate reductase, blocking the regeneration of tetrahydrofolate needed for DNA synthesis. Its most common adverse effect at DMARD doses is hepatotoxicity (elevated liver enzymes, and with cumulative use, risk of fibrosis), alongside bone marrow suppression and mucositis. Because its toxicity stems from folate depletion, it is treated or prevented with folinic acid (leucovorin, also called citrovorum factor) — a reduced folate that bypasses the dihydrofolate reductase block and directly restocks the cell's usable folate pool, rescuing normal cells from methotrexate's antimetabolite effect.

## explicit_objective
State that hepatotoxicity is methotrexate's most common adverse effect, and that folinic acid (leucovorin) reverses methotrexate toxicity by bypassing dihydrofolate reductase.

## pitfalls
Confusing folinic acid (leucovorin, a reduced/active folate that bypasses the enzyme block) with folic acid (the oxidised, inactive form, which must itself be reduced by the very enzyme methotrexate blocks and so cannot rescue acute toxicity as effectively).

Anti-CD20 antibody

## definition
Rituximab is a chimeric monoclonal antibody — part mouse-derived, part human-derived, distinguishing it from a fully humanised or fully human antibody — directed against CD20, a surface marker expressed on B lymphocytes from the pre-B stage through to mature B cells (but not on the earliest stem cells or on fully differentiated plasma cells). Binding CD20 triggers B-cell depletion through complement- and antibody-dependent cytotoxicity, which is the basis for rituximab's use as a biologic DMARD in rheumatoid arthritis refractory to other agents, alongside its original oncology indication in B-cell lymphomas.

## explicit_objective
Identify rituximab as a chimeric anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody and state its mechanism (B-lymphocyte depletion).

## pitfalls
Confusing rituximab's target (CD20, a B-cell marker) with TNF-alpha (the target of infliximab/etanercept/adalimumab) or IL-6 receptor (tocilizumab) — several biologic DMARDs are usually listed together as options, each with a distinct molecular target.
