The brainstem has three parts stacked medulla-pons-midbrain, with the medulla lowest and continuous with the spinal cord and the midbrain highest and continuous with the cerebrum

## arabic_label

## definition
The brainstem connects the spinal cord to the cerebrum and is built of three parts stacked one on the other. The medulla oblongata is the lowest part, continuous below with the spinal cord at the foramen magnum. The pons lies above the medulla, in the middle of the three. The midbrain is the highest part, continuous above with the diencephalon and cerebrum. A question naming "the lower part of the brainstem" is asking for the medulla, and a question naming "the upper part" is asking for the midbrain, with the pons as the unnamed middle part between them.

## explicit_objective
State which of the three brainstem parts is lowest (medulla), middle (pons) and highest (midbrain).

## pitfalls
Swapping medulla and midbrain when a question asks only for "upper" or "lower" without naming the pons. The order to fix in memory is medulla-pons-midbrain from the spinal cord upward, so the medulla stays lowest and the midbrain stays highest regardless of which two ends a question tests.

## definition
Each cerebral hemisphere contains one lateral ventricle, a C-shaped, fluid-filled cavity that is the largest of the brain's four ventricles. The two lateral ventricles communicate with the single midline third ventricle, which lies in the diencephalon, through the interventricular foramina. A question naming "the cavity of the cerebral hemisphere" is naming the lateral ventricle specifically, as distinct from the third ventricle (diencephalon), the cerebral aqueduct (midbrain) and the fourth ventricle (between pons/medulla and cerebellum).

## explicit_objective
State that the lateral ventricle is the cavity contained within each cerebral hemisphere, one of the brain's four ventricles.

## pitfalls
Assigning the lateral ventricle to the diencephalon or midbrain. Each of the brain's subdivisions has its own named ventricle or aqueduct — cerebral hemisphere (lateral), diencephalon (third), midbrain (cerebral aqueduct), hindbrain (fourth) — and only the cerebral hemisphere's is called "lateral".

# Item

## label
The brain has four ventricles — the two lateral ventricles, the third and the fourth — all filled with and connected by the circulating cerebrospinal fluid

## id
CON-NEU-88E3F3DA93D02A

## canonical_key
neuroanatomy.ventricles.four-csf-filled-cavities

## aliases
Ventricles of the brain
CSF-filled cavities

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases

## definition
The brain contains four ventricles, all lined by ependymal cells and filled with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). The two lateral ventricles, one in each cerebral hemisphere, drain through the interventricular foramina into the single third ventricle in the diencephalon. The third ventricle continues through the narrow cerebral aqueduct in the midbrain into the fourth ventricle, which lies between the pons/medulla and the cerebellum and opens into the subarachnoid space. This chain is what makes the ventricles, collectively, "the cavities inside the brain filled with CSF" rather than any one of the meningeal or subarachnoid CSF spaces.

## explicit_objective
State that the four brain ventricles (two lateral, third, fourth) are the CSF-filled cavities inside the brain, as opposed to the central canal, cisterns or sinuses.

## pitfalls
Confusing "ventricles" with "cisterns" or "sinuses". Cisterns are subarachnoid CSF pools outside the brain substance, and venous sinuses carry blood, not CSF; only the four named ventricles are the CSF-filled cavities within the brain itself.

## definition
Most of the cerebral cortex is folded into gyri separated by shallow sulci that vary between individuals, but a handful of sulci are constant enough to be named and used as landmarks: the central sulcus (separating frontal and parietal lobes), the lateral sulcus (separating temporal from frontal/parietal), the parieto-occipital sulcus (separating parietal from occipital) and the calcarine sulcus (on the medial surface of the occipital lobe, marking the primary visual cortex). Of these four, the calcarine sulcus is the one specific to the occipital lobe's medial surface and to vision.

## explicit_objective
Name the calcarine sulcus as one of the brain's four major, constantly present sulci, and locate it on the medial occipital lobe.

## pitfalls
Treating every fold on the cerebral cortex as equally landmark-worthy. Most sulci vary between individuals and are not named; only the central, lateral, parieto-occipital and calcarine sulci are constant enough to serve as fixed landmarks.

## label
Nucleus, ganglion, tract and bundle are the four basic organisational terms of the nervous system, distinguished by CNS-versus-PNS location and by shared-versus-mixed fibre origin

## id
CON-NEU-A3F2BBBF499EAA

## canonical_key
neuroanatomy.terminology.nucleus-ganglion-tract-bundle

## aliases
Ganglion definition
Nucleus definition (nervous system)
Tract versus bundle

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases
[clear]

## definition
Four terms organise how nerve cells and fibres are described. A nucleus is an aggregation of nerve cell bodies inside the central nervous system. A ganglion is an aggregation of nerve cell bodies outside the central nervous system, in the peripheral nervous system. A tract is a collection of nerve fibres that share the same origin, termination and function. A bundle is a collection of nerve fibres that do not all share the same origin, termination or function. The first pair (nucleus/ganglion) is split by CNS-versus-PNS location; the second pair (tract/bundle) is split by whether the fibres inside it are functionally uniform.

## explicit_objective
Distinguish nucleus from ganglion by CNS-versus-PNS location, and tract from bundle by whether the fibres share one origin, termination and function.

## pitfalls
Assuming "bundle" is simply a synonym for any collection of fibres. A bundle is specifically a collection whose fibres do not all share the same origin, termination and function — the opposite case from a tract, where they do.

## label
A synapse is named by what the presynaptic axon terminal meets — another neuron's dendrite (axo-dendritic), cell body (axo-somatic) or axon (axo-axonic), or a muscle fibre at the motor end plate

## id
CON-NEU-4DEBC99A5D71DD

## canonical_key

## aliases
Types of synapse
Axo-somatic synapse

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases

## definition
A synapse is the site where one neuron's axon terminal communicates with the next excitable cell, and synapses are classified by what that terminal meets. An axo-dendritic synapse meets the dendrites of another neuron. An axo-somatic synapse meets the cell body (soma) of another neuron. An axo-axonic synapse meets the axon of another neuron. When the axon terminal instead meets a skeletal muscle fibre rather than another neuron, the specialised junction is called the motor end plate rather than a synapse of these three types.

## explicit_objective
Classify a described neuron-to-neuron contact as axo-dendritic, axo-somatic or axo-axonic by naming which part of the second neuron the axon terminal meets.

## pitfalls
Calling the axon-to-muscle junction an "axo-somatic synapse". The three axo- terms name neuron-to-neuron contacts specifically; the axon-to-muscle-fibre junction has its own separate name, the motor end plate.

A question asking which brainstem level(s) the parasympathetic cranial outflow arises from is therefore answered by all three — midbrain, pons and medulla together — not by any one of them alone.

## explicit_objective
State that the parasympathetic cranial outflow leaves the brainstem at three levels together (midbrain via III, pons via VII, medulla via IX/X), not from a single level.

## pitfalls
Picking only one brainstem level because a single cranial nerve (such as the vagus) is the most familiar parasympathetic carrier. The cranial outflow as a whole spans midbrain, pons and medulla together, through four different cranial nerves (III, VII, IX, X), not through the vagus alone.

## pitfalls
