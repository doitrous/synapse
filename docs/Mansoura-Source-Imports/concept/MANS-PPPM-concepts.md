An additive effect is exactly the sum of two same-effect drugs' individual effects; synergism exceeds that sum, potentiation is one inactive-or-weak agent boosting an active one, and antagonism reduces the combined effect

## definition
When two drugs with the same effect are given together, the outcome is classified by how the combined effect compares with the two individual effects taken separately. An additive effect is exactly the arithmetic sum of the two individual effects. Synergism is a combined effect greater than that simple sum. Potentiation is the specific case where one agent, with little or no effect of its own, increases the effect of a second, active drug. Antagonism is a combined effect smaller than expected, one drug reducing the action of the other.

## explicit_objective
Classify a described combination of two drugs' effects as additive, synergistic, potentiating or antagonistic from how the net effect compares with the individual effects.

## pitfalls
Treating "synergism" and "potentiation" as interchangeable. Synergism describes two active drugs whose combined effect exceeds the simple sum; potentiation specifically involves an agent with little or no effect of its own boosting a second, active drug. Also confusing an additive effect (exactly the sum) with synergism (more than the sum) — the arithmetic comparison, not just "the effects add up", is what distinguishes them.

## definition
Salbutamol (albuterol) is a short-acting adrenergic agonist selective for beta-2 receptors over beta-1. Selective beta-2 stimulation relaxes bronchial smooth muscle, producing bronchodilation, while producing relatively little of the cardiac (beta-1-mediated) stimulation seen with non-selective agonists such as isoprenaline or ephedrine. This selectivity makes salbutamol the standard bronchodilator for quick relief of acute asthma attacks and, alongside longer-acting agents, for chronic asthma management, whereas non-selective beta blockers such as propranolol or timolol are avoided in asthma because they can precipitate bronchospasm.

## explicit_objective
Identify salbutamol as the beta-2 selective adrenergic agonist used as a bronchodilator in both acute and chronic asthma, distinguishing it from non-selective agonists and from beta blockers.

## pitfalls
Confusing a beta-2 selective agonist (bronchodilator, used in asthma) with a beta blocker (contraindicated in asthma) purely because both names mention "beta". Also assuming any adrenergic agonist relieves bronchospasm — only beta-2 selective agonism does so cleanly; agents with mixed alpha/beta1/beta2 activity, such as ephedrine, carry more cardiovascular side effects for the same bronchodilator benefit.

## definition
Neostigmine is an indirect-acting, reversible cholinesterase inhibitor. By blocking acetylcholinesterase, it raises acetylcholine levels at muscarinic receptors in the gut wall, stimulating smooth muscle contraction and increasing gastrointestinal motility. This is the opposite clinical direction from antimuscarinic drugs (atropine, scopolamine, ipratropium, benztropine), which instead reduce GIT motility and secretions, and is why neostigmine is used clinically for postoperative paralytic ileus and for urinary retention from bladder atony.

## explicit_objective
Identify neostigmine as increasing GIT motility via cholinesterase inhibition and muscarinic stimulation, distinguishing it from antimuscarinic drugs that reduce motility.

## pitfalls
Confusing neostigmine's GIT-motility use with its more commonly tested neuromuscular-junction use (reversing non-depolarising neuromuscular blockade). Both actions share the same underlying mechanism — raising acetylcholine by inhibiting cholinesterase — but act at different receptor sites (muscarinic in the gut versus nicotinic at the neuromuscular junction) and serve different clinical purposes.

## id

Sodium channel blockade

## arabic_label

## definition
Local anaesthetic drugs act by binding voltage-gated sodium channels in the neuronal cell membrane, blocking the rapid sodium influx needed for the rising (depolarising) phase of the action potential. By decreasing the membrane's permeability to sodium, they prevent the nerve fibre from generating or propagating an action potential past the blocked segment, producing local, reversible loss of sensation. This mechanism is distinct from that of neuromuscular blockers (which act on nicotinic receptors), cholinesterase inhibitors (which raise acetylcholine at the synapse) or drugs that block transmitter release from nerve terminals.

## explicit_objective
State that local anaesthetics act by blocking voltage-gated sodium channels, decreasing membrane sodium permeability to prevent nerve impulse conduction.

## pitfalls
Confusing the sodium-channel mechanism of local anaesthetics with mechanisms acting at synaptic receptors (nicotinic blockade, acetylcholinesterase inhibition) or at transmitter release — local anaesthetics act on axonal conduction itself, upstream of any synapse, not at a synaptic receptor.

## definition
Alpha-adrenergic antagonists differ in receptor-subtype selectivity. Yohimbine is a selective alpha-2 antagonist; because alpha-2 receptors are presynaptic autoreceptors that normally inhibit further noradrenaline release, blocking them increases sympathetic outflow. Tamsulosin is selective for the alpha-1A and alpha-1D subtypes that predominate in prostatic and bladder-neck smooth muscle, relative to the alpha-1B subtype that predominates in vascular smooth muscle; this subtype selectivity is why tamsulosin relieves the smooth-muscle-mediated urinary symptoms of benign prostatic hyperplasia with comparatively little effect on blood pressure, unlike the broader alpha-1 blockade of prazosin or the non-selective, irreversible blockade of phenoxybenzamine.

## explicit_objective
Distinguish yohimbine's selective alpha-2 antagonism from tamsulosin's selective alpha-1A/alpha-1D antagonism, and state why tamsulosin's uroselectivity spares blood pressure relative to less selective alpha-1 blockers.

## pitfalls
Assuming every alpha blocker lowers blood pressure to the same degree — subtype selectivity (alpha-1A/alpha-1D versus alpha-1B versus alpha-2) is exactly what separates a uroselective drug such as tamsulosin, with little BP effect, from broader alpha-1 blockers such as prazosin, with a significant antihypertensive effect.
