The definitive host is the host in which a parasite reaches sexual maturity (its adult stage)
## id
CON-INF-54A77E6DD1F2CA
## canonical_key
parasitology.foundations.definitive-host-adult-sexual-maturity
## aliases
Final host
Site of parasite sexual maturity
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases
[clear]
## definition
The definitive (final) host is the host in which a parasite reaches sexual maturity -- either by sexual reproduction, or by completing its adult development for parasites that reproduce asexually. This is distinct from an intermediate host (which carries a larval/asexual developmental stage), a paratenic host (which merely transports the infective stage without further development) and a dead-end host (in which the parasite cannot complete its cycle or transmit onward).
## explicit_objective
State that the definitive host is the host in which a parasite reaches sexual maturity (the adult stage), correctly distinguishing it from intermediate, paratenic and dead-end hosts.
## pitfalls
Confusing the definitive host with the intermediate host, or assuming any host that merely carries the parasite (paratenic, dead-end) is where it matures.

Wire screening (mesh fitted over windows, doors and other openings) is a mechanical mosquito-control measure that physically excludes the winged, host-seeking adult mosquito from entering a building. ## pitfalls
Assuming any mosquito-control measure acts on the aquatic larval/pupal stages by default; screening is specifically an adult-exclusion method.

## definition
Trematodes (flukes) characteristically require a freshwater snail as their first intermediate host: the miracidium penetrates the snail and develops through sporocyst and redia stages into free-swimming cercariae. This snail-obligate step distinguishes trematode life cycles from those of cestodes (which use arthropod or vertebrate intermediate hosts), nematodes (mostly direct or arthropod/vertebrate-hosted) and intestinal/urogenital protozoa (which use no intermediate host at all).
## explicit_objective
Identify Trematoda as the helminth group whose life cycle requires a snail intermediate host.
## pitfalls
Assuming any helminth with an intermediate host uses a snail; only trematodes are snail-obligate in this way, while cestodes and nematodes use different intermediate hosts.

Flagella (in Mastigophora, e.g. Giardia, Trypanosoma, Trichomonas) and pseudopodia (in Sarcodina, e.g. Entamoeba) are both surface/cytoplasmic organelles whose function is locomotion: the flagellum propels the organism by a whip-like beat, while pseudopodia produce amoeboid movement by cytoplasmic flow. Each organelle is the defining, group-naming locomotor feature of its respective classical protozoan group, parallel to ciliary movement in Ciliophora.

State that both flagella and pseudopodia function as locomotor organelles, identifying which protozoan group each defines.
## pitfalls
Assigning a non-locomotor function (nutrition, secretion, excretion, respiration) to flagella or pseudopodia, or confusing which classical group each organelle defines.

## label
Specific (obligatory) myiasis is fly larvae invading living tissue; myiasis is diagnosed by finding the larvae
## id
CON-INF-D5E61BA9B36DB1
## canonical_key
parasitology.myiasis.definition-specific-form-diagnosis
## aliases
Myiasis definition and diagnosis
Obligatory myiasis living-tissue invasion
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases
[clear]
## definition
Myiasis is the invasion of human (or other vertebrate) tissue by fly larvae. In its specific (obligatory) form, larvae -- such as Dermatobia hominis, Cordylobia anthropophaga and Hypoderma -- require living tissue to complete development and actively invade it, unlike semi-specific (facultative) species that normally breed in dead/decaying matter and only opportunistically enter necrotic wound tissue, or accidental myiasis from incidentally ingested/inhaled larvae. Myiasis is diagnosed directly by finding and identifying the larvae themselves within the affected tissue, rather than by an indirect laboratory test.
## explicit_objective
Define myiasis as fly-larva tissue invasion, distinguish specific (living-tissue) myiasis from semi-specific and accidental forms, and state that diagnosis is by direct larval identification.
## pitfalls
Confusing specific myiasis (living tissue, obligatory) with semi-specific myiasis (dead/necrotic tissue, facultative) or accidental myiasis (incidental ingestion/inhalation); assuming an indirect test (serology, skin test) is used for a condition confirmed by direct visualisation.

## label
Cestodes are identified by a segmented body (chain of proglottids) and, in armed species, a scolex bearing a rostellum with hooks
## id
CON-INF-C405EA15163815
## canonical_key
parasitology.helminths.cestode-segmentation-rostellum-hooks
## aliases
Cestode identifying features
Tapeworm body plan
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases
[clear]
## definition
Cestodes (tapeworms) have a body built as a chain of repeating segments (proglottids), budded from a growth zone behind the scolex (head). In armed species (e.g. Taenia solium, Echinococcus granulosus), the scolex additionally bears a rostellum -- a small, retractable, hook-bearing protrusion -- alongside suckers; unarmed species (e.g. Taenia saginata) have suckers but lack the rostellum and hooks. Segmentation and, where present, an armed rostellum together distinguish cestodes from the unsegmented nematodes and trematodes.
## explicit_objective
Identify segmentation (proglottid chain) and an armed rostellum with hooks as the classical cestode-identifying features, distinguishing cestodes from nematodes, trematodes and protozoa.
## pitfalls
Assuming any helminth can be segmented, or that all cestode species have a rostellum with hooks (only armed species do; unarmed species have suckers only).

## definition
Tick paralysis is an ascending flaccid paralysis caused by a neurotoxin secreted in the saliva of certain feeding female ticks (e.g. Dermacentor species), which blocks neuromuscular transmission. It characteristically resolves rapidly once the feeding tick is found and removed, a feature that helps distinguish it from other causes of acute paralysis.
## explicit_objective
Identify the tick as the arthropod that causes leg (ascending) paralysis via a salivary neurotoxin, and state that removal of the tick resolves it.
## pitfalls
Confusing tick paralysis (a direct salivary-toxin effect, rapidly reversible on tick removal) with a tick-transmitted infectious disease requiring treatment (e.g. Lyme disease, Texas cattle fever), or attributing paralysis to a different arthropod (mosquito, flea, louse, mite).

## definition
Sarcoptes scabiei is an obligate ectoparasite of human skin: the fertilised female burrows into the epidermis (stratum corneum), creating linear burrows where she lays eggs, producing the intense itching of scabies -- most often at the finger webs, wrists, elbows and other skin-fold sites. This intraepidermal burrowing habitat, confined strictly to the skin, defines the mite's parasitic niche.
## explicit_objective
State that Sarcoptes scabiei's habitat is the epidermis of human skin, where it burrows and lays eggs.
## pitfalls
Assigning Sarcoptes scabiei a habitat outside the skin (mucosal surfaces, gut, conjunctiva); those are not where this particular mite lives.
