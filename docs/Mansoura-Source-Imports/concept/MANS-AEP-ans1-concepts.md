## label
Sympathetic supply to the pelvic viscera contracts the vas deferens, seminal vesicles and prostate to produce ejaculation, and vasoconstricts the vessels of the external genitalia, causing shrinkage rather than the erection produced by the parasympathetic pelvic nerve

## id

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases

## definition
Sympathetic fibres to the pelvic viscera (from the lower thoracic and upper lumbar preganglionic outflow, relaying in the paravertebral and pelvic sympathetic chain) contract the smooth muscle of the vas deferens, seminal vesicles and prostate — the ejaculatory response — and vasoconstrict the blood vessels of the sex organs and external genitalia, producing shrinkage of the penis rather than the vasodilatation that produces erection. This is the functional opposite of the parasympathetic pelvic nerve (nervi erigentes, S2-S4), which vasodilates the same vessels to produce erection, so the two outflows together give the sexual response its two phases: parasympathetic-driven erection and sympathetic-driven ejaculation.

## explicit_objective
State that sympathetic stimulation of the pelvic viscera produces ejaculation (contraction of vas deferens/seminal vesicles/prostate) and vasoconstriction/shrinkage of the external genitalia, contrasting this with the parasympathetic vasodilator (erectile) effect.

## pitfalls
Attributing vasodilatation, erection, or micturition to sympathetic pelvic outflow — these are parasympathetic (pelvic nerve) effects; sympathetic pelvic outflow instead vasoconstricts (shrinkage) and drives ejaculation, and it INHIBITS micturition and defecation (by contracting the internal urethral and anal sphincters) rather than mediating them.

## label
The parasympathetic pelvic nerve (nervi erigentes, S2-S4) vasodilates the blood vessels of the external genitalia to produce erection — the functional opposite of the vasoconstrictor, ejaculation-producing sympathetic pelvic outflow

## definition
The pelvic nerve (nervi erigentes), carrying parasympathetic preganglionic fibres from spinal segments S2 to S4, is the parasympathetic supply to the pelvic viscera. Its stimulation vasodilates the blood vessels of the external genitalia (producing erection, the vascular-engorgement phase of the sexual response), and elsewhere mediates micturition (bladder detrusor contraction with internal urethral sphincter relaxation) and defecation-promoting effects on the rectum — functions the sympathetic pelvic outflow instead inhibits. It does not affect bronchial smooth muscle, which is supplied by the vagus, not the pelvic nerve.

## explicit_objective
State that pelvic nerve (parasympathetic, S2-S4) stimulation vasodilates the external genitalia's blood vessels to produce erection, contrasting this with the vasoconstrictor, ejaculation-producing sympathetic pelvic outflow.

## pitfalls
Confusing the pelvic nerve's own effects with those of the internal anal or urethral sphincters, which it RELAXES (permitting defecation/micturition) rather than contracts — sphincter contraction is instead the sympathetic (retention) effect; and assigning it any role in bronchial smooth muscle, which is outside the pelvic nerve's distribution.

The adrenal medulla is a modified sympathetic ganglion that receives its preganglionic supply directly, via cholinergic fibres from the lower thoracic spinal cord segments' lateral horn (not the anterior/motor horn), and secretes catecholamines continuously at a basal rate that decreases during sleep

Basal adrenal medullary secretion

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases

## definition
The adrenal medulla is functionally a modified sympathetic ganglion: unlike other sympathetic effector organs, which receive postganglionic fibres, it is innervated directly by preganglionic cholinergic sympathetic fibres, arising from the lateral horn (intermediolateral cell column) of the lower thoracic spinal cord segments — not the anterior (motor) horn, which instead gives rise to somatic motor neurons. On reaching the medulla these preganglionic fibres synapse directly on the chromaffin cells, which act as the "postganglionic" secretory cells, releasing catecholamines into the blood rather than onto a synaptic effector. This secretion is not all-or-none: it is basal and continuous, reflecting ongoing sympathetic tone, and its rate normally falls during sleep, when overall sympathetic activity is reduced.

## explicit_objective
State that the adrenal medulla receives its sympathetic supply directly as preganglionic cholinergic fibres from the lateral (not anterior) horn of the lower thoracic cord, and that its catecholamine secretion is basal, continuous, and decreased during sleep.

## pitfalls
Attributing the adrenal medulla's preganglionic supply to the ANTERIOR horn — the anterior horn gives rise to somatic motor neurons; sympathetic preganglionic neurons instead arise from the LATERAL horn (intermediolateral column). Also assuming adrenal medullary secretion is off except during stress — a basal, tonic level of secretion continues at rest and falls further during sleep.

## definition
The parasympathetic (craniosacral) outflow from the brainstem travels in only four of the twelve cranial nerves: the oculomotor (III, to the sphincter pupillae and ciliary muscle), facial (VII, to the lacrimal, submandibular and sublingual glands), glossopharyngeal (IX, to the parotid gland via the otic ganglion) and vagus (X, to thoracic and abdominal viscera). The remaining cranial nerves — including the abducent (VI), trochlear (IV), optic (II, a sensory tract rather than a peripheral nerve) and accessory (XI, a motor nerve to the sternocleidomastoid and trapezius) — carry no parasympathetic fibres.

## explicit_objective
Identify the glossopharyngeal (IX) nerve as a carrier of parasympathetic fibres (to the parotid gland), and name III, VII and X as the other three cranial nerves that carry parasympathetic fibres.

## pitfalls
Assuming any cranial nerve besides III, VII, IX and X carries parasympathetic fibres — the abducent, trochlear, optic and accessory nerves listed as distractors here are all purely motor or sensory, with no autonomic component.

## label
Sympathetic ganglia sit close to the spinal cord (paravertebral chain), giving sympathetic outflow short preganglionic and long postganglionic fibres whose cell bodies originate at a distance from the effector organ, while parasympathetic ganglia sit at or within the effector organ, giving long preganglionic and short postganglionic fibres

## id
CON-NEU-3DC19307F11AB3

## canonical_key
autonomic.sympathetic-vs-parasympathetic-ganglion-location-fibre-length

## aliases
Sympathetic vs parasympathetic ganglion location
Preganglionic and postganglionic fibre length
Paravertebral chain ganglia

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases

## definition
Sympathetic and parasympathetic outflows differ systematically in where their ganglia sit and how that shapes fibre length. Sympathetic ganglia (the paravertebral chain and the prevertebral/collateral ganglia) lie close to the spinal cord, so sympathetic preganglionic fibres are short while the postganglionic fibres that continue on to the effector organ are long — meaning the postganglionic neuron's own cell body originates at a distance from (distal to, in the sense of being far from) the effector organ it eventually supplies. Parasympathetic ganglia, by contrast, lie at or within the wall of the effector organ itself (terminal ganglia), so parasympathetic preganglionic fibres are long and the postganglionic fibres are short.

## explicit_objective
State that sympathetic postganglionic neurons originate at a distance from the effector organ (short preganglionic, long postganglionic fibres, ganglia near the spinal cord), the opposite arrangement to the parasympathetic system.

## pitfalls
Assuming sympathetic postganglionic neurons are short, myelinated, or purely cholinergic — they are LONG, generally UNMYELINATED, and mostly ADRENERGIC (noradrenergic), with sweat glands as a notable cholinergic exception; and reversing which division has long preganglionic versus long postganglionic fibres (sympathetic: short pre-/long post-; parasympathetic: long pre-/short post-).

## label
Sympathetic outflow to the head and neck originates from the T1-T2 spinal segments, ascending the cervical sympathetic chain to relay in the superior, middle and inferior cervical ganglia before reaching the eye, face and neck structures

## id
CON-NEU-6D0F9F0CBDD4E7

## canonical_key
autonomic.sympathetic-outflow-to-head-and-neck

## aliases
Sympathetic origin to head and neck
T1-T2 sympathetic outflow
Cervical sympathetic chain origin

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases
[clear]

## definition
Although the sympathetic preganglionic outflow as a whole arises from the T1 to L2/L3 spinal cord segments (the thoracolumbar outflow), the fibres that ultimately supply the head and neck originate specifically from the upper end of that range — the T1 and T2 segments. These preganglionic fibres ascend within the sympathetic trunk (without synapsing at their own level) to relay in the cervical sympathetic ganglia — chiefly the superior cervical ganglion — before postganglionic fibres are distributed to the eye (dilator pupillae, superior tarsal muscle), the sweat glands and blood vessels of the face, and other head and neck structures. This is why a lesion anywhere along this T1-T2-to-superior-cervical-ganglion pathway, including cervical spinal cord injury, can produce a Horner's-type picture.

## explicit_objective
State that sympathetic outflow to the head and neck originates from the T1-T2 spinal segments and ascends the cervical sympathetic chain to relay in the cervical ganglia.

## pitfalls
Assuming head-and-neck sympathetic outflow originates lower in the thoracic cord (e.g. T3-T4 or below) — it specifically starts at the rostral end of the thoracolumbar outflow, T1-T2, which is also why lower cervical/upper thoracic spinal injuries can interrupt it and produce a Horner's-type picture.

The adrenal medulla secretes epinephrine and norepinephrine in roughly an 80:20 ratio, making epinephrine the most abundant catecholamine signalling molecule it releases

## definition
The adrenal medulla synthesises and secretes both epinephrine (adrenaline) and norepinephrine (noradrenaline), but not in equal amounts: epinephrine makes up about 80% of its catecholamine output and norepinephrine about 20%, because most of the medulla's chromaffin cells express the enzyme phenylethanolamine N-methyltransferase, which methylates norepinephrine into epinephrine as the final step of catecholamine synthesis. Epinephrine is therefore the single most abundant signalling molecule released by the adrenal medulla, distinguishing its output from that of ordinary sympathetic postganglionic nerve endings, which release norepinephrine almost exclusively.

## explicit_objective
State that the adrenal medulla secretes catecholamines in roughly an 80:20 epinephrine-to-norepinephrine ratio, making epinephrine its most abundant secreted signalling molecule.

## pitfalls
Assuming the adrenal medulla releases norepinephrine as its main catecholamine, as ordinary sympathetic nerve endings do — the adrenal medulla's own methylating enzyme (phenylethanolamine N-methyltransferase) converts most of its norepinephrine to epinephrine, making epinephrine, not norepinephrine, the dominant secreted molecule.

## definition
Every preganglionic autonomic neuron, whether it belongs to the sympathetic or the parasympathetic division, releases acetylcholine at its synapse with the postganglionic neuron, acting on nicotinic (ganglionic-type) acetylcholine receptors to depolarise the postganglionic cell. This is true regardless of what the postganglionic neuron itself releases downstream — most sympathetic postganglionic fibres switch to noradrenaline, while parasympathetic postganglionic fibres and the cholinergic sympathetic exceptions (sweat glands) continue to release acetylcholine, but the preganglionic step is uniformly cholinergic across the entire autonomic nervous system.

## explicit_objective
State that acetylcholine, acting on nicotinic receptors, is the neurotransmitter released by every preganglionic autonomic neuron, sympathetic and parasympathetic alike.

## pitfalls
Assuming the preganglionic transmitter differs between the sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions, or confusing it with the transmitters released downstream — sympathetic postganglionic fibres are mostly noradrenergic and the adrenal medulla itself secretes epinephrine/norepinephrine, but the PREganglionic step, in both divisions, is always cholinergic.

## label
Acetylcholine is the only neurotransmitter released by parasympathetic postganglionic neurons, unlike the sympathetic division, whose postganglionic fibres are mostly noradrenergic with cholinergic sweat glands as the notable exception

## id
CON-NEU-66CA185B9EBEDD

## canonical_key
autonomic.acetylcholine-exclusive-parasympathetic-postganglionic-transmitter

## aliases
Parasympathetic postganglionic transmitter
Acetylcholine as the sole parasympathetic transmitter
Cholinergic parasympathetic fibres

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases
[clear]

## definition
Parasympathetic postganglionic neurons release only one neurotransmitter, acetylcholine, acting on muscarinic receptors at their effector organs. This is a genuine point of contrast with the sympathetic division: sympathetic postganglionic fibres are mostly noradrenergic (releasing norepinephrine onto alpha- and beta-adrenergic receptors), with the sweat glands as the well-known cholinergic exception among sympathetic effectors. Acetylcholine's action at the parasympathetic effector organ (muscarinic) is pharmacologically distinct from its action at the somatic neuromuscular junction (nicotinic, blocked by curare-type drugs, not atropine) and from its own synthesis, which is catalysed by choline acetyltransferase, not acetylcholinesterase (the enzyme that instead breaks it down).

## explicit_objective
State that acetylcholine is the only neurotransmitter secreted by parasympathetic postganglionic neurons, contrasting this with the mostly noradrenergic sympathetic postganglionic division.

## pitfalls
Assuming acetylcholine's motor end-plate action is blocked by atropine — atropine blocks MUSCARINIC receptors (the parasympathetic effector type), not the NICOTINIC receptors at the skeletal-muscle motor end plate; and confusing acetylcholine's synthesising enzyme (choline acetyltransferase) with its degrading enzyme (acetylcholinesterase).
