## definition
Chronic infestation of the urinary bladder by Schistosoma haematobium (urinary bilharziasis, or schistosomiasis) is a well-recognised predisposing factor for cancer of the urinary bladder. The eggs deposited in the bladder wall provoke chronic inflammation, fibrosis and squamous metaplasia of the normally transitional (urothelial) lining, and it is against this background of long-standing irritation and metaplastic change that malignant transformation occurs. Unlike bladder cancer overall, which is most often transitional-cell (urothelial) carcinoma, bilharzial bladder cancer is characteristically squamous cell carcinoma, reflecting the squamous-metaplastic epithelium from which it arises. This link is one of the best-established examples of a parasitic infection acting as a carcinogen.
## explicit_objective
Identify chronic urinary bilharziasis as a predisposing factor for squamous cell carcinoma of the bladder, distinguishing it from the transitional-cell carcinoma that is the most common bladder cancer overall.
## pitfalls
Choosing a different bilharzia-associated cancer that the department book does not support (such as skin malignancy or leukaemia), or assuming bilharzial bladder cancer is transitional-cell rather than squamous.

## definition
Several histological features can accompany a prolonged inflammatory process, but they differ in how specifically they point to chronicity rather than to acute or resolving inflammation. Congested blood vessels, exudate, and even macrophages and necrosis can all be seen in acute or subacute inflammation as well as in chronic inflammation, so none of them reliably distinguishes the two states. Fibroblastic proliferation, however, reflects the repair-and-matrix-deposition component that develops only once an inflammatory process has persisted long enough for fibrosis to begin, and it is therefore regarded as the single most reliable piece of histological evidence that an inflammatory process is chronic rather than acute.
## explicit_objective
Identify fibroblastic proliferation, rather than vascular congestion, exudate, macrophages or necrosis, as the most reliable histological evidence of chronic inflammation.
## pitfalls
Choosing a feature that can also occur in acute or subacute inflammation, such as congested capillaries, exudate, the mere presence of macrophages, or necrosis, none of which is specific to chronicity the way ongoing fibroblastic proliferation is.

## definition
Immune-mediated granulomatous inflammation is driven by a type IV (delayed-type, cell-mediated) hypersensitivity reaction, distinct from the antibody-mediated type I, II and III reactions. The sequence begins when a macrophage processes a persistent, poorly degradable antigen and presents it to CD4+ T helper lymphocytes; this antigen presentation is the first step in granuloma pathogenesis. The sensitised T cells then release cytokines, chiefly interferon-gamma, that recruit and activate further macrophages, driving their transformation into epithelioid cells and, with continued stimulation, their fusion into multinucleated giant cells around the persistent antigen.
## explicit_objective
State that granuloma formation is mediated by type IV hypersensitivity and that macrophage antigen presentation to T lymphocytes is its first step, rather than events such as fibrosis, giant-cell formation or antibody secretion that occur later or belong to a different immune pathway.
## pitfalls
Selecting an antibody-mediated hypersensitivity type (I, II or III) for granuloma pathogenesis, or choosing a later event of granuloma formation — such as giant-cell formation, fibrosis, or release of chemical mediators — as if it were the first step, when antigen presentation to T cells precedes and triggers all of these.

## definition
Rhinoscleroma is a chronic granulomatous infection of the nose and upper respiratory tract caused by Klebsiella rhinoscleromatis. Its histology is distinctive: sheets of large, vacuolated macrophages (Mikulicz cells) containing the causative bacilli are accompanied by numerous plasma cells, a proportion of which become so distended with stored immunoglobulin that their cytoplasm forms rounded, eosinophilic, hyaline-change inclusions known as Russell bodies. While Russell bodies can occur wherever plasma cells accumulate in chronic inflammation, their combination with Mikulicz cells is a classic, exam-favourite histological marker of rhinoscleroma specifically.
## explicit_objective
Associate Russell bodies with rhinoscleroma among the conditions in which plasma cells accumulate, rather than with an unrelated acute or viral condition.
## pitfalls
Choosing an unrelated acute or viral condition (such as the common cold or influenza) that does not feature the chronic, plasma-cell-rich granulomatous infiltrate in which Russell bodies form.

## definition
The speed of wound healing is strongly influenced by the local blood supply of the region in which the wound lies, because the vascular response of early repair — angiogenesis, delivery of inflammatory and repair cells, and oxygen and nutrient supply to proliferating fibroblasts — depends directly on that supply. Highly vascular regions such as the face and scalp heal comparatively quickly and with minimal scarring for this reason, in contrast to poorly vascularised regions such as the distal leg or foot, where wounds heal more slowly and are more prone to complications such as infection or dehiscence. This regional variation is a well-recognised surgical principle, distinct from the general local and systemic factors, such as nutrition, infection or corticosteroid use, that affect healing everywhere.
## explicit_objective
Identify a highly vascular region such as the face as healing fastest among surgical wound sites, attributing the difference to regional blood supply.
## pitfalls
Choosing a poorly vascularised distal region (such as the foot or leg) as healing fastest, or attributing regional healing-speed differences to a factor other than local vascularity.

## label
Escape of plasma proteins into inflamed tissue raises interstitial osmotic pressure and draws in more fluid
## id
CON-FND-365D4E084CF004
## canonical_key
pathology.inflammation.exudate-plasma-protein-escape-raises-osmotic-pressure
## aliases
Mechanism of inflammatory exudate formation
Osmotic pressure in acute inflammation
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases
[clear]
## definition
Acute inflammation increases vascular permeability, chiefly through the action of mediators such as histamine and leukotrienes acting on venular endothelium, which allows protein-rich plasma to escape from the vessels into the interstitial tissue. As plasma proteins, particularly the smaller albumin molecules and, as permeability increases further, larger proteins too, accumulate in the interstitial space, they raise the interstitial (tissue) colloid osmotic pressure above that of the plasma. This reversed osmotic gradient then draws additional fluid out of the vessels and into the tissue by osmosis, compounding the vascular leakage and producing the protein-rich, oedematous exudate characteristic of acute inflammation, in contrast to a transudate, which forms from hydrostatic imbalance alone without this protein-driven osmotic component.
## explicit_objective
Explain that the escape of plasma proteins into inflamed interstitial tissue raises tissue osmotic pressure, and that this reversed osmotic gradient is what draws additional fluid into the tissue during acute inflammation.
## pitfalls
Attributing the increased tissue osmotic pressure of acute inflammation to electrolyte concentration, particle size, intracellular protein content, or simple salt-and-water retention, rather than to the escape of plasma proteins into the interstitial space.

## definition
Acute inflammation is fundamentally a protective response: it dilutes and neutralises toxins, delivers phagocytes and antibodies to the site of injury, and helps limit the spread of infection. However, the same processes that make it protective can also cause harm to the host. Digestive enzymes released from activated leukocytes and dead cells can damage normal adjacent tissue, and the oedema fluid of the inflammatory exudate produces tissue swelling that, depending on its location, can itself be directly harmful, for example by compressing a vital structure such as the airway in laryngeal oedema, so tissue swelling has to be recognised as a genuine bad effect of acute inflammation rather than only as a harmless accompaniment of a beneficial process.
## explicit_objective
Recognise tissue swelling from oedema as a genuine harmful effect of acute inflammation, in contrast to its protective effects such as toxin dilution, phagocytosis and the stimulation of immune responses.
## pitfalls
Selecting one of acute inflammation's protective, beneficial effects, such as dilution of toxins, phagocytosis, stimulation of immune response, or fibrin formation aiding localisation, when asked for a harmful one, or dismissing swelling as never harmful.

Pigments are classified as endogenous, made within the body, or exogenous, introduced from outside it
## id

pathology.pigments.endogenous-vs-exogenous-classification
## aliases
Endogenous pigments
Exogenous pigments
Pigment classification
## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Pathological pigments are classified by their origin into two groups. Endogenous pigments are synthesised within the body itself and include melanin, made by melanocytes, lipofuscin, a wear-and-tear lipid-peroxidation product, haemosiderin, stored iron derived from haemoglobin breakdown, bilirubin, and hemozoin, a malarial-parasite pigment derived from haemoglobin. Exogenous pigments, by contrast, are introduced into the body from an outside source and include inhaled carbon or coal dust (anthracosis and other pneumoconioses), tattoo ink, and heavy metals such as lead; because these substances are not produced by the body's own metabolism, inhaled particulate pigments such as carbon are typically taken up and retained by macrophages rather than being broken down.
## explicit_objective
Classify a named pigment correctly as endogenous, produced by the body's own metabolism such as melanin, lipofuscin or haemosiderin, or exogenous, introduced from an external source such as carbon dust/pneumoconiosis or tattoo ink, distinguishing the two categories by origin rather than by colour or organ.
## pitfalls
Classifying an inhaled or externally introduced pigment such as anthracosis/pneumoconiosis (carbon dust) or tattoo ink as endogenous because it accumulates within body tissue, when its origin, from outside the body, is what defines the exogenous category; conversely, misclassifying a body-synthesised pigment such as melanin as exogenous.

## label
Rheumatoid arthritis is classified as an immune-mediated chronic inflammatory disease
## id
CON-FND-118331797C5049
## canonical_key

## aliases

## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Chronic inflammatory diseases can be classified by their underlying cause into persistent-infective, immune-mediated (autoimmune), and prolonged-toxic-exposure categories. Rheumatoid arthritis is classified as an immune-mediated chronic inflammatory disease: it is an autoimmune condition in which the synovium of joints is chronically infiltrated by lymphocytes, plasma cells and macrophages, driven by autoantibodies such as rheumatoid factor and anti-citrullinated peptide antibody and by self-perpetuating cytokine-mediated immune activity, rather than by a persistent infectious organism or a toxic exposure. This immune-mediated mechanism explains rheumatoid arthritis's systemic features and its response to immunosuppressive and biological, cytokine-targeted, therapy, unlike inflammation from a distinct infective or metabolic cause.
## explicit_objective
Classify rheumatoid arthritis as an immune-mediated chronic inflammatory disease, rather than as an acute inflammatory condition, a foreign-body reaction, a metabolic disease, or a primarily vascular disease.
## pitfalls
Misclassifying rheumatoid arthritis as an acute inflammatory condition, a foreign-body reaction, a metabolic disease, or a vascular disease, when its underlying mechanism is autoimmune, immune-mediated, chronic inflammation of the synovium.
