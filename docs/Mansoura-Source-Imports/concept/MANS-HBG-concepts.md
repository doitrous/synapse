## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases

## definition
Prophase I of meiosis is prolonged and divided into five substages, each named for what happens in it. Leptotene is first: the chromosomes condense into visible thin threads, each still a single chromatid. Zygotene follows: homologous chromosomes pair along their length, an event called synapsis, held together by a protein scaffold, the synaptonemal complex. Pachytene is next: with the homologs now fully paired, non-sister chromatids exchange segments — crossing over — the source of genetic recombination. Diplotene follows: the synaptonemal complex breaks down and the paired homologs begin to separate, remaining joined only at the crossover points, now visible as chiasmata (this is the stage at which a primary oocyte arrests from fetal life until ovulation). Diakinesis is last: the chromosomes condense further, the nucleolus disappears and the nuclear envelope breaks down, completing the transition to metaphase I.

## explicit_objective
Name the five substages of meiotic prophase I in order and state which event (chromosome condensation, synapsis, crossing over, chiasma formation, nuclear envelope breakdown) defines each one.

## pitfalls
Confusing synapsis (zygotene, homologs pairing) with crossing over (pachytene, homologs exchanging material) — pairing has to happen before an exchange between the paired strands is possible, which is why the two events are assigned to consecutive but distinct substages rather than treated as one event.

A normal human somatic cell has 46 chromosomes, and the same 46 chromosomes carry 46 or 92 chromatids depending on the cell-cycle phase

## id

## canonical_key

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases

## definition
A normal human somatic cell carries 46 chromosomes: 44 autosomes, arranged as 22 homologous pairs, plus one pair of sex chromosomes (XX or XY). This count of 46 is fixed through most of the cell cycle, but the number of DNA strands (chromatids) making up each chromosome changes: in G1, before DNA replication, each of the 46 chromosomes is a single chromatid, written 46-s (46 chromosomes, single chromatid each). After S-phase replication, each of the same 46 chromosomes is duplicated into two identical sister chromatids joined at the centromere, written 46-d (46 chromosomes, double chromatid each, 92 chromatids in total) — this doubled state is carried through G2 and mitotic prophase/metaphase. At anaphase the sister chromatids separate and move to opposite poles, so each daughter cell again receives 46 single-chromatid chromosomes (46-s), restoring the starting notation.

## explicit_objective
State that a normal somatic cell has 46 chromosomes in 22 autosome pairs plus one sex-chromosome pair, and give the chromosome/chromatid notation (46-s or 46-d) for a stated cell-cycle phase.

## pitfalls
Reading a doubled chromatid count as a doubled chromosome count. Replication in S-phase doubles the DNA content and the chromatid count (46-d, 92 chromatids) but not the chromosome count, which stays 46 until the sister chromatids actually separate at anaphase.

Glands are classified as exocrine, which secrete through a duct, or endocrine, which are ductless and secrete into the blood

## id
CON-FND-1B037A042672DF

## canonical_key
gland.classification.endocrine-versus-exocrine

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases

## definition
Glands are classified by how their secretion reaches its target. An exocrine gland retains a duct connecting the secretory unit to a surface (skin or a mucosal lining) and releases its product through that duct — sebaceous, salivary, sweat and ceruminous glands are all exocrine. An endocrine gland has no duct: during development it loses its connection to the surface epithelium it arose from, and its secretory cells release their product (a hormone) directly into the surrounding capillaries for distribution by the blood. The pituitary gland is the standard example of a purely endocrine gland offered against a list of exocrine (duct-bearing) skin glands.

## explicit_objective
Distinguish an endocrine gland from an exocrine gland by the presence or absence of a duct, and name the pituitary gland as a standard example of an endocrine gland.

## pitfalls
Assuming any gland embedded in skin must be exocrine because most skin glands are (sebaceous, sweat, ceruminous). The distinguishing feature is the duct, not the location — an endocrine gland can develop from surface epithelium and only becomes ductless secondarily, once its connection to the surface is lost.

## label
Myosin is the protein of the thick filament; actin is the protein of the thin filament

## id
CON-FND-2DDE69B311CAA5

## canonical_key
cytoskeleton.myosin-as-the-thick-filament-protein

## aliases
Thick filament protein
Myosin thick filament

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases

## definition
The contractile filament system of muscle, and of the contractile machinery shared by cells generally, is built of two filament types named for their diameter and identified by their protein. The thick filament, about 15 nanometres across, is built of myosin, a motor protein whose globular heads bind actin and use ATP hydrolysis to generate the sliding force of contraction. The thin filament, about 5-7 nanometres across, is built of actin. When a basic-science question asks which protein forms the thick filament, among a list of cytoskeletal proteins (actin, myosin, tubulin, desmin, cytokeratin), the answer is myosin — actin belongs to the thin filament, tubulin builds microtubules, and desmin and cytokeratin are intermediate filament proteins, neither of them a filament of the actin-myosin contractile system.

## explicit_objective
Name myosin as the protein of the thick filament and actin as the protein of the thin filament, and distinguish both from tubulin (microtubules) and the intermediate filament proteins (desmin, cytokeratin) offered as distractors.

## pitfalls
Picking actin for the thick filament because it is the more familiar name. Diameter is the distinguishing clue: the thick filament (myosin, ~15 nm) is roughly twice the diameter of the thin filament (actin, ~5-7 nm).

## label
Elastic fibers need a special stain — Verhoeff's, orcein, or resorcin-fuchsin — because they do not show well with H&E or Masson's trichrome

## id
CON-FND-F0C32F89547AB1

## canonical_key
elastic.fiber-special-stains-verhoeff-and-orcein

## aliases
Verhoeff's stain
Elastic fiber special stains
Orcein stain

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases

## definition
Elastic fibers stain poorly and inconsistently with the routine stains used for the other connective tissue fibers — H&E shows them faintly, and Masson's trichrome (the standard stain for collagen) and silver impregnation (the standard stain for reticular fibers) do not demonstrate them reliably either. A dedicated special stain is needed: Verhoeff's stain (an iron-haematoxylin-based method) colours elastic fibers blue-black to purple-black against a background counterstained by van Gieson; orcein and resorcin-fuchsin are alternative special stains that colour elastic fibers brown to purple-brown. Any of these is used specifically to demonstrate elastic fibers and their distribution (as fine fibres, sheets, or lamellae) when they would not be visible on a routine section.

## explicit_objective
Name Verhoeff's stain (or orcein/resorcin-fuchsin) as a special stain specific for elastic fibers, as distinct from the stains used for collagen (Masson's trichrome, van Gieson) or reticular fibers (silver impregnation).

## pitfalls
Reaching for Masson's trichrome or a silver stain when the question asks for elastic fibers specifically. Those two are the standard stains for collagen and reticular fibers respectively; elastic fibers need their own stain (Verhoeff's, orcein or resorcin-fuchsin) precisely because they do not take up the collagen/reticular stains reliably.
