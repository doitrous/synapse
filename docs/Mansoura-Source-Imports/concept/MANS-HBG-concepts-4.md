# Item

## label
Meiosis is restricted to the sex cells (germ cells) undergoing gametogenesis, not somatic cells

## id
CON-FND-3A8D5C1E92B764

## canonical_key
meiosis.occurs-only-in-germ-cells-not-somatic-cells

## aliases
Meiosis site
Germ cells undergo meiosis
Gametogenesis

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases
[clear]

## definition
Meiosis, the reduction division that halves the chromosome number from diploid (46) to haploid (23), takes place only in the germ (sex) cells of the gonads as part of gametogenesis — oogenesis in the ovary, spermatogenesis in the testis. Every other tissue in the body — liver, intestinal epithelium, neurons, skin — is made of somatic cells, which divide (when they divide at all) by mitosis, producing two diploid daughter cells identical to the parent, never by meiosis. This restriction of meiosis to one specialised cell lineage, and its absence from ordinary somatic turnover, is the fact this style of question isolates.

## explicit_objective
State that meiosis is confined to the sex cells (germ cells) of the gonads, and that somatic cells (liver, intestinal, nerve, skin) divide by mitosis instead.

## pitfalls
Treating meiosis as a general cell-division option alongside mitosis for any actively dividing tissue. Only the germ-cell lineage undergoing gametogenesis performs meiosis; every actively renewing somatic tissue (gut epithelium, epidermis) still divides by mitosis alone.

# Item

## label
A motor (efferent) nerve carries the impulse from the CNS out to a muscle

## id
CON-NEU-6B1F84A0D3C752

## canonical_key
nerve.motor-fiber-carries-impulse-from-cns-to-muscle

## aliases
Motor nerve
Efferent nerve
Nerve fibre direction of conduction

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases
[clear]

## definition
Peripheral nerve fibres are classed by the direction they conduct the impulse. A motor (efferent) fibre carries the impulse away from the brain or spinal cord out to an effector — skeletal, smooth or cardiac muscle, or a gland — driving its contraction or secretion. This is the opposite direction to a sensory (afferent) fibre, which carries an impulse from a receptor towards the CNS. A mixed nerve carries both fibre types together in one bundle, so it is not itself a single-direction answer; the direction "from the brain to the muscle" specifically identifies the motor (efferent) fibre.

## explicit_objective
Identify the motor (efferent) nerve fibre as the one that conducts the impulse from the CNS to a muscle, as opposed to a sensory (afferent), mixed, or interneuron fibre.

## pitfalls
Picking "mixed nerve" for a strictly one-direction description. A mixed nerve carries both motor and sensory fibres together; a question asking for a single, defined direction (brain to muscle) wants the specific motor/efferent fibre, not the umbrella "mixed" category.

# Item

## label
The neuron's nucleus is large, rounded, centrally placed and vesicular (pale, open chromatin)

## id
CON-NEU-58E2A916F7C304

## canonical_key
neuron.nucleus-large-rounded-central-vesicular

## aliases
Neuron nucleus
Vesicular nucleus

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases
[clear]

## definition
The typical multipolar neuron's cell body (perikaryon) contains one nucleus that is large, rounded and centrally placed, with finely dispersed (open, "vesicular") chromatin and a single prominent nucleolus — the pale, transcriptionally active appearance expected of a long-lived, highly synthetic, permanently postmitotic cell. This distinguishes it from cells with a heterochromatic, kidney-shaped, or eccentric nucleus. The neuron's cell body does contain mitochondria and its Nissl substance (rough ER and free ribosomes) extends into the dendrites but stops at the axon hillock, never entering the axon itself; centrioles, present early in a differentiating neuron, are lost once the mature cell becomes permanently postmitotic and are no longer used for protein synthesis, a function they never had.

## explicit_objective
Describe the neuron's nucleus as large, rounded, central and vesicular, and correct the companion misconceptions that the cell body lacks mitochondria, that Nissl granules extend into the axon, and that centrioles function in protein synthesis.

## pitfalls
Extending Nissl substance (rough ER) into the axon — it is confined to the cell body and dendrites and is absent from the axon hillock and axon; and assigning centrioles a protein-synthesis role, when they are microtubule-organising structures, unrelated to translation.
