## label
Vascular resistance follows Poiseuille's law: it varies directly with blood viscosity and vessel length, and inversely with the fourth power of the vessel radius, which makes radius — the one of the three that changes physiologically — by far the most powerful determinant of resistance

## aliases

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases

## definition
Poiseuille's equation states that the resistance a blood vessel offers to flow is directly proportional to blood viscosity and to the vessel's length, and inversely proportional to the fourth power of its radius. Of these three, only radius changes significantly and rapidly under physiological control — vessel length is essentially fixed and viscosity changes only slowly (with haematocrit) — so small changes in vessel radius dominate changes in resistance far more than length or viscosity do, and the diameter of arterioles specifically is the main determinant of total peripheral resistance. Resistance also decreases whenever blood viscosity falls, as it does in anaemia and hypoproteinaemia, and increases with the longer vessel length of the systemic circulation compared with the pulmonary circulation.

## explicit_objective
State that vascular resistance is directly proportional to viscosity and vessel length, inversely proportional to the fourth power of radius, and that radius (via arteriolar diameter) is the dominant, physiologically variable determinant.

## pitfalls
Reversing the direction of the viscosity or length relationship — both are DIRECTLY, not inversely, related to resistance, so a fall in viscosity (as in anaemia or hypoproteinaemia) lowers resistance, and it is only radius, not length or viscosity, that the body varies moment-to-moment to control resistance.

# Item

## label
Blood flow through a vessel equals the pressure gradient divided by resistance (F = ΔP / R): flow rises when arterioles dilate, since dilation lowers R, and blood flow is directly — not inversely — proportional to vessel diameter

## id
CON-CVS-B9F755F2784109

## canonical_key
hemodynamics.flow-equation-and-arteriolar-radius-control

## aliases
F = ΔP / R
Blood flow equation

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases
[clear]

## definition
Blood flow through a vessel (F) is given by F = ΔP / R, where ΔP is the pressure gradient driving flow and R is the vascular resistance of the vessel; flow therefore rises when the driving pressure gradient rises or when resistance falls, and falls when either the reverse happens. Because resistance is inversely related to the fourth power of vessel radius (Poiseuille's law), arteriolar dilatation sharply lowers R and so increases flow, both locally through the dilated bed and, downstream, into the capillary bed it feeds; arteriolar constriction has the opposite effect. Following the same relationship, blood flow through a vessel is directly proportional to its diameter — a wider vessel carries more flow for a given pressure gradient, not less.

## explicit_objective
State the blood flow equation (F = ΔP/R), that arteriolar dilatation increases flow by lowering resistance, and that flow is directly proportional to vessel diameter.

## pitfalls
Writing the flow equation with radius rather than resistance in the denominator, or assuming flow falls (rather than rises) with arteriolar dilatation or with a wider vessel diameter — flow moves in the SAME direction as ΔP and diameter, and the OPPOSITE direction from resistance.

Blood flow is reliably laminar when the Reynolds number is below about 2000 and becomes turbulent whenever it exceeds about 3000, with the intervening range transitional

## definition
The Reynolds number is a dimensionless index used to predict whether blood flow in a vessel will be laminar (smooth, streamlined) or turbulent (disorganised, with eddy currents). Below a Reynolds number of roughly 2000, flow is reliably laminar; above roughly 3000, flow is reliably turbulent; the intervening range (roughly 2000-3000) is a transitional zone where either pattern may occur. Flow becomes turbulent once the blood's velocity exceeds a "critical velocity" for that vessel, which is the velocity at which the Reynolds number crosses this upper threshold.

## explicit_objective
State that a Reynolds number below about 2000 predicts laminar flow and above about 3000 predicts turbulent flow, with the region between them transitional.

## pitfalls
Citing a single sharp cutoff (e.g. exactly 2000) for the onset of turbulence, or choosing an order-of-magnitude-wrong threshold (such as 200) — the reliably-turbulent threshold this course tests is 3000, with 2000 marking the upper edge of reliably-laminar flow rather than the point turbulence becomes certain.

## label
Within a vessel, blood flow velocity is highest at the axial centre and falls to almost zero at the vessel wall (a parabolic profile); across vessel types, velocity is set by the total cross-sectional area available to flow and is lowest in the capillaries, despite their small individual radius, because their combined cross-sectional area is the largest of any vessel type

## canonical_key
hemodynamics.blood-flow-velocity-profile-and-vessel-type

## aliases
Parabolic velocity profile
Velocity at the vessel wall
Capillary blood flow velocity
Cross-sectional area and flow velocity

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases

## definition
Within a single blood vessel, laminar flow forms a parabolic velocity profile: the layer of blood in contact with the vessel wall is essentially stationary (velocity falls to almost zero there) because of friction with the wall, while velocity rises progressively toward the vessel's axial centre, where it is greatest. Across different vessel types, blood flow velocity is set by the total cross-sectional area available to flow at that level of the circulation, not by an individual vessel's own radius: although each capillary is far narrower than an artery, the combined cross-sectional area of all the capillaries in parallel vastly exceeds that of the arteries, so blood flow velocity is at its lowest in the capillaries of any vessel type — the same principle by which vessel diameter (through its effect on cross-sectional area) is the single factor with the greatest influence on flow velocity.

## explicit_objective
State that blood flow velocity falls to near zero at the vessel wall and rises toward the vessel centre, and that velocity is lowest in the capillaries because of their large total cross-sectional area, with vessel diameter/cross-sectional area the dominant influence on velocity.

## pitfalls
Assuming capillary blood flow velocity is high because capillaries are individually so narrow — velocity is governed by the TOTAL cross-sectional area of all parallel vessels at that level, and the capillary bed's enormous combined cross-sectional area makes its flow velocity the lowest, not the highest, of any vessel type.

# Item

## label
Blood viscosity is set chiefly by haematocrit (red-cell concentration): dehydration (haemoconcentration) raises viscosity, while anaemia and hypoproteinaemia lower it

## id
CON-HEM-713C1A4F63A877

## canonical_key
hemodynamics.blood-viscosity-hematocrit-determinants

## aliases
Determinants of blood viscosity
Dehydration and blood viscosity
Haematocrit and viscosity

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases
[clear]

## definition
Blood viscosity — the internal friction that resists blood flow, and one of the three variables in Poiseuille's law — is determined chiefly by the concentration of red blood cells (haematocrit) and, to a lesser extent, plasma protein concentration. Dehydration reduces the plasma (fluid) volume relative to the cellular volume, effectively haemoconcentrating the blood, which raises the red-cell concentration and so raises viscosity. Conversely, conditions that lower the effective concentration of blood's solid/protein content — anaemia (fewer red cells) and hypoproteinaemia (less plasma protein) — both lower blood viscosity, which is why both are also listed among the factors that lower vascular resistance and peripheral resistance more broadly.

## explicit_objective
State that dehydration raises blood viscosity via haemoconcentration, while anaemia and hypoproteinaemia lower it.

## pitfalls
Assuming any change in blood composition raises viscosity — vasodilation and low cholesterol do not raise blood viscosity, and anaemia/hypoproteinaemia specifically LOWER it; only a rise in the concentration of red cells or plasma protein, as in dehydration, raises it.
