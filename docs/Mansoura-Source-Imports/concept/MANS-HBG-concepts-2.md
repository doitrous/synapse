Interphase, not any single mitotic stage, is the longest phase of the cell cycle

## id
CON-FND-9A2E5C41D0B7F3

## canonical_key
cell-cycle.interphase-is-the-longest-phase

## aliases
Longest phase of the cell cycle
Interphase duration

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases
[clear]

## definition
The cell cycle is divided into interphase (G1, S and G2) and the four mitotic stages (prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase). Interphase occupies the great majority of total cycle time — commonly on the order of 90% or more — while the four mitotic stages together take up only a small remaining fraction. During interphase the cell grows, carries out its ordinary metabolic work, and replicates its DNA in S-phase, all before the comparatively brief, visually dramatic business of chromosome condensation and segregation in mitosis proper. A cell that looks "resting" under the microscope between divisions is, in fact, in this longest and most metabolically active stage of the cycle, not in a dormant state.

## explicit_objective
State that interphase, not any single mitotic stage, is the longest phase of the cell cycle, and that mitosis itself occupies only a small fraction of total cycle time.

## pitfalls
Picking one of the mitotic stages (most often prophase, the longest of the four) as the cycle's longest phase overall. Prophase is the longest of the four mitotic stages, but all four together are still far shorter than interphase.

## label
The DNA-to-cytoplasm ratio peaks at the end of S-phase, once DNA content has doubled but cytoplasmic growth has not yet caught up

## id
CON-FND-3B6F82AC59E1D4

## canonical_key
cell-cycle.dna-cytoplasm-ratio-peaks-end-of-s-phase

## aliases
DNA to cytoplasm ratio
Nuclear to cytoplasmic DNA content timing

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases
[clear]

## definition
S-phase replicates the cell's entire DNA content, doubling it from a single (46-s) to a doubled (46-d) chromatid state, while the cell's cytoplasmic volume has not yet had the chance to grow further or divide. The instant S-phase completes is therefore the point at which the cell holds its full, doubled DNA content within the smallest cytoplasmic volume it will have for the rest of the cycle, giving the highest DNA-to-cytoplasm ratio of the entire cell cycle. G2 and mitosis keep the same doubled DNA content, but the cell's cytoplasmic volume continues to increase through G2 until division halves it again, so the ratio is highest right at the S-to-G2 transition and falls gradually afterward.

## explicit_objective
State that the DNA-to-cytoplasm ratio is highest at the end of S-phase, once DNA content has doubled but before further cytoplasmic growth in G2 dilutes it again.

## pitfalls
Assuming the ratio keeps rising through G2 and into mitosis because the DNA content is still doubled there. The DNA content does stay doubled through G2 and mitosis, but the cytoplasm keeps growing across that same span, so the ratio itself is highest right at the end of S-phase and falls afterward, not later in the cycle.

## definition
The thyroid follicle is a spherical structure lined by a single layer of follicular epithelial cells enclosing a central store of colloid, the site where thyroglobulin is synthesised, iodinated and stored before hormone release. At the follicle's normal, moderately active resting state this lining is simple cuboidal epithelium. The same follicular cells flatten toward simple squamous when the gland is under-stimulated and become taller, columnar when strongly stimulated, so cell height itself is a marker of the gland's activity level, but the standard resting description taught for the thyroid follicle is simple cuboidal.

## explicit_objective
State that the thyroid follicle is lined by simple cuboidal epithelium at its normal activity state, and that this height varies with the gland's stimulation level.

## pitfalls
Calling the thyroid follicle's lining "columnar" or "squamous" without qualifying which activity state is meant. Cuboidal is the standard resting description; the same epithelium becomes columnar when hyperactive and squamous when hypoactive.

Some of the Golgi's trans-face secretory vesicles are retained inside the cell as primary lysosomes rather than being exported

## id

Trans-Golgi network sorting

## definition
Vesicles budded from the Golgi apparatus's mature (trans) face are not all destined for immediate secretion outside the cell: the same trans-Golgi network sorts its output into at least two destinies, exporting some vesicles as secretory vesicles while retaining others within the cell as primary lysosomes — membrane-bound sacs of newly packaged hydrolytic enzymes not yet fused with any digestive substrate. This dual sorting is why the Golgi apparatus is described as central to both the secretory pathway and the lysosomal (digestive) pathway, rather than serving secretion alone.

## explicit_objective
State that some vesicles budded from the Golgi's trans face are retained inside the cell as primary lysosomes rather than being exported as secretory vesicles.

## pitfalls
Assuming every vesicle that buds from the Golgi's exit face is secreted from the cell. The same trans face also supplies the lysosomal pathway, retaining some of its budded vesicles internally as primary lysosomes.

A structural chromosomal abnormality changes a chromosome's own architecture, as in Wolf (Wolf-Hirschhorn) syndrome's 4p deletion, distinct from a numerical extra or missing whole chromosome

## id

## canonical_key
chromosomal-aberration.structural-vs-numerical-wolf-syndrome-example

## aliases
Wolf syndrome
Wolf-Hirschhorn syndrome
Structural chromosomal abnormality

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases
[clear]

## definition
Chromosomal abnormalities are classed as either numerical, a change in the total count of whole chromosomes (trisomy, as in Down syndrome; monosomy, as in Turner syndrome; or an extra sex chromosome, as in triple-X or XYY syndrome), or structural, a change to a chromosome's own internal architecture through breakage and rejoining — deletion, inversion, translocation or ring formation — while the total chromosome count may remain normal. Wolf (Wolf-Hirschhorn) syndrome is a standard example of a structural abnormality: it is caused by a partial deletion of the short arm of chromosome 4, not by an extra or missing whole chromosome. Cri-du-chat syndrome, a deletion of the short arm of chromosome 5, is the more commonly taught partner example of this same structural, deletion-based category.

## explicit_objective
Classify Wolf (Wolf-Hirschhorn) syndrome, a 4p deletion, as a structural chromosomal abnormality, distinct from the numerical abnormalities of Down, Turner, triple-X and XYY syndromes.

## pitfalls
Grouping every named chromosomal syndrome together without separating structural (architecture-altering, count-preserving) causes from numerical (count-altering) ones. Down, Turner, triple-X and XYY are all numerical; Wolf and cri-du-chat are structural deletions.

The small and large intestine are lined by simple columnar absorptive epithelium, whose surface (absorptive) cells carry an apical brush border of microvilli that vastly increases the surface area available for absorbing digested nutrients, water and electrolytes. This absorptive columnar lining, interspersed with mucus-secreting goblet cells, is the epithelium's defining functional feature, distinguishing the intestine's lining from the stomach's secretory (non-absorptive) simple columnar epithelium and from the oesophagus's protective stratified squamous lining.

## explicit_objective

## pitfalls
Assuming any simple columnar epithelium in the gastrointestinal tract must be absorptive. The stomach's simple columnar lining is secretory, not absorptive; only the intestine's columnar epithelium is specifically the brush-bordered, absorptive type.
