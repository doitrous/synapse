# Item

## label
Flexion decreases the angle at a joint and extension increases it, each measured from the neutral anatomical position

## id
CON-MSK-CE112D092D2F49

## canonical_key
movement-terms-flexion-extension

## aliases
Bending and straightening a joint

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases
[clear]

## definition
Flexion is the movement at a joint that decreases the angle between the two bones on either side of it, typically bending the joint away from the anatomical position. Extension is the opposite movement, increasing that angle, typically straightening the joint back towards (or past) the anatomical position. The pair applies to most hinge and synovial joints of the limbs and spine — for example, decreasing the angle between the thigh and the leg at the knee is flexion, and increasing it again is extension. Flexion and extension are distinguished from abduction/adduction (movement towards or away from a reference line) and from supination/pronation or inversion/eversion (rotations or tilts of the forearm and foot), which are separate named movements.

## explicit_objective
State which of flexion or extension decreases the angle at a joint and which increases it, and apply the pair to a described joint movement.

## pitfalls
Assuming flexion always means "bending forward". At the knee, flexion bends the leg backward, and at the shoulder, flexion swings the arm forward — the defining feature is the decrease in joint angle, not a fixed direction in space.

# Item

## label
Abduction moves a part of the body away from the median plane (or, at the fingers/toes, away from the axis of the hand/foot) and adduction brings it back towards that line

## id
CON-MSK-4A43892E6FE5B4

## canonical_key
movement-terms-abduction-adduction

## aliases
Moving a limb away from / towards the midline

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases
[clear]

## definition
Abduction is the movement of a part of the body away from a reference line: for a whole limb this is the median plane of the body, and for the fingers or toes it is instead the axis of the hand (the middle finger) or the foot (the second toe). Adduction is the opposite movement, bringing the part back towards that same reference line. The pair is distinct from flexion/extension (which changes the angle at a joint rather than the distance from a reference line) and from medial/lateral rotation (which turns a limb about its own long axis rather than moving it as a whole).

## explicit_objective
State that abduction moves a part away from its reference line (the median plane for a limb, the axis of the hand/foot for digits) and adduction moves it back towards that line, and distinguish the pair from rotation and from flexion/extension.

## pitfalls
Confusing abduction/adduction with medial/lateral rotation. Abduction and adduction move the whole limb towards or away from a reference line; medial and lateral rotation turn the limb about its own long axis without changing that distance.

# Item

## label
Supination and pronation are the rotations of the forearm at the radioulnar joints that turn the palm to face forwards or backwards

## id
CON-MSK-25C23DDC02366D

## canonical_key
forearm-rotation-supination-pronation

## aliases
Turning the forearm palm-up / palm-down

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases
[clear]

## definition
Supination is the rotation of the forearm at the radioulnar joints that turns the palm to face forwards (or, with the elbow flexed, upwards). Pronation is the opposite rotation, turning the palm to face backwards (or downwards). The anatomical position is defined with the forearm fully supinated, which is why the palm faces forwards in that reference posture. The pair describes rotation of the forearm about its long axis and is distinct from flexion/extension of the elbow, which changes how bent the joint is rather than which way the palm faces.

## explicit_objective
State that supination turns the palm forwards/upwards and pronation turns it backwards/downwards, and explain why the anatomical position requires a supinated forearm.

## pitfalls
Confusing supination/pronation (a rotation of the forearm) with flexion/extension of the elbow (a change in joint angle). A fully extended, pronated forearm and a fully extended, supinated forearm have the same elbow angle but opposite palm directions.

## label
Dorsiflexion lifts the foot up towards the shin at the ankle and plantarflexion points it down, as in standing on tiptoe

## id
CON-MSK-44BD6F3315F786

## canonical_key
ankle-movement-dorsiflexion-plantarflexion

## aliases
Lifting / pointing the foot at the ankle

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases
[clear]

## definition
Dorsiflexion is the movement at the ankle joint that lifts the dorsum (top) of the foot up towards the shin, decreasing the angle between them, as when walking on the heels. Plantarflexion is the opposite movement, pointing the foot downwards away from the shin, as when pressing a pedal or rising onto the toes. The pair describes movement of the whole foot up or down at the ankle, and is distinct from inversion/eversion, which instead tilts the sole of the foot to face medially or laterally without lifting or lowering it.

## explicit_objective
State that dorsiflexion lifts the foot towards the shin and plantarflexion points it away, and distinguish the pair from inversion/eversion.

## pitfalls
Confusing dorsiflexion/plantarflexion (movement of the whole foot up or down at the ankle) with inversion/eversion (a tilt of the sole medially or laterally). Standing on tiptoe requires plantarflexion, not inversion, even though both are foot movements.

Inversion turns the sole of the foot to face medially and eversion turns it to face laterally, both at the subtalar joint

## id

## aliases

## arabic_label

## definition
Inversion is the movement of the foot, chiefly at the subtalar joint, that tilts the sole to face medially (inward, towards the other foot). Eversion is the opposite movement, tilting the sole to face laterally (outward, away from the other foot). Both are tilting movements of the sole and are distinct from dorsiflexion/plantarflexion, which instead move the whole foot up or down at the ankle joint without tilting the sole.

## explicit_objective
State that inversion tilts the sole medially and eversion tilts it laterally, and distinguish the pair from dorsiflexion/plantarflexion.

## pitfalls
Confusing inversion/eversion (a tilt of the sole medially or laterally) with dorsiflexion/plantarflexion (raising or lowering the whole foot at the ankle). The two pairs describe movements at different joints and in different planes.

## id

## arabic_label

## definition
Ipsilateral means on the same side of the body as a stated reference point, structure or lesion. Contralateral means on the opposite side of the body from that reference. Both are comparative terms — they are always used to relate one structure's side to another's, most often when describing which side of the body a clinical sign or finding appears on relative to a lesion — rather than absolute positions like medial or lateral.

## explicit_objective
Define ipsilateral (same side) and contralateral (opposite side) relative to a stated reference structure.

## pitfalls
Treating ipsilateral/contralateral as absolute positions like medial/lateral. They only have meaning relative to a stated reference point — "ipsilateral" alone, without saying same side as what, is incomplete.

## definition
Within a long bone, the two types of bone tissue are distributed differently by region. The diaphysis (shaft) is built as a thick tube of compact bone surrounding the central medullary cavity, making compact bone the dominant tissue there. The epiphysis (each expanded end) is, by contrast, chiefly cancellous (spongy) bone internally, wrapped in only a thin outer shell of compact bone. This reversal — compact-dominant shaft, spongy-dominant ends — is also seen in the short and flat bone shape classes, which are built as spongy bone under a thin compact shell (short bones) or as spongy diploe between two compact plates (flat bones).

## explicit_objective
State that compact bone dominates the diaphysis of a long bone while cancellous (spongy) bone dominates the epiphysis, under only a thin compact shell.

## pitfalls
Assuming a long bone is uniformly compact bone throughout. Only the shaft (diaphysis) is predominantly compact; each end (epiphysis) is predominantly spongy bone, which is what keeps the bone light while still resisting the compressive loads transmitted across the nearby joint.
