QST-MANSHIS203-BIOCHEMISTRY-HEME-HEME-SYNTHESIS-SITE

## title
Main site of heme synthesis

## question
The main site of heme synthesis is:

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Bone marrow

## explanation_a
Correct. The bone marrow (the erythroid series) is the main site of haem synthesis, since haem is needed in huge quantity to fill the haemoglobin of every new red cell being produced. The liver is a secondary site, contributing especially to cytochrome and other haem-protein synthesis rather than to the bulk erythroid demand.

## answer_b
Kidney

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The kidney is the main adult site of erythropoietin production, not of haem synthesis itself.

## answer_c
Liver

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The liver does synthesise haem, mainly for its own cytochromes, but it is a secondary site; the erythroid marrow's bulk demand for haemoglobin production makes it the main site.

## answer_d
Mature RBCs

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The mature RBC has already completed haemoglobin synthesis before it loses its nucleus and mitochondria; it can no longer synthesise new haem once it enters the circulation.

## answer_e
White blood cells

## explanation_e
Incorrect. White blood cells do not require haem in the way erythroid cells do, and are not a site of haem synthesis.

QST-MANSHIS203-BIOCHEMISTRY-HEME-ALA-SYNTHASE-REGULATORY

## title
Regulatory (rate-limiting) enzyme for heme synthesis

## question
The regulatory enzyme for heme synthesis is:

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
ALA synthase

## explanation_a
Correct. ALA synthase, the mitochondrial enzyme that condenses glycine with succinyl-CoA to form delta-aminolevulinic acid, catalyses the first step of haem synthesis. Haem itself feeds back on this enzyme to switch production down when haem is abundant, which is what makes it the pathway's rate-limiting, regulatory step. Every later enzyme in the pathway can only act as fast as ALA synthase supplies its substrate.

## answer_b
Haem synthase

## explanation_b
Incorrect. "Haem synthase" is not a standard name for any single enzyme in this pathway; ferrochelatase (which inserts iron into protoporphyrin IX) is sometimes informally called this, but it is the final step, not the regulatory one.

## answer_c
Uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase acts partway through the cytosolic steps of the pathway; it is not the rate-limiting, first-step enzyme.

## answer_d
Protoporphyrinogen oxidase

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Protoporphyrinogen oxidase acts later in the mitochondrial steps, close to the pathway's end, not at its regulatory first step.

## answer_e
Ferrochelatase

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Ferrochelatase catalyses the final step (inserting iron into protoporphyrin IX), not the first, rate-limiting one.

## title
Enzyme completing heme synthesis from protoporphyrin

## question
The synthesis of heme from protoporphyrin is catalysed by the enzyme:

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. ALA synthase catalyses the first step of the pathway (forming ALA from glycine and succinyl-CoA), not the final step completing haem.

## answer_b
Ferroreductase

## explanation_b
Incorrect. "Ferroreductase" is not one of the named enzymes of the haem synthesis pathway.

## answer_c
Ferrooxidase

## explanation_c
Incorrect. "Ferrooxidase" is likewise not one of the named enzymes of this pathway.

## answer_d
Ferrochelatase

## explanation_d
Correct. Ferrochelatase catalyses the final step of haem synthesis, inserting ferrous iron into protoporphyrin IX to complete the haem molecule. ## explanation_e
Incorrect. ALA dehydratase acts early, in the cytosol, converting ALA to porphobilinogen — the second step of the pathway, not the final one.

Site of bilirubin formation

## question
Bilirubin is formed in:

## subject

## status

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The kidney excretes water-soluble breakdown products but is not itself a site where bilirubin is formed.

## answer_b
Spleen and bone marrow

## explanation_b
Correct. Bilirubin is formed in three places in turn: first in the reticuloendothelial system (spleen and bone marrow, where macrophages break down haemoglobin from destroyed red cells), then further processed in the liver, and finally in the intestine, where bacteria convert it to urobilinogen. The lungs

Incorrect. The lungs have no role in hameoglobin breakdown or bilirubin formation.

## answer_d
Skeletal muscle

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Skeletal muscle contains myoglobin, not the erythrocyte mass that generates bilirubin on breakdown, and plays no role in bilirubin formation.

## answer_e
Adipose tissue

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Adipose tissue stores triglycerides and has no role in haemoglobin breakdown or bilirubin formation.

QST-MANSHIS203-BIOCHEMISTRY-HEME-DIRECT-BILIRUBIN-CONJUGATED

## title
Identity of direct bilirubin

## question
Direct bilirubin is:

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Unconjugated bilirubin

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin is fat-soluble and albumin-bound in plasma; it is the "indirect" fraction on the van den Bergh reaction, not the "direct" one.

## answer_b
Conjugated bilirubin

## explanation_b
Correct. "Direct" bilirubin refers to conjugated bilirubin — bilirubin the liver has attached to glucuronic acid via glucuronyl transferase, which makes it water-soluble and able to react directly with the van den Bergh reagent without first needing an accelerant, hence the name. Its water solubility is also why conjugated bilirubin, unlike unconjugated, can appear in the urine when its plasma level rises.

## answer_c
Biliverdin

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Biliverdin is the green intermediate formed before bilirubin itself, from haem oxygenase's action on haem; it is not what "direct bilirubin" refers to.

## answer_d
Urobilinogen

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Urobilinogen is a downstream intestinal breakdown product of bilirubin (formed by gut bacteria), not the conjugated bilirubin fraction itself.

## answer_e
Stercobilin

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Stercobilin is the further-oxidised product responsible for stool colour, downstream of urobilinogen, not the conjugated bilirubin fraction.

QST-MANSHIS203-BIOCHEMISTRY-HEME-HEMOLYTIC-JAUNDICE-UROBILINOGEN

## title
Urobilinogen pattern in haemolytic jaundice

## question
In haemolytic jaundice, urine and faecal urobilinogen are typically:

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Both decreased

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Decreased urobilinogen in both urine and stool is closer to the pattern in obstructive jaundice, where little or no bilirubin reaches the gut to be converted to urobilinogen at all — the opposite of haemolytic jaundice's pattern.

## answer_b
Urine increased, faecal decreased

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Haemolytic jaundice increases faecal urobilinogen too, not just urinary; the excess bilirubin delivered to the gut raises both.

## answer_c
Both increased

## explanation_c
Correct. Haemolytic jaundice increases both urine and faecal urobilinogen, because increased red cell breakdown delivers more bilirubin to the liver and gut than normal, and more of it is converted to urobilinogen by gut bacteria and reabsorbed or excreted. Faecal stercobilinogen (urobilinogen's gut-stage name) tracks how much bilirubin reaches the intestine, so it rises in haemolysis, falls in hepatocellular jaundice, and disappears in obstruction.

## answer_d
Both absent

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Urobilinogen being absent from both urine and stool describes complete obstruction, not haemolysis, where the opposite (an excess) is seen.

## answer_e
Urine absent, faecal increased

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Urinary urobilinogen is not absent in haemolytic jaundice; both urine and faecal urobilinogen rise together in this condition.

## title
Treatment for iron-deficiency anaemia presenting with pica and koilonychia

## question
Which is an appropriate treatment for a nutritional anaemia that presents as a hunger for ice (pagophagia) and/or upward curvature of the fingernails (koilonychia)?

## vignette

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Vitamin B12 treats pernicious/B12-deficiency anaemia, which presents with neurological signs rather than pica or koilonychia.

## title
Deficiency priority in an elderly patient with pallor but no koilonychia

## question
An 81-year-old woman presents with progressive weakness, fatigue, and confusion. Her physical exam is positive for pallor but negative for koilonychia or cracking at the corners of the mouth (angular stomatitis). Which deficiency would be the highest priority in this patient's workup?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Vitamin B12

## explanation_a
Correct. The absence of koilonychia and angular stomatitis argues against iron deficiency as the leading explanation, since those are iron deficiency's own characteristic signs. Combined with the neurological/cognitive picture (progressive weakness, fatigue, confusion), vitamin B12 deficiency becomes the higher-priority differential in an elderly patient — B12 deficiency can progress to subacute combined degeneration of the cord if not identified and treated.

## answer_b
Iron

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Iron deficiency would be expected to show its own additional signs (koilonychia, angular stomatitis, pica), which this patient specifically lacks, making iron a lower priority than B12 here.

## answer_c
Folate

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Folate deficiency can also cause megaloblastic anaemia, but it does not typically produce the neurological/cognitive picture this patient shows; B12 deficiency is the more specific explanation for confusion in this context.

## answer_d
Calcium

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Calcium deficiency causes neuromuscular irritability (tetany) and bone disease, not the anaemia-with-confusion picture described here.
