## title
Role of calcium in excitation-contraction coupling

## question
Role of Ca ++ ions excitation contraction coupling:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Binding of Ca++ ions with troponin cause tropomyosin to move away exposing the binding sites present on actin molecules.

## explanation_a
Correct. Calcium released from the terminal cisternae of the sarcoplasmic reticulum binds troponin C; the troponin complex changes conformation and moves tropomyosin off the myosin-binding site on actin, allowing the cross-bridges to attach. This same troponin C/tropomyosin mechanism is the thin-filament-based calcium switch specific to skeletal and cardiac muscle, distinct from the myosin-based calmodulin switch smooth muscle uses instead.

## answer_b
Muscle relaxation occurs so long as Ca++ ions combine with troponin.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Relaxation happens once calcium is removed from troponin (by the SR calcium pump), not for as long as calcium stays bound — bound calcium is what permits contraction to continue.

## answer_c
Ca++ ions make tropomyosin to move and cover the binding sites on acting

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Calcium moves tropomyosin off, not onto, the actin binding sites; covering the site is tropomyosin's resting, calcium-free position.

## answer_d
Binding of Ca++ ions with myosin heads. Leads to cross bridge cycling

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Calcium binds troponin, not the myosin head directly; the myosin head's ATPase activity and cross-bridge cycling follow from the actin site being uncovered, not from calcium binding myosin itself.

## answer_e
Propagation of action potential to release of Ca++ ions from T tubules.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Calcium is released from the terminal cisternae of the sarcoplasmic reticulum, not from the T tubules, which only carry the depolarisation signal and extracellular fluid.

## title
Event NOT part of skeletal excitation-contraction coupling

## question
Excitation-contraction coupling in skeletal muscles involves all the following events except one. Which one is this exception?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
ATP hydrolysis.

## explanation_a
Incorrect (this IS part of skeletal EC coupling). ATP hydrolysis by the myosin head powers cross-bridge cycling once calcium has uncovered the actin binding site.

## answer_b
binding of Ca+2 to calmodulin.

## explanation_b
Correct — this is the exception. Skeletal muscle's calcium sensor is troponin C, not calmodulin; calcium binding calmodulin to activate myosin light-chain kinase is the smooth-muscle mechanism, not part of skeletal excitation-contraction coupling. Recognising this exception is what separates a skeletal-muscle mechanism question from a smooth-muscle one, since the two tissues use structurally related but mechanistically distinct calcium sensors.

## answer_c
conformational change in dihydropyridine receptor.

## explanation_c
Incorrect (this IS part of skeletal EC coupling). The T-tubule's voltage-sensitive dihydropyridine receptor undergoes a conformational change on depolarisation, which mechanically opens the ryanodine receptor on the sarcoplasmic reticulum.

## answer_d
depolarization of the transverse tubule (T-tubule) membrane,

## explanation_d
Incorrect (this IS part of skeletal EC coupling). The action potential first depolarises the sarcolemma and then spreads into the T-tubule membrane, the trigger step for the whole sequence.

## answer_e
increased Na+ conductance of sarcolemma.

## explanation_e
Incorrect (this IS part of skeletal EC coupling). Increased Na+ conductance of the sarcolemma is what produces the action potential's depolarisation in the first place, upstream of T-tubule depolarisation.

## title
Mechanism of smooth muscle depolarization

## question
Depolarization in smooth muscles is mainly due to:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Na+ inflow.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Sodium inflow is the main depolarising current in skeletal and nerve action potentials, not in smooth muscle, which relies mainly on calcium entry.

## answer_b
K+ outflow.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Potassium outflow is a repolarising current, moving the membrane potential away from, not toward, depolarisation.

## answer_c
Ca++ inflow.

## explanation_c
Correct. Depolarisation of the smooth muscle membrane opens voltage-gated Ca2+ channels, and calcium flows into the cell down its electrochemical gradient, which both depolarises the membrane and, by raising intracellular calcium, directly triggers contraction via calmodulin and myosin light-chain kinase. Because this calcium entry itself both depolarises the membrane and (via calmodulin) directly triggers contraction, smooth muscle's excitation and contraction are more tightly coupled to calcium than skeletal muscle's are.

## answer_d
Ca++ outflow.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Calcium outflow would remove, not add, positive charge from the cytoplasm, opposing depolarisation.

## answer_e
Na+ outflow.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Sodium outflow would also oppose, not cause, depolarisation.

## title
Protein structurally related to calmodulin

## question
Calmodulin is structurally related to:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Actin

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Actin is a thin-filament structural protein, not a calcium-binding sensor protein, and shares no structural family with calmodulin.

## answer_b
Myosin light chain

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The myosin light chain is the target that calmodulin's downstream kinase (myosin light-chain kinase) phosphorylates, not a protein structurally related to calmodulin itself.

## answer_c
Tropomyosin

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Tropomyosin is the elongated protein that blocks the actin binding site; it is not a calcium-binding EF-hand protein and is not structurally related to calmodulin.

## answer_d
Troponin C

## explanation_d
Correct. Calmodulin and troponin C are both members of the EF-hand family of calcium-binding proteins, sharing a closely related helix-loop-helix structural motif, even though they trigger contraction through different downstream mechanisms in smooth versus striated muscle. This is why calmodulin can, under some experimental conditions, substitute for troponin C's calcium-binding role, even though their normal physiological jobs are on opposite sides of the cross-bridge cycle.

## title
Defining character of malignant hyperthermia

## question
Which of the following is a character of Malignant Hyperthermia?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Excessive sweating

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Excessive sweating is a compensatory response to heat, not the defining pathophysiological character of the syndrome itself.

## answer_b
Vasodilation

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Malignant hyperthermia produces intense, sustained muscle contracture rather than a primary vasodilation process.

## answer_c
Production of Cytokines

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Malignant hyperthermia is a pharmacogenetic calcium-channel disorder, not a cytokine-mediated inflammatory process.

## answer_d
Massive increase in metabolic rate

## explanation_d
Correct. The mutated ryanodine receptor floods muscle fibres with calcium uncontrollably, and the resulting sustained contracture and the fibres' attempt to pump the excess calcium back out drive a massive rise in aerobic and anaerobic metabolic rate, producing the syndrome's hallmark heat, CO2 and lactate production. This same hypermetabolic state is what produces the syndrome's other hallmark findings on blood gas analysis: severe hypercapnia, metabolic and respiratory acidosis, and hyperkalaemia.

## answer_e
Brown fat thermogenesis

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Brown fat thermogenesis is a distinct, normal, non-shivering heat-production mechanism unrelated to the muscle-fibre calcium crisis of malignant hyperthermia.

## title
Diagnosis after halothane and succinylcholine trigger rigidity and hyperthermia

## question
A 25-year-old man undergoes general anesthesia with halothane and succinylcholine. Within minutes, he develops muscle rigidity, tachycardia, and a rapid rise in body temperature to 42°C. This condition is most likely:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Neuroleptic malignant syndrome

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome is triggered by antipsychotic (dopamine-blocking) drugs over days, not by volatile anaesthetic/succinylcholine exposure within minutes.

## answer_b
Serotonin syndrome

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Serotonin syndrome follows serotonergic drug combinations, not anaesthetic exposure, and is not the mechanism described here.

Malignant hyperthermia

## explanation_c
Correct. Rapid-onset muscle rigidity, tachycardia and severe hyperthermia within minutes of exposure to a volatile anaesthetic (halothane) plus succinylcholine is the classic trigger and presentation of malignant hyperthermia, caused by a ryanodine receptor mutation that releases sarcoplasmic calcium uncontrollably. Prompt recognition matters because untreated malignant hyperthermia is rapidly fatal, while immediate dantrolene (which blocks the ryanodine receptor directly) is highly effective.

Thyroid storm

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Thyroid storm is precipitated by acute stress or illness in a patient with underlying hyperthyroidism, not specifically by these two anaesthetic drugs.

## answer_e
Sepsis

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Sepsis develops over a longer clinical course from infection, not within minutes of anaesthetic induction.

## title
Receptor mutated in malignant hyperthermia

## question
In malignant hyperthermia, the underlying defect is a mutation in which receptor?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Acetylcholine receptor

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The acetylcholine receptor is a neuromuscular junction protein; its mutation is not the basis of malignant hyperthermia.

## answer_b
Dihydropyridine receptor (DHPR)

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The dihydropyridine receptor is the T-tubule voltage sensor that normally signals the ryanodine receptor to open; it is the receptor the mutated channel responds abnormally to, not the mutated one itself.

## answer_c
Ryanodine receptor (RyR1)

## explanation_c
Correct. Malignant hyperthermia is most often caused by a mutation in the ryanodine receptor 1 (RyR1) gene, the calcium-release channel on the terminal cisternae of the sarcoplasmic reticulum, which becomes abnormally sensitive to volatile anaesthetics and succinylcholine and releases calcium uncontrollably. Because the defect is in the receptor's response to specific pharmacologic triggers, carriers are usually entirely asymptomatic until exposed to a triggering anaesthetic agent.

## answer_d
SERCA pump

## explanation_d
Incorrect. SERCA is the pump that removes calcium back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum during relaxation; it is not the mutated channel in malignant hyperthermia, though its normal reuptake capacity is overwhelmed by the excess calcium release.

## answer_e
Sodium channel

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Sodium channel mutations underlie other muscle channelopathies (such as periodic paralysis), not malignant hyperthermia.

## title
Structure formed by the T-tubule and adjacent cisterns

## question
In skeletal muscles, the transverse tubules and adjacent cisterns consulate?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
A triad.

## explanation_a
Correct. A T-tubule flanked by two terminal cisternae of the sarcoplasmic reticulum, one on each side, forms a triad, positioned at the A-I band junction in skeletal muscle. This structural arrangement, one T-tubule flanked by two cisternae, is what allows a single depolarisation event to trigger calcium release symmetrically from both sides at once.

## answer_b
The sarcolemma.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The sarcolemma is the muscle fibre's own cell membrane, of which the T-tubule is an invagination, not the same structure as the triad it forms with the cisternae.

## answer_c
A myofibril

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A myofibril is a contractile bundle of thick and thin filaments running the length of the fibre; the triad is a membranous junction alongside it, not the myofibril itself.

## answer_d
A muscle fiber.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A muscle fibre is the entire cell, which contains many triads distributed along its myofibrils, not a single triad itself.

## answer_e
The sarcomere.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The sarcomere is the contractile unit between two Z discs; the triad sits at the A-I junction near, but is a distinct structure from, the sarcomere boundary itself.

QST-MANSMSS202-PHYSIO3-MINT-MU-Q09

## title
Event that terminates a single skeletal muscle contraction

## question
A single contraction of skeletal muscle is most likely to be terminated by which of the following actions?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
closure of post-synaptic nicotinic acetylcholine receptors.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Closure of the postsynaptic nicotinic receptors is a fast, brief event ending the end-plate potential itself, well before the mechanical contraction it triggers has finished.

## answer_b
removal of acetylcholine from the neuro-muscular junction.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Acetylcholine is removed from the cleft within milliseconds of release, again long before the resulting mechanical twitch has run its course; this ends transmission, not the contraction directly.

## answer_c
removal of Cat2 from the terminal of the motor neuron.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Calcium leaves the presynaptic terminal quickly after triggering release; this presynaptic event precedes and is not what directly ends the muscle's own mechanical response.

## answer_d
removal of sarcoplasmic Ca+2.

## explanation_d
Correct. The mechanical contraction is terminated by relaxation, which follows once the sarcoplasmic reticulum's calcium pump removes calcium from the cytoplasm; without calcium bound to troponin C, tropomyosin re-covers the actin binding site and cross-bridge cycling stops. This is why the muscle can respond to a second stimulus arriving before relaxation is complete, since the fibre's electrical excitability recovers long before the mechanical contraction (and its calcium-dependent termination) is finished.

## answer_e
return of the dihydropyridine receptor to its resting conformation.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The dihydropyridine receptor's return to its resting conformation is an early step in ending the calcium-release signal, but the contraction itself persists until the calcium already released is actually pumped back out of the cytoplasm.

## title
Mechanism of rigor mortis

## question
Rigor mortis is due to:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Failure of detachment between actin and myosin

## explanation_a
Correct. After death, ATP production stops; without fresh ATP the myosin heads cannot detach from actin once cross-bridges have formed, so the muscle locks into total, permanent contracture — rigor mortis — used to help estimate time of death. This is also why rigor mortis is temporary in a forensic sense: it resolves over one to three days as the muscle proteins themselves begin to break down through autolysis and putrefaction.

Excessive release of Ca++ from SR

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Calcium does rise after death as membrane pumps fail, which does allow cross-bridge formation, but the defining, irreversible problem is that ATP is unavailable to detach the bridges once formed, not that release itself is excessive.

Damage of troponin protein molecules.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Troponin is not structurally damaged in rigor mortis; the problem is a lack of ATP to allow cross-bridge cycling, not destruction of the regulatory proteins.

## answer_d
Lack of binding sites of myosin on actin

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Binding sites on actin are, if anything, exposed (calcium having risen after membrane failure); the muscle locks up because the myosin heads that do bind cannot let go, not because binding sites are lacking.

Failure of propagation of action potential along muscle fibres

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Action potential propagation is irrelevant after death; the mechanical lock of rigor mortis is a direct consequence of ATP depletion at the level of the cross-bridge itself.

## answer_a
Skeletal muscle has a longer refractory period than cardiac muscle

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The relationship is reversed: cardiac muscle's refractory period, prolonged by the action potential's plateau phase, is much longer than skeletal muscle's brief refractory period.

## answer_b
Cardiac muscle has a long refractory period that prevents tetanus

## explanation_b
Correct. Because of the plateau in its action potential, cardiac muscle's absolute and relative refractory periods span almost the whole of contraction and into early relaxation, which prevents the heart from being tetanised — a sustained, unrelaxing contraction that would not suit a pump that must fill between beats. This functional difference, tetanisable skeletal muscle versus non-tetanisable cardiac muscle, is a frequently tested point of contrast between the two striated muscle types.

## answer_c
Smooth muscle has no refractory period

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Smooth muscle does have refractory behaviour associated with its action potentials, even though its electrical activity differs from skeletal and cardiac muscle; "no refractory period at all" is not accurate.

## answer_d
Skeletal muscle cannot be tetanized due to its long refractory period

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Skeletal muscle's refractory period is brief, ending well before the mechanical twitch is over, which is exactly why skeletal muscle can be tetanised by sufficiently frequent stimulation.

## answer_e
Cardiac muscle can be tetanized like skeletal muscle

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Cardiac muscle specifically cannot be tetanised the way skeletal muscle can, because its long refractory period blocks the closely-spaced restimulation that tetanus requires.
