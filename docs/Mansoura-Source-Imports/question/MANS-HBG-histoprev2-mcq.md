## title
Skin macrophage of the epidermis

## question
The cells of the epidermis that act as skin macrophages are:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Keratinocytes

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Keratinocytes are the epidermis's dominant, keratin-forming cell — they build the barrier, they do not patrol it for antigen.

## answer_b
Melanocytes

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Melanocytes are the epidermis's pigment-forming cell, supplying melanin to surrounding keratinocytes; they have no phagocytic or antigen-presenting role.

## answer_c
Langerhans cells

## explanation_c
Correct. Langerhans cells are the epidermis's resident dendritic antigen-presenting cell, part of the mononuclear phagocyte system despite sitting among keratinocytes rather than in connective tissue. They arise from monocyte precursors like every other member of that system, take up antigen that penetrates the epidermis, and migrate to regional lymph nodes to present it to T cells, making them the skin's own local macrophage-lineage sentinel. This is the same distribution logic that places a monocyte-derived phagocyte under a different name in nearly every organ, Kupffer cells in the liver and microglia in the CNS among them.

## answer_d
Merkel cells

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Merkel cells are the epidermis's mechanoreceptor cell, associated with slowly adapting touch sensation, not phagocytosis.

## answer_e
Basal cells

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Basal cells are the proliferative stem-cell layer of the epidermis; their job is renewal, not immune surveillance.

## title
Where the sebaceous gland duct opens

## question
Where does the duct of the sebaceous gland open?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Skin surface

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Reaching the skin surface directly is how an eccrine sweat gland duct behaves; the sebaceous gland's own short, wide duct empties into the hair follicle rather than opening independently onto the surface.

## answer_b
The upper 1/3 of the hair follicle

## explanation_b
Correct. The sebaceous gland's short, wide excretory duct, lined by stratified squamous epithelium continuous with the follicle's outer root sheath, opens into the upper third of the hair follicle. This lets sebum coat the hair shaft as it emerges and spread onto the adjacent skin surface once the hair exits, keeping both hair and skin lubricated. The gland itself sits as an outgrowth from the follicle's external sheath, which is why its duct terminates back into that same follicle rather than forming an independent surface opening.

## answer_c
The middle 1/3 of the hair follicle

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The middle third of the follicle is not where the sebaceous duct empties; the arrector pili muscle inserts near this general region of the follicle, not the gland's duct.

## answer_d
The lower 1/3 of the hair follicle

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The lower third of the follicle, near the bulb and matrix, is where the hair itself is generated, not where the sebaceous duct opens.

## answer_e
The duct of the sweat gland

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The sebaceous gland's duct is entirely separate from the sweat gland's duct; the two glandular systems do not share an outflow.

## title
The extra chromosome in Down syndrome

## question
Down syndrome has an extra chromosome number:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
9

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Chromosome 9 is not the chromosome duplicated in Down syndrome; no named clinical syndrome in this teaching set is built on trisomy 9.

## answer_b
21

## explanation_b
Correct. Down syndrome is trisomy 21 — the presence of three copies of chromosome 21 instead of the normal two, most often from nondisjunction during maternal meiosis I. It is the most common autosomal trisomy compatible with survival to birth, and the extra copy of this specific, gene-dense chromosome produces the syndrome's characteristic intellectual disability, dysmorphic facies and congenital heart defects. Chromosome 21 is deliberately chosen among exam distractors precisely because it is the smallest autosome, which is part of why trisomy 21 is survivable while trisomies of larger chromosomes generally are not.

Incorrect. Chromosome 4 is associated with a partial short-arm deletion (Wolf-Hirschhorn syndrome), a structural loss, not an extra whole chromosome in Down syndrome.

## answer_d
5

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Chromosome 5 is associated with a partial short-arm deletion (cri-du-chat syndrome), not with Down syndrome's extra chromosome.

## answer_e
22

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Chromosome 22 is not the trisomic chromosome in Down syndrome; it is deliberately placed near 21 among the options as a numerically close distractor.

## title
Autosome count in human sperm

## question
How many autosomes are present in human sperm?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
11

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Eleven is half of 22, not the haploid autosome count; no meiotic product carries this number.

## answer_b
22

## explanation_b
Correct. A human sperm is haploid, carrying 23 chromosomes in total — 22 autosomes plus a single sex chromosome (X or Y) — half of the diploid somatic formula of 44 autosomes plus one pair of sex chromosomes. This halving is exactly what meiosis achieves: each gamete receives one member of every homologous pair, so 22 autosomes rather than the 44 (22 pairs) present in a somatic cell. Fertilisation then restores the diploid 44+XX or 44+XY somatic formula by combining this sperm's 22+X/Y with the oocyte's own 22+X.

## answer_c
44

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Forty-four autosomes is the diploid, somatic-cell count (22 pairs), not the haploid count carried by a single sperm.

## answer_d
45

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Forty-five is neither the haploid nor the diploid autosome count; it does not correspond to a normal cell's chromosome complement.

## answer_e
46

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Forty-six is the total chromosome number of a normal diploid somatic cell (44 autosomes plus 2 sex chromosomes), not the autosome count of a haploid gamete.

## title
The longest stage of the cell cycle

## question
Which of the following represents the longest stage in the cell cycle?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Interphase

## explanation_a
Correct. Interphase — comprising G1, S and G2 — occupies the great majority of a dividing cell's cycle, commonly on the order of 90% or more of total cycle time, while the four mitotic stages together take up only a small remaining fraction. This is the period in which the cell grows, carries out its normal metabolic work, and replicates its DNA in S-phase, all before the comparatively brief, visually dramatic business of chromosome segregation in mitosis proper. A cell that appears "resting" under the microscope between divisions is, in fact, in this longest and most metabolically active stage.

## answer_b
Prophase

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Prophase is one of the four short mitotic stages; even though it is typically the longest of the four mitotic stages themselves, it is still far shorter than interphase as a whole.

## answer_c
Metaphase

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Metaphase, the brief alignment of chromosomes at the equatorial plate, is one of the shortest phases of the entire cycle.

## answer_d
Anaphase

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Anaphase, the rapid poleward separation of sister chromatids, is a brief mitotic stage, not the longest part of the cycle.

## answer_e
Telophase

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Telophase, reforming the nuclear envelope and completing division, is a short closing mitotic stage, not the cycle's longest phase.

## title
Interpreting a 47,XY+21 karyotype

## question
If chromosomal karyotyping was performed and resulted in a 47, XY+21 karyotype, what is the possible diagnosis of the case?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Edward syndrome

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Edward syndrome is trisomy 18, karyotype 47,XY+18 or 47,XX+18, not +21.

## answer_b
Down syndrome

## explanation_b
Correct. The notation 47,XY+21 reads as 47 total chromosomes, a male sex-chromosome constitution (XY), and one extra copy of chromosome 21 — exactly the karyotype of Down syndrome (trisomy 21). Reading a karyotype string this way — total count, sex chromosomes, then any named extra or missing chromosome after the plus or minus sign — is the standard notation used across every trisomy and monosomy syndrome. Here the "+21" is unambiguous: three copies of chromosome 21 rather than two is the chromosomal basis of Down syndrome regardless of the patient's sex chromosomes.

## answer_c
Cri du chat

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Cri-du-chat syndrome is a partial deletion of the short arm of chromosome 5 (46,XY or XX,5p-), a structural loss on a normal chromosome count, not an extra chromosome 21.

## answer_d
Wolf syndrome

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Wolf (Wolf-Hirschhorn) syndrome is a partial deletion of the short arm of chromosome 4, again a structural deletion, not an extra chromosome 21.

## answer_e
YY syndrome

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A "YY syndrome" (47,XYY) is an extra Y sex chromosome, not an extra autosome 21, and would be written 47,XYY rather than 47,XY+21.

## title
Commonest mechanism behind Down syndrome

## question
Which of the following represents the commonest mechanism of genetic abnormality in Down syndrome?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Non-disjunction in maternal gamete

## explanation_a
Correct. The great majority of Down syndrome cases arise from nondisjunction — failure of the paired chromosome 21 homologues to separate — during the first meiotic division of the primary oocyte, i.e. in the maternal gamete. Because a human primary oocyte remains arrested in meiosis I from fetal life until the cycle in which it is finally ovulated (sometimes for decades), this arrested state is thought to make maternal meiosis I especially prone to nondisjunction, which is also why the risk of trisomy 21 rises sharply with advancing maternal age. Paternal nondisjunction, translocation and mosaicism all also cause Down syndrome, but each accounts for only a small minority of cases compared with this maternal meiosis I mechanism.

## answer_b
Non-disjunction in paternal gamete

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Nondisjunction can occur in the paternal gamete too, but it is a much less common source of trisomy 21 than maternal meiosis I nondisjunction.

## answer_c
Translocation to chromosome 14

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Robertsonian translocation involving chromosome 14 (and chromosome 21) causes a minority of Down syndrome cases, notably the familial, inherited form, but it is not the commonest mechanism overall.

## answer_d
Translocation to any autosome

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Translocation to any autosome describes the translocation mechanism broadly, but translocation as a whole is still a minority cause next to maternal nondisjunction.

## answer_e
Mosaicism

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Mosaic Down syndrome, in which only some of the body's cells carry the extra chromosome 21 from a post-fertilisation mitotic error, is the rarest of the recognised mechanisms.

## title
Highest DNA-to-cytoplasm ratio in the mitotic cycle

## question
At which stage of the mitotic cell cycle is there the highest ratio of DNA to cytoplasm?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Beginning of G1 phase

## explanation_a
Incorrect. At the beginning of G1 the cell has just completed mitosis and cytokinesis, so it holds an unreplicated (single, 46-s) DNA content in a full-sized cytoplasm — the lowest, not the highest, DNA-to-cytoplasm ratio of the cycle.

## answer_b
End of S phase

## explanation_b
Correct. S-phase replicates the cell's entire DNA content, doubling it from 46-s to 46-d chromosomes, while the cytoplasm itself has not yet had the chance to grow further or divide. The instant S-phase completes is therefore the point at which the cell holds its full doubled DNA content within the smallest cytoplasmic volume it will have for the rest of the cycle, before G2's further growth or mitosis's eventual division dilutes that ratio again. G2 and mitosis keep the same doubled DNA content, but the cell's cytoplasmic volume only increases from this point onward until division halves it, so the ratio is highest right at the S-to-G2 transition.

## answer_c
End of G2 phase

## explanation_c
Incorrect. By the end of G2 the DNA content is still doubled (46-d) as it was at the end of S-phase, but the cytoplasm has continued to grow through G2, so the ratio has fallen slightly from its S-phase peak.

## answer_d
End of telophase

## explanation_d
Incorrect. By the end of telophase the cell has completed cytokinesis and division, restoring the single (46-s) DNA content in each daughter cell — a low ratio, not the cycle's highest.

## answer_e
Beginning of metaphase

## explanation_e
Incorrect. At the beginning of metaphase the DNA content is still doubled, but cytoplasmic growth through G2 has already diluted the ratio below its S-phase peak.

## title
Naming the process that exchanges chromosome material

## question
Which of the following represents the process by which maternal and paternal chromosomes exchange their material?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Synapsis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Synapsis is the pairing of homologous maternal and paternal chromosomes at zygotene; it brings the chromosomes together but is not itself the exchange of material between them.

## answer_b
Crossing over

## explanation_b
Correct. Crossing over is the reciprocal exchange of segments between non-sister chromatids of paired homologous (one maternal, one paternal) chromosomes, occurring at pachytene of meiotic prophase I once synapsis has aligned the homologues. It is the mechanism absent from mitosis but present in meiosis, and it is exactly what shuffles maternal and paternal genetic material into new combinations on each chromatid, which is the source of genetic recombination and variation among gametes. Crossing over depends on synapsis having already paired the homologues, since an exchange between two DNA strands can only happen once they are physically aligned.

## answer_c
Interphase

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Interphase is the period of growth and DNA replication before division begins; it precedes meiosis's pairing and exchange events rather than being the exchange itself.

## answer_d
Dyad forming

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Dyad formation describes the paired sister-chromatid structure of a replicated chromosome, not the process of exchanging material between maternal and paternal homologues.

## answer_e
Cytokinesis

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Cytokinesis is the physical division of the cytoplasm into two daughter cells at the end of a division cycle, unrelated to chromosomal material exchange.

## title
The site of flagella in the human body

## question
Which of the followings is a site of flagella?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Esophagus

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The oesophagus is lined by stratified squamous epithelium, which carries no cilia or flagella at all.

## answer_b
Fallopian tube

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The Fallopian tube is lined in part by ciliated cells whose motile cilia help move the oocyte toward the uterus — true cilia, present in numbers over the cell surface, not a single flagellum.

## answer_c
Small intestine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The small intestine's absorptive epithelium carries microvilli, not cilia or a flagellum.

## answer_d
Tail of sperm

## explanation_d
Correct. The sperm tail is the human body's own flagellum — a single, long motile process built on the same 9+2 microtubule axoneme as a cilium, but present singly per cell rather than in the numerous short processes typical of ciliated epithelium, and specialised for propelling the whole cell forward rather than for moving fluid or particles over a stationary epithelial surface. This single-process, whole-cell-propelling arrangement is what distinguishes a flagellum from the many shorter cilia of a ciliated epithelial cell, even though both share the same internal axoneme structure.

## answer_e
Ureter

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The ureter is lined by transitional epithelium, which carries neither cilia nor a flagellum.

## title
The function of the nucleolus

## question
What is the function of nucleolus?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Bile acid metabolism

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Bile acid metabolism is a liver-cell biochemical function unrelated to any nuclear organelle; it is not a nucleolar job.

## answer_b
Exchange between nucleus and cytoplasm

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Exchange between nucleus and cytoplasm is carried out through the nuclear pore complexes of the nuclear envelope, not by the nucleolus itself.

## answer_c
Lipid metabolism

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Lipid metabolism is a smooth endoplasmic reticulum function; the nucleolus plays no role in it.

## answer_d
Secretory function

## explanation_d
Incorrect. General secretory function belongs to the Golgi apparatus and the secretory pathway, not to the nucleolus.

## answer_e
Synthesis of ribosomal RNA (rRNA)

## explanation_e
Correct. The nucleolus is a rounded, deeply basophilic, unbounded mass inside the nucleus whose function is to synthesise ribosomal RNA and assemble ribosomal subunits, which then pass out through the nuclear pores to the cytoplasm to build ribosomes. Its three ultrastructural dark components trace this production line directly — the pars amorpha (the rDNA genes themselves), the pars fibrosa (newly synthesised rRNA) and the pars granulosa (maturing ribosomal subunits) — and a cell's nucleolar size scales with how much protein it is making, which is why large, prominent nucleoli are a feature of both normal protein-secreting cells and rapidly growing malignant ones.

## title
The connective tissue cell with a chart-wheel nucleus

## question
Which of the following connective tissue cells has Chart-Wheel nucleus?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Adipose cells

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The adipose cell's nucleus is flattened and pushed to the cell's periphery by its single large fat droplet, not cartwheel-shaped.

## answer_b
Fibroblasts

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The fibroblast has an oval or spindle-shaped, pale-staining, open (euchromatic) nucleus appropriate to its high protein-synthetic activity, not a cartwheel pattern.

## answer_c
Macrophages

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The macrophage has a kidney-shaped or oval nucleus, not the cartwheel pattern.

## answer_d
Mast cells

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The mast cell has a central, spherical, pale nucleus, distinct from the plasma cell's eccentric cartwheel nucleus even though both cells are basophilic and can otherwise be confused.

## answer_e
Plasma cells

## explanation_e
Correct. The plasma cell is identified by its cartwheel (clock-face) nucleus — heterochromatin clumped radially around the nuclear periphery like the spokes of a wheel — set eccentrically to one side of the cell, beside a pale-staining area corresponding to its well-developed Golgi apparatus. This nuclear pattern, reflecting the cell's intense, sustained antibody-manufacturing activity, is one of the standard cell-identification landmarks taught alongside the neutrophil's multilobed nucleus and the small lymphocyte's dense round one.
