## title
Effect of a superior cervical ganglion lesion

## question
What is the effect of a lesion in the superior cervical ganglion?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Anhidrosis on the opposite side of the lesion

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Loss of sympathetic supply reduces sweating on the SAME side as the lesion (anhidrosis is ipsilateral, part of the Horner's-type picture), not the opposite side.

## answer_b
Exophthalmos on the opposite side of the lesion

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A sympathetic lesion produces enophthalmos (a slight sinking of the eye from loss of tone in the smooth muscle of the orbit), not exophthalmos, and it is ipsilateral, not on the opposite side.

## answer_c
Increased sweat secretion

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Sweat glands are sympathetically innervated, so a sympathetic lesion REDUCES, not increases, sweat secretion on the affected side.

## answer_d
Miosis on the same side of the lesion

## explanation_d
Correct. The superior cervical ganglion is the relay for sympathetic outflow to the eye. Losing it removes sympathetic tone to the dilator pupillae, so the parasympathetically-driven constrictor pupillae is unopposed. This produces miosis (pupillary constriction) on the same side as the lesion, one component of the Horner's-type picture.

## answer_e
Pale and cold skin

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Loss of sympathetic (vasoconstrictor) tone instead produces a flushed, warm skin from unopposed vasodilation, not pale and cold skin.

## title
Cause of ptosis after cervicothoracic spinal surgery

## question
A 38-year-old man underwent spinal cord surgery at the lower cervical and upper thoracic segments. After recovery, he developed ptosis. Which of the following is the possible cause of the ptosis in this case?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Paralysis of the ciliary muscle

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The ciliary muscle is parasympathetically controlled (accommodation), and its paralysis would impair near focusing, not cause ptosis.

## answer_b
Paralysis of the constrictor pupillae muscle

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The constrictor (sphincter) pupillae is parasympathetically controlled; its paralysis would cause mydriasis (pupil dilation), not ptosis.

## answer_c
Paralysis of the dilator pupillae muscle

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The dilator pupillae is sympathetically controlled and its paralysis would cause miosis (part of the same Horner's-type picture), but it is a pupil sign, not the cause of the eyelid drooping (ptosis) itself.

## answer_d
Paralysis of the medial rectus muscle

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The medial rectus is a somatic extraocular muscle (oculomotor nerve), unrelated to the autonomic sympathetic pathway interrupted by this surgery, and its paralysis would cause an outward deviation of the eye, not ptosis.

## answer_e
Paralysis of the upper (superior) tarsal muscle of the eyelid

## explanation_e
Correct. Surgery at the lower cervical/upper thoracic spinal cord can interrupt the sympathetic outflow (originating at T1-T2) to the head and neck. This removes sympathetic tone to the superior (upper) tarsal muscle, the small smooth-muscle component of the levator palpebrae superioris that sympathetic fibres normally keep partly contracted. Its paralysis produces the partial ptosis characteristic of Horner's syndrome.

## title
Effect of sympathetic supply to the pelvic viscera

## question
Sympathetic supply to the pelvic viscera causes which one of the following?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Contraction of the external anal sphincter

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The external anal sphincter is skeletal muscle under somatic (pudendal nerve), not sympathetic, control.

## answer_b
Defecation

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Defecation is a parasympathetic effect (via the pelvic nerve); sympathetic pelvic outflow instead inhibits it, by contracting the internal anal sphincter.

## answer_c
Micturition

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Micturition is a parasympathetic effect; sympathetic pelvic outflow instead inhibits it, by contracting the internal urethral sphincter and relaxing the detrusor.

## answer_d
Stimulation of seminal fluid and prostatic secretions

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Sympathetic pelvic outflow contracts the smooth muscle of the vas deferens, seminal vesicles and prostate to expel their secretions during ejaculation, but this option describes stimulating secretion production, not the emission/ejaculation contraction that is the actual sympathetic effect.

## answer_e
Vasoconstriction of the sex organs and their shrinkage

## explanation_e
Correct. Sympathetic stimulation vasoconstricts the blood vessels of the sex organs and external genitalia. This produces shrinkage, not engorgement. It is the functional opposite of the vasodilatation (erection) produced by the parasympathetic pelvic nerve.

## title
A characteristic of the adrenal medulla

## question
Which one of the following characterizes the adrenal medulla?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
It antagonizes the effects of sympathetic stimulation.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The adrenal medulla is itself part of the sympathetic system and REINFORCES, rather than antagonises, sympathetic effects, by releasing catecholamines that act on the same adrenergic receptors as sympathetic nerve endings.

## answer_b
It is a modified parasympathetic ganglion.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The adrenal medulla is a modified SYMPATHETIC, not parasympathetic, ganglion — its chromaffin cells are the functional equivalent of postganglionic sympathetic neurons.

## answer_c
It receives preganglionic nerve supply from the anterior horn cells of the lower 6 thoracic segments of the spinal cord.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The adrenal medulla's preganglionic supply arises from the LATERAL horn (intermediolateral cell column) of the spinal cord, not the anterior horn, which instead gives rise to somatic motor neurons.

## answer_d
It secretes epinephrine 20% and norepinephrine 80%.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The ratio is reversed: the adrenal medulla secretes roughly 80% epinephrine and 20% norepinephrine, not the other way round.

## answer_e
Its secretion is basal and decreased during sleep.

## explanation_e
Correct. The adrenal medulla's catecholamine secretion is not all-or-none. It is basal and continuous, reflecting ongoing sympathetic tone. This rate normally decreases during sleep, when overall sympathetic activity falls.

## subject

Incorrect. The trochlear nerve is a purely somatic motor nerve, supplying only the superior oblique muscle, with no parasympathetic component.

## title
A feature of sympathetic postganglionic neurons

## question
Which one of these is a feature of postganglionic neurons in the sympathetic nervous system?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Mainly cholinergic and white in colour.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Sympathetic postganglionic fibres are mostly ADRENERGIC (noradrenergic), not cholinergic (sweat glands are the notable cholinergic exception), and unmyelinated fibres appear greyish, not white, in colour.

## answer_b
Myelinated.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Sympathetic postganglionic fibres are generally UNmyelinated (type C fibres); it is the PREganglionic fibres that are myelinated (type B).

## answer_c
Originating distal to the effector organ.

## explanation_c
Correct. Sympathetic ganglia (the paravertebral chain) lie close to the spinal cord. The postganglionic neuron's own cell body therefore originates at a distance from — distal to — the effector organ it ultimately supplies. A long postganglionic axon bridges that distance.

## answer_d
Short.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Sympathetic postganglionic fibres are LONG, not short, because their ganglion sits far from the effector organ; it is the preganglionic fibre that is short.

## answer_e
Type B fibres.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Sympathetic postganglionic fibres are type C (unmyelinated); type B fibres describe the myelinated preganglionic autonomic fibres instead.

## title
Most abundant signalling molecule from the adrenal medulla

## question
What is the most abundant signalling molecule released from the adrenal medulla?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Acetylcholine

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Acetylcholine is the transmitter released BY the preganglionic fibres SUPPLYING the adrenal medulla, not a molecule the medulla itself secretes into the blood.

## answer_b
Dopamine

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Dopamine is a biosynthetic intermediate on the pathway from tyrosine to norepinephrine and epinephrine, but it is not the dominant molecule secreted by the medulla itself.

## answer_c

## title
Mechanism of organophosphate insecticide poisoning

## question
Hassan, a 45-year-old farm worker, was admitted to the emergency department with increased saliva and tear production, diarrhoea, nausea, vomiting, small pupils, sweating, muscle tremors, bronchospasm and confusion, with a history of using insecticide on the farm. What is the mechanism of action of this insecticide?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Blocks the action of the cholinesterase enzyme

## explanation_a
Correct. Organophosphate insecticides irreversibly inhibit acetylcholinesterase, letting acetylcholine accumulate at muscarinic receptors throughout the body. This produces the muscarinic-excess (SLUDGE-type) picture seen here — salivation, lacrimation, GI cramps with vomiting and diarrhoea, urination-type increased secretions, miosis (small pupils), sweating and bronchospasm. This pattern is the recognisable bedside signature of organophosphate/anticholinesterase poisoning.

## answer_b
Blocks cholinergic muscarinic receptors

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Blocking muscarinic receptors (an antimuscarinic action, like atropine) would produce the OPPOSITE picture — dry mouth, mydriasis and urinary retention — not this muscarinic-excess presentation.

## answer_c
Blocks cholinergic nicotinic receptors

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Blocking nicotinic receptors would impair neuromuscular and ganglionic transmission, not produce a muscarinic-excess picture of increased secretions and miosis.

## answer_d
Causes excessive uptake of acetylcholine by cholinergic neurons

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Organophosphates work by blocking acetylcholine's BREAKDOWN (via acetylcholinesterase inhibition), not by increasing its uptake into neurons.

## answer_e
Inhibits the release of acetylcholine from cholinergic neurons

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Organophosphates do not inhibit acetylcholine release; they instead prevent its breakdown, letting released acetylcholine accumulate and act repeatedly at the receptor.

## title
A true statement about acetylcholine

## question
Which one of the following is true about acetylcholine?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Its action at the motor end plate is blocked by atropine.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Atropine blocks MUSCARINIC receptors; the motor end plate is a NICOTINIC receptor site, so atropine does not block acetylcholine's action there.

## answer_b
Its action at the motor end plate is blocked by a small dose of nicotine.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A small dose of nicotine instead STIMULATES the nicotinic receptors at the motor end plate (mimicking acetylcholine); it does not block them — only a large, desensitising dose would eventually block transmission.

## answer_c
It is released by both pre- and postganglionic sympathetic nerves.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Acetylcholine is released by sympathetic PREganglionic fibres and by the (cholinergic exception) sympathetic postganglionic fibres to sweat glands, but the great majority of sympathetic postganglionic fibres are noradrenergic, not cholinergic — so it is not accurate to say acetylcholine is released by sympathetic postganglionic nerves in general.

## answer_d
It is the only neurotransmitter secreted by parasympathetic neurons.

## explanation_d
Correct. Parasympathetic postganglionic neurons release only acetylcholine, acting on muscarinic receptors at their effector organ. This is unlike the sympathetic division, whose postganglionic fibres are mostly noradrenergic. Sweat glands are the notable cholinergic exception among sympathetic effectors.

## answer_e
Its synthesis is catalysed by the cholinesterase enzyme.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Acetylcholine's SYNTHESIS is catalysed by choline acetyltransferase; acetylcholinesterase instead catalyses its BREAKDOWN — the two enzymes are commonly confused but perform opposite reactions.
