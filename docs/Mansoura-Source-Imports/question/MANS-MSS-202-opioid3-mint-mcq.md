## title
Cause of morphine's poor oral bioavailability

## question
What is the cause of Morphine poor bioavailability (25%) from oral dosing?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
High First-pass metabolism

## explanation_a
Correct. Morphine is well absorbed from the gut but undergoes extensive hepatic first-pass glucuronidation before reaching the systemic circulation. Only about 25% of an oral dose therefore becomes bioavailable. This is why oral morphine doses must be substantially higher than equivalent parenteral doses.

## answer_b
Poor intestinal absorption

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Poor bioavailability here is not due to poor absorption; morphine crosses the gut wall well. The loss happens afterward, during hepatic processing, not at the point of intestinal uptake.

## answer_c
Digestive enzymes effect

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Digestive enzymes in the gut lumen do not meaningfully destroy morphine before absorption; the relevant metabolism (glucuronidation) happens in the liver, not through luminal digestive enzyme breakdown.

## answer_d
Stimulated intestinal peristalsis.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Morphine actually slows, rather than stimulates, intestinal peristalsis, and altered peristalsis is not the mechanism behind its low oral bioavailability in any case.

## answer_e
Vasoconstrictor effect on intestinal vasculature

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Morphine's oral bioavailability problem is hepatic (first-pass metabolism), not a vasoconstrictor effect limiting absorption into the intestinal vasculature.

## title
Why a morphine pain pump improved chronic diarrhoea

## question
A 39-year-old man who suffered a work-related injury has chronic back pain. A morphine pain pump was implanted to control his chronic pain. Formerly, he had chronic diarrhea which is now much improved. What is the most likely explanation for this finding?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Improved gastrointestinal motility

## explanation_a
Incorrect. 'Improved motility' implies faster, more coordinated forward movement, but morphine actually slows transit; describing the effect as improved motility mischaracterises the direction of the change.

## answer_b
Improved intestinal circular muscle tone

## explanation_b
Correct. Morphine acts on mu-opioid receptors in the enteric nervous system, increasing circular smooth muscle tone and segmenting, non-propulsive contractions. This increased tone with reduced propulsive coordination is exactly what slows transit enough to control his previously troublesome diarrhoea.

## answer_c
Weakened anal sphincter tone

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Morphine increases, not weakens, anal sphincter tone, which helps retain rather than release bowel contents. A weakened sphincter would worsen, not improve, diarrhoea.

## answer_d
Weakened rectal balloon dilation pressure

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Morphine's GI effects work through increased tone and reduced propulsion, not through a change specifically described as weakened rectal balloon dilation pressure, which is not the mechanism being tested here.

## answer_e
Weakened transverse colonic musculature

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Weakened colonic musculature would tend to worsen bowel dysfunction rather than improve diarrhoea; morphine's actual effect is increased tone, the opposite of weakening.

Opioid effect that resists tolerance

## question
Tolerance develops to all of the following actions of opioids except?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Constipation

## explanation_a
Correct. Constipation is a classic exception: it persists essentially unchanged no matter how long a patient has been on chronic opioid therapy. Most other opioid effects fade with tolerance, but this one does not. This is why chronic opioid patients need indefinite laxative prophylaxis.

## answer_b
Analgesia

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Analgesia is one of the effects to which clear tolerance develops, meaning progressively higher doses are needed over time to achieve the same pain relief.

Euphoria

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Euphoria also shows tolerance with repeated opioid use, reflecting adaptation in the CNS reward pathway, so it is not the exception being tested.

## answer_d
Nausea and vomiting

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Nausea and vomiting typically improve (tolerance develops) with continued opioid use, often resolving after the first days of treatment, unlike constipation, which does not improve.

Incorrect. In biliary colic, morphine can raise sphincter of Oddi tone and worsen biliary pain, making it a less favourable choice compared with other analgesics in that specific condition.

Treatment of choice for heroin overdose

## question
A young man is with known heroin addiction is brought in the emergency in unconscious state. On examination, the patient has decreased bowel sounds, depressed respiration and pinpoint pupil. The treatment of choice for this patient is:

IV naloxone

## explanation_b
Correct. This presentation, coma, decreased bowel sounds, respiratory depression and pinpoint pupils, is the classic opioid overdose triad. Intravenous naloxone, a rapid-onset competitive opioid antagonist, is the emergency treatment of choice, reversing respiratory depression and coma quickly.

Oral Buprenorphine

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Buprenorphine is a partial opioid agonist used in opioid dependence treatment programmes, not an emergency antidote for acute overdose; the oral route and its partial agonist action are both wrong for this acute reversal need.

Diagnosis: tramadol plus fluoxetine, confusion, hyperthermia

## question
A 50-year-old woman on tramadol for chronic back pain is started on fluoxetine (SSRI) for depression. She develops confusion, agitation, tachycardia, and hyperthermia. This is most likely:

## subject

B

## explanation_a

Correct. Tramadol's monoaminergic (serotonin/noradrenaline reuptake-inhibiting) component, combined with an SSRI like fluoxetine, raises synaptic serotonin excessively. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome

Anaphylaxis

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Anaphylaxis presents with urticaria, angioedema and hypotension from an IgE-mediated allergic reaction, not this confusion-tachycardia-hyperthermia pattern tied to a specific serotonergic drug combination.

A 53-year-old man is requesting meperidine for his chronic back pain. His physician is reluctant to use meperidine for the treatment of chronic pain because of:

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
metabolite toxicity.

## explanation_a
Correct. Meperidine's active metabolite, normeperidine, accumulates with repeated chronic dosing and is pro-convulsant, lowering the seizure threshold. This metabolite toxicity is the specific pharmacological reason chronic meperidine use is avoided.

## answer_b
poor oral absorption.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Poor oral absorption is not the concern driving meperidine's avoidance in chronic pain; the problem is what happens after repeated dosing (metabolite accumulation), not absorption itself.

## answer_c
increased addiction potential.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. While meperidine, like other opioids, carries addiction potential, that is not the specific, distinguishing reason it is avoided for chronic use compared with other opioids; the testable mechanism here is metabolite toxicity.

## answer_d
patient noncompliance.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Patient noncompliance is a general concern that could apply to any medication and is not the specific pharmacological reason meperidine is avoided in chronic pain.

## answer_e
likelihood that meperidine will be diverted or sale on the street.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Diversion or street sale is a general opioid-control concern, not the specific pharmacological reason (metabolite accumulation and seizure risk) that makes meperidine particularly unsuitable for chronic dosing.

## title
Safest analgesic for a patient with chronic renal failure

## question
A 65-year-old man with chronic renal failure requires analgesia for moderate pain. Which analgesic is safest regarding renal function?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Morphine

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Morphine's active metabolite, morphine-6-glucuronide, is renally cleared and can accumulate to toxic levels in renal impairment, making it a less safe choice in chronic renal failure than an agent without this risk.

## answer_b
Pethidine

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Pethidine's active metabolite, normeperidine, is likewise renally cleared and accumulates in renal failure, raising seizure risk; it carries the same category of renal-accumulation danger as morphine.

## answer_c
Paracetamol (acetaminophen)

## explanation_c
Correct. Paracetamol is metabolised hepatically rather than depending on renal clearance. This avoids both the renally-cleared toxic-metabolite risk of morphine/pethidine and the direct renal-blood-flow-reducing risk of NSAIDs. It is therefore the comparatively safest choice in chronic renal failure.

## answer_d
NSAIDs

## explanation_d
Incorrect. NSAIDs directly reduce renal prostaglandin-mediated blood flow, which can worsen renal function further in a patient whose kidneys are already compromised, making them a poor choice here.

## title
Analgesic with mixed agonist-antagonist actions

## question
Which one of the following analgesic drugs has mixed agonistic and antagonistic actions?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
heroin.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Heroin is a full mu-opioid receptor agonist, not a mixed agonist-antagonist; it lacks any antagonist component at opioid receptors.

## answer_b
fentanyl.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Fentanyl is likewise a full, pure mu-opioid receptor agonist, used for potent analgesia, without any antagonist activity mixed in.

## answer_c
pentazocine.

## explanation_c
Correct. Pentazocine is a mixed opioid agonist-antagonist: an agonist at kappa opioid receptors, producing analgesia, combined with antagonist or partial agonist activity at mu receptors. This is a distinct pharmacological category from pure agonists or pure antagonists. It can precipitate withdrawal if given to a patient dependent on a pure mu agonist.

## answer_d
naloxone.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Naloxone is a pure opioid receptor antagonist, blocking receptors without producing any agonist analgesic effect at all, the opposite pharmacological profile from a mixed agonist-antagonist.

## title
Molecular mechanism mediating morphine's analgesia

## question
A 34-year-old woman was admitted to the emergency department because of multiple fractures sustained in a car accident. The patient complained of severe pain, and an intramuscular injection of morphine was given. Which of the following molecular actions most likely mediated the analgesic effect of the drug in this patient?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Opening of Ca2+ channels on presynaptic nerve terminals

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Morphine's mu-receptor action closes, rather than opens, presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channels, reducing calcium influx and hence neurotransmitter release, the opposite of what this option describes.

## answer_b
Closing of chloride channels on postsynaptic neurons

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Morphine's classic postsynaptic action is opening potassium channels, not closing chloride channels; chloride-channel modulation is more characteristic of GABA-A receptor pharmacology, not the opioid mechanism.

## answer_c
Stimulation of substance P release from nociceptive nerve terminals

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Morphine suppresses, rather than stimulates, substance P release from nociceptive nerve terminals, since reduced presynaptic calcium influx lowers release of pain-signalling transmitters including substance P.

## answer_d
Opening of K+ channels on postsynaptic neurons

## explanation_d
Correct. Morphine's mu-opioid receptor activation opens potassium channels on postsynaptic neurons, hyperpolarising the cell and reducing its excitability. This is one of two complementary inhibitory mechanisms underlying opioid analgesia. The other is presynaptic calcium channel closure, which reduces neurotransmitter release.

## answer_e
Closing of Na+ channels on presynaptic nerve terminals

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Morphine's presynaptic action closes calcium channels, not sodium channels; sodium channel blockade is more characteristic of local anaesthetic mechanisms, not the opioid mu-receptor pathway.
