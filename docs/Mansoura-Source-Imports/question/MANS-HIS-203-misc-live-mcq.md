## title
Largest blast cell in the haemopoietic series

## question
The largest blast cell in the hemopoietic cell line is:

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Proerythroblast

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The proerythroblast, the earliest cell of the erythroid series, is a mid-sized blast cell, not the largest across all the haemopoietic lines.

## answer_b
Normoblast

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The normoblast is a later, smaller stage of the erythroid series, well past the blast stage, and is not the cell being compared here.

## answer_c
Lymphoblast

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The lymphoblast, the earliest cell of the lymphoid series, is comparable in size to the other blast cells but not the largest of them.

## answer_d
Monoblast

## explanation_d
Correct. The monoblast, the earliest precursor of the monocyte-macrophage line, is the largest blast cell among the haemopoietic series. This matches the mature monocyte it eventually gives rise to, which is likewise the largest cell normally found in a peripheral blood smear.

## answer_e
Megakaryoblast

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The megakaryoblast is the earliest cell of the thrombopoietic series; it is the megakaryocyte later in that same series, not the megakaryoblast itself, that becomes exceptionally large through endomitosis.

## title
Eosinophil nucleus shape

## question
What is the shape of the nucleus of eosinophils?

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Horse shoe (bilobed) nucleus

## explanation_a
Correct. The eosinophil's nucleus is bilobed, shaped like a horseshoe, with two lobes joined by a thin strand of chromatin. That two-lobe shape, together with its large acidophilic (eosin-staining) specific granules, is what identifies the eosinophil on a blood film. It is this bilobed nucleus that distinguishes it at a glance from the neutrophil's multilobed one.

## answer_b
Irregular nucleus

## explanation_b
Incorrect. "Irregular" is too vague to describe the eosinophil's nucleus, which has a specific, recognisable bilobed shape rather than an arbitrary irregular outline.

## answer_c
Segmented nucleus

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A segmented (multilobed, usually 2-5 lobes) nucleus describes the neutrophil, not the eosinophil, which has only two lobes.

## answer_d
Rounded nucleus

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A rounded nucleus describes lymphocytes and monocytes' nuclear shape more closely than the eosinophil's distinctly bilobed one.

## answer_e
Kidney shaped nucleus

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A kidney (or indented oval) shaped nucleus describes the monocyte, not the eosinophil, whose nucleus is bilobed rather than singly indented.

## title
Largest cell in the blood smear (past-exam block, دفعة 63)

## question
Which of the following cells is the largest in the blood smear?

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Neutrophil

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The neutrophil, at about 10-12 micrometres, is smaller than the monocyte.

## answer_b
Eosinophil

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The eosinophil is a similar size to the neutrophil, again smaller than the monocyte.

## answer_c
Platelet

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The platelet, at only 2-4 micrometres, is in fact the smallest formed element of the blood, the opposite extreme from what this question asks for.

## answer_d
Monocyte

## explanation_d
Correct. The monocyte, at up to 20 micrometres, is the largest normal cell in a peripheral blood smear. It carries a large, eccentric, kidney- or horseshoe-shaped nucleus and abundant grey-blue cytoplasm, and it leaves the circulation to become the tissue macrophage.

## answer_e
Basophil

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The basophil, the least numerous granulocyte, is similar in size to the neutrophil and eosinophil, again smaller than the monocyte.

## title
Main site of destruction of old RBCs (Lecture 3&4)

## question
Which of the following is the main site of destruction of old RBCs?

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Liver

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The liver plays a secondary role in removing aged red cells and processing the breakdown products, but it is not the MAIN site — that is the spleen.

## answer_b
Lymph node

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The lymph node filters lymph, not blood, and screens it for antigen; it is not the site where aged red cells are destroyed.

## answer_c
Peyer's patches

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Peyer's patches are gut-associated lymphoid tissue in the ileum, involved in mucosal immune surveillance, not red cell destruction.

## answer_d
Spleen

## explanation_d
Correct. The spleen is the main site of destruction of old red blood cells. Its red pulp forces circulating red cells through narrow splenic cords and stave-cell sinusoids, and resident macrophages phagocytose the ones too fragile to deform through that filter.

## answer_e
Tonsils

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The tonsils are lymphoid tissue guarding the oropharynx against inhaled and ingested antigen; they play no role in red cell destruction.
