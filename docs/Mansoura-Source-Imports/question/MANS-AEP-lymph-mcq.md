## title
Vessels with valves

## question
Which of these vessels have valves?

## subject
imm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Arteries and veins

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Arteries carry blood under high pulsatile pressure away from the heart and do not need valves; only veins, the low-pressure return side, have them.

## answer_b
Arterioles and venules

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Arterioles, like arteries, are on the high-pressure outflow side and have no valves; venules are too small and numerous to be a standard teaching pairing here.

## answer_c
Capillaries and lymphatic vessels

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Capillaries are single-cell-thick exchange vessels with no muscular wall and no valves.

## answer_d
Veins and capillaries

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Veins do have valves, but capillaries do not, so this pairing is wrong.

## answer_e
Lymphatic vessels and veins

## explanation_e
Correct. Both veins and lymphatic vessels carry fluid against gravity back toward the heart at low pressure, and both rely on multiple one-way valves, together with surrounding skeletal muscle contraction, to keep that flow moving in a single direction. Arteries and capillaries, by contrast, have no valves because they do not need to prevent backflow in the same way. This shared low-pressure, one-directional design is why veins and lymphatics are grouped together for this fact.

Abdomen

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The abdomen has extensive lymphatic drainage, including the mesenteric and aortic (para-aortic) node groups.

## answer_e
Peripheral nervous system

## explanation_e
Incorrect. "Peripheral nervous system" is not one of the standard avascular exceptions taught for this fact; the exception is the central nervous system, not the peripheral nerves.

## title
Tonsils versus lymphoid organs

## question
What differentiates tonsils from lymphoid organs?

## subject
imm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Tonsils have a capsule.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This states the opposite of the true distinguishing feature; a true lymphoid organ has a capsule, and the tonsils do not.

## answer_b
Tonsils do not have a capsule.

## explanation_b
Correct. Lymphoid tissue is divided into encapsulated lymphoid organs, such as lymph nodes, the spleen and the thymus, and unencapsulated (diffuse) lymphoid tissue, such as the tonsils and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT). The tonsils are collections of lymphoid tissue that lack a surrounding fibrous capsule, which is exactly why they are classed as lymphoid tissue rather than a true lymphoid organ. This capsule/no-capsule distinction is the standard teaching line dividing the two categories.

## answer_c
Tonsils break down old RBCs.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Breaking down old red blood cells is a spleen function, not a feature that distinguishes tonsils from lymphoid organs.

## answer_d
Tonsils are responsible for T cell maturation.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. T cell maturation is a thymus function, not a tonsil feature, and does not address the capsule distinction being asked about.

## answer_e
Tonsils are grouped in different body regions (cervical, axillary, inguinal).

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Grouping by body region describes lymph nodes generally (cervical, axillary, inguinal, and so on), not a feature specific to tonsils.

## title
Lymph capillaries joining

## question
Lymph capillaries join to form….....

## subject
imm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Lymph vessels.

## explanation_a
Correct. Lymph begins in blind-ended lymphatic capillaries within tissues, which unite with one another to form progressively larger lymphatic vessels. Those lymphatic vessels, after passing through one or more lymph nodes for filtering, unite in turn to form larger lymph trunks, which finally drain into one of the two large lymphatic ducts (thoracic duct or right lymphatic duct). Lymph capillaries are therefore the first step in this chain, joining directly to form lymph vessels, not trunks, nodes or ducts.

## answer_b
Lymph trunks.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Lymph trunks are formed further along the chain, by the union of multiple lymph vessels, not directly from capillaries.

## answer_c
Lymph nodes.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Lymph nodes are filtering stations that lymphatic vessels pass through, not structures formed by capillaries joining together.

## answer_d
Lymph ducts.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The lymph ducts (thoracic duct and right lymphatic duct) are the final, largest structures in the chain, formed by the union of lymph trunks, not directly from capillaries.

## answer_e
Thoracic duct.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The thoracic duct is the single largest lymphatic vessel in the body, the end point of the whole collecting chain, not the immediate product of capillaries joining.

## question
The right upper limb drains its lymph in ……………

## subject
imm

## status

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Thoracic duct.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The thoracic duct drains lymph from the whole body below the diaphragm and from the left side of the head, neck, thorax and left upper limb, not the right upper limb.

## answer_b
Superior vena cava.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The superior vena cava is a blood vessel that receives venous blood, not a lymphatic collecting structure.

## answer_c
Right internal jugular vein.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The right internal jugular vein carries venous blood from the head and neck; it is where the right lymphatic duct ultimately empties, but it is not itself the lymph trunk draining the upper limb.

## answer_d
Right lymph trunk.

## explanation_d
Correct. Lymph from the right upper limb, together with lymph from the right side of the head/neck and right thorax, drains into the right lymphatic duct (or directly into the right subclavian/jugular venous confluence via separate trunks including the right subclavian lymph trunk). This right-sided drainage is confined to the right side of the body, in contrast to the much larger thoracic duct, which drains everywhere else. The right lymph trunk is therefore the correct destination named among these options for right upper limb lymph.

## answer_e
Right atrium.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The right atrium receives venous blood returning to the heart; lymph enters the venous system further upstream, at the great veins of the neck, not directly into the heart.

## title
Structure without lymphatic vessels

## question
Which of the following structures does not contain lymphatic vessels?

## subject
imm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Liver

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The liver has a rich lymphatic drainage, producing a large proportion of the body's total lymph flow.

## answer_b
Kidney

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The kidney has lymphatic vessels draining toward the lateral aortic (lumbar) lymph nodes.

## answer_c
Brain

## explanation_c

Which one of the followings share in the formation of the external jugular vein?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Brachiocephalic vein.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The brachiocephalic vein is formed much further downstream, by the union of the internal jugular and subclavian veins, and does not itself contribute to forming the external jugular vein.

## answer_b
Common lingual vein.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. "Common lingual vein" is not a standard named tributary of the external jugular vein.

## answer_c
Retromandibular vein.

## explanation_c
Correct. The external jugular vein is formed in the substance of the parotid gland, or just below it, by the union of the posterior division of the retromandibular vein with the posterior auricular vein. It then descends superficially across sternocleidomastoid to drain into the subclavian vein near the root of the neck. The retromandibular vein's posterior division is therefore one of the two vessels whose union directly forms the external jugular vein, complementing its anterior division, which instead joins the facial vein.

## answer_d
Sigmoid sinus.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The sigmoid sinus is the intracranial origin of the internal jugular vein, an entirely separate vessel from the external jugular vein.

## answer_e
Subclavian vein.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The subclavian vein is where the external jugular vein drains at its lower end, not one of the vessels forming it at its upper end.

## title
Level of origin of the maxillary artery

## question
What is the level of origin of maxillary artery?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Lower border of hyoid bone.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The lower border of the hyoid bone is not the landmark used for the external carotid's terminal division.

## answer_b
Lower border of mandible.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The lower border of the mandible is where the facial artery crosses onto the face, not where the maxillary artery originates.

## answer_c
Neck of mandible.

## explanation_c
Correct. The maxillary artery arises, together with the superficial temporal artery, from the terminal division of the external carotid artery behind the neck of the mandible, within the substance of the parotid gland. From there it runs forward, initially deep to the neck of the mandible, to reach the infratemporal and pterygopalatine fossae. This origin behind the neck of the mandible is the same landmark used for the external carotid's terminal division overall.

## answer_d
Sternoclavicular joint.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The sternoclavicular joint is a landmark used for structures at the root of the neck, unrelated to the maxillary artery's origin.

## answer_e
Upper border of thyroid cartilage.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The upper border of the thyroid cartilage is the landmark for the common carotid artery's bifurcation, not the maxillary artery's origin.
