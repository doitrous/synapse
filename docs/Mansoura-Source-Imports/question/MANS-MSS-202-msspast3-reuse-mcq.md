# Item

QST-MANSMSS202-MSSPAST3-REUSE-MSSPAST3-Q01

## title
Effects of common peroneal nerve injury

## question
Effects of common peroneal nerve injury include one of the following:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Foot drop and inversion of the foot

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Foot drop is correctly attributed to loss of dorsiflexion, but inversion is not lost in a common peroneal lesion. Inversion is a tibialis posterior action, and tibialis posterior is a tibial nerve muscle in the deep posterior compartment, which the common peroneal nerve does not supply. The eversion muscles, not the invertors, are the ones lost alongside dorsiflexion here.

## answer_b
Foot drop and eversion of the foot

## explanation_b
Correct. The common peroneal nerve divides into the deep peroneal nerve, which supplies the anterior compartment dorsiflexors, and the superficial peroneal nerve, which supplies the lateral compartment evertors. A single common peroneal lesion denervates both compartments at once, so the patient loses dorsiflexion (foot drop) and eversion together, and walks with a high-stepping gait to clear the dropped foot.

## answer_c
Dorsiflexion and eversion of the foot

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Dorsiflexion is indeed lost, matching the nerve's actual effect, but the option pairs it with eversion as if eversion were preserved. Eversion is lost too, since the superficial peroneal nerve that performs it is a branch of the same common peroneal trunk. The deficit is a loss of both movements, not dorsiflexion with intact eversion.

## answer_d
Dorsiflexion and inversion of the foot

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This option gets both halves backwards: common peroneal palsy causes loss of dorsiflexion, not a paradoxical preservation of it, and loss of eversion, not inversion. The muscles it denervates are extensors and evertors, not flexors and invertors.

## answer_e
Loss of sensation on the medial side of the leg

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Sensory loss from a common peroneal lesion is over the lateral leg and dorsum of the foot, the territory of the superficial and deep peroneal nerves' cutaneous branches. The medial side of the leg is saphenous nerve territory, a branch of the femoral nerve that has no relation to the common peroneal nerve at all.

## title
Muscle supplied by the superior gluteal nerve

## question
The superior gluteal nerve supplies one of the following muscles:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Gluteus medius

## explanation_a
Correct. The superior gluteal nerve leaves the pelvis above piriformis through the greater sciatic foramen and runs between gluteus medius and gluteus minimus, supplying both. These two muscles abduct the hip and, more importantly, stabilise the pelvis in single-leg stance, so their denervation produces a Trendelenburg gait.

## answer_b
Gluteus maximus

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Gluteus maximus, the body's largest muscle and the main hip extensor, is supplied by the inferior gluteal nerve, not the superior one. The two gluteal nerves are easily confused because of their similar names, but they leave the pelvis at different points and supply different muscle groups.

## answer_c
Obturator externus

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Obturator externus is a deep lateral rotator of the hip supplied by the obturator nerve, which reaches it through the obturator canal rather than the greater sciatic foramen. It has no relationship to the superior gluteal nerve's distribution at all.

## answer_d
Inferior gemellus

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Inferior gemellus is one of the short external rotators of the hip and is supplied by the nerve to quadratus femoris, a small branch of the sacral plexus distinct from both gluteal nerves. Its position below piriformis puts it outside the superior gluteal nerve's territory.

## answer_e
Biceps femoris

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Biceps femoris is a hamstring in the posterior thigh, supplied by the sciatic nerve, mainly its tibial division for the long head and its common peroneal division for the short head. It lies well below the gluteal region that the superior gluteal nerve serves.

Muscle supplied by the inferior gluteal nerve

The inferior gluteal nerve supplies one of the following muscles:

## subject

## answer_b
Gluteus maximus

Correct. The inferior gluteal nerve leaves the pelvis below piriformis through the greater sciatic foramen and supplies gluteus maximus alone. Biceps femoris

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Biceps femoris, part of the hamstring group, is supplied by the sciatic nerve rather than either gluteal nerve. The gluteal nerves supply only the gluteal muscles proper, not the posterior thigh muscles below them.

## title
Structure damaged by a first-layer sole laceration

## question
A 6 year old boy playing barefooted in his backyard steps on a piece of broken glass and suffers a large transverse cut on his sole, at the level of the midfoot. In the emergency room, the examining physician determines the cut is to the depth of the first layer of the plantar muscles. Which of the following structures is most likely damaged in this injury?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Plantar arterial arch

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The plantar arterial arch is formed deep in the sole by the lateral plantar artery, lying at the level of the fourth (deepest) layer alongside the interossei. A cut only as deep as the first layer would not reach it.

## answer_b
Tendons of the flexor digitorum longus muscle

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The tendons of flexor digitorum longus pass through the second layer of the sole, together with quadratus plantae and the lumbricals. A first-layer cut stops short of this plane, so these tendons would be spared.

## answer_c
Tendon of the peroneus longus muscle

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The tendon of peroneus longus crosses the sole in the fourth, deepest layer on its way to the base of the first metatarsal. It lies well below the plane reached by a wound confined to the first layer.

## answer_d
Abductor hallucis muscle

## explanation_d
Correct. The first, most superficial layer of the sole holds three muscles: abductor hallucis, flexor digitorum brevis and abductor digiti minimi. A transverse cut at the midfoot that reaches only this depth is positioned exactly where abductor hallucis lies, on the medial side of the sole.

## answer_e
Superficial peroneal nerve

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The superficial peroneal nerve is a leg and dorsum-of-foot nerve, not a structure found within the layers of the sole at all. It has no course through the plantar muscles that a sole laceration could reach.

## title
Muscle affected by lateral plantar nerve damage

## question
Damage to the lateral plantar nerve would affect one of the following muscles:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Adductor hallucis

## explanation_a
Correct. The lateral plantar nerve, the smaller terminal branch of the tibial nerve, supplies every intrinsic muscle of the sole except the four the medial plantar nerve keeps for itself. Adductor hallucis is one of those lateral plantar muscles, along with quadratus plantae, the interossei and the lateral three lumbricals.

## answer_b
Abductor hallucis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Abductor hallucis is one of the medial plantar nerve's own four muscles, described as the sole's equivalent of the hand's median-nerve thenar muscles. A lateral plantar nerve lesion leaves it untouched.

## answer_c
Flexor digitorum brevis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Flexor digitorum brevis is likewise one of the medial plantar nerve's four muscles, not a lateral plantar one. It sits in the first layer of the sole alongside abductor hallucis, both spared by a lateral plantar lesion.

## answer_d
Flexor hallucis brevis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Flexor hallucis brevis is the third of the medial plantar nerve's muscles, despite lying in the third, deeper layer of the sole with the lateral-plantar-supplied adductor hallucis right beside it. Layer position does not predict nerve supply here, only the classic four-muscle list does.

## answer_e
First lumbrical

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The first lumbrical is the fourth and last of the medial plantar nerve's muscles; the other three lumbricals belong to the lateral plantar nerve. A lateral plantar lesion would weaken those other three lumbricals, not the first.

## title
Action of popliteus that unlocks the knee

## question
Which of the following is the action of popliteus muscle?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Medial rotation of the tibia

## explanation_a
Correct. Popliteus rotates the femur laterally on the fixed tibia to start flexion, which is mechanically identical to describing the tibia rotating medially on the femur. Either description names the same unlocking action, freeing the knee from the rigid, fully extended position it locks into.

## answer_b
Medial rotation of the femur

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The locking mechanism at full extension is described as lateral rotation of the tibia on the femur, driven passively by quadriceps femoris, not as a rotation of the femur produced by popliteus at that stage. Popliteus acts at the start of flexion, to unlock, not to lock.

## answer_c
Locking of the knee

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Locking of the fully extended knee is a passive, obligatory rotation happening automatically during the last 15 degrees of extension, produced by the pull of quadriceps femoris rather than by popliteus. Popliteus performs the opposite action, unlocking the joint.

## answer_d
Extension of the hip

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Popliteus acts entirely at the knee joint, rotating the tibia or femur to unlock the joint at the start of flexion. It has no attachment or action at the hip joint.

## answer_e
Extension of the knee

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Extension of the knee is quadriceps femoris' action alone. Popliteus instead assists the hamstrings in initiating flexion, the opposite movement, by first unlocking the extended joint.

## title
Nerve supplying the dorsiflexors of the ankle

## question
Dorsiflexors of the ankle are supplied by….. nerve?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Deep peroneal

## explanation_a
Correct. The deep peroneal nerve supplies every muscle of the anterior compartment of the leg: tibialis anterior, extensor hallucis longus, extensor digitorum longus and peroneus tertius. All four are dorsiflexors of the ankle, so the whole dorsiflexor group shares a single nerve.

## answer_b
Posterior tibial

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The posterior tibial (tibial) nerve supplies the posterior compartment, whose muscles are plantar flexors and invertors, the opposite action to dorsiflexion. It has no role in the movement this question asks about.

## answer_c
Superficial peroneal

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The superficial peroneal nerve supplies the lateral compartment evertors, peroneus longus and brevis, not the anterior compartment dorsiflexors. Eversion and dorsiflexion are separate movements carried by separate nerves within the same common peroneal territory.

## answer_d
Sciatic

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Sciatic is the parent nerve that later divides into the tibial and common peroneal nerves; naming it does not specify which of its two divisions actually supplies the dorsiflexors. The deep peroneal branch, not the undivided sciatic trunk, is the precise answer being tested.

## answer_e
Plantar

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Plantar nerves, medial and lateral, are branches of the tibial nerve distributed to the sole of the foot, well distal to the ankle dorsiflexors. They play no part in ankle dorsiflexion.

## title
Axillary artery — parts and course

## question
Axillary artery:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Becomes brachial artery at lower border of teres minor

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The axillary artery becomes the brachial artery at the lower border of teres major, not teres minor. Teres minor is a rotator-cuff muscle on the posterior scapula with no relation to this arterial landmark.

## answer_b
Axillary vein lies laterally to the artery

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The axillary vein lies medial to the artery throughout their course, not lateral to it. This medial position is what keeps the vein out of the way of the cords of the plexus, which cluster around the artery's second part.

## answer_c
Is divided into three parts by pectoralis minor

## explanation_c
Correct. Pectoralis minor crosses in front of the axillary artery and divides it into three parts by its relation to the muscle: the first part lies above pectoralis minor, the second part lies behind it, and the third part lies below it. Each part gives its own distinct branches.

## answer_d
Second part is clasped by the two heads of median nerve

## explanation_d
Incorrect. It is the third part of the axillary artery that is flanked by the two roots of the median nerve, which join in front of it, not the second part. The second part instead lies surrounded by the cords of the brachial plexus, which take their names from their position there.

## answer_e
Gives off the superior thoracic artery from its second part

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The superior (highest) thoracic artery is the sole branch of the first part of the axillary artery, arising above pectoralis minor before the vessel even reaches the muscle. It is not a branch of the second part.

## title
Origin of the cephalic vein

## question
The cephalic vein:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Arises in the region of the anatomical snuffbox

## explanation_a
Correct. The cephalic vein begins at the lateral end of the dorsal venous arch of the hand, over the anatomical snuffbox region on the radial side of the wrist. From there it ascends along the lateral (radial) border of the forearm and arm.

## answer_b
At the elbow, is deep to the lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm

## explanation_b
Incorrect. At the elbow the cephalic vein lies superficial to, not deep to, the lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm, since both structures run in the superficial fascia with the vein closer to the skin. Being deep to a cutaneous nerve would be unusual for a superficial vein.

## answer_c
Terminates by joining the brachial vein

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The cephalic vein terminates by piercing the clavipectoral fascia in the deltopectoral groove and draining into the axillary vein, not the brachial vein. The brachial veins are the deep venae comitantes accompanying the brachial artery, a separate system the cephalic vein does not join.

## answer_d
Is medial to biceps in the arm

## explanation_d
Incorrect. In the arm, the cephalic vein runs in the deltopectoral groove on the lateral side of biceps, not medial to it. Its whole course, from wrist to shoulder, stays on the lateral (radial) aspect of the limb, which is exactly what distinguishes it from the medially running basilic vein.

## answer_e
Has no valves

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Superficial veins of the limbs, including the cephalic vein, carry valves like other veins that return blood against gravity. The claim of having no valves is not a feature attributed to it.

## title
Origin of the suprascapular nerve

## question
Suprascapular nerve is branch from ……

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
roots of brachial plexus

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The dorsal scapular and long thoracic nerves are the classic examples of branches arising directly from the roots of the brachial plexus, before any trunk has formed. The suprascapular nerve arises one stage later, from the upper trunk.

## answer_b
upper trunk

## explanation_b
Correct. The suprascapular nerve arises from the upper trunk of the brachial plexus, carrying C5 and C6 fibres. It runs laterally across the posterior triangle of the neck and passes through the suprascapular notch to supply supraspinatus and infraspinatus.

## answer_c
middle trunk

## explanation_c
Incorrect. There is no 'middle trunk' in the brachial plexus; the three trunks are upper, middle and lower, but the suprascapular nerve specifically arises from the upper trunk, not from an intermediate level between it and the lower trunk.

## answer_d
lower trunk

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The lower trunk is formed from the C8 and T1 roots and gives rise to structures feeding the medial cord and ultimately the ulnar nerve territory. The suprascapular nerve's C5, C6 fibres place its origin at the upper trunk instead.

## answer_e
posterior cord

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The posterior cord is a later stage of the plexus, formed from the posterior divisions of all three trunks, giving the axillary and radial nerves among others. The suprascapular nerve branches off much earlier, at the trunk level, before the cords have even formed.

## title
Where the dorsalis pedis artery begins

## question
Dorsalis pedis artery begins .. ………

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
behind medial malleolus

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Behind the medial malleolus is where the posterior tibial artery passes on its way into the sole, not where the dorsalis pedis artery begins. Dorsalis pedis is a dorsal, not a posteromedial, vessel.

## answer_b
mid way between 2 malleoli

## explanation_b
Correct. The anterior tibial artery becomes the dorsalis pedis artery in front of the ankle, midway between the medial and lateral malleoli, deep to the extensor retinacula. From there it crosses the dorsum of the foot before diving into the sole to complete the deep plantar arch.

## answer_c
behind flexor retinaculum

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The flexor retinaculum is a wrist structure of the upper limb; the lower-limb structures analogous to it at the ankle are the extensor and flexor retinacula, and even the ankle's flexor retinaculum lies posteromedially, not at the site where dorsalis pedis begins anteriorly.

## answer_d
behind lateral malleolus

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Behind the lateral malleolus is where the peroneal tendons and the small saphenous vein's territory lie, not the starting point of dorsalis pedis, which begins anteriorly rather than posterolaterally.

## answer_e
at the sole

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The sole is where dorsalis pedis ends, diving between the heads of the first dorsal interosseous to complete the deep plantar arch, not where it begins. Its origin is dorsal, at the front of the ankle, well before it ever reaches the sole.
