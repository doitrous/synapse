QST-MANSHIS203-PHARMACOLOGY-ANEMIA-VIGNETTES-IRON-TOXICITY-GASTROENTERITIS

## title
Primary manifestation of acute iron toxicity

## question
Which of the following is a primary manifestation of acute iron toxicity?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Hemochromatosis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Haemochromatosis is a chronic iron-overload condition (organ iron deposition over years), not the acute manifestation of a single toxic ingestion.

## answer_b
Necrotizing gastroenteritis

## explanation_b
Correct. Acute iron toxicity (classically from accidental ingestion of iron tablets, especially in children) primarily damages the gastrointestinal mucosa, producing necrotizing gastroenteritis as its primary manifestation. Corrosive injury to the gut lining causes vomiting, abdominal pain and gastrointestinal bleeding, which can progress to systemic toxicity (metabolic acidosis, shock, hepatic injury) if untreated.

## answer_c
Respiratory alkalosis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Acute iron toxicity tends toward metabolic acidosis as toxicity progresses, not respiratory alkalosis.

## answer_d
Hypertension

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Hypotension and shock (from fluid loss and direct cardiotoxicity), not hypertension, are features of severe iron toxicity.

## answer_e
Bradycardia

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Tachycardia (from hypovolaemia and shock), not bradycardia, is the expected cardiovascular finding in significant iron toxicity.

QST-MANSHIS203-PHARMACOLOGY-ANEMIA-VIGNETTES-DESFERRIOXAMINE-IRON-ANTIDOTE

## title
Antidote for decontamination in acute iron toxicity

## question
What is the specific antidote used for decontamination in acute iron toxicity?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Deferasirox

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Deferasirox is an oral iron chelator used for chronic iron overload (as in repeated transfusions), not the specific decontamination antidote for acute iron poisoning.

## answer_b
Dimercaprol

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Dimercaprol is a chelator used for arsenic, mercury and lead poisoning, not iron.

## answer_c
Penicillamine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Penicillamine is a chelator used mainly for copper (Wilson's disease) and sometimes lead, not iron.

## answer_d
Desferroxamine

## explanation_d
Correct. Desferrioxamine (deferoxamine), an iron-chelating agent, binds free iron and allows its excretion, and is the specific antidote used for decontamination in acute iron toxicity. It is given parenterally in significant poisoning to bind circulating free iron before it causes further tissue damage.

## answer_e
Flumazenil

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Flumazenil is the antidote for benzodiazepine overdose, unrelated to iron toxicity.

## title
Poisoning treated by hydroxocobalamin

## question
A therapeutic use of Vitamin B12 (specifically hydroxocobalamin) is in the treatment of which type of poisoning?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Lead poisoning

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Lead poisoning is treated with chelators such as dimercaprol, EDTA, or succimer, not hydroxocobalamin.

## answer_b
Cyanide poisoning

## explanation_b
Correct. Hydroxocobalamin, a form of vitamin B12, given in high dose, is a specific antidote for cyanide poisoning. Its cobalt centre binds free cyanide ion with high affinity, converting it into cyanocobalamin (a stable, non-toxic B12 form) that is excreted renally — removing cyanide from mitochondrial cytochrome oxidase, where it would otherwise block the electron transport chain and cause cellular asphyxiation.

## answer_c
Arsenic poisoning

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Arsenic poisoning is treated with dimercaprol, not hydroxocobalamin.

## answer_d
Iron poisoning

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Iron poisoning is treated with desferrioxamine, not hydroxocobalamin.

## answer_e
Carbon monoxide poisoning

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Carbon monoxide poisoning is treated with 100% oxygen (or hyperbaric oxygen), not hydroxocobalamin.

## title
Dihydrofolate reductase inhibitor causing folate-responsive anemia

## question
Folic acid can be used therapeutically to alleviate anemia associated with dihydrofolate reductase inhibitors. Which of the following is an example of such a drug?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Phenytoin

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Phenytoin can reduce folate levels through altered absorption/metabolism, but it does not act as a dihydrofolate reductase inhibitor.

## answer_b
Oral contraceptives

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Oral contraceptives are not dihydrofolate reductase inhibitors.

## answer_c
Methotrexate

## explanation_c
Correct. Methotrexate inhibits dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR), the enzyme that regenerates active tetrahydrofolate — its anticancer and immunosuppressive action comes from starving rapidly dividing cells of active folate. This same mechanism, applied systemically, can also produce a folate-deficiency-pattern (megaloblastic) anaemia as an adverse effect, which is why folinic acid (leucovorin, a folate form that bypasses the DHFR block) is given as rescue therapy in high-dose methotrexate regimens.

## answer_d
Erythropoietin

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Erythropoietin stimulates red cell production directly; it is not a dihydrofolate reductase inhibitor.

## answer_e
Iron dextran

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Iron dextran is a parenteral iron preparation, not a dihydrofolate reductase inhibitor.

QST-MANSHIS203-PHARMACOLOGY-ANEMIA-VIGNETTES-MEGALOBLASTIC-B12-VIGNETTE-DIAGNOSIS

## title
Diagnosis in a vignette of macrocytic anemia with neurological symptoms

## question
A 63-year-old woman came to the clinic because of tiredness. She has been increasingly fatigued over the past year but in recent weeks has become breathless on exertion. Her feet have become numb and she has started to become unsteady on her feet. Her lab investigations showed Haemoglobin 8.2 g/dL (normal 11.7-15.7 g/dL) and Mean corpuscular volume (MCV) 112 fL (normal 80-99 fL). What is the most probable diagnosis?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Anemia of chronic disease

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Anaemia of chronic disease is typically normocytic or mildly microcytic, not markedly macrocytic (raised MCV) as in this vignette.

## answer_b
Hemolytic anemia

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Haemolytic anaemia does not typically present with the described neurological symptoms (numbness, unsteady gait), and while it can raise MCV modestly via reticulocytosis, it does not fit this vignette's overall picture as well as megaloblastic anaemia.

## answer_c
Iron deficiency anemia

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Iron deficiency anaemia produces a microcytic, not macrocytic, picture — the opposite MCV direction from what is described here.

## answer_d
Megaloblastic anemia

## explanation_d
Correct. The combination of anaemia, a markedly raised MCV (macrocytosis, 112 fL against a normal upper limit of 99 fL), and neurological symptoms (numbness, unsteady gait/ataxia) points to megaloblastic anaemia from vitamin B12 deficiency. Folate deficiency causes an identical megaloblastic blood picture, but only B12 deficiency produces this kind of neurological involvement, which is the key discriminator in this vignette.

## title
Cause of the neurological manifestation in the B12-deficiency vignette

## question
Neurological manifestation in this patient is due to:

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Autonomic neuropathy

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Autonomic neuropathy affects the autonomic nervous system (blood pressure, bowel/bladder control), not the sensory ataxia and upper motor neuron pattern described here.

## answer_b
Carpal tunnel syndrome

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Carpal tunnel syndrome is a focal median nerve compression at the wrist, an unrelated, localised condition.

## answer_c
Diabetic neuropathy

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Diabetic neuropathy is a plausible cause of peripheral numbness in an older patient, but nothing in this vignette suggests diabetes, and the combination with megaloblastic anaemia points specifically to B12 deficiency's own neurological complication instead.

## answer_d
Peripheral neuritis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Peripheral neuritis is a nonspecific term for peripheral nerve inflammation, not the specific named complication of B12 deficiency this vignette is testing.

## answer_e
Subacute combined degeneration of spinal cord

## explanation_e
Correct. Subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord is B12 deficiency's specific neurological complication — a demyelination of the dorsal columns and lateral corticospinal tracts that produces the characteristic combination of impaired proprioception/vibration sense (unsteady gait, numbness) and upper motor neuron signs. It is the mechanistic link between this patient's megaloblastic anaemia and her neurological symptoms.

QST-MANSHIS203-PHARMACOLOGY-ANEMIA-VIGNETTES-PERNICIOUS-ANEMIA-ATROPHIC-GASTRITIS

## title
Vignette of atrophic gastritis with hypersegmented neutrophils

## question
A 60-year-old patient presented with anorexia, weakness, paresthesia and mental changes. His tongue was red, tendon reflexes were diminished, hemoglobin was 6 g% with large red cells and neutrophils had hypersegmented nuclei. Endoscopy revealed atrophic gastritis. Supplementation of which factor is likely to correct his condition?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Folic acid

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Folic acid would correct the anaemia but not the atrophic-gastritis mechanism or the neurological findings, and could mask ongoing B12-deficiency neurological damage if given alone.

## answer_b
Vitamin B12

## explanation_b
Correct. Pernicious anaemia is vitamin B12 deficiency caused by autoimmune destruction of gastric parietal cells (and the intrinsic factor they secrete), producing the atrophic gastritis seen on endoscopy. The vignette's B12-specific findings — glossitis (red tongue), diminished reflexes, paraesthesia, mental changes, and hypersegmented neutrophils (six or more nuclear lobes, reflecting impaired DNA synthesis in the myeloid line as well as the erythroid line) — together with the large red cells (macrocytosis) point specifically to vitamin B12 supplementation as the corrective treatment.

## answer_c
Pyridoxine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Pyridoxine (vitamin B6) deficiency causes a sideroblastic-pattern anaemia, not this megaloblastic, atrophic-gastritis picture.

## answer_d
Riboflavin

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Riboflavin (vitamin B2) deficiency is not associated with this vignette's findings.

## answer_e
Iron

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Iron supplementation would not address this patient's macrocytic, hypersegmented-neutrophil, atrophic-gastritis presentation, which points to B12 deficiency rather than iron deficiency (which would instead show microcytic red cells).
