## title
Definition of first-pass metabolism

## question
What does the term 'first pass metabolism' mean?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Degradation of drugs at the site of administration before reaching systemic circulation.

## explanation_a
Correct. First-pass metabolism is the loss of a drug to metabolism before it ever reaches the systemic circulation — most classically hepatic metabolism during an oral drug's passage through the portal circulation and liver, but the same idea extends to metabolism at the gut wall or wherever the drug first passes through metabolically active tissue. This is why an orally administered drug with extensive first-pass metabolism needs a much larger oral dose than an intravenous dose to achieve the same systemic exposure. It is the mechanism behind incomplete bioavailability for many oral drugs.

## answer_b
Fraction of a drug excreted in the feces.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Fecal excretion of unabsorbed or biliary-excreted drug is a separate elimination pathway, not the definition of first-pass metabolism.

## answer_c
Fraction of a drug reaching the systemic circulation following any route administration.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The fraction reaching systemic circulation by any route is the definition of bioavailability, the consequence first-pass metabolism reduces, not first-pass metabolism itself.

## answer_d
Plasma protein binding degree of substance.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Plasma protein binding is an independent pharmacokinetic property that affects distribution and free drug fraction, unrelated to first-pass loss.

## answer_e
Amount of a substance in urine relative to the initial dose.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The amount of a substance recovered in urine relative to the initial dose describes urinary excretion (part of renal clearance), a separate elimination measure from first-pass loss.

## title
Example of secondary active transport

## question
Which of the followings is an example of 2ry active transport?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Na-Ca exchange.

## explanation_a
Correct. The Na-Ca exchanger moves calcium against its gradient using the energy stored in the sodium gradient, a gradient itself built by the Na-K ATPase — it does not hydrolyse ATP directly, which is exactly what makes it secondary rather than primary active transport. Because it borrows an existing ion gradient instead of spending ATP itself, it is the textbook example of secondary (indirect) active transport. Blocking the Na-K ATPase that maintains the sodium gradient will eventually stop the exchanger too, since its energy supply is downstream of the primary pump.

## answer_b
H-K ATPase.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. H-K ATPase (the gastric proton pump) hydrolyses ATP directly to move ions, making it primary, not secondary, active transport.

## answer_c
H+ ATPase.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. H+ ATPase hydrolyses ATP directly, the defining feature of primary active transport.

## answer_d
Na-K ATPase.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Na-K ATPase is the prototype primary active transporter — it is also the pump that builds the sodium gradient secondary transporters like Na-Ca exchange then borrow.

## answer_e
Ca ATPase.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Ca ATPase (the plasma membrane and SERCA calcium pumps) hydrolyses ATP directly to extrude or sequester calcium, making it primary active transport.

## title
Reversible cholinesterase inhibitor used for Alzheimer disease

## question
Which of the following reversible cholinesterase inhibitors is used for Alzheimer disease?

## subject
pharm

## status

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Physostigmine.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Physostigmine is a reversible cholinesterase inhibitor, but it is a tertiary amine used for atropine/anticholinergic poisoning (it crosses the blood-brain barrier) and for glaucoma, not as a standard Alzheimer's therapy.

## answer_b
Neostigmine.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Neostigmine is a reversible cholinesterase inhibitor used in myasthenia gravis treatment and reversal of neuromuscular blockade; its quaternary ammonium structure keeps it out of the CNS, so it is not used for Alzheimer disease.

## answer_c
Edrophonium.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Edrophonium is a very short-acting reversible cholinesterase inhibitor used diagnostically (the Tensilon test for myasthenia gravis), not therapeutically for Alzheimer disease.

## answer_d
Pyridostigmine.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Pyridostigmine is a longer-acting reversible cholinesterase inhibitor used for chronic myasthenia gravis management; like neostigmine it does not cross into the CNS well.

## answer_e
Donepezil.

## explanation_e
Correct. Donepezil is a centrally-acting, reversible acetylcholinesterase inhibitor that crosses the blood-brain barrier to raise acetylcholine levels in the CNS. This is why it, along with rivastigmine and galantamine, is used to treat the cognitive symptoms of Alzheimer disease, a condition marked by loss of central cholinergic neurons. Unlike neostigmine or pyridostigmine, its lipophilicity lets it act centrally rather than only at peripheral neuromuscular and autonomic synapses.

## title
Cause of down-regulated receptors

## question
Down regulated receptors for the daily dose are due to:

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Continuous agonist.

## explanation_a
Correct. Continuous, uninterrupted exposure of a receptor to its agonist drives receptor down-regulation: the cell responds to sustained stimulation by internalising and degrading receptors, reducing the number available at the surface and blunting the response to further agonist at the same dose. This is a receptor-level (pharmacodynamic) adaptation, distinct from simply using an agonist over a long period with breaks or from a pharmacokinetic change in drug handling.

## answer_b
Continuous antagonist.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Continuous antagonist exposure removes agonist tone rather than adding it, and typically drives the opposite adaptation — receptor up-regulation, as the cell compensates for reduced stimulation, not down-regulation.

## answer_c
Prolonged use of agonist.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Prolonged agonist use describes duration without specifying continuity; the option that precisely names continuous, unbroken exposure is the sharper and printed-correct answer, since down-regulation specifically follows sustained occupancy rather than mere prolonged intermittent dosing.

## answer_d
Tolerance.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Tolerance is the resulting decreased response itself, which can arise from several mechanisms including but not limited to receptor down-regulation; it names the outcome, not the specific continuous-agonist cause the question asks for.

## answer_e
Repeated use of antagonist.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Repeated (rather than continuous) antagonist use does not drive agonist-receptor down-regulation; if anything, blocking a receptor repeatedly tends toward up-regulation as the cell senses reduced signalling.

## title
Characteristic of simple diffusion

## question
Simple diffusion is characterized by which one of the following?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Is rate limited.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Being carrier-mediated and having a finite number of carrier proteins is what makes facilitated diffusion and active transport rate-limited (saturable); simple diffusion has no such ceiling because it does not depend on a limited number of protein carriers.

## answer_b
It is not saturable.

## explanation_b
Correct. Simple diffusion is the passive movement of a solute directly across the lipid bilayer (or through a channel) down its concentration gradient, without a carrier protein. Because it does not depend on a fixed number of carrier binding sites, its rate simply keeps rising as concentration difference increases, so it is not saturable, unlike carrier-mediated facilitated diffusion or active transport, both of which plateau once all carriers are occupied.

## answer_c
Needs the presence of a carrier.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Needing a carrier is the defining feature of facilitated diffusion and active transport, not simple diffusion, which crosses the membrane directly.

## answer_d
Occurs uphill.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Simple diffusion moves downhill, from high to low concentration; movement uphill (against the gradient) requires active transport and energy input.

## answer_e
Requires energy.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Simple diffusion is passive and needs no metabolic energy; it is driven entirely by the concentration gradient itself.

## status

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Naloxone is a competitive antagonist.

## explanation_a
Correct. ## answer_b
Morphine is a full agonist, and naloxone is a partial agonist.

Morphine is less efficacious than is naloxone.

## answer_d
Morphine is less potent than is naloxone.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. ## answer_e
Naloxone is a noncompetitive antagonist.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A noncompetitive antagonist would reduce the maximum effect achievable (lower the ceiling) no matter how much agonist is given; here, morphine can still achieve full pain relief once its concentration is raised enough, which rules out noncompetitive antagonism.

Partial agonist.

Irreversible antagonist.

Inverse agonist

Incorrect. An inverse agonist reduces the receptor's baseline (constitutive) activity below the unstimulated level, producing an effect opposite to the agonist's, not a submaximal version of the agonist's own effect.
