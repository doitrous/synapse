# Item

QST-MANSMSS202-ANATOMY2-REUSE-AR-Q01

## title
Muscle preventing pelvic tilt on one-leg stance

## question
Which muscle prevents pelvic tilt to the unsupported side when standing on one leg?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Gluteus maximus

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Gluteus maximus is the powerful hip extensor used in climbing and rising from sitting, but it plays only a minor role in stabilising the pelvis during single-leg stance. It is not the muscle this question is testing.

## answer_b
Gluteus medius

## explanation_b
Correct. Gluteus medius (with gluteus minimus), supplied by the superior gluteal nerve, contracts on the stance-leg side during single-leg support and pulls the pelvis down on that side, which keeps the unsupported side from dropping. This is the exact mechanism disrupted in a Trendelenburg (waddling) gait when the superior gluteal nerve or these abductors fail. It is the muscle group specifically responsible for pelvic stabilisation during walking and single-leg stance. This is a common "which muscle is missing" exam pattern, so recognising gluteus medius specifically (rather than the more familiar gluteus maximus) is the point being tested.

## answer_c
Obturator externus

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Obturator externus is a small lateral rotator of the hip, supplied by the obturator nerve, and takes no part in frontal-plane pelvic stabilisation during stance.

## answer_d
Obturator internus

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Obturator internus is also a lateral rotator of the extended hip (and an abductor when the hip is flexed), not a pelvic stabiliser during single-leg standing.

## answer_e
Quadratus femoris

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Quadratus femoris is a short lateral rotator of the hip near the intertrochanteric crest; like the other short rotators it does not stabilise the pelvis in the coronal plane during gait.

QST-MANSMSS202-ANATOMY2-REUSE-AR-Q02

## title
Movement lost completely in femoral nerve paralysis

## question
After complete paralysis of the femoral nerve, which of the following movements is lost completely?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Flexion of thigh

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Hip flexion is produced mainly by iliopsoas (partly femoral-nerve-supplied via sartorius/pectineus, but psoas major is innervated directly by lumbar plexus roots) and by other flexors such as sartorius; it is weakened, not completely lost, since several non-femoral contributors remain.

## answer_b
Adduction of thigh

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Thigh adduction is the job of the adductor group, supplied by the obturator nerve, which is untouched by a femoral nerve lesion.

## answer_c
Lateral rotation of thigh

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Lateral rotation of the thigh is produced chiefly by the short rotators and gluteal muscles (obturator and sciatic-nerve-related territory), not by femoral-nerve-supplied muscles.

## answer_d
Extension of knee

## explanation_d
Correct. Quadriceps femoris is the only knee extensor in the body, and it is supplied entirely by the femoral nerve; no other muscle can substitute for it. A complete femoral nerve palsy therefore abolishes knee extension completely, unlike hip flexion, which several non-femoral muscles can still partly produce. This is why an isolated femoral nerve palsy is tested by asking a patient to actively extend the knee against resistance rather than by testing hip flexion alone.

## answer_e
Flexion of knee

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Knee flexion is produced by the hamstrings (tibial nerve) and other posterior thigh and leg muscles, none of which are femoral-nerve-supplied.

## title
Muscle supplied by the tibial nerve

## question
Which of the following muscles is supplied by the tibial nerve?

## subject

## status

## explanation_a

## answer_b
Tibialis anterior

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Tibialis anterior lies in the anterior compartment of the leg and is supplied by the deep peroneal nerve, the opposite compartment's nerve to the tibial nerve's posterior-compartment territory.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Peroneus longus lies in the lateral compartment and is supplied by the superficial peroneal nerve, again not the tibial nerve.

Correct. Tibialis posterior lies in the deep posterior compartment of the leg, and like the rest of that compartment (gastrocnemius, soleus, the long toe flexors) it is supplied by the tibial nerve, the sciatic nerve's medial terminal division. This deep-compartment placement, alongside the tibial nerve's other targets (gastrocnemius, soleus, flexor digitorum longus, flexor hallucis longus), is a frequently tested contrast with the anterior and lateral compartments' separate nerve supplies.

## title
Muscle that unlocks the knee joint

## question
Which muscle performs its function in the unlocking of knee joint?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Plantaris

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Plantaris is a vestigial muscle with a long thin tendon that assists weakly in plantar flexion; it has no role in unlocking the knee.

## answer_b
Popliteus

## explanation_b
Correct. Popliteus laterally rotates the femur on the fixed tibia (in weight-bearing), reversing the screw-home mechanism that locks the fully extended knee, which is exactly what lets flexion begin. This is a classic "which muscle is different" pairing question, since popliteus's rotational action is easy to confuse with a simple flexor action.

## answer_c
Soleus

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Soleus is a posterior-leg plantar flexor of the ankle that does not cross the knee joint at all.

## answer_d
Gastrocnemius

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Gastrocnemius does cross the knee and assists weak flexion, but the specific unlocking action belongs to popliteus, whose rotation initiates the release of the screw-home lock.

## answer_e
Biceps femoris

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Biceps femoris is a hamstring that flexes the knee once it is already unlocked, but it is not the muscle credited with the unlocking rotation itself.

## title
Nerve damaged in buttock knife wound causing waddling gait

## question
Patient with a deep knife wound in the buttock walks with a waddling gait that is characterized by the pelvis falling toward one side at each step. Which of the following nerves is damaged?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Obturator nerve

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Obturator nerve injury weakens hip adduction and produces medial thigh sensory loss, not a waddling gait.

## answer_b
Nerve to obturator internus

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The nerve to obturator internus supplies a small lateral rotator; its loss does not produce a Trendelenburg gait.

## answer_c
Superior gluteal nerve

## explanation_c
Correct. The superior gluteal nerve supplies gluteus medius and minimus, which stabilise the pelvis during single-leg stance; its injury produces exactly this waddling (Trendelenburg) gait, with the pelvis dropping toward the unsupported side with each step on the injured side. This Trendelenburg-type gait is a classic bedside clue pointing directly to superior gluteal nerve damage rather than to a more proximal sciatic or lumbosacral plexus lesion.

## answer_d

Incorrect. Inferior gluteal nerve injury weakens gluteus maximus (hip extension, rising from sitting) but does not produce this pelvis-drop gait pattern.

## answer_e
Femoral nerve

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Femoral nerve injury weakens knee extension and hip flexion, producing a different gait disturbance (buckling knee), not a waddling gait.
