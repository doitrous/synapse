## title
The expanded end of the hair follicle

## question
Expanded end of the hair at the bottom of the follicle:-

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Arrector pili muscle

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The arrector pili is a small smooth-muscle bundle attached obliquely to the follicle's outer root sheath, not the expanded end of the follicle itself.

## answer_b
Hair bulb

## explanation_b
Correct. The hair bulb is the swollen, bulbous expansion at the very bottom of a growing hair follicle. It caps the dermal hair papilla and its walls are the hair matrix, the actively dividing epithelial cells that build the hair shaft and inner root sheath as the follicle grows.

## answer_c
Hair root

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The hair root is the general term for the portion of the hair below the skin surface, within the follicle — it is not itself the specific expanded structure at the follicle's base.

## answer_d
Hair shaft

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The hair shaft is the visible, fully keratinised portion of the hair projecting above the skin surface, the opposite end of the structure from the expanded bulb at the follicle's base.

## answer_e
Hair sheath

## explanation_e
Incorrect. "Hair sheath" is not the standard term for the expanded base; the follicle's covering layers are named the inner and outer root sheaths, neither of which is itself an expanded terminal structure.

## title
The neuron type of the mesencephalic nucleus of the trigeminal nerve

## question
Type of nerve cell in mesencephalic nucleus of trigeminal nerve:-

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Unipolar

## explanation_a
Correct. The mesencephalic nucleus of the trigeminal nerve is the classic exception in histology: its primary sensory (proprioceptive) neuron cell bodies lie inside the brainstem itself, and unlike ordinary primary sensory neurons found in peripheral ganglia, they are described as unipolar — the one nucleus of unipolar cells found within the CNS.

## answer_b
Pseudounipolar

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Pseudounipolar is the shape of typical primary sensory neurons in a peripheral ganglion (such as the dorsal root or trigeminal ganglion) — the mesencephalic nucleus's cells are the distinct exception, described as unipolar, not pseudounipolar.

## answer_c
Bipolar

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Bipolar neurons, with one dendrite and one axon, are found in special sensory pathways such as the retina and olfactory epithelium, not in the mesencephalic nucleus of the trigeminal nerve.

## answer_d
Pseudobipolar

## explanation_d
Incorrect. "Pseudobipolar" is not the recognised shape of the mesencephalic nucleus's neurons, nor a standard neuron-shape category tested alongside unipolar, bipolar, pseudounipolar and multipolar.

## answer_e
Multipolar

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Multipolar neurons, with many dendrites and one axon, are the typical shape of motor neurons and most CNS interneurons — not the sensory cells of the mesencephalic trigeminal nucleus, which are unipolar.

## subject

Connected by gap junctions

## explanation_b
Correct — this is the FALSE statement, and so the answer. Release of neurotransmitters (Acetylcholine)

## answer_e
Slower conduction than electrical synapse

## explanation_e
Incorrect (i.e. this IS true of the chemical synapse, so it is not the answer). Because it depends on vesicle release, diffusion across the cleft and receptor binding, a chemical synapse conducts more slowly than the direct ionic coupling of an electrical synapse.

## question
The commonest type of synapse according to structure:-

## subject

## answer_a
Axo-axonic

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Axo-axonic synapses (axon terminal onto another axon), used for presynaptic inhibition, are a real but comparatively uncommon structural type, not the most frequent.

## answer_b
Axo-dendritic

## explanation_b
Correct. Synapses classified structurally by which neuronal parts meet include axo-dendritic, axo-somatic, axo-axonic and dendro-dendritic; the axo-dendritic synapse — an axon terminal contacting a dendrite, often on a dendritic spine — is the most common of these, reflecting how heavily dendrites are used to receive convergent input.

Incorrect. Axo-somatic synapses (axon terminal onto the cell body) are common but less frequent than axo-dendritic synapses, since dendrites carry the greater share of a neuron's synaptic input.

## answer_d
Dendro-somatic

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Dendro-somatic contact is not a standard, frequently occurring synapse category in this classification, and is not the commonest type.

## answer_e
Dendro-dendritic

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Dendro-dendritic synapses do occur (for example in the olfactory bulb) but are a specialised, comparatively rare arrangement, not the commonest structural type overall.

## question

## subject

Incorrect. The basal layer contains prekeratin (tonofilament) intermediate filaments, the earliest step of keratinisation — not eleidin, which appears much later in the sequence.

Spinous layer

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The spinous (prickle-cell) layer, like the basal layer beneath it, contains prekeratin tonofilaments and is joined by desmosomal spines; eleidin has not yet formed at this stage.

## answer_e
Horny layer

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The fully keratinised horny layer contains soft keratin in its flattened, anucleate cells, not eleidin, which is characteristic of the clear layer immediately beneath it.

## question

## subject

## answer_a
Basal layer

## explanation_a
Correct. The basal (germinative) layer, the single row of mitotically active cells resting on the basement membrane, is where prekeratin (tonofilament) intermediate filaments first appear as the keratinocyte's cytoskeletal keratin content — the earliest step of the keratinisation programme, continuing into the spinous layer above.

Horny layer

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The horny layer is the end product of keratinisation, containing fully formed soft keratin in dead, flattened cells — not the precursor prekeratin filaments of the deeper, living basal layer.

## answer_e
Spinous layer

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The spinous layer does carry prekeratin (tonofilament) bundles too, converging on the desmosomal spines, but it is the basal layer beneath it where these filaments first appear as keratinocytes begin their upward differentiation.

## question
Type of sweat gland:-

## subject

Simple alveolar gland

## answer_e
Simple coiled tubular gland

## explanation_e
Correct. The (eccrine) sweat gland has a single, unbranched duct connecting to a long, coiled secretory tubule deep in the dermis, with neither the duct nor the secretory portion branching — the definition of a simple coiled tubular gland.

## title
The cause of goose skin

## question
Which of the followings is responsible for appearance of goose skin:-

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Sweat gland

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The sweat gland secretes sweat through its duct; it has no muscular action on the hair follicle and does not produce the goose-skin effect.

## answer_b
Hair bulb

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The hair bulb is the growth engine at the follicle's base; it is a passive structure with no contractile ability of its own, so it cannot pull the follicle upright.

## answer_c
Arrector pili muscle

## explanation_c
Correct. The arrector pili is a small smooth-muscle bundle running from the papillary dermis to the follicle's outer root sheath. Its contraction (for example with cold or fright) pulls the follicle into a more vertical position and dimples the skin surface above it, producing the visible bumps of goose skin.

## answer_d
Sebaceous duct

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The sebaceous duct simply conveys sebum to the follicle; it has no contractile tissue and plays no role in raising the hair or dimpling the skin.

## answer_e
Sebaceous alveoli

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The sebaceous alveoli are the gland's holocrine secretory units producing sebum — a secretory, not contractile, structure, so they cannot be responsible for goose skin.
