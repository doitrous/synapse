## title
Artery auscultated for blood pressure at the cubital fossa

## question
A 20-year-old male came to clinic and the doctor asked for blood pressure measurement. The nurse measured blood pressure over the cubital fossa via placing a stethoscope over which artery?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Axillary artery

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The axillary artery lies proximal to the arm, under cover of pectoralis major, well above the cubital fossa where the cuff and stethoscope are placed.

## answer_b
Brachial artery

## explanation_b
Correct. The brachial artery terminates in the cubital fossa, medial to the biceps tendon, exactly where a stethoscope diaphragm is placed under the lower edge of the cuff to auscultate the Korotkoff sounds during a manual blood pressure reading. Any other named artery in the answer options would be either too proximal (axillary) or too distal (radial, ulnar) for this specific auscultation point.

## answer_c
Median artery

## explanation_c
Incorrect. There is no named "median artery" as a standard named upper-limb vessel in this context; it is a distractor, not a real auscultation site.

## answer_d
Radial artery

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The radial artery is used for the palpatory pulse and for the palpatory pre-estimate of systolic pressure, not for the auscultatory reading itself, and it lies distal to the cubital fossa at the wrist.

## answer_e
Ulnar artery

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The ulnar artery, like the radial, is a forearm/wrist-level vessel distal to the cubital fossa, not the artery auscultated for blood pressure.

## title
Medial thigh muscle that can extend the hip

## question
Which muscle on the medial aspect of the thigh can extend the hip joint?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Pectineus

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Pectineus flexes and adducts the hip; it has no hip-extension action.

## answer_b
Adductor longus

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Adductor longus is purely a hip adductor and weak flexor, with no extensor action.

## answer_c
Adductor brevis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Adductor brevis, like adductor longus, adducts and flexes the hip, without an extensor component.

## answer_d
Adductor magnus

## explanation_d
Correct. Adductor magnus has two functionally distinct parts: an adductor part (obturator-nerve-supplied) and a hamstring (ischial) part arising from the ischial tuberosity, supplied by the tibial division of the sciatic nerve, which acts with the true hamstrings to extend the hip — making it the one medial-thigh muscle that can both adduct and extend. This dual action is the reason adductor magnus is sometimes called a "third hamstring," despite being grouped anatomically with the adductor compartment.

## answer_e
Gracilis

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Gracilis adducts the hip and flexes the knee; it has no hip-extension action.

## title
Nerve injured by a fracture of the neck of the fibula

## question
Which nerve can be injured if neck of fibula was fractured?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer

## answer_a
Sciatic

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The sciatic nerve has already divided into its tibial and common peroneal divisions well above the knee, so a fibular neck fracture does not injure the sciatic trunk itself.

## answer_b
Sural

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The sural nerve is a small cutaneous branch running down the back of the calf; it is not wrapped around the fibular neck.

## answer_c
Tibial

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The tibial nerve runs deep in the posterior compartment of the leg, well away from the subcutaneous fibular neck.

## answer_d
Saphenous

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The saphenous nerve is a cutaneous branch of the femoral nerve running down the medial leg, unrelated to the fibula.

## answer_e
Common peroneal

## explanation_e
Correct. The common peroneal nerve winds superficially and subcutaneously around the neck of the fibula before dividing into its superficial and deep branches, which is exactly why a fracture there so commonly injures it, producing foot drop. This subcutaneous, superficial course is exactly why the nerve is also vulnerable to tight casts, prolonged leg-crossing and direct blows at this site, not only to fractures.

## title
Termination of the cephalic vein

## question
The cephalic vein drains its venous blood into ...............

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
The brachial vein

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The brachial vein(s) are deep venae comitantes of the brachial artery; the cephalic vein is superficial and does not drain into them.

## answer_b
The basilic vein.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The basilic vein is the cephalic vein's sibling superficial vein, running up the medial side of the arm; it joins the venae comitantes of the brachial artery to form the axillary vein, but the cephalic vein does not drain into the basilic.

## answer_c
The subclavian vein

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The subclavian vein is more proximal, continuing on from the axillary vein at the outer border of the first rib; the cephalic vein ends before this point.

## answer_d
The axillary vein

## explanation_d
Correct. The cephalic vein begins at the lateral end of the dorsal venous arch of the hand, ascends the lateral side of the forearm and arm, runs in the deltopectoral groove, pierces the clavipectoral fascia, and ends by draining into the axillary vein. The basilic vein, by contrast, joins the brachial venae comitantes to form the axillary vein from the opposite (medial) side, a frequently tested point of comparison.

## answer_e
The median vein of the forearm

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The median cubital vein (connecting cephalic and basilic in the cubital fossa) is a tributary the cephalic vein passes, not its point of termination.

Anterior thigh muscle innervated high in the abdomen

Which muscle in the front of thigh receives its innervation high in the abdomen?

Incorrect. Iliacus is supplied by the femoral nerve in the iliac fossa/thigh region, not by direct high-abdominal root branches the way psoas major is.

## title
Artery of neurovascular concern in a distal radius plus scaphoid fracture

## question
A 56 years old female patient admitted to Mansoura Emergency Hospital after falling on her outstretched right hand. Her right hand X-ray showed the presence of fractures involving both the distal end of the radius and the scaphoid bones. Which artery will you be concerned about during your neurovascular evaluation of this case?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Ulnar artery.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The ulnar artery runs on the medial side of the wrist through Guyon's canal, away from the distal radius and the anatomical snuffbox, so it is not the vessel most directly threatened by this fracture pattern.

## answer_b
Radial artery.

## explanation_b
Correct. The radial artery crosses the floor of the anatomical snuffbox, immediately adjacent to the distal radius and the scaphoid (which forms part of the snuffbox floor), so a fracture through both bones directly threatens it; the scaphoid's own blood supply, entering mainly through its distal pole, is also put at risk of avascular necrosis by this same injury. This is why a scaphoid fracture in particular raises two separate concerns together: the bone's own risk of avascular necrosis, and the adjacent radial artery's risk of injury.

## answer_c
Median artery.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. There is no named "median artery" at the wrist; it is a distractor.

## answer_d
Brachial artery .

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The brachial artery has already ended in the cubital fossa, well proximal to the wrist, and is not at direct risk from a distal radius/scaphoid fracture.

## answer_e
Axillary artery

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The axillary artery is even more proximal than the brachial, in the axilla, far from this injury.

## title
Structure damaged by a destructive injury of the anatomic snuffbox

## question
A construction worker suffers a destructive injury of the structures related to the anatomic snuffbox. Which of the following structures would most likely be damaged?

## subject

## title
Classification of the elbow joint

## question
The type of elbow joint is

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Fibrousjoint

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The elbow is a synovial joint, not a fibrous one; fibrous joints (like sutures) permit little or no movement.

## answer_b
Synovial ball and socket

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A ball-and-socket joint (like the shoulder or hip) is triaxial, permitting rotation in all planes, which the elbow does not do.

## answer_c
Synovial condylar

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Condyloid (ellipsoid-like) joints such as the metacarpophalangeal joints are biaxial; the elbow is uniaxial.

## answer_d
Synovial hinge

## explanation_d
Correct. The elbow, like the interphalangeal joints, is a uniaxial hinge joint, permitting flexion and extension only, never abduction or adduction. This uniaxial hinge action is what distinguishes the elbow from the biaxial wrist and the triaxial shoulder in questions comparing joint types across the upper limb.

## answer_e
Synovial ellipsoid

## explanation_e
