## title
Basis of the Gram-positive / Gram-negative distinction

## question
The difference between Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria resides in the:

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Cell wall.

## explanation_a
Correct. The Gram reaction is a cell-wall stain: Gram-positive bacteria retain the crystal-violet-iodine complex because their wall is a thick, multilayered peptidoglycan sheet, while Gram-negative bacteria decolorise and take up the safranin counterstain because their wall is a thin peptidoglycan layer covered by an outer lipopolysaccharide membrane. It is this difference in wall architecture, not any other structure, that the stain is actually reading, which is why Gram reaction and wall structure are taught as the same fact.

## answer_b
Nucleus.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Bacteria have no true membrane-bound nucleus at all (that is what makes them prokaryotes), so the nucleus cannot be the structure the Gram stain distinguishes.

## answer_c
Cell membrane.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The cytoplasmic membrane lies beneath the cell wall in both groups and is not itself stained differently by the Gram method; it is the wall external to it that differs.

## answer_d
Mesosomes.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Mesosomes are cytoplasmic-membrane invaginations found to varying degrees in many bacteria and are not the structural basis the Gram stain distinguishes.

## answer_e
Ribosomes.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Ribosomes are 70S in essentially all bacteria regardless of Gram reaction, so they cannot account for the staining difference.

## title
Enzyme used to cut recipient DNA in recombinant DNA technology

## question
Which of the following enzymes are used to cut the recipient DNA?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Endonuclease.

## explanation_a
Correct. Restriction endonucleases are the bacterial enzymes used in recombinant DNA technology to cut both donor and recipient (vector) DNA at specific palindromic recognition sequences; it is this internal, sequence-specific cutting -- not random or end-directed cutting -- that lets the two DNA molecules be spliced together at matching sites. Because the enzyme cuts only at its specific recognition sequence rather than randomly, it leaves predictable, reproducible fragment ends that can be matched to a similarly cut donor DNA fragment for ligation.

## answer_b
Exonuclease.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Exonucleases degrade DNA progressively from a free end rather than cutting at an internal recognition site, so they are not the enzyme used to open recipient DNA for cloning.

## answer_c
Ligases.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Ligases join (seal) DNA fragments together after cutting, acting at the opposite step of the cloning process from the one this question asks about.

## answer_d
Polymerase.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Polymerases synthesise new DNA strands; they do not cut existing DNA.

## answer_e
Helicase.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Helicases unwind the DNA double helix ahead of replication or transcription; they do not cut DNA.

Structure present in Gram-negative but not Gram-positive bacteria

## question
Which one of the following structures is present in Gram negative but not in Gram positive bacteria:

## subject

Peptidoglycan.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Peptidoglycan is present in both Gram-positive and Gram-negative walls, just in very different amounts (a thick multilayer in Gram-positives, a thin single layer in Gram-negatives).

## answer_b
Lipid A.

## explanation_b
Correct. Lipid A is the toxic lipid component of the lipopolysaccharide (LPS, endotoxin) that forms the outer leaflet of the Gram-negative outer membrane. Capsule.

Incorrect. A capsule can be present in either Gram-positive (for example pneumococcus) or Gram-negative organisms; it is not restricted to Gram-negatives.

Flagella.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Flagella, when present, occur in both Gram-positive and Gram-negative species; motility is independent of Gram reaction.

## answer_e
Voultin granules

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Volutin (metachromatic) granules are cytoplasmic storage inclusions seen in organisms such as Corynebacterium diphtheriae, a Gram-positive bacterium, so they are not a Gram-negative-only feature.

## title
Plasmid type unstable and easily degraded by exonucleases

## question
Which plasmid type is unstable and can be easily attacked by exonucleases?

## subject
inf

## status

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Broad host range plasmid

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Host range (broad vs narrow) describes how many bacterial species a plasmid can replicate in, not its physical stability against exonucleases.

## answer_b
Narrow host range plasmid

## explanation_b
Incorrect. As with broad host range, narrow host range describes replication compatibility, not resistance to exonuclease attack.

## answer_c
Linear plasmid

## explanation_c
Correct. Most bacterial plasmids are covalently closed circular DNA, which has no free ends for exonucleases to act on and is therefore relatively stable. A linear plasmid, by contrast, has two free DNA ends exposed to the cytoplasm, making it an easy substrate for exonucleases and comparatively unstable -- which is why circular topology is the typical, more stable form.

## answer_d
Shuttle vector

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A shuttle vector is a plasmid engineered to replicate in two different host types (for example bacteria and yeast); this describes function, not the linear-vs-circular structural feature that determines exonuclease vulnerability.

## answer_e
Conjugative plasmid

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Conjugative plasmids are defined by their ability to mediate their own transfer between cells (carrying transfer/tra genes), not by unusual susceptibility to exonucleases.

## answer_a
Lack of flagella.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Bacteria also lack flagella in many species without being obligate intracellular, so flagellar absence is not the reason viruses must live inside a host cell.

## answer_b
Small genome.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Genome size varies enormously between viruses (from a few genes to hundreds); a small genome alone does not force an obligate intracellular lifestyle.

## answer_c
Lack of ATP production.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Lack of ATP production is closely related to option E but is not, on its own, the standard textbook phrasing of why viruses are obligate intracellular; the classic reason given is the inability to make their own proteins.

## answer_d
Absent mitochondria.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Viruses have no organelles at all (mitochondria included), but the specific, classically taught reason for their obligate intracellular status is protein synthesis dependence, not organelle absence per se.

## answer_e
Inability to synthesize own proteins.

## explanation_e
Correct. Viruses have no ribosomes or translation machinery of their own and so cannot synthesise their own proteins; they multiply only by hijacking a living host cell's ribosomes and tRNA to translate viral mRNA into viral proteins, which is precisely why viruses are obligate intracellular parasites. Because it has no ribosomes, no tRNA and no amino-acid-charging enzymes of its own, a virus is entirely dependent on hijacking a living host cell's translation apparatus to convert its genetic message into functional viral proteins, which is the root cause of its obligate intracellular lifestyle.

Role of the macrophage during the natural (innate) immune response

## question
What is the role of the macrophage during natural immune response?

## subject
imm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Activates plasma cells.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Activating plasma cells is not a direct macrophage role; plasma cells arise from activated B cells, chiefly with T-cell (not macrophage) help.

## answer_b
Ingest and destroy the antigen.

## explanation_b
Correct. During the innate (natural) immune response, the macrophage's core role is phagocytosis: it ingests and destroys the antigen/microbe through recognition, engulfment and intracellular killing, in addition to secondary roles in antigen presentation and cytokine production. This phagocytic role sits alongside the macrophage's secondary functions of presenting captured antigen to T cells and secreting cytokines that recruit and activate other immune cells, but ingestion and destruction of the antigen itself is the core innate-response function this question asks about.

## answer_c
Kills the virus-infected cells by perforins and granzymes.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Killing via perforins and granzymes is the mechanism used by cytotoxic T cells and NK cells, not macrophages, which kill by phagocytosis rather than granule-mediated cytotoxicity.

## answer_d
Kills tumor cells by perforins and granzymes.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. As above, perforin/granzyme-mediated tumour cell killing is an NK-cell/cytotoxic-T-cell mechanism, not a macrophage one.

## answer_e
Make specific antibody.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Making specific antibody is a B-cell (plasma cell) function of adaptive humoral immunity, not a macrophage role in the innate response.
