## title
What decreases capillary filtration rate

## question
An increase in which one of the following tends to decrease capillary filtration rate?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Arteriolar diameter

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A wider arteriolar diameter lowers arteriolar resistance, which raises the hydrostatic pressure transmitted downstream into the capillary — increasing, not decreasing, filtration.

## answer_b
Capillary hydrostatic pressure

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Capillary hydrostatic pressure is the dominant outward (filtration-favouring) Starling force; a rise in it increases filtration.

## answer_c
Interstitial colloid osmotic pressure

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Interstitial colloid osmotic pressure pulls fluid out of the capillary and into the interstitium; a rise in it increases, not decreases, filtration.

## answer_d
Plasma colloid osmotic pressure

## explanation_d
Correct. Plasma colloid osmotic pressure is the principal inward (absorption-favouring) Starling force. Raising it strengthens the pull of fluid back into the capillary, which opposes and so decreases the net capillary filtration rate.

## answer_e
Venous hydrostatic pressure

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A rise in venous hydrostatic pressure backs up into the capillary bed, raising capillary hydrostatic pressure and so increasing, not decreasing, filtration.

## title
What drives fluid movement between plasma and interstitial fluid

## question
Which one of the following is primarily responsible for the movement of fluid between plasma and interstitial fluid?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Hydrostatic pressure and osmotic pressure

## explanation_a
Correct. The Starling forces — capillary and interstitial hydrostatic pressure, and plasma and interstitial colloid osmotic pressure — set the balance of bulk fluid movement across the capillary wall between plasma and the interstitial space. This is passive bulk flow driven by pressure gradients. It is not a carrier- or channel-mediated process.

## answer_b
Membrane channels

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Membrane channels move specific solutes (mainly ions and small molecules) across cell membranes; capillary fluid exchange is bulk flow through the capillary wall's pores, driven by Starling pressures, not by channel-mediated transport.

## answer_c
Osmosis and active transport

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Osmosis moves water down an osmotic gradient and active transport moves solutes against their gradient using energy; neither term captures the hydrostatic-pressure component that, together with colloid osmotic pressure, actually governs capillary fluid exchange.

## answer_d
Primary and secondary active transport

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Primary and secondary active transport are energy-dependent solute-transport mechanisms at cell membranes; capillary fluid movement is passive, pressure-driven bulk flow, not an active transport process.

## title
Percent of filtered fluid that returns at the venous end

## question
Approximately what percent of fluid that exits the capillaries at the arterial end re-enters the capillaries at the venous end?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
60%

## explanation_a
Incorrect. 60% understates how much of the filtered fluid is reabsorbed before the venous end; the true figure is about 90%.

## answer_b
70%

## explanation_b
Incorrect. 70% is too low; about 90% of the fluid filtered at the arterial end is reabsorbed before the blood reaches the venous end.

## answer_c
80%

## explanation_c
Incorrect. 80% is close but still below the standard figure of about 90%.

## answer_d
90%

## explanation_d
Correct. About 90% of the fluid filtered out of the capillaries at the arterial end is reabsorbed back into the capillaries before the venous end. This is driven by the shift in Starling-force balance along the vessel's length, as capillary hydrostatic pressure falls while plasma colloid osmotic pressure stays essentially constant. The remaining roughly 10% is instead returned to the circulation via the lymphatics.

## answer_e
100%

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Not all filtered fluid returns directly to the capillary — about 10% instead enters the lymphatics as lymph, so the figure is not 100%.

## title
A mechanism that decreases capillary filtration rate

## question
Which mechanism would tend to decrease capillary filtration rate?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Increased capillary hydrostatic pressure.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Capillary hydrostatic pressure is the dominant outward, filtration-favouring force; increasing it increases, not decreases, filtration.

## answer_b
Decreased plasma colloid osmotic pressure.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Plasma colloid osmotic pressure opposes filtration (favours absorption); decreasing it weakens that opposition and so increases, not decreases, net filtration.

## answer_c
Increased interstitial colloid osmotic pressure.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Interstitial colloid osmotic pressure favours filtration (it pulls fluid out into the interstitium); increasing it increases, not decreases, filtration.

## answer_d
Positive interstitial hydrostatic pressure.

## explanation_d
Correct. A positive interstitial hydrostatic pressure pushes fluid from the interstitium back into the capillary, opposing outward movement. It works against, not with, filtration. A positive value therefore decreases the net filtration rate.

## answer_e
Negative interstitial hydrostatic pressure.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A negative interstitial hydrostatic pressure adds to, rather than opposes, the net outward force (subtracting a negative value increases the outward pressure gradient), so it increases, not decreases, filtration.

## question
Which pressure is negative in a muscle capillary bed in the lower extremities?

## subject

## status

Interstitial hydrostatic pressure.

Correct. Interstitial (tissue) hydrostatic pressure in loose tissue such as skeletal muscle is normally slightly subatmospheric, about -3 mmHg. ## answer_e
Venous hydrostatic pressure.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Venous hydrostatic pressure is positive (low, but positive) under normal conditions — it is not the negative pressure being described here.

## title
The normal pressure of the interstitial fluid

## question
The pressure of the interstitial fluid is normally:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Equal to the capillary pressure.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Interstitial fluid pressure (about -3 mmHg in loose tissue) is nowhere close to capillary hydrostatic pressure (about 10 to 30 mmHg) — the two are set by entirely different mechanisms and are not normally equal.

## answer_b
Slightly greater than the capillary pressure.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Interstitial fluid pressure is far lower, not greater, than capillary hydrostatic pressure.

## answer_c
Slightly lesser than the capillary pressure.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. While numerically true that interstitial pressure is lower than capillary pressure, this option understates the relationship — the defining normal feature is that interstitial pressure is itself slightly SUBATMOSPHERIC (negative), not merely 'slightly lesser' than a comparator pressure.

## answer_d
Slightly negative relative to the atmospheric pressure.

## explanation_d
Correct. In loose tissues such as skeletal muscle, interstitial fluid pressure is normally slightly negative relative to atmospheric pressure, about -3 mmHg. This negative value is maintained by ongoing lymphatic drainage of any fluid filtered into the interstitium. Encapsulated organs such as the kidney and brain instead run a positive interstitial fluid pressure, since their capsule resists tissue swelling.

## title
The plasma colloidal osmotic pressure

## question
The plasma colloidal osmotic pressure is:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
The main filtering force in tissue fluid formation.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Plasma colloid osmotic pressure is the main ABSORBING force, pulling fluid back into the capillary; capillary hydrostatic pressure, not plasma colloid osmotic pressure, is the main filtering (fluid-forming) force.

## answer_b
Determined only by the albumin concentration of the plasma.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Albumin is the main determinant of plasma colloid osmotic pressure, because of its high plasma concentration, but it is not the ONLY contributor — the globulins and fibrinogen also contribute a smaller share.

## answer_c
Equivalent to the osmotic pressure of 0.9% NaCl solution.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Plasma colloid osmotic pressure (about 28 mmHg) is a small fraction of the total osmotic pressure of plasma, which (like 0.9% saline) is around 5,000 to 6,000 mmHg — the two are not equivalent; colloid osmotic pressure comes specifically from the large, non-diffusible plasma proteins.

## answer_d
Normally about 28 mmHg.

## explanation_d
Correct. Plasma colloid osmotic pressure is normally about 28 mmHg. It is generated mainly by albumin, because of its high concentration relative to the other plasma proteins, not because it is individually a stronger osmotic particle. This 28 mmHg value is the principal inward, absorption-favouring Starling force.

## answer_e
Determined only by the globulin concentration of the plasma.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The globulins contribute only a minor share of plasma colloid osmotic pressure; albumin, not the globulins, is the main determinant.

It is antagonized by interstitial fluid osmotic pressure.

Incorrect (i.e. this statement is TRUE, so it is not the answer). Interstitial fluid (colloid) osmotic pressure pulls fluid out into the interstitium, working against (antagonising) absorption.

## title
The correct statement about capillary hydrostatic pressure

## question
Which of the following is correct regarding capillary hydrostatic pressure?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Normally equals 10 mmHg on the arterial end.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Capillary hydrostatic pressure is highest, not lowest, at the arterial end — about 30 mmHg there, not 10 mmHg; this option reverses the arterial-end value with the venous-end one.

## answer_b
Normally equals 30 mmHg on the venous end.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Capillary hydrostatic pressure is lowest, not highest, at the venous end — about 10 mmHg there, not 30 mmHg; this option again reverses the two end values.

## answer_c
Drives water inward through the capillary membrane.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Capillary hydrostatic pressure drives water OUTWARD, out of the capillary into the interstitium, not inward.

## answer_d
It is determined by the albumin level inside the capillaries.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Albumin level determines plasma colloid osmotic pressure, an entirely separate Starling force, not capillary hydrostatic pressure.

## answer_e
Functional mean capillary pressure normally equals 17.3 mmHg.

## explanation_e
Correct. Capillary hydrostatic pressure falls along the vessel's length, from about 30 mmHg at the arteriolar end to about 10 mmHg at the venular end. Because a simple average of the two ends would not reflect the actual pressure profile, physiologists instead use the functional mean capillary pressure, calculated from that profile. This functional mean is normally about 17.3 mmHg.

## title
The incorrect statement about interstitial hydrostatic pressure

## question
Which of the following is incorrect regarding interstitial hydrostatic pressure?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
It is directed inward the capillary when it is positive.

## explanation_a
Incorrect (i.e. this statement is TRUE, so it is not the answer). A positive interstitial hydrostatic pressure pushes fluid from the interstitium into the capillary, i.e. it acts inward, toward the capillary.

## answer_b
It is directed outward the capillary when it is negative.

## explanation_b
Incorrect (i.e. this statement is TRUE, so it is not the answer). A negative interstitial hydrostatic pressure instead favours movement out of the capillary, i.e. it acts outward, away from the capillary.

## answer_c
It is equal to -3 mmHg in a muscle capillary bed.

## explanation_c
Incorrect (i.e. this statement is TRUE, so it is not the answer). Interstitial hydrostatic pressure in a muscle capillary bed (loose tissue) is normally about -3 mmHg.

## answer_d
It is -6 mmHg in a kidney capillary bed.

## explanation_d
Correct — this is the FALSE statement being asked for. The kidney is enclosed by a tight, non-distensible renal capsule that resists tissue swelling. This makes its interstitial hydrostatic pressure POSITIVE, about +6 mmHg, not -6 mmHg as this option states. The same capsule-driven reversal to a positive value also occurs in the rigidly enclosed brain.

## answer_e
It is +4 mmHg in a brain capillary bed.

## explanation_e
Incorrect (i.e. this statement is TRUE, so it is not the answer). The brain, enclosed by the rigid cranium, likewise runs a positive interstitial hydrostatic pressure, about +4 mmHg.

## title
Starling's equilibrium

## question
According to Starling's equilibrium:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Interstitial fluid pressure equals +3 mmHg.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Interstitial fluid pressure in the classic Starling table is -3 mmHg (negative), not +3 mmHg.

## answer_b
Interstitial colloid osmotic pressure equals 28 mmHg.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Interstitial colloid osmotic pressure in the classic table is about 8 mmHg; 28 mmHg is instead the value of plasma (capillary) colloid osmotic pressure, a common trap swapping the two figures.

## answer_c
Total outward force equals 28.3 mmHg.

## explanation_c
Correct. Three forces favour outward movement: capillary hydrostatic pressure (mean 17.3 mmHg), the outward pull contributed by a negative interstitial hydrostatic pressure (3 mmHg), and interstitial colloid osmotic pressure (8 mmHg). Summing these three gives a total outward force of about 28.3 mmHg. This is balanced against a single inward force, plasma colloid osmotic pressure, of about 28 mmHg.

## answer_d
Total inward force equals 19 mmHg.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The single inward force, plasma colloid osmotic pressure, is about 28 mmHg, not 19 mmHg.

## answer_e
Mean effective capillary pressure equals 30 mmHg.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The functional mean capillary pressure is about 17.3 mmHg, not 30 mmHg — 30 mmHg is instead the pressure at the arteriolar end alone.

## title
The dynamics of interstitial fluid formation

## question
About the dynamics of interstitial fluid formation:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Net outward force = 40 mmHg.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Neither the whole-capillary mean net force (about +0.3 mmHg outward) nor the venous-end local net force reaches 40 mmHg — this greatly overstates the actual balance.

## answer_b
Net inward force = 7 mmHg.

## explanation_b
Correct. Capillary hydrostatic pressure falls sharply along the vessel and, by the venous end, has dropped well below plasma colloid osmotic pressure. The local Starling balance at that end therefore favours a net INWARD (reabsorptive) force, drawing fluid back into the capillary. This venous-end net inward force is about 7 mmHg.

## answer_c
Interstitial fluid pressure = +3 mmHg.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Interstitial fluid pressure in loose tissue is normally about -3 mmHg (negative), not +3 mmHg.

## answer_d
Interstitial fluid colloid osmotic pressure = 28 mmHg.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Interstitial fluid colloid osmotic pressure is about 8 mmHg, not 28 mmHg — 28 mmHg is instead the value of plasma colloid osmotic pressure, a recurring trap swapping the two figures.
