## title
Site of vitamin D3 synthesis

## question
Synthesis of vit D3 occurs in ........... under the effect of..….

## subject
derm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Eye, ultraviolet rays

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The eye has no role in vitamin D3 synthesis; there is no 7-dehydrocholesterol store or photochemical vitamin D pathway in ocular tissue. The eye's photoreceptors respond to light for vision, an entirely separate physiological process from steroid synthesis. Neither the organ nor the mechanism named here is correct.

## answer_b
Skin, ultraviolet rays

## explanation_b
Correct. Vitamin D3 synthesis begins in the skin, where ultraviolet B radiation converts a store of 7-dehydrocholesterol into pre-vitamin D3, which body heat then isomerises to vitamin D3 itself. Both the organ (skin) and the trigger (ultraviolet rays) named in this option match the actual pathway. Further activation to calcitriol happens later, in the liver and kidney, but the initial synthesis step is specifically in the skin under UV.

## answer_c
Skin, infrared rays

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Skin is the correct organ, but infrared rays do not carry the photon energy needed to drive the 7-dehydrocholesterol-to-pre-vitamin-D3 photochemical reaction; that reaction specifically requires UVB wavelengths. Infrared radiation is felt mainly as heat, not as a trigger for this photochemistry. This option gets the organ right but the trigger wrong.

## answer_d
Eye, infrared rays

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Neither part of this option is correct — the eye is not the synthesis site, and infrared rays are not the trigger. Combining two wrong answers does not make a correct one. Both the organ and the mechanism are mismatched.

## answer_e
Skin, visible light

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Skin is the correct organ, but visible light lacks the UV wavelength needed for the photochemical conversion of 7-dehydrocholesterol. Only ultraviolet B radiation, not the visible spectrum, drives this specific reaction. This option again gets the organ right but the trigger wrong.

## title
Primary function of melanin

## question
The primary function of melanin in the skin is to:

## subject
derm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Synthesize vitamin D

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Vitamin D synthesis depends on 7-dehydrocholesterol in keratinocytes reacting to UVB, a separate skin function from melanin's role. Melanin actually competes with this pathway by absorbing UV before it can trigger vitamin D synthesis. The two functions are related only in that both respond to UV exposure, not because melanin performs the synthesis itself.

## answer_b
Protect against UV-induced DNA damage

## explanation_b
Correct. Melanin's principal physiological role is absorbing and scattering ultraviolet radiation, which limits the UV-induced DNA photodamage that would otherwise accumulate in keratinocyte nuclei. Melanocytes transfer melanin to surrounding keratinocytes, where it forms a protective cap over the nucleus facing the skin surface. This is why individuals with less melanin carry higher UV-related skin damage and malignancy risk for the same sun exposure.

## answer_c
Regulate skin temperature

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Skin temperature regulation depends on the sweat glands and cutaneous blood vessels adjusting heat loss, not on melanin. Melanin has no direct thermoregulatory function. This option confuses two separate skin physiological roles.

## answer_d
Excrete nitrogenous wastes

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Nitrogenous waste excretion is a kidney function, not a skin function at all, let alone one performed by melanin specifically. Skin plays only a minor, indirect role in excretion via sweat, and melanin has no part in that process.

## answer_e
Produce antimicrobial peptides

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Antimicrobial peptides such as cathelicidin and the defensins are produced by keratinocytes as a separate chemical defence mechanism, unrelated to melanin's pigment-based UV protection. These are two distinct skin defence systems, not the same function under different names.

## title
Antimicrobial peptide from keratinocytes

## question
Which of the following antimicrobial peptides is produced by keratinocytes and acts against a broad spectrum of bacteria?

## subject
derm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Sebumin

## explanation_a
Incorrect. 'Sebumin' is not a recognised antimicrobial peptide; sebum itself (the oily secretion of sebaceous glands) has some antimicrobial properties from its component fatty acids, but it is not the peptide this question is describing. This option does not name a genuine keratinocyte-produced antimicrobial peptide.

## answer_b
Cathelicidin

## explanation_b
Correct. Cathelicidin is an antimicrobial peptide produced by keratinocytes that disrupts the cell membranes of a broad range of bacteria, some fungi and enveloped viruses. It gives the skin surface constant, non-adaptive chemical defence without needing prior pathogen exposure. It is one of the two main families of skin antimicrobial peptides, alongside the defensins.

## answer_c
Ceramide

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Ceramides are lipid molecules forming part of the stratum corneum's intercellular lipid matrix, contributing to the skin's mechanical/permeability barrier rather than acting as an antimicrobial peptide. They are structural lipids, not peptides with direct antibacterial action.

## answer_d
Urocanic acid

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Urocanic acid is a UV-absorbing breakdown product of the amino acid histidine found in the stratum corneum, contributing to UV protection rather than antimicrobial defence. It is not classified as an antimicrobial peptide.

## answer_e
Elastin

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Elastin is a structural protein giving skin its elastic recoil, found in the dermis, unrelated to antimicrobial defence. It has a purely mechanical/structural role, not a chemical defence one.

## title
Normal core body temperature range

## question
The normal physiological range of core body temperature in humans is:

## subject
derm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
35.0-36.0°C

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This range sits below the normal core temperature band and would be considered mildly hypothermic if sustained. It does not match the range the hypothalamus normally defends. A person at these temperatures would typically show signs of cold stress.

## answer_b
36.5-37.2°C

## explanation_b
Correct. Normal human core body temperature is physiologically maintained within a narrow range, classically cited as about 36.5-37.2°C, by the hypothalamic thermoregulatory centre balancing heat production against heat loss. This is core (internal), not peripheral skin, temperature. The narrowness of this range is what makes deviations from it, such as fever, clinically meaningful.

## answer_c
37.5-38.0°C

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This range is already in the low-grade fever territory, above the normal core temperature band. It would typically prompt evaluation for an underlying cause of elevated temperature rather than being considered a normal reading.

## answer_d
34.5-35.5°C

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This range is well below normal core temperature and would represent a more significant degree of hypothermia if sustained. It does not correspond to the physiologically defended core range.

## answer_e
38.0-39.0°C

## explanation_e
Incorrect. This range represents a clear fever, well above the normal core temperature band. It reflects a raised hypothalamic set point from illness or another pyrogenic stimulus, not the resting normal range.

## title
Substances that penetrate intact skin most readily

## question
Which of the following substances can penetrate the intact skin most readily?

## subject
derm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Water-soluble ions

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Water-soluble ions are resisted by the lipid-rich stratum corneum, which is a poor medium for charged, hydrophilic particles to diffuse through. Their aqueous solubility works against them here, since the rate-limiting barrier layer is lipid-based, not water-based. They cross intact skin only with difficulty.

## answer_b
Large proteins

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Large proteins are far too big and too hydrophilic to cross the tightly packed corneocyte/lipid architecture of the stratum corneum. Their size alone makes penetration through intact skin essentially negligible. This is part of why most protein-based drugs cannot be delivered transdermally.

## answer_c
Lipid-soluble molecules

## explanation_c
Correct. Lipid-soluble (lipophilic) molecules dissolve directly into and diffuse through the lipid-rich intercellular matrix of the stratum corneum, the skin's outermost barrier layer. This is the physiological basis for transdermal drug delivery systems, which are deliberately formulated to be lipid-soluble to exploit this route. Small size also helps, but lipid solubility is the dominant factor determining ease of penetration.

## answer_d
Polysaccharides

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Polysaccharides are large, hydrophilic carbohydrate molecules that do not dissolve into the lipid matrix and are effectively blocked by the stratum corneum. Their size and water solubility both work against penetration. They behave similarly to large proteins in this respect.

## answer_e
Nucleic acids

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Nucleic acids are large, highly charged, water-soluble molecules that cannot cross the lipid-rich stratum corneum under normal conditions. Their combination of size and charge makes intact-skin penetration essentially impossible without specialised delivery techniques.
