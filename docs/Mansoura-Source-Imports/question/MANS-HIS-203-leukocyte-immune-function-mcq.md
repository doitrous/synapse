## title
First line of defense against bacterial infection

## question
What is the first line of defense against bacterial infections?

## subject
imm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Basophils

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Basophils mediate immediate (type I) hypersensitivity through IgE-triggered histamine release; they are not the first phagocytic responder to bacterial infection.

## answer_b
Neutrophils

## explanation_b
Correct. Neutrophils are the most numerous circulating granulocyte and the first phagocytic cell to arrive at a bacterial infection site, making them the innate immune system's first line of defence. They are recruited rapidly by chemotaxis, cross the capillary wall by diapedesis, and kill the ingested bacterium using myeloperoxidase and other granule contents once phagocytosed.

## answer_c
Eosinophils

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Eosinophils are specialised against large parasites (helminths) and participate in allergic reactions; bacterial defence is not their primary role.

## answer_d
Monocytes

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Monocytes (and the tissue macrophages they become) are important phagocytes but arrive later than neutrophils and are not considered the first line of defence.

## answer_e
Lymphocytes

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Lymphocytes mediate adaptive, antigen-specific immunity, which takes days to develop; they play no role in the immediate innate response to bacteria.

QST-MANSHIS203-LEUKOCYTE-IMMUNE-FUNCTION-NEUTROPHIL-MYELOPEROXIDASE

## title
Enzyme that kills ingested bacteria in neutrophils

## question
Which enzyme is present in neutrophils and kills the ingested bacteria?

## subject
imm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Carbonic anhydrase

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Carbonic anhydrase speeds the interconversion of carbon dioxide and bicarbonate in erythrocytes; it plays no part in neutrophil bacterial killing.

## answer_b
Myeloperoxidase

## explanation_b
Correct. Myeloperoxidase, carried in the neutrophil's azurophilic granules, combines with hydrogen peroxide and a halide (chloride) to generate hypochlorous acid and other potent oxidants once a bacterium has been phagocytosed into a phagosome. This oxidative killing mechanism is central to the neutrophil's ability to destroy ingested organisms, and its absence (myeloperoxidase deficiency) predisposes to certain fungal infections.

## answer_c
Histaminase

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Histaminase degrades histamine and is associated with eosinophils, not with intracellular bacterial killing in neutrophils.

## answer_d
G-6-P dehydrogenase

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase supports the NADPH-generating pentose phosphate pathway that feeds the oxidative burst, but it is not itself the enzyme that directly kills the ingested bacterium.

## answer_e
Major basic protein

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Major basic protein is an eosinophil granule constituent toxic mainly to parasites, not a neutrophil bacterial-killing enzyme.

# Item

QST-MANSHIS203-LEUKOCYTE-IMMUNE-FUNCTION-DIAPEDESIS

## title
WBC movement through the capillary wall into tissue

## question
Which step in phagocytosis involves the movement of white blood cells through the capillary wall into tissue spaces?

## subject
imm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Phagosome formation

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Phagosome formation is the step in which the ingested particle is enclosed within a membrane-bound vesicle inside the phagocyte, occurring after the cell has already reached the target, not the vessel-crossing step itself.

## answer_b
Chemotaxis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Chemotaxis is directed movement along a chemical gradient toward the infection site; it describes the direction of travel, not the specific act of crossing the capillary wall.

## answer_c
Opsonization

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Opsonization is the coating of a pathogen with molecules (antibody, complement) that facilitate its recognition and uptake by phagocytes, unrelated to crossing the vessel wall.

## answer_d
Margination

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Margination is the preceding step in which the leukocyte rolls along and adheres to the vessel wall, positioning it for the crossing that follows — it precedes diapedesis rather than being the crossing itself.

## answer_e
Diapedesis

## explanation_e
Correct. Diapedesis is the specific step in which the neutrophil physically squeezes between adjacent endothelial cells of the capillary wall, moving from the bloodstream into the tissue space. It occurs immediately after margination (rolling and adhesion against the vessel wall) and before the cell reaches the infection site by chemotaxis. This three-step sequence — margination, diapedesis, chemotaxis — is how a circulating neutrophil gets from blood to a tissue infection site.

Incorrect. Digestion is the intracellular breakdown of the ingested material after phagocytosis, occurring well after the movement described here.

Tolerance

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Tolerance describes the immune system's non-response to self or harmless antigens, an unrelated adaptive-immunity concept.

Incorrect. Polycythemia is an increased, not decreased, red cell count and is unrelated to white cell production.

## answer_d
Osteocytes in the bone

Incorrect. Osteocytes are the mature, non-phagocytic bone-maintaining cell derived from osteoblasts; the bone's own tissue-macrophage-lineage cell is instead the osteoclast.

## title
Locomotion mechanism of tissue macrophages and fibroblasts at a wound

## question
Asim, a 5 years old boy falls from his bicycle and receives a cut over the knee. His tissue macrophages & fibroblasts will move to the site of injury for repair by:

## subject
imm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Ciliary movement

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Ciliary movement is the coordinated beating of cilia (as on respiratory epithelium) that moves fluid or mucus over a stationary cell surface, not the locomotion of an individual migrating cell.

## answer_b
Ameboid movement

## explanation_b
Correct. Tissue macrophages and fibroblasts converging on a wound migrate by ameboid movement — the same flowing, pseudopod-driven locomotion an amoeba uses. The cell extends and retracts cytoplasmic projections (pseudopods) to crawl through tissue toward the site of injury, rather than using cilia or a flagellum. This is the general locomotion mechanism for connective-tissue and immune cells migrating through tissue, not a specialised mechanism unique to any one cell type.

## answer_c
Chemotaxis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Chemotaxis names the directional cue (moving along a chemical gradient) rather than the physical locomotion mechanism itself, which is ameboid movement.

## answer_d
Margination

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Margination is the vascular step in which a leukocyte adheres to the endothelium before crossing into tissue; it does not describe locomotion through tissue toward a wound.

## answer_e
Whip-like movement

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Whip-like (flagellar) movement propels cells such as spermatozoa or certain protozoa, not migrating macrophages or fibroblasts.

## title
Definition of opsonins

## question
Opsonins are molecules that:

## subject
imm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Are secreted by neutrophils to kill pathogens

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Opsonins coat the pathogen itself rather than being secreted killing agents; the neutrophil's own granule enzymes (such as myeloperoxidase) are what actually kill the ingested organism.

## answer_b
Attach to pathogens to facilitate their recognition and uptake by neutrophils

## explanation_b
Correct. Opsonins (such as antibody or complement fragments like C3b) attach to a pathogen's surface, coating it in a way that phagocyte surface receptors specifically recognise. This coating markedly increases the efficiency of recognition and uptake compared with unopsonised phagocytosis — a process called opsonization. Opsonization is a key link between the adaptive immune system (antibody) or complement cascade and the effector phagocytosis carried out by neutrophils and macrophages.

## answer_c
Block the phagocytic receptors on neutrophils

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Opsonins promote, rather than block, phagocytic receptor engagement — this option describes the opposite of their function.

## answer_d
Inhibit the release of inflammatory mediators by neutrophils

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Opsonins act on recognition and uptake, not on suppressing inflammatory mediator release.

## title
Average life span of granulocytes

## question
The average life span for granulocytes is:

## subject
imm

## status

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer

## answer_a
4-5 days

## explanation_a
Correct. Granulocytes (neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils) have a short average circulating life span of about 4-5 days from release into the blood to their eventual destruction or migration into tissue. This short lifespan reflects their role as rapidly deployed, short-lived first-responder cells, in contrast to the erythrocyte's much longer 120-day lifespan. Continuous marrow production is therefore needed to sustain a normal granulocyte count, which is why marrow suppression causes granulocyte counts to fall within days.

## answer_b
12-14 days

## explanation_b
Incorrect. 12-14 days is longer than the granulocyte's actual short circulating life span.

## answer_c
4-5 weeks

## explanation_c
Incorrect. 4-5 weeks is far longer than the granulocyte's actual life span and is not a standard reference value for any major blood cell.

## answer_d
120 days

## explanation_d
Incorrect. 120 days is the erythrocyte's life span, not the granulocyte's — a common distractor pairing in this bank's numeric reference-range questions.

## title
Leukocyte with the longest life span

## question
Which of the following WBCs have the longest life span?

## subject
imm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Neutrophils

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Neutrophils, despite being the most numerous leukocyte, circulate for only about 4-5 days on average — one of the shortest leukocyte lifespans, not the longest.

## answer_b
Eosinophils

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Eosinophils circulate briefly, similar to other granulocytes, and do not have the longest leukocyte life span.

## answer_c
Basophils

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Basophils, like other granulocytes, are short-lived in circulation and do not have the longest leukocyte life span.

## answer_d
Monocytes

## explanation_d
Correct. Monocytes have the longest circulating life span among the leukocytes, spending a comparatively long period in the blood before migrating into tissue. Once in tissue they differentiate into the long-lived tissue macrophage population (such as Kupffer cells or microglia), extending their functional lifespan far beyond that of the short-lived granulocytes. This contrast — numerous but short-lived granulocytes versus less numerous but long-lived monocytes — is the distinction this bank tests.
