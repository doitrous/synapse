## title
Membrane permeability change during depolarization

## question
In nerve fibers, during the depolarization phase of an action potential, the permeability of the membrane:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
To K+ is greatly increased

## explanation_a
Incorrect. K+ permeability rises during repolarization, not during the depolarization phase itself.

## answer_b
To Na+ is greatly increased

## explanation_b
Correct. Depolarization is caused by a sudden, massive increase in the membrane's permeability to Na+, as voltage-gated Na+ channels open in response to the membrane reaching threshold. Na+ then rushes into the cell down its steep electrochemical gradient, driving the membrane potential rapidly from the resting level through zero to a positive overshoot. This regenerative Na+ influx, feeding further channel opening as the membrane depolarizes, is the defining ionic event of the depolarization phase.

## answer_c
To Ca2+ is greatly increased

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Ca2+ permeability increases significantly in some cell types (such as cardiac and smooth muscle) but is not the main driver of the nerve fibre action potential's depolarization phase.

## answer_d
To Cl- is greatly increased

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Cl- permeability is not what rises sharply during depolarization.

## answer_e
Is unchanged

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Membrane permeability changes dramatically during depolarization; it is far from unchanged.

## title
Cause of the after-hyperpolarization phase

## question
The after-hyperpolarization phase of the action potential is caused by which one of the following?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
An outward chloride current

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Chloride currents are not what drives the after-hyperpolarization phase.

## answer_b
An outward potassium current

## explanation_b
Correct. The after-hyperpolarization (positive after-potential) is caused by a continuing outward potassium current, produced because voltage-gated K+ channels remain open slightly longer than it takes the membrane to return to its resting level. This persisting K+ efflux drives the membrane potential briefly past the resting level, making it transiently more negative (hyperpolarized) before the K+ channels finally close and the potential settles back to rest. This is a direct continuation of the same K+ conductance responsible for repolarization, simply outlasting it slightly.

## answer_c
An inward chloride current

## explanation_c
Incorrect. An inward chloride current is not the mechanism of after-hyperpolarization.

## answer_d
An outward sodium current

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Sodium current, when present, is inward (depolarizing), not outward, and Na+ channels are inactivated by this phase in any case.

## answer_e
An outward calcium current

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Outward calcium current is not the basis of after-hyperpolarization in the nerve action potential.

## title
Ion producing the greatest depolarization

## question
The membrane potential will depolarize by the greatest amount if the membrane permeability increases for which of the following ions?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Sodium and potassium

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Combining Na+ and K+ permeability increases would partly offset each other's opposing effects on membrane potential, producing less depolarization than a Na+ increase alone.

## answer_b
Chloride

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Increasing Cl- permeability tends to hyperpolarize or stabilize the membrane near its resting level, not depolarize it, since the Cl- equilibrium potential is near or below the resting potential.

## answer_c
Potassium

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Increasing K+ permeability drives the membrane potential toward the very negative K+ equilibrium potential, hyperpolarizing rather than depolarizing it.

## answer_d
Sodium

## explanation_d
Correct. Because the Na+ equilibrium potential is strongly positive (around +60 mV), increasing membrane permeability to Na+ drives the membrane potential toward that positive value, producing the greatest depolarization of any single-ion permeability increase. This is exactly the mechanism that generates the action potential's depolarization phase: a selective, large increase in Na+ permeability. No other major ion's equilibrium potential is positive enough to depolarize the membrane this strongly.

## answer_e
Potassium and chloride

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Combining K+ and Cl- permeability increases moves the membrane potential toward negative equilibrium potentials, causing hyperpolarization rather than depolarization.

## title
Na+ channel gate open at rest

## question
As regards Na+ voltage-gated channels, which gate is open at rest?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
M (activation) gate

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The activation (M) gate is closed at rest; it is the inactivation gate that is open at rest.

## answer_b
H (inactivation) gate

## explanation_b
Correct. The voltage-gated Na+ channel has two gates: an activation gate near the outer surface and an inactivation gate on the inner surface. At the resting membrane potential, the activation gate is closed while the inactivation gate (H gate) is open, so the channel as a whole is in the resting (closed but available) state. Depolarization to threshold opens the activation gate first, letting Na+ flow in; only afterwards does the slower inactivation gate close, ending Na+ entry.

## answer_c
N gate

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The voltage-gated Na+ channel is described by activation and inactivation gates, not a separate 'N' gate.

## answer_d
None

## explanation_d
Incorrect. One of the two gates (the inactivation/H gate) is indeed open at rest, so the answer is not 'none'.

## title
Trigger for neuronal firing

## question
A neuron fires when which one of the following events occurs?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Depolarization at the axon hillock exceeds the threshold for excitation

## explanation_a
Correct. A neuron fires — generates a propagated action potential — specifically when the summed depolarization at the axon hillock reaches or exceeds the threshold for excitation, the voltage at which enough voltage-gated Na+ channels open to make the regenerative depolarizing cycle self-sustaining. Below this threshold, only a local, non-propagated response occurs, no matter how many depolarizing inputs summate. This threshold-crossing at the axon hillock, specifically, is what converts graded synaptic inputs into an all-or-none, propagated impulse.

## answer_b
There is an excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP)

## explanation_b
Incorrect. An EPSP is a depolarizing synaptic potential that can contribute toward firing, but by itself, without reaching threshold at the axon hillock, it does not guarantee the neuron fires.

## answer_c
Hyperpolarization occurs at the axon hillock

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Hyperpolarization moves the membrane potential further from threshold, making firing less, not more, likely.

## answer_d
Neurotransmitters dock onto receptor proteins

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Neurotransmitter-receptor binding can initiate a postsynaptic potential, but firing itself requires that potential to summate and cross threshold at the axon hillock.

## answer_e
Chloride channels open

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Opening Cl- channels typically produces an inhibitory (hyperpolarizing or stabilizing) effect, working against firing rather than triggering it.

## title
Mechanism of repolarization

## question
About repolarization, which of the following is TRUE?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Results from closure of sodium gates and opening of potassium gates

## explanation_a
Correct. Repolarization results from the closure of the (already-inactivating) voltage-gated Na+ channels together with the opening of voltage-gated K+ channels, which had opened more slowly in response to depolarization. As Na+ entry stops and K+ efflux rises, the membrane potential is driven back down from its positive overshoot toward the resting level. This combination of falling Na+ conductance and rising K+ conductance is the defining ionic mechanism of repolarization.

## answer_b
Occurs at first gradual then becomes fast

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Repolarization occurs rapidly for roughly its first 70 percent and then more slowly for the remainder, the reverse of 'gradual then fast'.

## answer_c
Is followed by appearance of the response

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Repolarization is itself part of the response (the action potential), not something that precedes and is 'followed by' the response.

## answer_d
Occurs due to K+ influx

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Repolarization occurs due to K+ efflux (outward movement), not K+ influx.

## answer_e
Is represented by the ascending limb of the spike

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The ascending limb of the spike is the depolarization phase; repolarization is represented by the descending limb.

## title
Effect of hypokalemia on the resting potential

## question
Hypokalemia (decrease in plasma K+ concentration) would be expected to result in:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Increased neuronal excitability

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Hypokalemia hyperpolarizes the resting potential, which moves it further from threshold and tends to decrease, not increase, excitability.

## answer_b
A more negative resting membrane potential (RMP)

## explanation_b
Correct. By the Nernst equation, the K+ equilibrium potential depends on the ratio of intracellular to extracellular K+ concentration; lowering extracellular K+ (hypokalemia) increases that ratio, making the K+ equilibrium potential — and, because the resting membrane sits close to it, the resting membrane potential itself — more negative than normal. This hyperpolarization moves the membrane further from the threshold needed to fire an action potential. This Nernst-based relationship is why clinically significant hypokalemia disturbs the excitability of nerve and muscle.

## answer_c
No change in RMP

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Because the resting potential is heavily K+-dependent, a change in extracellular K+ concentration does change the RMP, not leave it unchanged.

## answer_d
Decrease in the firing level of neurons

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The threshold (firing level) itself is not directly lowered by hypokalemia; if anything, the larger gap between the more negative RMP and the (relatively unchanged) firing level makes the neuron harder, not easier, to fire.

QST-MANSAEP-AEPPHYSIO14-AEPPHYSIO14-Q10

## title
Channel activation and inactivation during the action potential

## question
An action potential in nerve fibers is characterized by which one of the following?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Includes changes due to channel activation and inactivation

## explanation_a
Correct. The nerve action potential is characterized by sequential changes in voltage-gated Na+ and K+ channel states — activation (opening) and inactivation (closing) — that together produce depolarization, repolarization and the refractory period. The Na+ channel's own activation followed by inactivation, and the K+ channel's slower activation, together account for the entire shape of the spike. This is fundamentally different from a channel that is simply passively open or closed.

## answer_b
Is a local response

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Unlike a subthreshold local response, the action potential is a propagated, all-or-none event, not confined to the site of stimulation.

## answer_c
