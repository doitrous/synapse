## title
Tissue most susceptible to liquefactive necrosis after infarction

## question
Which tissue is most susceptible to liquefactive necrosis following infarction?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Pancreas

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The pancreas is rich in its own digestive enzymes and can undergo enzymatic fat necrosis after injury such as acute pancreatitis, but this is not the tissue classically named for liquefactive necrosis after simple ischaemic infarction.

## answer_b
Liver

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Hepatic infarction, like most solid-organ infarcts, typically produces coagulative necrosis, in which the tissue architecture is preserved as a firm, pale 'ghost' outline rather than liquefying.

## answer_c
Spleen

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Splenic infarction likewise follows the coagulative pattern common to solid organs with an end-arterial blood supply, not liquefaction.

## answer_d
Brain

## explanation_d
Correct. The brain is the classic tissue for liquefactive necrosis after infarction. Its high lipid content and lack of a substantial supportive connective-tissue framework mean that dead neural tissue is rapidly digested by its own hydrolytic enzymes (autolysis) and by phagocytic microglia, softening and eventually liquefying into a fluid-filled cystic cavity rather than leaving a firm 'ghost' architecture behind. This liquefactive pattern is why an old cerebral infarct is eventually seen as a cystic, fluid-filled cavity on imaging rather than as a firm scar.

## answer_e
Intestine

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Intestinal infarction typically progresses to gangrenous necrosis, a coagulative pattern modified by superimposed bacterial putrefaction, not primary liquefaction.

## title
Cause of coagulative necrosis

## question
Coagulative necrosis usually results from which of the following conditions?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Abscess

## explanation_a
Incorrect. An abscess is a localised collection of pus formed by liquefactive, suppurative necrosis, not the coagulative pattern this question asks about.

## answer_b
Ischemia

## explanation_b
Correct. Coagulative necrosis is the pattern typically produced by ischaemia (loss of blood supply) in solid organs such as the heart, kidney and spleen. Denaturation of structural and enzymatic proteins by the resulting acidosis and hypoxic injury outpaces the enzymatic digestion (autolysis) that would otherwise liquefy the tissue, so the dead cells keep their basic outline as a firm, pale, 'ghost' architecture for several days before macrophages clear the debris.

## answer_c
Trauma

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Trauma more classically produces fat necrosis (traumatic or enzymatic) in fat-rich tissue, or simple mechanical tissue disruption, rather than the ischaemia-driven coagulative pattern.

## answer_d
Tuberculosis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Tuberculosis classically produces caseation necrosis, a distinct 'cheese-like' pattern in which the tissue architecture is completely lost, unlike the preserved outline of coagulative necrosis.

## answer_e
Syphilis

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Syphilis is associated with gummatous necrosis, a variant with some rubbery, gummy character, not the ischaemia-driven coagulative pattern this question asks about.

Description of an abscess

## question
Which of the following describes an abscess?

## subject

## status

## vignette

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Acute diffuse inflammation in soft tissue describes cellulitis, the diffuse suppurative pattern driven by streptococcal fibrinolysin, which is the opposite of an abscess's localised, walled-off pattern.

## answer_b
Chronic inflammation following acute inflammation

## explanation_b
Incorrect. An abscess is an acute lesion by pattern, not a chronic inflammation that has followed on from an earlier acute episode.

## answer_c
Localized chronic inflammation

## explanation_c
Incorrect. An abscess is acute, not chronic, even though it can persist or recur if not drained.

## answer_e
Acute non-suppurative inflammation

## explanation_e
Incorrect. An abscess is defined precisely by its substantial purulent (suppurative) exudate, so calling it non-suppurative directly contradicts its defining feature.

## question

## subject

## status

## vignette

## answer_a
Type I hypersensitivity reaction

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Type I hypersensitivity is the immediate, IgE-mediated reaction of allergy and anaphylaxis, driven by mast-cell degranulation, not by the delayed, cell-mediated process behind granuloma formation.

## answer_b
Type II hypersensitivity reaction

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Type II hypersensitivity is antibody-mediated cytotoxicity against cell-surface or tissue antigens, a different mechanism from the T-cell/macrophage process that builds a granuloma.

## answer_c
Type III hypersensitivity reaction

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Type III hypersensitivity is driven by circulating immune complexes depositing in tissue, again a distinct, antibody-based mechanism from granuloma formation.

## answer_d
Type IV hypersensitivity reaction

## explanation_d
Correct. Immune-mediated granuloma formation is a type IV, delayed-type, cell-mediated hypersensitivity reaction. Sensitised T lymphocytes, activated after a macrophage presents them with a persistent antigen, release cytokines that recruit and activate further macrophages, driving their transformation into the epithelioid cells and giant cells that make up a granuloma. This is why granulomas form specifically around persistent, poorly clearable antigens, such as the tubercle bacillus, rather than around antigens the immune system can eliminate quickly.

## answer_e
Not a hypersensitivity reaction

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Immune-mediated granuloma formation is very much a hypersensitivity reaction, specifically the type IV, delayed, cell-mediated type, so this option directly contradicts the established mechanism.

## question
What is a scar tissue?

## subject

## status

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Avascular strong fibrous tissue

## explanation_a
Correct. A mature scar is avascular, strong, fibrous tissue. ## answer_d
Skin tissue rich in appendages

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Mature scar tissue lacks skin appendages such as hair follicles and glands, since these are not regenerated during fibrous repair.

## answer_e
Malignant spindle cell proliferation

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Malignant spindle-cell proliferation describes a sarcoma, not the benign, reparative fibrous tissue of a scar.
