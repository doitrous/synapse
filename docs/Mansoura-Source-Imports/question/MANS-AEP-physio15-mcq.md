## title
A true character of the nerve action potential

## question
Which of the following is a character of the action potential?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Is a graded potential

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The action potential is an all-or-none event, not a graded one — once threshold is reached its amplitude is fixed and does not scale with stimulus strength.

## answer_b
Is conducted slower in thin nerve fibers

## explanation_b
Correct. Conduction velocity rises with fibre diameter, so thinner nerve fibres conduct action potentials more slowly than thicker ones of the same type. This is why the thin C fibres are the slowest-conducting class and the thick A fibres the fastest, and it is one of the three determinants of conduction velocity alongside myelination and temperature. A thin fibre offers more internal resistance to the local current that depolarises the next patch of membrane, so that current spreads a shorter distance ahead of the active zone per unit time.

## answer_c
Is produced by a sub-threshold stimulus

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A sub-threshold stimulus produces only a local, non-propagated response; an action potential requires an adequate, threshold-or-greater stimulus.

## answer_d
Requires no energy

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Restoring the ionic gradients the action potential disturbs (via the Na+-K+ pump) does require ATP, even though the spike itself is driven by passive ion flow down existing gradients.

## answer_e
Starts with repolarization caused by outward movement of Cl-

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The action potential starts with depolarization driven by inward Na+ movement, not repolarization or outward Cl- movement.

## title
Site of maximum Na+ channel density in a myelinated neuron

## question
The number of Na+ channels per square micrometer of membrane in myelinated neurons is maximum at which one of the following sites?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Axon terminal

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The axon terminal is specialised for neurotransmitter release via voltage-gated Ca2+ channels, not for the highest density of voltage-gated Na+ channels.

## answer_b
Cell body

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The cell body carries a relatively low density of voltage-gated Na+ channels compared with the nodes; it is mainly a site of integration, not regeneration of the spike.

## answer_c
Dendritic zone

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Dendrites are largely a receiving surface and carry few voltage-gated Na+ channels relative to the nodes of Ranvier.

## answer_d
Initial segment

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The initial segment does carry a high Na+ channel density, since it is where the action potential is first generated, but the internodal membrane's own Na+ channel density is concentrated even further, at each node of Ranvier along the axon's length.

## answer_e
Node of Ranvier

## explanation_e
Correct. In a myelinated axon the internodal membrane is insulated by myelin, and it is only at the nodes of Ranvier that the membrane is exposed and richly supplied with voltage-gated Na+ channels. Because each successive action potential is only regenerated at a node, this concentrated channel density at the nodes is what makes saltatory conduction possible, letting the impulse jump from node to node instead of being regenerated continuously along the whole membrane.

## title
What increases nerve conduction velocity

## question
The velocity of conduction of action potentials along a nerve will be increased by which one of the following?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Decreasing the diameter of the nerve fiber

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Decreasing fibre diameter slows conduction, since conduction velocity rises with diameter, not falls.

## answer_b
Lengthening the nerve fiber

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The length of the fiber does not, by itself, change the velocity at which an action potential is conducted along it.

## answer_c
Myelinating the nerve

## explanation_c
Correct. Myelinating a nerve fibre insulates the internode and restricts the voltage-gated Na+ channels to the nodes of Ranvier, so the action potential is regenerated only at the nodes and jumps between them (saltatory conduction) rather than being regenerated at every adjacent patch of membrane. This raises conduction velocity by up to fifty-fold compared with an unmyelinated fibre of the same diameter, while also conserving the energy the Na+-K+ pump would otherwise spend restoring ionic gradients along the whole membrane.

## answer_d
Local anesthetics

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Local anesthetics decrease membrane permeability to Na+ and reduce excitability, which slows or blocks conduction rather than speeding it up.

## answer_e
Pressure on the axons

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Pressure on a nerve trunk impairs or abolishes conduction (especially in the larger A fibers), it does not increase conduction velocity.

## title
Basis of saltatory conduction in myelinated axons

## question
Saltatory conduction in myelinated axons results from which one of the following?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Salt concentration is increased beneath the myelin segments

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Saltatory conduction is not explained by any change in salt concentration beneath the myelin; the myelin segments act as an electrical insulator, not a site of altered ionic concentration.

## answer_b
Capacitance is decreased at the nodes of Ranvier

## explanation_b
Incorrect. It is the internodal (myelinated) membrane's capacitance that is decreased by the insulating myelin sheath, not the capacitance at the nodes themselves — the nodes are where the exposed, low-resistance membrane and the Na+ channels are concentrated.

## answer_c
Non-gated ion channels are present beneath the segments of myelin

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The myelin-covered internode has very few functional ion channels exposed to the extracellular fluid; it is the insulated, low-conductance segment, the opposite of what this option describes.

## answer_d
Voltage-gated Na+ channels are concentrated at nodes of Ranvier

## explanation_d
Correct. In a myelinated axon the internode is insulated by myelin and depolarises passively, while voltage-gated Na+ channels are concentrated at the exposed nodes of Ranvier. An action potential generated at one node acts as the stimulus for the next, so the impulse is regenerated only at the nodes and appears to jump between them, which is what raises conduction velocity so sharply in myelinated fibres.

## answer_e
Membrane resistance is decreased beneath the segments of myelin

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Membrane resistance is increased, not decreased, beneath the myelin segments — the myelin's insulating effect raises resistance to current leak across the internodal membrane, which is part of why current spreads passively and efficiently toward the next node.

## title
Mechanism of action of local anesthetic drugs

## question
Local anesthetic drugs take effect by which of the following mechanisms?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Internal block of the axonal voltage-gated sodium channel

## explanation_a
Correct. Local anesthetics act on the axon itself by entering the nerve fibre and blocking the voltage-gated Na+ channel from its internal (cytoplasmic) side. This decreases the membrane's permeability to Na+, so the membrane can no longer depolarise fast enough to reach the firing level, and the action potential — and with it, conduction — is abolished in the affected segment of nerve.

## answer_b
Inhibiting the action of acetylcholinesterase in the synapse

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Inhibiting acetylcholinesterase would prolong, not block, cholinergic transmission at a synapse; that is not how local anesthetics act on a nerve fibre.

## answer_c
Internal block of axonal voltage-gated potassium channels

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Local anesthetics act on the voltage-gated Na+ channel, not the K+ channel, and they act from within the axon rather than blocking K+ conductance.

## answer_d
Blocking nicotinic acetylcholine receptors at the synapse

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Blocking nicotinic acetylcholine receptors describes a neuromuscular- or ganglionic-blocking drug acting at a synapse, not the axonal mechanism of a local anesthetic.

## answer_e
Blocking neurotransmitter release from neuronal terminals

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Local anesthetics block conduction along the axon itself; they do not act by blocking neurotransmitter release from a nerve terminal.

## title
A property of continuous conduction

## question
Which of the following is true of continuous conduction?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Occurs by jumping of charges from one node of Ranvier to another

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Jumping of charges from node to node describes saltatory conduction in myelinated fibers, which is the opposite of continuous conduction.

## answer_b
Is relatively slow in unmyelinated nerve fibers

## explanation_b
Correct. Continuous conduction is the mode of propagation in unmyelinated axons: the action potential regenerates at every adjacent patch of membrane via a local circuit of current rather than jumping between insulated segments. Because every patch of membrane must be depolarised in turn, and conduction speed is proportional only to the square root of the fibre's diameter (with no saltatory speed boost from myelin), this mode is relatively slow compared with saltatory conduction in a myelinated fibre of similar size.

## answer_c
Occurs in the neuro-muscular junction

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The neuro-muscular junction is a chemical synapse, not a form of axonal conduction; continuous conduction refers to propagation along an unmyelinated axon.

## answer_d
Occurs in myelinated nerve fibers

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Continuous conduction occurs in unmyelinated, not myelinated, nerve fibers — myelinated fibers instead use saltatory conduction.

## answer_e
Velocity of conduction is fast (120 met/sec)

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A velocity around 120 m/sec is characteristic of the fastest myelinated (large A) fibers using saltatory conduction, not of unmyelinated fibers using continuous conduction, which are far slower.

## title
Effect of myelination on axon signal propagation

## question
What effect does myelination have on axons?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
It protects them from damage

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Myelination is not primarily a protective/structural coating in the sense of shielding the axon from injury; its physiological role tested here is on conduction speed.

## answer_b
It slows the propagation of signals along them

Incorrect. Myelination speeds up, rather than slows, the propagation of the nerve signal, by restricting the regenerated action potential to the nodes of Ranvier.

It prevents cross talk between adjacent axons

It is responsible for regeneration of the nerve

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Myelination is not the mechanism of nerve regeneration; regeneration of a damaged peripheral nerve fibre is a separate, distinct physiological process.

## title
Speed of saltatory conduction

## question
Regarding saltatory conduction, which of the following is true?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Occurs in unmyelinated fibers

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Saltatory conduction occurs in myelinated fibers, where the insulated internode forces the action potential to be regenerated only at the nodes of Ranvier; unmyelinated fibers instead conduct continuously.

## answer_b
May reach 120 m/sec

## explanation_b
Correct. Saltatory conduction is the fastest mode of nerve conduction, and in the largest, most heavily myelinated A-alpha fibers it can reach up to about 120 m/sec — the top of the A-fiber conduction-velocity range (20 to 120 m/sec). This speed comes from the impulse jumping between nodes of Ranvier rather than being regenerated at every point along the membrane.

## answer_c
Occurs by jumping from one neuron to another

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Saltatory conduction is the jumping of the action potential from one node of Ranvier to the next along a single axon, not a jump from one neuron to another (that would describe synaptic transmission).

## answer_d
Decreases gradually with distance till it disappears

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Because saltatory conduction is an actively regenerated, all-or-none process at each node, it does not decrease in amplitude or fade with distance; its amplitude stays constant along the length of the axon.

## answer_e
Occurs in post-ganglionic nerve fibers

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Post-ganglionic autonomic fibers are C fibers, which are unmyelinated and therefore use continuous, not saltatory, conduction.

## topic
Physiology

## subtopic

## title
Where saltatory conduction occurs

## question
Regarding saltatory conduction, which of the following is true?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Occurs in myelinated nerve fibers

## explanation_a
Correct. Saltatory conduction occurs specifically in myelinated nerve fibers, where the myelin sheath insulates the internode and forces the action potential to be regenerated only at the exposed nodes of Ranvier, so the impulse appears to jump between them along the axon. This node-to-node regeneration is what gives myelinated fibers their much higher conduction velocity compared with unmyelinated fibers of similar diameter.

## answer_b
Is fast in unmyelinated nerve fibers

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Unmyelinated fibers use continuous conduction, not saltatory conduction, and continuous conduction is relatively slow rather than fast.

## answer_c
Occurs by jumping from one neuron to another

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Saltatory conduction jumps between nodes of Ranvier along one axon, not from one neuron to another; that would instead describe synaptic transmission.

## answer_d
Velocity of conduction is slow (0.5-2.0 meter/sec)

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A conduction velocity of 0.5-2.0 m/sec is in the C-fiber (unmyelinated) range; saltatory conduction in myelinated fibers is much faster, reaching up to about 120 m/sec in the largest fibers.

## answer_e
Decreases gradually with distance till it disappears

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Saltatory conduction is actively regenerated at each node, so its amplitude does not decrease or fade with distance along the axon.

## title
A defining feature of Type A nerve fibers

## question
Regarding Type A nerve fibers, which of the following is true?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
They have the greatest diameter

## explanation_a
Correct. Type A fibers are the largest class of peripheral nerve fibers, ranging from 2 to 20 micrometres in diameter, which — together with their myelination — gives them the fastest conduction velocities of the three fiber classes (20 to 120 m/sec). They are exemplified by somatic motor fibers, and further subdivide into alpha, beta, gamma and delta subtypes.

## answer_b
They have a prolonged spike duration (2.0 msec)

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Type A fibers have the shortest spike duration of the three classes, about 0.5 msec; a prolonged 2.0 msec spike duration instead describes Type C fibers.

## answer_c
They conduct impulses with slowed velocity (0.5-2 m/sec)

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A conduction velocity of only 0.5-2 m/sec describes the slow Type C fibers, not Type A fibers, which are the fastest-conducting class.

## answer_d
They include postganglionic fibers

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Postganglionic autonomic fibers are Type C fibers; Type A fibers instead include somatic motor and sensory fibers.

## answer_e
They are very sensitive to local anesthetic drugs

## explanation_e
Incorrect. It is the thin, slow Type C fibers that local anesthetics depress first; Type A fibers are relatively the least sensitive to local anesthetics.

## title
A defining feature of Type C nerve fibers

## question
Regarding Type C nerve fibers, which of the following is true?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
They have the greatest diameter

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Type C fibers have the smallest diameter of the three classes, under 1 micrometre; Type A fibers have the greatest diameter.

## answer_b
They carry the highest-magnitude nerve impulse

## explanation_b
Incorrect. There is no distinct property of a 'highest-magnitude' impulse — action potentials are all-or-none events of comparable amplitude regardless of fiber class.

## answer_c
They conduct impulses with the slowest velocity (0.5-2 m/sec)

## explanation_c
Correct. Type C fibers are the thinnest and slowest of the three fiber classes, conducting impulses at only 0.5-2 m/sec — far slower than Type A (20-120 m/sec) or Type B (5-15 m/sec) fibers. This is because Type C fibers are unmyelinated and use continuous rather than saltatory conduction, so each adjacent patch of membrane must be depolarised in turn rather than the impulse jumping between nodes.

## answer_d
They include somatic sensory and motor fibers

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Somatic sensory and motor fibers are Type A fibers; Type C fibers are instead exemplified by postganglionic autonomic fibers.

## answer_e
They are very sensitive to hypoxia

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Type B fibers are the class most susceptible to hypoxia, while Type C fibers are actually the least affected by it.

## title
Effect of local anesthetic drugs on nerve excitability

## question
Regarding the excitability of nerve fibers, which of the following is true?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Is increased by decreased temperature

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This is not the tested direction of the temperature-excitability relationship in this source; the fact this question tests is that local anesthetics completely abolish nerve excitability, listed as option E.

## answer_b
Is decreased by increased temperature

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Excitability is not described as decreased by increased temperature in this source's own tested fact; the correct fact tested here concerns local anesthetics abolishing excitability entirely.

## answer_c
Is increased by decreased Na+

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A decreased extracellular Na+ shrinks the size of the action potential rather than increasing overall excitability; it is a decreased extracellular Ca2+ that raises membrane Na+ permeability and increases excitability.

## answer_d
Is decreased by decreased Ca++ ions

## explanation_d
Incorrect. It is an increased, not a decreased, extracellular Ca2+ that stabilises the membrane and lowers excitability.

## answer_e
Is completely lost by local anesthetic drugs

## explanation_e
Correct. Local anesthetics such as those used clinically act by decreasing the membrane's permeability to Na+, so strongly that the membrane can no longer depolarise fast enough to reach the firing level. Because no action potential can then be generated at all in the anesthetised segment, the nerve's excitability there is described as completely lost.

## title
Identifying a nerve blocker

## question
Which of the following is a nerve blocker?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Local anesthesia

## explanation_a
Correct. Local anesthetic drugs act by markedly decreasing the membrane's permeability to Na+, which prevents the membrane from depolarising to the firing level. Because no action potential can then be generated, conduction along the nerve is blocked, which is why local anesthetics are classed as nerve blockers.

## answer_b
Increased Na+ ions

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Increased extracellular Na+ permeability raises, rather than blocks, excitability; it is a fall in Na+ availability or permeability that impairs conduction.

## answer_c
Slight pressure

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Only a strong (not slight) sustained pressure on a nerve trunk is capable of impairing conduction, and even then it affects the large A fibers first while sparing the C fibers.

## answer_d
Strong catelectrotonus

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Catelectrotonus (the region under a cathode) is associated with increased, not blocked, excitability — it is anelectrotonus (under the anode) that is associated with decreased excitability.

## answer_e
Decreased Ca2+ ions

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A decreased extracellular Ca2+ raises membrane Na+ permeability and increases excitability rather than blocking the nerve.

## title
Mechanism of local anesthetic drug action on membrane permeability

## question
Local anesthetic drugs act by which of the following mechanisms?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Blocking nicotinic acetylcholine receptors at the synapse

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Blocking nicotinic acetylcholine receptors describes a neuromuscular- or ganglionic-blocking drug's synaptic mechanism, not the axonal mechanism of a local anesthetic.

## answer_b
Decreasing membrane permeability to potassium

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Local anesthetics act on Na+, not K+, permeability; decreasing K+ permeability is not their mechanism.

## answer_c
Blocking neurotransmitter release from neuronal terminals

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Local anesthetics block conduction along the axon rather than acting at the nerve terminal to block neurotransmitter release.

## answer_d
Decreasing membrane permeability to sodium

## explanation_d
Correct. Local anesthetics act by decreasing the membrane's permeability to Na+, entering the axon and blocking the voltage-gated Na+ channel from its internal side. With Na+ permeability reduced, the membrane can no longer depolarise fast enough to reach the firing level, so the action potential — and conduction along that segment of nerve — is abolished.

## answer_e
Inhibiting the action of acetylcholinesterase at the synapse

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Inhibiting acetylcholinesterase would prolong, not block, transmission at a cholinergic synapse; it is not how a local anesthetic acts on the axon.

QST-MANSAEP-AEPPHYSIO15-AEPPHYSIO15-Q18

## title
False statement about the nerve action potential (EXCEPT)

## question
In the action potential of the nerve, all the following are true EXCEPT:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
The rapid repolarization is due to increased K+ permeability

## explanation_a
Incorrect (i.e. this statement is TRUE). Rapid repolarization is indeed caused by increased K+ permeability, as the voltage-gated K+ channels open (later and more slowly than the Na+ channels) and carry K+ out of the cell, returning the membrane toward its resting level.

## answer_b
The duration of the isoelectric interval (latent period) is inversely proportionate to the speed of conduction

## explanation_b
Incorrect (i.e. this statement is TRUE). The latent period is the time the impulse takes to travel from the stimulating to the recording electrode, so for a fixed distance a shorter latent period means faster conduction — the two are inversely related.

## answer_c
Decreasing the external Na+ concentration reduces the size of the action potential

## explanation_c
Incorrect (i.e. this statement is TRUE). Decreasing the external Na+ concentration reduces the inward Na+ driving force during depolarization, which shrinks the size (amplitude) of the action potential.

## answer_d
The absolute refractory period is the period from the firing level until repolarization is completed

## explanation_d
Correct (i.e. this is the FALSE statement, the answer to the EXCEPT). The absolute refractory period runs from the firing level to only the early part of repolarization, not until repolarization is completed. It exists because the voltage-gated Na+ channels have all opened and then been rapidly inactivated, and it ends once enough of those channels have returned to their resting state to be available again — well before the membrane potential has fully returned to rest.

## title
Effect of doubling extracellular K+ on the resting potential

## question
Regarding nerve excitability, which of the following is true?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Chronaxie means the intensity of the stimulus that produces a response

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Chronaxie is a duration, not an intensity — it is the time needed for a stimulus at twice rheobase intensity to excite the nerve; rheobase is the intensity term.

## answer_b
An anelectrotonic response decreases the membrane potential and increases nerve excitability

## explanation_b
Incorrect. An anelectrotonic response (under the anode) hyperpolarises the membrane and decreases excitability; it is the catelectrotonic response (under the cathode) that depolarises the membrane and increases excitability.

## answer_c
K+ is the least permeable cation through nerve membranes

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Resting membrane permeability to K+ is relatively high, which is why the resting membrane potential lies close to the K+ equilibrium potential; it is not the least permeable cation at rest.

## answer_d
The resting potential becomes less negative if the K+ concentration outside the nerve membrane is doubled

## explanation_d
Correct. The resting membrane potential depends heavily on the K+ concentration gradient across the membrane. Doubling the extracellular K+ concentration reduces the steepness of the outward K+ gradient the resting potential depends on, which depolarises the cell — making the resting potential less negative (closer to zero) — and this is the mechanism by which hyperkalaemia raises nerve and muscle excitability.

QST-MANSAEP-AEPPHYSIO15-AEPPHYSIO15-Q21

## title
How increased K+ raises nerve fibre excitability

## question
Increased K+ ions increase the excitability of nerve fibers by which of the following mechanisms?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Producing depolarization, thus reducing action potential amplitude

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This describes an effect on action potential amplitude, not the mechanism by which raised extracellular K+ produces the initial depolarization that increases excitability.

## answer_b
Producing depolarization, thus activating voltage-gated Na+ channels

## explanation_b
Incorrect. While depolarization toward threshold does bring the membrane closer to activating Na+ channels, the mechanism specifically asked for here is how the depolarization itself is produced by the raised K+, which is via reduced K+ efflux, not via direct Na+ channel activation.

## answer_c
Producing depolarization through decreased K+ efflux

## explanation_c
Correct. Raising extracellular K+ concentration narrows the K+ concentration gradient across the membrane, so less K+ leaves the cell down its (now shallower) gradient — a decreased K+ efflux. Because the resting potential depends heavily on that outward K+ movement, reduced efflux depolarises the membrane, bringing it closer to threshold and thereby increasing excitability.

## answer_d
Inhibiting the activity of the Na+-K+ pump

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Increased extracellular K+ is not described as acting by inhibiting the Na+-K+ pump; its effect on excitability is via the resting potential and K+ efflux.

## answer_e
Hyperpolarization of nerve fibers

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Increased extracellular K+ depolarises (does not hyperpolarise) the nerve fibre, which is exactly why it raises rather than lowers excitability.

## title
Calculating conduction velocity from latent period and distance

## question
If the distance between a stimulating electrode and a recording electrode is 2 cm and the latent period is 1 millisecond, the conduction velocity of the axon is:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
100 meter/sec

## explanation_a
Incorrect. 100 m/sec does not match the calculation for this distance and latent period.

## answer_b
50 meter/sec

## explanation_b
Incorrect. 50 m/sec does not match the calculation for this distance and latent period.

## answer_c
40 meter/sec

## explanation_c
Incorrect. 40 m/sec does not match the calculation for this distance and latent period.

## answer_d
20 meter/sec

## explanation_d
Correct. Conduction velocity is calculated as distance divided by the latent period, since the latent period is the time the impulse takes to travel from the stimulating electrode to the recording electrode. Here, 2 cm (0.02 m) divided by 1 millisecond (0.001 sec) gives 0.02 m / 0.001 sec = 20 meters/sec.

## answer_e
This cannot be determined from the given information

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The conduction velocity can be determined directly from the given distance and latent period, using velocity = distance / time; the information given is sufficient.

## title
Loss of movement without loss of pain under sustained nerve pressure

## question
A patient sitting for a prolonged time with deep pressure on a nerve trunk (e.g. sitting on the leg) loses movement in the limb but pain sensation is preserved. The best explanation is that:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
A fibers are more susceptible to hypoxia than B fibers

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This vignette is explained by differential sensitivity to pressure, not to hypoxia, and it is B fibers (not A fibers) that are most susceptible to hypoxia.

## answer_b
A fibers are more sensitive to pressure than C fibers

## explanation_b
Correct. Sustained pressure on a nerve trunk preferentially abolishes conduction in the larger, thicker A fibers, while the thin C fibers remain relatively intact. Since A fibers carry somatic motor function (and fast, sharp pain/touch), their loss explains the paralysis, while the surviving C fibers — which carry slow, dull pain — explain why pain sensation is preserved.

## answer_c
Nerve fibers carrying slow pain sensations are A fibers

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Slow (dull, aching) pain sensation is carried by C fibers, not A fibers; A fibers instead carry fast, sharp pain, touch and motor function.

## answer_d
C fibers are more sensitive to pressure than A fibers

## explanation_d
Incorrect. It is the A fibers, not the C fibers, that are more sensitive to sustained pressure, which is exactly why movement (an A-fiber, motor function) is lost while pain (partly a C-fiber function) is preserved.

## answer_e
C fibers are somatic motor nerves carrying voluntary movements

## explanation_e
Incorrect. C fibers are postganglionic autonomic and slow-pain sensory fibers; somatic motor fibers carrying voluntary movement are A fibers, not C fibers.

## title
Functional role of the absolute refractory period

## question
Regarding the absolute refractory period, which of the following is true?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
It limits the number of action potentials that can be produced and propagated along nerve fibers

## explanation_a
Correct. During the absolute refractory period no stimulus of any strength, however strong, can produce a second action potential, because all the voltage-gated Na+ channels have opened and then been inactivated. By imposing a minimum interval between successive action potentials, the absolute refractory period sets an upper limit on the number of action potentials a nerve fibre can produce and propagate per unit time.

## answer_b
It increases the velocity of conduction along the nerve fibers

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The refractory period does not increase conduction velocity; it instead limits how frequently a fibre can fire, which is a separate property from the speed of any single impulse.

## answer_c
It is the period of time when a strong stimulus can initiate a second action potential

## explanation_c
Incorrect. That is the definition of the relative, not the absolute, refractory period — during the absolute refractory period, no stimulus of any strength can produce a second action potential.

## answer_d
It reduces the energy utilized by nerve fibers

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The refractory period is not described as an energy-saving mechanism; its role is functional (limiting firing frequency and enforcing one-way propagation), not metabolic.

## answer_e
None of the above

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Option A correctly describes a true function of the absolute refractory period, so 'none of the above' is not correct.

## title
Timing of peak K+ efflux relative to the spike

## question
During nerve cell excitation, the peak of K+ efflux occurs:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Before the spike

## explanation_a
Incorrect. K+ efflux is not the event that precedes the spike; the spike (the sharp Na+-driven rise and fall) comes first, with K+ efflux following.

## answer_b
Before the peak of Na+ influx

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The voltage-gated K+ channels open later than the Na+ channels, not earlier, so peak K+ efflux comes after, not before, peak Na+ influx.

## answer_c
Coincident with the peak of Na+ influx

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The two are not coincident — the K+ channels are deliberately slower to open than the Na+ channels, which is what separates depolarization from repolarization in time.

## answer_d
After both the spike and the peak of Na+ influx

## explanation_d
Correct. Two events end the spike: inactivation of the Na+ channels stops the Na+ influx, while the K+ channels — which open shortly after the Na+ channels, more slowly and stay open for longer — carry K+ out to complete repolarization. Because the K+ channels open later than the Na+ channels and remain open after the Na+ channels have already inactivated, the peak of K+ efflux occurs after both the spike itself and the peak of Na+ influx that produced it.

## answer_e
In the after-depolarization phase of the action potential

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Peak K+ efflux drives repolarization (and the subsequent hyperpolarization/after-potential), so it occurs before, not during, the after-depolarization/after-potential phase — it is the cause of that later phase, not simultaneous with it.

## title
A true statement about Type A fibers

## question
Regarding Type A fibers, which of the following is true?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
They are very sensitive to local anesthetic drugs

## explanation_a
Incorrect. It is the thin, slow Type C fibers that local anesthetics depress first; Type A fibers are relatively resistant to local anesthetics compared with C fibers.

## answer_b
They are very sensitive to O2 lack

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Type B fibers, not Type A, are the class most susceptible to hypoxia (O2 lack).

## answer_c
They conduct impulses with slow velocity

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Type A fibers conduct impulses with the fastest, not the slowest, velocity of the three fiber classes (20-120 m/sec).

## answer_d
They have the smallest diameter

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Type A fibers have the greatest, not the smallest, diameter (2-20 micrometres); Type C fibers have the smallest diameter.

## answer_e
They include somatic sensory and motor fibers

## explanation_e
Correct. Type A fibers are exemplified by somatic motor fibers, and the class further subdivides into alpha, beta, gamma and delta subtypes, which together carry somatic sensory functions (touch, proprioception, fast pain) as well as somatic motor function. This mix of sensory and motor roles distinguishes Type A fibers from the autonomic Type B (preganglionic) and Type C (postganglionic) classes.
