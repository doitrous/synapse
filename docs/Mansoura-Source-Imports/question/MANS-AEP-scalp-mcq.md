## title
Not a muscle of facial expression

## question
Which of the following is NOT a muscle of facial expression?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Frontal belly of occiptofrontalis.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The frontal belly of occipitofrontalis is a genuine muscle of facial expression, supplied by the facial nerve.

## answer_b
Orbicularis oculi.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Orbicularis oculi is a genuine muscle of facial expression, closing the eye.

## answer_c
Platysma

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Platysma is a genuine muscle of facial expression, developing with the other facial muscles from the second pharyngeal arch and supplied by the facial nerve's cervical branch.

## answer_d
Buccinator.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Buccinator is a genuine muscle of facial expression, despite its role in chewing.

## answer_e
Temporalis.

## explanation_e
Correct. Temporalis is a muscle of mastication, developing from the first pharyngeal arch and supplied by the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve (V3), unlike the true muscles of facial expression, which all develop from the second pharyngeal arch and are supplied by the facial nerve (CN VII). This different embryological origin and nerve supply is exactly what separates the muscles of mastication (temporalis, masseter, medial and lateral pterygoids) from the muscles of facial expression. Temporalis elevates and retracts the mandible during chewing, an action unrelated to facial expression.

## title
Infection from the dangerous area of the face

## question
Infection from dangerous area of face may be transmitted to the following venous sinus:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Superior petrosal.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The superior petrosal sinus is not the sinus classically linked to facial infection spread; it drains the cavernous sinus toward the transverse sinus, further along the venous pathway.

## answer_b
Sigmoid

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The sigmoid sinus continues as the internal jugular vein and is not the classic destination for dangerous-area facial infection.

## answer_c
Inferior sagittal.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The inferior sagittal sinus runs along the free margin of the falx cerebri and has no direct connection to facial venous drainage.

## answer_d
Superior sagittal.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The superior sagittal sinus runs along the vault of the skull and has no direct connection to facial venous drainage.

## answer_e
Cavernous.

## explanation_e
Correct. The "dangerous area" of the face is the triangular region around the nose and upper lip, between the two anterior facial veins, so named because it drains via valveless veins, the facial vein to the deep facial vein to the pterygoid venous plexus to emissary veins, and via the ophthalmic veins, directly into the cavernous sinus. Because these facial veins lack valves, infection (for example from a squeezed nasal or upper-lip pimple) can spread backward into the cavernous sinus, causing potentially fatal cavernous sinus thrombosis. This is the classic clinical reason facial infections in this triangle are treated with particular caution.

## title
Not true regarding buccinator

## question
Which of the following is NOT true regarding the buccinator?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
It is a muscle of mastication.

## explanation_a
Correct. Buccinator is, despite its role in chewing, classified as a muscle of facial expression, not a muscle of mastication: it develops from the second pharyngeal arch and is supplied by the buccal branch of the facial nerve (CN VII), unlike the true muscles of mastication (temporalis, masseter, medial and lateral pterygoids), which develop from the first arch and are supplied by the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve (V3). The statement that it "is a muscle of mastication" is therefore the false one among these options. All of buccinator's listed actions (pressing the cheek against the molars, expelling air, drawing the mouth to one side) are true.

## answer_b
It is innervated by the facial nerve.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This statement is true: buccinator is innervated by the buccal branch of the facial nerve, consistent with its facial-expression classification.

## answer_c
It presses the cheek against the molar teeth to assist in chewing.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This statement is true: buccinator presses the cheek against the molar teeth, keeping food between the teeth during chewing and preventing it from pooling in the vestibule of the mouth.

## answer_d
It expels air from the oral cavity.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This statement is true: buccinator can expel air forcibly from the oral cavity, the action used in playing wind instruments (sometimes nicknamed the "trumpeter's muscle").

## answer_e
It draws the mouth to one side when acting unilaterally.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. This statement is true: unilateral buccinator contraction draws the angle of the mouth to that side.

QST-MANSAEP-AEPSCALP-Q04

## title
Plane of movement of the scalp

## question
Plane of Movement of the scalp is between:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Skin and epicranial aponeurosis.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The skin is firmly bound to the epicranial aponeurosis by the dense connective tissue layer between them, so there is no free plane of movement here; the two move together as a unit.

## answer_b
Epicranial aponeurosis (galea aponeurotica) and pericranium.

## explanation_b
Correct. The scalp's five layers (Skin, dense subcutaneous Connective tissue, epicranial Aponeurosis, Loose areolar tissue, Pericranium) move as two functional units: the outer three layers (skin, connective tissue, aponeurosis) are firmly bound together and glide as a single unit over the pericranium, with the fourth layer, loose areolar tissue, acting as the plane of movement between the aponeurosis above and the pericranium below. This is the same loose areolar layer classically described as the "danger area" of the scalp, since it also allows blood or infection to spread widely. The scalp's mobility, for example when wrinkling the forehead, occurs through this aponeurosis-to-pericranium gliding plane.

## answer_c
Skin and subcutaneous fat.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. There is no free gliding plane between the skin and its own subcutaneous fat; they are bound together as part of the same outer scalp unit.

## answer_d
Pericranium and skull.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The pericranium is the periosteum of the skull and is directly applied to bone; there is no significant gliding movement between them under normal circumstances.

## answer_e
No movement of the scalp.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The scalp does move, most obviously when the underlying occipitofrontalis muscle contracts to wrinkle the forehead or move the scalp backward.

## title
Nerve supply behind the auricle

## question
The scalp is:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Supplied by the great auricular nerve in front of the auricle.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The great auricular nerve, a cutaneous branch of the cervical plexus (C2-C3), supplies the skin over the parotid gland, the angle of the mandible and the auricle itself, not the scalp in front of the auricle.

## answer_b
Supplied by the supratrochlear and supraobital arteries branches of the facial artery.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The supratrochlear and supraorbital arteries are branches of the ophthalmic artery (via the internal carotid), not the facial artery.

## answer_c
Supplied posteriorly by the greater occipital nerve from the ventral ramus of C2.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The greater occipital nerve does supply the posterior scalp, but it is the dorsal (posterior), not ventral, ramus of the second cervical spinal nerve (C2) that gives rise to it.

## answer_d
Supplied behind the auricle by the lesser occipital nerve from the ventral ramus of C2.

## explanation_d
Correct. The lesser occipital nerve, a cutaneous branch of the cervical plexus arising from the ventral ramus of C2 (with a C3 contribution), ascends behind the auricle to supply the scalp in this region, complementing the greater occipital nerve (posterior scalp, from the dorsal ramus of C2) and the great auricular nerve (over the auricle and angle of mandible, also ventral rami of C2-C3). This anterior/posterior, ventral/dorsal ramus distribution is a common source of confusion and a frequent exam point.

## answer_e
Drained by supratrochlear and supraorbital veins, which unite to form the superior ophthalmic vein.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The supratrochlear and supraorbital veins unite at the medial angle of the eye to form the (anterior) facial vein, not the superior ophthalmic vein; the superior ophthalmic vein is instead the route by which the facial vein connects onward to the cavernous sinus.

# Item

## id
QST-MANSAEP-AEPSCALP-Q06

## title
Emissary veins and infection spread from the scalp

## question
Choose one correct statement regarding the scalp:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Its wounds gape if superficial to aponeurosis.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Scalp wounds gape when they cut through the epicranial aponeurosis (deep to it, not merely superficial to it), because the aponeurosis's elastic fibres retract the wound edges once it is divided.

## answer_b
Its blood vessels and nerves lie in the loose areolar connective tissue layer.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The scalp's blood vessels and cutaneous nerves run in the second (dense connective tissue) layer, not the fourth (loose areolar) layer.

## answer_c
Its wounds do not bleed easily.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Scalp wounds bleed profusely, not sparingly, because vessels in the dense connective tissue layer are tethered open by fibrous septa and cannot easily retract or constrict.

## answer_d
Bleeding beneath the periosteum usually extends under its whole extent.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Subperiosteal (subpericranial) bleeding is limited by the periosteum's firm attachment at the suture lines, so it stays confined to the boundary of one skull bone, unlike bleeding in the loose areolar layer, which can spread freely across the whole scalp.

## answer_e
Its infections can spread through emissary veins to cranial cavity.

## explanation_e
Correct. Emissary veins are valveless veins that pass through small skull foramina (including the parietal foramen and, further afield, the foramen ovale and foramen lacerum), connecting the scalp's extracranial veins directly to the intracranial dural venous sinuses. Because they are valveless, infection in the scalp can travel along them inward to the cranial cavity, producing intracranial complications such as sinus thrombosis or meningitis. This is the same valveless-vein mechanism, applied to the scalp rather than the face, as the dangerous-area-of-the-face fact tested elsewhere in this lecture set.

## question
Which nerve supplies the vertex of the scalp:

## subject

## status

Greater occipital.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The greater occipital nerve supplies the posterior scalp, behind the vertex, not the vertex itself.

Incorrect. The third occipital nerve supplies a small area near the nape of the neck, well posterior to the vertex.

## answer_c
Auriculo-temporal.

Supratrochlear.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The supratrochlear nerve supplies a more medial strip of the forehead and anterior scalp, close to the midline, rather than specifically the vertex.

Nerve supply of orbicularis oculi

## question
The orbicularis oculi is innervated by the:

## subject

## id
QST-MANSAEP-AEPSCALP-Q09

## title
Cause of gaping in facial wounds

## question
The most important factor responsible for gaping of the facial wounds is the …….

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
thick and elastic nature of the skin.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Facial skin is thin, not thick, and its elasticity is not the main factor behind wound gaping; the muscle attachment pattern is.

## answer_b
cutaneous attachment of the facial muscles.

## explanation_b
Correct. Unlike muscles elsewhere in the body, which typically attach to bone at both ends, the muscles of facial expression are unusual in taking origin from bone or fascia but inserting directly into the skin (cutaneous insertion). ## title
Facial nerve palsy vignette

## question
Patient is unable to close his right eye with dropping of the right angle of his mouth, which nerve is affected in that case?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Ophthalmic nerve.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The ophthalmic nerve is a purely sensory branch of the trigeminal nerve; its lesion would cause sensory loss over the forehead and eye, not weakness of eye closure or the mouth.

## answer_b
Maxillary nerve.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The maxillary nerve is a purely sensory branch of the trigeminal nerve, supplying the mid-face; its lesion would not cause motor weakness of the eyelid or mouth.

## answer_c
Great auricular nerve.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The great auricular nerve is a purely sensory cervical plexus branch supplying skin over the parotid and mandible angle; its lesion would not cause the described motor weakness.

## answer_d
Mandibular nerve.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The mandibular nerve carries both sensory fibres (to the lower face) and motor fibres to the muscles of mastication, not to orbicularis oculi or the muscles around the mouth.

## answer_e
Facial nerve.

## explanation_e
Correct. Orbicularis oculi (closing the eye) and the muscles around the mouth, including orbicularis oris and the muscles pulling up the angle of the mouth, are all muscles of facial expression supplied by the facial nerve (CN VII). A facial nerve lesion on one side therefore produces exactly this combination: inability to close the eye on the affected side (loss of orbicularis oculi function) together with drooping of the same-side angle of the mouth (loss of the muscles supporting it), the classic presentation of a lower motor neuron facial nerve palsy such as Bell's palsy.

## title
Origin of the supraorbital artery

## question
Supraorbital artery is a branch of which of the following arteries?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Superficial temporal artery

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The superficial temporal artery is a separate terminal branch of the external carotid, unrelated to the supraorbital artery's own origin.

## answer_b
Maxillary artery

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The maxillary artery is the other terminal branch of the external carotid, likewise unrelated to the supraorbital artery's origin.

## answer_c
Facial artery

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The facial artery is another external carotid branch supplying the superficial face, not the parent vessel of the supraorbital artery.

## answer_d
Transverse facial artery

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The transverse facial artery is a small branch of the superficial temporal artery, unrelated to the supraorbital artery's origin.

## answer_e
Ophthalmic artery

## explanation_e
Correct. The supraorbital artery is a branch of the ophthalmic artery, itself a branch of the internal carotid artery, arising within the orbit before passing through the supraorbital notch or foramen to supply the forehead and anterior scalp. This is the arterial counterpart of the supraorbital nerve's own path and territory, and it illustrates the general anastomosis between the internal carotid's ophthalmic branches and the external carotid's facial/superficial temporal branches over the forehead and scalp.

## title
Anterior boundary of the scalp

## question
Which one of the following is the anterior boundary of the scalp?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
The hair line

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The hairline is a variable, individual feature that does not define the scalp's fixed anatomical boundary.

## answer_b
The root of the nose

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The root of the nose lies well below and medial to the scalp's own anterior boundary.

## answer_c
The eyebrows

## explanation_c
Correct. The scalp is conventionally defined as extending from the eyebrows anteriorly to the superior nuchal line posteriorly, with the superior temporal line marking its lateral extent on each side. The eyebrows therefore mark the fixed anterior boundary used in anatomical description, regardless of an individual's actual hairline, which lies further back and is not itself the boundary used to define the scalp as an anatomical region.

## answer_d
The upper eye lids

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The upper eyelids lie below the eyebrows, well anterior to and outside the scalp's own boundary.

## answer_e
The highest nuchal line

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The (superior) nuchal line marks the scalp's posterior, not anterior, boundary.

## title
Cervical branch of the facial nerve and platysma

## question
Which of the following muscles in supplied by the cervical branch of the facial nerve?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Stylohyoid muscle

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Stylohyoid is supplied by a separate branch of the facial nerve arising within the parotid gland (before it divides into its five terminal branches), not the cervical branch.

## answer_b
Platysma

## explanation_b
Correct. The facial nerve's five conventional terminal branches, temporal, zygomatic, buccal, mandibular and cervical, fan out from the parotid gland in that order from top to bottom. The most inferior of these, the cervical branch, descends into the neck to supply platysma, the broad, thin sheet of facial-expression muscle that lies in the superficial fascia of the neck. This placement is consistent with platysma's own status as a facial-expression (second-arch, facial-nerve) muscle despite lying outside the face proper.

## answer_c
Orbicularis oris

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Orbicularis oris is supplied by the buccal and mandibular branches of the facial nerve, not the cervical branch.

## answer_d
Orbicularis occuli

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Orbicularis oculi is supplied by the temporal and zygomatic branches of the facial nerve, not the cervical branch.

## title
Palpebral branch of the infraorbital nerve

## question
The palpebral branch of the infraorbital nerve supplies the …….

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
upper lid.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The upper lid is supplied by branches of the ophthalmic division (supraorbital, supratrochlear, infratrochlear, lacrimal nerves), not the infraorbital nerve.

## answer_b
lower lid.

## explanation_b
Correct. The infraorbital nerve, the continuation of the maxillary division of the trigeminal nerve (V2) after it passes through the infraorbital foramen, gives off palpebral, nasal and superior labial branches as it fans out over the mid-face. Its palpebral branch supplies the lower lid, complementing its nasal branch (side of the nose) and superior labial branch (upper lip). This fan-out pattern is why a single nerve, the infraorbital, supplies three adjacent but distinct regions.

## answer_c
upper lip.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The upper lip is supplied by the infraorbital nerve's superior labial branch, a separate branch from the palpebral branch.

## answer_d
lower lip.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The lower lip is supplied by the mental nerve, a branch of the mandibular division (V3), not the infraorbital nerve.

## answer_e
side of the nose

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The side of the nose is supplied by the infraorbital nerve's nasal branch, a separate branch from the palpebral branch.
