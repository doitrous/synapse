## title
Non-narcotic agent inhibiting COX mainly in the CNS

## question
Which one of the following non-narcotic agents inhibits mainly cyclooxygenase (COX) in CNS?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Morphine

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Morphine is a narcotic (opioid) analgesic, not a non-narcotic agent, and it acts through opioid receptors rather than COX inhibition. It does not fit either the drug class or the mechanism named in the question. It is excluded from consideration on the class criterion alone.

## answer_b
Paracetamol

## explanation_b
Correct. Paracetamol inhibits cyclooxygenase predominantly within the central nervous system, giving useful analgesic and antipyretic effects there. It is a weak, largely ineffective COX inhibitor in peripheral tissue, unlike aspirin or ibuprofen, which act substantially in the periphery. This central-predominant mechanism is exactly what the question is asking for.

## answer_c
Ketorolac

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Ketorolac is a potent peripherally-acting NSAID, inhibiting COX broadly in peripheral tissue rather than showing the central-predominant pattern paracetamol shows. It is a non-narcotic agent, but its mechanism does not match 'mainly in the CNS.'

## answer_d
Acetylsalicylic acid

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Acetylsalicylic acid (aspirin) inhibits COX broadly, including strongly in peripheral tissue (which is why it has meaningful anti-inflammatory and antiplatelet effects). Its action is not predominantly central, unlike paracetamol's.

## answer_e
Ibuprofen

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Ibuprofen is a non-selective NSAID acting substantially in peripheral tissue, giving it real anti-inflammatory effect. Like aspirin and ketorolac, its COX inhibition is not centrally predominant, so it does not match the mechanism described.

## title
Aspirin use in children with viral illness

## question
Use of aspirin in children with viral disease is associated with:

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Metabolic acidosis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Metabolic acidosis can occur in severe salicylate overdose generally, but it is not the specific, named association tested here between aspirin and viral illness in children. That specific association has its own name and syndrome.

## answer_b
Reye's syndrome

## explanation_b
Correct. Reye's syndrome, a rare but potentially fatal combination of acute encephalopathy and hepatic failure, is epidemiologically linked to aspirin (or other salicylate) use during a viral illness in children and adolescents, classically influenza or varicella. This association is exactly why aspirin is avoided in children with a suspected viral infection, with paracetamol or ibuprofen used instead.

## answer_c
Renal tubular acidosis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Renal tubular acidosis is not the classic paediatric aspirin-viral-illness association; it is a distinct, unrelated group of kidney disorders. It does not match the specific syndrome this question is testing.

## answer_d
Fixed drug eruption

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Fixed drug eruption is a dermatologic hypersensitivity reaction that can occur with various drugs, but it is not the specific complication linked to aspirin use during viral illness in children. That link is Reye's syndrome specifically.

## answer_e
Ototoxicity

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Ototoxicity (tinnitus, hearing changes) is a recognised effect of high-dose or chronic salicylate use generally, but it is not the specific viral-illness-triggered paediatric association this question describes. It is a separate, dose-related salicylate effect.

## title
Aspirin's undesirable effects — the exception

## question
All of the following are undesirable effects of aspirin EXCEPT:

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Gastritis with focal erosions

## explanation_a
Incorrect (i.e. this IS a genuine undesirable effect). Gastritis with focal mucosal erosions is a real aspirin adverse effect, from local irritation and systemic COX-1 inhibition reducing protective gastric prostaglandins. It is one of aspirin's most commonly cited adverse effects, so it is not the exception.

## answer_b
Tolerance and physical addiction

## explanation_b
Correct — this is the exception. Tolerance and physical addiction are hallmark risks of opioid (narcotic) analgesics, not of aspirin or other NSAIDs. Aspirin's mechanism (COX inhibition) does not produce the neuroadaptive changes that underlie tolerance and dependence, so listing this as an aspirin adverse effect is incorrect — students often over-generalise addiction risk from opioids to all analgesics, which is exactly the trap this question tests.

## answer_c
Bleeding due to a decrease of platelet aggregation

## explanation_c
Incorrect (i.e. this IS a genuine undesirable effect). Bleeding from decreased platelet aggregation is a real, well-documented aspirin adverse effect, caused by irreversible platelet COX-1 inhibition. It is a genuine, clinically important effect, not the exception being asked for.

## answer_d
Reversible renal insufficiency

## explanation_d
Incorrect (i.e. this IS a genuine undesirable effect). Reversible renal insufficiency is a real adverse effect in susceptible patients, from reduced renal prostaglandin-mediated blood flow. It is a recognised aspirin effect, not the exception here.

## answer_e
Rye syndrome

## explanation_e
Incorrect (i.e. this IS a genuine undesirable effect). Reye's syndrome is a real, if rare, aspirin-associated complication in children with viral illness. It is a genuine adverse association, so it is not the exception this EXCEPT question is looking for.

## title
Property combination peculiar to most NSAIDs

## question
Which of the following property combinations is peculiar to the majority of NSAIDs?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Antihistaminic, antipyretic, analgesic

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Antihistaminic activity is not a shared NSAID property; it belongs to a completely different drug class (antihistamines) with a different receptor mechanism. Combining it with two genuine NSAID properties does not make this option correct as a whole.

## answer_b
Analgesic, immunosuppressive, anti-inflammatory

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Immunosuppressive activity is not a general NSAID property; it belongs to specific DMARDs and other immunosuppressant drug classes. Most NSAIDs reduce inflammation locally through COX inhibition without producing broad immune suppression.

## answer_c
Antipyretic, analgesic, anti-inflammatory

## explanation_c
Correct. Because nearly all NSAIDs share the same core mechanism, inhibiting cyclooxygenase and reducing peripheral prostaglandin synthesis, they share this characteristic triad: antipyretic, analgesic and anti-inflammatory effects. This triad follows directly and specifically from COX inhibition, unlike the other options, which each mix in a property from an unrelated drug class.

## answer_d
Anti-inflammatory, immunosuppressive, antihistaminic

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Both immunosuppressive and antihistaminic activity are outside the general NSAID mechanism; only 'anti-inflammatory' among these three is a genuine shared NSAID property. This option mixes one correct property with two that do not belong.

## answer_e
Narcotic, analgesic, anti-inflammatory

## explanation_e
Incorrect. 'Narcotic' describes opioid analgesics, an entirely different drug class and mechanism from NSAIDs. NSAIDs are specifically non-narcotic analgesics, so pairing 'narcotic' with NSAID properties is a direct contradiction.

## title
NSAID choice sparing GI symptoms in chronic low back pain

## question
A 52-year-old man with chronic low back pain. He is complaining from severe hyperacidity. Which of the following agents may improve his pain without worsening his gastrointestinal symptoms?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Aspirin

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Aspirin is a non-selective NSAID, inhibiting both COX-1 and COX-2, which reduces protective gastric prostaglandins and worsens hyperacidity/gastric irritation. It is a poor choice for a patient with significant GI symptoms.

## answer_b
Celecoxib

## explanation_b
Correct. Celecoxib selectively inhibits COX-2 while relatively sparing COX-1, the isoform that maintains protective gastric mucus and bicarbonate secretion. This selectivity gives comparable analgesic benefit to non-selective NSAIDs with less gastric mucosal damage, making it the preferred choice for a patient who needs NSAID-class therapy but has significant GI risk such as this patient's hyperacidity.

## answer_c
Ketorolac

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Ketorolac is a potent non-selective NSAID with substantial COX-1 inhibition, and it carries a particularly high risk of GI adverse effects among NSAIDs. It would be expected to worsen, not spare, this patient's hyperacidity.

## answer_d
Diclofenac

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Diclofenac is a non-selective NSAID, inhibiting both COX isoforms and carrying meaningful GI risk, similar to aspirin. It does not offer the GI-sparing profile this patient needs.

## answer_e
Indomethacin

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Indomethacin is a potent non-selective NSAID with a relatively high rate of GI adverse effects, among the more GI-irritating NSAIDs available. It is not an appropriate choice for a patient with significant hyperacidity.

## title
Most common adverse effect of methotrexate

## question
The most common adverse effect of methotrexate is:

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Arrhythmia

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Arrhythmia is not a characteristic methotrexate adverse effect. Methotrexate's toxicity profile centres instead on organs and tissues with high cell turnover or metabolic load — liver, bone marrow, gut lining. Cardiac conduction is not among its typical targets.

## answer_b
Hepatotoxicity

## explanation_b
Correct. Methotrexate inhibits dihydrofolate reductase, and at the doses used as a DMARD in rheumatoid arthritis, hepatotoxicity is its most common adverse effect. This shows up as elevated liver enzymes, and with cumulative use, a risk of fibrosis. It is why liver enzyme monitoring is routine during methotrexate therapy.

## answer_c
Renal failure

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Renal failure is not the most common methotrexate adverse effect. The kidneys can be affected, especially at high oncologic doses through drug crystallisation. But at the lower DMARD doses used in rheumatoid arthritis, hepatotoxicity is the more prominent and more common concern.

## answer_d
Convulsions

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Convulsions are not a typical methotrexate adverse effect at standard DMARD doses. Neurotoxicity is more a concern with high-dose intrathecal methotrexate, used in oncology. That is a very different clinical context from DMARD dosing.

## answer_e
Hair loss

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Hair loss can occur with methotrexate, particularly at higher doses. It is not, however, the most common adverse effect at DMARD doses. Hepatotoxicity is both more prominent and more frequently monitored for.

## title
Agent reversing methotrexate toxicity

## question
Which of following agents is used to reverse toxic effects of methotrexate?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Hydrocortisone

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Hydrocortisone is a corticosteroid with anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects, but it does not reverse methotrexate's underlying folate-antagonist mechanism. It treats inflammation generally, not the specific biochemical block methotrexate causes.

## answer_b
Aspirin

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Aspirin has no role in reversing methotrexate toxicity. In fact, aspirin can increase methotrexate levels by displacing it from protein binding. This worsens, rather than reverses, toxicity risk.

## answer_c
Hydroxychloroquine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Hydroxychloroquine is itself a separate DMARD with an unrelated antimalarial-derived mechanism. It plays no role in rescuing a cell from methotrexate's folate-antagonist toxicity.

## answer_d
Leucovorin

## explanation_d
Correct. Leucovorin (folinic acid, also called citrovorum factor) is a reduced folate that bypasses the dihydrofolate reductase block methotrexate causes, directly restocking the cell's usable folate pool. This rescues normal cells from methotrexate's antimetabolite effect and is used both prophylactically with high-dose regimens and to treat overt toxicity.

## answer_e
Papaverine

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Papaverine is a smooth-muscle relaxant/vasodilator with no relationship to folate metabolism or methotrexate's mechanism. It has no role in reversing methotrexate toxicity.

## title
Leflunomide's immunosuppressant mechanism

## question
Which of the following sentences best explain immunosuppressant effect of leflunomide?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Inhibits phospholipase A2

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Phospholipase A2 inhibition is more characteristic of corticosteroid mechanisms (via induced lipocortin), not leflunomide. Leflunomide's mechanism operates further downstream, in nucleotide synthesis, not at the phospholipase step.

## answer_b
Inhibits binding of TNF alpha to its receptor

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Inhibiting TNF-alpha binding describes biologic DMARDs instead. Etanercept works as a TNF receptor decoy, and infliximab/adalimumab are anti-TNF antibodies. Neither mechanism matches leflunomide's small-molecule pyrimidine-synthesis block.

## answer_c
Inhibits topoisomerase enzyme

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Topoisomerase inhibition is a mechanism associated with certain chemotherapy and antibiotic drug classes. It is not leflunomide's mechanism. It is unrelated to leflunomide's actual pyrimidine-synthesis-blocking action.

## answer_d
Inhibits calcineurin activity

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Calcineurin inhibition describes cyclosporine and tacrolimus instead. Their mechanism blocks IL-2 gene transcription, a different immunosuppressant pathway. This is distinct from leflunomide's pyrimidine-synthesis block.

## answer_e
Inhibits pyrimidine base synthesis and suppresses T cell and B cell proliferation and activation

## explanation_e
Correct. Leflunomide's active metabolite inhibits dihydroorotate dehydrogenase, blocking de novo pyrimidine synthesis. Rapidly proliferating activated T and B lymphocytes depend heavily on this de novo pathway rather than the salvage pathway most resting cells use, so blocking it selectively suppresses their proliferation and activation, the basis for leflunomide's disease-modifying effect in rheumatoid arthritis.

Sulfasalazine's mechanism as a prodrug

## question
Which of the following statements is correct about sulfasalazine?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
It is a calcineurin inhibitor

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Calcineurin inhibition describes cyclosporine and tacrolimus instead. This is a completely different mechanism class from sulfasalazine's. Sulfasalazine's own mechanism is a bacterial-cleavage prodrug design, not receptor-level immunosuppression.

## answer_b
It is a biological DMARD

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Sulfasalazine is a small-molecule, orally-administered drug, not a biologic (antibody or receptor-fusion protein) DMARD such as etanercept or rituximab. It belongs to a different generation and manufacturing category of DMARD entirely.

## answer_c
A prodrug cleaved by gut bacteria into 5-aminosalicylic acid and sulphapyridine

## explanation_c
Correct. Sulfasalazine itself is largely inactive when swallowed and reaches the colon relatively unabsorbed, where bacterial azoreductase enzymes split it into 5-aminosalicylic acid (with local anti-inflammatory effects) and sulphapyridine (well absorbed, thought to carry most of the systemic, disease-modifying effect in rheumatoid arthritis). This bacterial-cleavage prodrug design is sulfasalazine's defining pharmacological feature.

## answer_d
It is a folate antagonist

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Folate antagonism describes methotrexate's mechanism, not sulfasalazine's. Sulfasalazine's mechanism is the prodrug-cleavage pathway described in option C, an entirely different biochemical route.

## answer_e
Its main side effect is retinopathy

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Retinopathy is the characteristic adverse effect of hydroxychloroquine, not sulfasalazine. Sulfasalazine's more characteristic adverse effects relate instead to its sulpha component (GI upset, rash, and rare blood dyscrasias).

## title
Rituximab's mechanism

## question
Which of the following statements is correct about rituximab?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
It is a chimeric antibody directed against CD20

## explanation_a
Correct. Rituximab is a chimeric monoclonal antibody, part mouse-derived and part human-derived, directed against CD20, a surface marker on B lymphocytes from the pre-B stage through to mature B cells. Binding CD20 triggers B-cell depletion through complement- and antibody-dependent cytotoxicity, the basis for its use as a biologic DMARD in rheumatoid arthritis refractory to other agents.

## answer_b
It inhibits IL-1

## explanation_b
Incorrect. IL-1 inhibition describes anakinra's mechanism, not rituximab's. Rituximab's target is a B-cell surface marker (CD20), a structurally and functionally different kind of target from a soluble cytokine like IL-1.

## answer_c
It is a humanized antibody of IL-6 receptors

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A humanised antibody against the IL-6 receptor describes tocilizumab, not rituximab. Rituximab is chimeric, not humanised, and its target is CD20, not the IL-6 receptor.

## answer_d
It inhibits TNFα

## explanation_d
Incorrect. TNF-alpha inhibition describes infliximab, adalimumab or etanercept, not rituximab. These are a distinct family of biologic DMARDs targeting a different cytokine pathway from B-cell depletion.

## answer_e
It is a CD80/86 inhibitor

## explanation_e
Incorrect. CD80/86 inhibition describes abatacept's mechanism (blocking T-cell costimulation), not rituximab's. Abatacept targets T-cell activation, while rituximab targets B cells directly via CD20.
