## title
Location of the NMJ's junctional folds

## question
Junctional folds of NMJ (neuro-muscular junction):

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Are present at the axonal terminal

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The axonal terminal (presynaptic side) holds the acetylcholine vesicles, not the junctional folds, which are a postsynaptic membrane feature.

## answer_b
Are present at motor end plate

## explanation_b
Correct. The postsynaptic muscle membrane at the neuromuscular junction is thickened and thrown into junctional folds, forming the motor end plate, which is rich in acetylcholine receptors. This structural feature is what allows the small motor end plate area to hold enough acetylcholine receptors to reliably trigger a muscle action potential.

## answer_c
Contains vesicles of acetylcholine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Acetylcholine vesicles are held in the presynaptic axon terminal, not in the junctional folds themselves.

## answer_d
Contain catechol-o-methyl transferase (COMT) enzyme

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Acetylcholine is broken down by acetylcholinesterase in the synaptic cleft, not by COMT, which degrades catecholamines elsewhere in the body.

## answer_e
Decrease the surface area of postsynaptic membrane

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Junctional folds increase, not decrease, the postsynaptic surface area, packing in more acetylcholine receptors than a flat membrane could hold.

Incorrect. All the motor nerve fibres supplying a whole muscle make up many motor units together, not one motor unit.

Correct. Incorrect. This describes an entire pool of motor neurons across several muscles, far broader than the definition of a single motor unit.

Nerve fibre type supplying skeletal muscle

## question
What is the type of nerve fibers supplying skeletal muscle?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
A alpha

## explanation_a
Correct. Skeletal muscle is supplied by A-alpha fibres, the largest, most heavily myelinated and fastest-conducting class of peripheral nerve fibre, carrying the efferent output of alpha motor neurons to extrafusal muscle fibres. This large-diameter, fast-conducting design ensures that all the muscle fibres of a motor unit are activated together, essentially simultaneously.

A beta

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A-beta fibres are smaller than A-alpha and mainly carry touch/pressure sensory information, not motor output to skeletal muscle.

## answer_c
A delta

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A-delta fibres are thinly myelinated and carry fast (sharp) pain and temperature sensation, not motor output.

## answer_d
B

## explanation_d
Incorrect. B fibres are lightly myelinated preganglionic autonomic fibres, unrelated to skeletal muscle motor supply.

## answer_e
C

## explanation_e
Incorrect. C fibres are unmyelinated, the slowest class, carrying dull/burning pain and postganglionic autonomic signals, not skeletal muscle motor output.

## question

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Anti-voltage-gated potassium channel antibody causes neuromyotonia or limbic encephalitis, not this myasthenic-with-SCLC picture.

## title
Mechanism of synaptic delay at the NMJ

## question
Synaptic delay is caused by:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Formation of Ach

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Acetylcholine is already synthesised and stored in vesicles well before the nerve impulse arrives; its formation is not the rate-limiting step causing the delay.

## answer_b
Packing of Ach into vesicles.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Packing acetylcholine into vesicles also happens continuously beforehand, not as the impulse-triggered step that produces the measured delay.

## answer_c
Breakdown of Ach

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Breakdown of acetylcholine by acetylcholinesterase happens after transmission, terminating the signal, not causing the initial delay.

## answer_d
Ach diffusion across the synaptic cleft.

## explanation_d
Correct. The synaptic delay is the time needed for the sequence of release and diffusion: calcium-triggered vesicle rupture, acetylcholine diffusion across the synaptic cleft, and receptor binding — with diffusion across the cleft being the physically rate-limiting step in this brief sequence. Because this whole sequence takes only a fraction of a millisecond, the synaptic delay at a chemical synapse like this one is still far longer than the near-instantaneous conduction along a nerve fibre.

## answer_e
Reuptake of choli

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Reuptake of choline (after acetylcholinesterase breaks acetylcholine down) is a recycling step following transmission, not the cause of the initial delay.

## title
Channel mediating presynaptic calcium influx

## question
At the nerve terminal which channel mediates Ca Influx in the axon terminals:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
ligand gated ion channels

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Ligand-gated channels open in response to a bound chemical messenger (like acetylcholine on the postsynaptic side), not to the presynaptic terminal's own depolarisation.

## answer_b
mechanically activated ion channel

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Mechanically activated channels open in response to physical deformation (as in touch receptors), not to the nerve terminal's depolarisation.

## answer_c
leak ion channel

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Leak channels are always open at a low level, independent of the arriving action potential, and do not mediate the impulse-triggered calcium surge.

## answer_d
voltage gated ion channel

## explanation_d
Correct. The arriving nerve impulse depolarises the presynaptic terminal, opening voltage-gated calcium channels; the resulting calcium influx triggers acetylcholine vesicle rupture and exocytosis into the synaptic cleft. This calcium-triggered exocytosis is the presynaptic half of the neuromuscular transmission sequence, immediately followed by acetylcholine diffusing across the cleft to the postsynaptic receptors.

## answer_e
Ca penetrates the cell membrane directly

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Calcium does not cross the lipid membrane directly; it requires a specific channel protein, which here is voltage-gated.

## title
Cause of synaptic fatigue at the NMJ

## question
Which of the followings causes synaptic fatigue of neuromuscular transmission?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Depletion of acetylcholine

## explanation_a
Correct. Neuromuscular transmission fatigues easily with repeated, rapid stimulation because the readily-releasable pool of acetylcholine-filled vesicles is exhausted faster than it can be replenished, reducing the amount of transmitter released with each successive impulse. This is why very rapid, sustained repetitive nerve stimulation in an experimental or clinical setting can produce a progressively weaker muscle response.

## answer_b
Depletion of Ca+2 ions

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Extracellular calcium is not depleted by repeated stimulation under normal conditions; it is the vesicle/transmitter supply that runs down, not the calcium available to trigger release.

## answer_c
Depletion of choline acetyl transferase enzyme

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Choline acetyltransferase is an enzyme, present in a catalytic (not consumed) role; its depletion is not the mechanism of synaptic fatigue.

## answer_d
Depletion of cholinergic receptors

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Postsynaptic receptors are not consumed or depleted by ordinary repeated transmission; the limiting factor is presynaptic transmitter supply.

## answer_e
Depletion of cholinesterase enzyme

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Depleting the enzyme that breaks acetylcholine down would prolong, not fatigue, transmission, by letting acetylcholine accumulate.

## title
Diagnosis in a vignette of autonomic symptoms with reflexes returning after exercise

## question
A 50-year-old man presents with dry mouth, constipation, blurred vision, and proximal muscle weakness. His deep tendon reflexes are absent but return briefly after exercise. He has a 40-pack-year smoking history. The most likely diagnosis is:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Myasthenia gravis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Myasthenia gravis causes fatiguable weakness that worsens with exercise, without the autonomic features (dry mouth, constipation, blurred vision) or the post-exercise reflex facilitation described here.

## answer_b
Lambert-Eaton syndrome

## explanation_b
Correct. Autonomic symptoms plus proximal weakness plus depressed reflexes that transiently return after exercise, in a heavy smoker (small cell lung cancer risk), is the classic Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome picture — autoantibodies against presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channels reduce acetylcholine release, but a brief burst of activity lets residual presynaptic calcium accumulate and transiently improve transmission and reflexes. The autonomic features here (dry mouth, constipation, blurred vision) are what point specifically toward Lambert-Eaton rather than toward myasthenia gravis, which does not typically produce autonomic symptoms.

## answer_c
Botulism

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Botulism causes descending paralysis with prominent bulbar/autonomic signs from toxin-mediated blockade of acetylcholine release, but it is an acute toxin exposure, not a chronic smoking-associated paraneoplastic picture with post-exercise reflex facilitation.

## answer_d
Polymyositis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Polymyositis is a primary inflammatory muscle disease causing proximal weakness, but it does not produce autonomic symptoms or the post-exercise reflex-facilitation pattern.

## answer_e
Guillain-Barré syndrome

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Guillain-Barré syndrome is an acute ascending demyelinating polyneuropathy, not associated with smoking, malignancy, or reflexes that transiently improve with exercise.

## title
Cause of miniature end-plate potentials

## question
Miniature end plate potentials (MEPPs) are caused by:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Spontaneous action potentials in the motor nerve

## explanation_a
Incorrect. MEPPs occur at rest, without any nerve impulse or action potential arriving at all; that is exactly what makes them "miniature" and spontaneous.

## answer_b
Release of a single quantum (vesicle) of acetylcholine

## explanation_b
Correct. At rest, a few acetylcholine-containing vesicles rupture spontaneously and release their contents even without a nerve impulse, producing a minute depolarisation at the motor end plate — the same quantal event that, multiplied many times over by an arriving action potential, produces the full end-plate potential. MEPPs are far too small to trigger a muscle action potential on their own, but they demonstrate that neurotransmitter release is fundamentally quantal, one vesicle at a time.

## answer_c
Large release of ACh during normal transmission

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A large, impulse-triggered release of acetylcholine produces the full end-plate potential, not the miniature one, which reflects only a single spontaneous vesicle.

## answer_d
Activation of voltage-gated sodium channels

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Voltage-gated sodium channel activation belongs to action potential propagation along the muscle fibre, a later step than the MEPP, which is a purely local, subthreshold event.

## answer_e
Influx of calcium into the muscle fiber

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Calcium influx into the presynaptic terminal (not the muscle fibre) triggers impulse-evoked release; the spontaneous single-vesicle rupture behind a MEPP does not require this triggered calcium entry.
