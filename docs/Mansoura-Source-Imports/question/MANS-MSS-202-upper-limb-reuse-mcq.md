# Item

## id
QST-MANSMSS202-UPPER-LIMB-REUSE-UL-R1

## title
Winging of the scapula after mastectomy

## question
A 42-year-old woman underwent a left modified radical mastectomy with axillary lymph node dissection for breast cancer. Two weeks postoperatively, she complains of difficulty lifting objects with her left arm and shoulder weakness. On examination, she has asymmetric prominence (winging) of the medial border of her left scapula when pushing against a wall. Which nerve was most likely injured?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Long thoracic nerve

## explanation_a
Correct. The long thoracic nerve runs on the surface of serratus anterior along the medial wall of the axilla, exactly where it is exposed during axillary lymph node dissection. Its injury paralyses serratus anterior, which normally rotates the scapula upward and holds its medial border against the chest wall, so the medial border stands off as a winged scapula, most visible when the patient pushes against a wall.

## answer_b
Nerve to latissimus dorsi

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The nerve to latissimus dorsi is the thoracodorsal nerve, which runs more posteriorly in the axilla. If injured, it weakens arm adduction and extension rather than producing scapular winging. Its course also puts it at a different, less exposed point in an axillary dissection than the long thoracic nerve.

## answer_c
Suprascapular nerve

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The suprascapular nerve supplies supraspinatus and infraspinatus and runs through the suprascapular notch on the posterior scapula, well away from the axillary dissection field. Its injury weakens the first 15 degrees of abduction and lateral rotation, not scapular positioning. It does not touch serratus anterior at all.

## answer_d
Axillary nerve

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The axillary nerve winds around the surgical neck of the humerus, not the medial axillary wall. Its injury weakens deltoid-driven abduction and flattens the shoulder contour rather than winging the scapula. That flattened-shoulder deformity is a different physical sign from the medial-border prominence described here.

## answer_e
Radial nerve

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The radial nerve runs in the radial groove of the humerus, far from the axillary dissection field. Its injury produces wrist drop, not scapular winging. Wrist drop and winged scapula are readily distinguished on examination, since they affect completely different joints.

## title
Root values of the long thoracic nerve

## question
What are the root values of the long thoracic nerve?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
C5, C6, C7

## explanation_a
Correct. The long thoracic nerve arises directly from the anterior rami of C5, C6 and C7, before the trunks or cords of the brachial plexus form. It then runs down over the surface of serratus anterior, on the medial wall of the axilla, to supply that muscle. This early, root-level origin is exactly why the nerve is easy to mistake for a cord-level branch in exam distractors.

## answer_b
C6, C7, C8

## explanation_b
Incorrect. C6-C8 is not the long thoracic nerve's root value. Shifting the range down by one root would predict a different pattern of preserved function in a partial root lesion. The nerve's true origin sits one root higher, at C5-C7.

## answer_c
C7, C8, T1

## explanation_c
Incorrect. C7-T1 corresponds more closely to the lower trunk's territory, which is ulnar nerve-dominant. The long thoracic nerve instead arises higher and earlier, directly from C5-C7. Its root value does not overlap with the lower trunk's contribution at all.

## answer_d
C5, C6, C7, C8

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A four-root span (C5-C8) overstates the long thoracic nerve's origin. It is classically described as a three-root nerve, C5-C7 only. Adding C8 would wrongly imply the lower trunk contributes to it.

## answer_e
C3 and C4

## explanation_e
Incorrect. C3-C4 are the phrenic nerve's principal root values, with a variable C5 contribution. They are not the long thoracic nerve's root value. Confusing the two nerves would predict the wrong deficit entirely — diaphragmatic paralysis instead of scapular winging.

## title
Muscles innervated by the axillary nerve

## question
Which muscles are innervated by the axillary nerve?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Supraspinatus and infraspinatus muscles

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Supraspinatus and infraspinatus are supplied by the suprascapular nerve, a separate branch that reaches the posterior scapula through the suprascapular notch. Neither muscle receives any fibres from the axillary nerve. Confusing the two nerves would predict the wrong pattern of rotator cuff weakness.

## answer_b
Infraspinatus and teres major muscles

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Infraspinatus is suprascapular-supplied, and teres major is supplied by the lower subscapular nerve. Neither is an axillary nerve target. Teres major's similar name to teres minor (the true axillary-supplied muscle) is exactly what makes this pairing a common distractor.

## answer_c
Pectoralis major and pectoralis minor muscles

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Pectoralis major and minor are supplied by the medial and lateral pectoral nerves, which arise from the medial and lateral cords. They have no axillary nerve contribution at all. Their nerve supply lies on an entirely different branch of the plexus from deltoid and teres minor.

## answer_d
Latissimus dorsi and teres major muscles

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Latissimus dorsi is supplied by the thoracodorsal nerve and teres major by the lower subscapular nerve. Both are posterior cord branches, like the axillary nerve, but neither is the axillary nerve itself. Their actions (adduction, medial rotation) also differ from deltoid's abduction, a further clue that this pairing is wrong.

## answer_e
Deltoid and teres minor muscles

## explanation_e
Correct. The axillary nerve winds around the surgical neck of the humerus, passing through the quadrangular space. From there it supplies deltoid, the main abductor of the shoulder, and teres minor, a lateral rotator and rotator cuff muscle. These are its only two muscular targets, which is why axillary nerve injury has a narrow, predictable motor deficit.

## title
Deformity from axillary nerve lesion

## question
Lesion or injury of axillary nerve causes:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Winging of scapula

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Winging of the scapula follows long thoracic nerve injury, from loss of serratus anterior. The axillary nerve does not supply serratus anterior at all. The two nerves arise at different levels of the plexus and injure different muscles.

## answer_b
Flat shoulder

## explanation_b
Correct. Axillary nerve injury paralyses deltoid, and as deltoid wastes, the normally rounded contour of the shoulder flattens. This is the classic deformity of axillary nerve palsy. It is usually accompanied by numbness over the 'regimental badge' area of skin over the lower deltoid, the nerve's cutaneous territory.

## answer_c
Claw hand

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Claw hand follows ulnar nerve injury, from loss of the medial two lumbricals' balance between MCP flexion and IP extension. The axillary nerve has no motor supply to the hand whatsoever. The two deformities affect completely different joints.

## answer_d
Wrist drop

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Wrist drop follows radial nerve injury, from loss of the wrist and finger extensors. The radial nerve is a different terminal branch of the posterior cord from the axillary nerve. Their injuries are distinguished clinically by testing wrist extension versus shoulder abduction.

## title
Deformity from median nerve injury

## question
Which one of the following deformities is caused by the median nerve injury?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Winging of the scapula

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Winging of the scapula follows long thoracic nerve, not median nerve, injury. The two nerves have no anatomical overlap in the muscles they supply. Serratus anterior is entirely outside the median nerve's territory.

## answer_b
Flat shoulder

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A flat shoulder follows axillary nerve injury, from deltoid wasting, not median nerve injury. The median nerve does not supply deltoid or any other shoulder muscle. Its territory begins in the forearm and hand instead.

## answer_c
Waiter's tip position

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Waiter's tip position is the classic deformity of Erb's palsy, an upper trunk (C5-C6) brachial plexus injury. It is distinct from an isolated median nerve lesion, which affects only median-supplied muscles rather than the broader upper-trunk pattern. The arm position in Erb's palsy (adducted, medially rotated, extended at the elbow) also looks nothing like ape hand.

## answer_d
Ape hand deformity

## explanation_d
Correct. Median nerve injury, classically at the wrist as in carpal tunnel syndrome, wastes and flattens the thenar eminence. The thumb becomes laterally rotated and adducted with loss of opposition. This combination is the ape (monkey's) hand deformity, the median nerve's signature motor loss.

## answer_e
Wrist drop

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Wrist drop follows radial nerve injury, from loss of the wrist and finger extensors, not median nerve injury. The median nerve instead impairs flexion and thumb opposition. The two deformities are opposites in a sense — one loses extension, the other loses flexion-related function.

## title
Nerve involved in carpal tunnel syndrome

## question
In carpal tunnel syndrome the nerve involved is:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Median nerve

## explanation_a
Correct. Carpal tunnel syndrome is compression of the median nerve as it passes deep to the flexor retinaculum at the wrist. It produces sensory loss over the lateral three and a half digits. Over time it also causes thenar muscle wasting, the ape hand deformity.

## answer_b
Ulnar nerve

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The ulnar nerve passes superficial to the flexor retinaculum through Guyon's canal, a separate compression site at the wrist. It can also be compressed at the elbow, in cubital tunnel syndrome. Neither of its compression sites is the carpal tunnel itself.

## answer_c
Radial nerve

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The radial nerve does not pass through the carpal tunnel at all. It terminates more proximally in the forearm as the posterior interosseous and superficial radial branches. By the level of the wrist it has already given off its major branches.

## answer_d
Axillary nerve

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The axillary nerve is confined to the shoulder region, within the quadrangular space. It has no course anywhere near the wrist. Its injury produces shoulder, not hand, symptoms.

## title
Branch of the lateral cord

## question
Which one of the following nerves is a branch of the lateral cord of the brachial plexus?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Upper subscapular nerve

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The upper subscapular nerve is a branch of the posterior cord, not the lateral cord. It supplies part of subscapularis. Its origin places it in a different tier of the plexus from the musculocutaneous nerve.

## answer_b
Long thoracic nerve

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The long thoracic nerve arises from the roots, C5-C7, directly. It forms before any cord exists. It is therefore neither a lateral nor a posterior cord branch.

## answer_c
Musculocutaneous nerve

## explanation_c
Correct. The musculocutaneous nerve is one of the lateral cord's two terminal branches, alongside the lateral root of the median nerve. It descends lateral to the axillary artery and pierces coracobrachialis, supplying the anterior arm muscles. It then continues past the elbow as the lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm.

## answer_d
Suprascapular nerve

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The suprascapular nerve arises from the upper trunk, proximal to any cord. It is not a branch of the lateral cord. It supplies supraspinatus and infraspinatus instead, on the posterior scapula.

## answer_e
Thoracodorsal nerve

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The thoracodorsal nerve is a posterior cord branch, supplying latissimus dorsi. It is not a lateral cord branch. Its posterior-cord origin places it alongside the axillary and radial nerves, not the musculocutaneous nerve.

## title
Wrist pain after a fall on an outstretched hand

## question
A 21-year-old man goes to his college campus health clinic complaining of soreness in his left wrist after falling on an outstretched hand during a basketball game the previous day. He is supporting his left wrist, and the pain worsens with movement and is minimised with inactivity. There is no loss of feeling in his hand, nor does he have trouble grasping or holding objects. The physician exacerbates the wrist pain by applying pressure to the base of the thumb in the anatomical snuffbox. Radiographic imaging will confirm a break of which carpal bone?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Capitate

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The capitate is the largest carpal bone and sits centrally in the carpal row. It is not the floor of the anatomical snuffbox. It is also not the classic fracture in this fall-on-outstretched-hand presentation.

## answer_b
Scaphoid

## explanation_b
Correct. The scaphoid floors the anatomical snuffbox and is by far the most commonly fractured carpal bone, classically from a fall onto an outstretched, dorsiflexed hand (FOOSH). Its blood supply enters mainly through its distal pole, so a scaphoid fracture may not even show on an initial X-ray and can progress to avascular necrosis of the proximal fragment if missed — which is why snuff-box tenderness after such a fall is treated as a probable scaphoid fracture even with a normal initial film.

## answer_c
Hamate

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The hamate sits on the ulnar side of the wrist, near Guyon's canal. It can fracture from a direct blow to its hook, such as gripping a golf club or bat. It is not the snuff-box floor, so it does not match this presentation.

## answer_d
Trapezium

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The trapezium lies at the base of the thumb metacarpal but is not the floor of the anatomical snuffbox. The scaphoid, not the trapezium, is the structure directly palpated there. Tenderness on snuff-box palpation is therefore a scaphoid, not trapezium, sign.

## answer_e
Pisiform

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The pisiform is the wrist's one sesamoid bone, embedded in the tendon of flexor carpi ulnaris. It sits on the ulnar side, well away from the radial-sided anatomical snuffbox. It is not palpated by the manoeuvre described in this vignette.
