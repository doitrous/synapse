## title
What non-narcotic agents cause

## question
Non-narcotic agents cause….

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Physical dependence

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Physical dependence is a hallmark risk of narcotic (opioid) analgesics, tied to their action on opioid receptors. Non-narcotic analgesics do not touch that receptor system, so they carry no such dependence risk.

## answer_b
Euphoria

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Euphoria is an opioid-receptor-mediated reward effect. Non-narcotic analgesics, working through cyclooxygenase inhibition rather than opioid receptors, do not produce it.

## answer_c
Antipyretic effect

## explanation_c
Correct. Non-narcotic analgesics reduce central prostaglandin E2 synthesis in the hypothalamus, resetting the elevated thermoregulatory set point of fever back toward normal. This antipyretic action, together with analgesia, is a defining shared property of the group.

## answer_d
Antitussive

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Antitussive (cough-suppressant) action, like codeine's, is an opioid-receptor effect in the brainstem cough centre. Non-narcotic analgesics have no such receptor target and no antitussive action.

## answer_e
Respiratory depression

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Respiratory depression is a classic opioid-receptor-mediated effect, dangerous at high doses of narcotic analgesics. Non-narcotic analgesics do not depress respiration through this mechanism.

## title
NSAID side effect not shared with the group

## question
One of the following is not a side effect shared by NSAIDs:

## subject
pharm

## status

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Physical dependence

## explanation_a
Correct. Physical dependence is an opioid-receptor phenomenon. NSAIDs, including aspirin, do not act on opioid receptors and so do not produce tolerance or physical dependence, unlike narcotic analgesics.

## answer_b
Gastrointestinal ulceration

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Gastrointestinal ulceration is a genuine, shared NSAID side effect, from reduced protective gastric prostaglandins when cyclooxygenase-1 is inhibited. It is one of the class's best-known risks.

## answer_c
Hypersensitivity

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Hypersensitivity reactions, including NSAID-exacerbated respiratory disease, are recognised across the NSAID class. This is a real shared risk, not the exception being asked for.

## answer_d

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Nephropathy from reduced renal prostaglandin-mediated blood flow is a genuine shared NSAID risk, especially in volume-depleted or renally impaired patients. It is not the odd one out here.

## answer_e
Nausea and vomiting

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Nausea and vomiting are common, genuine NSAID side effects from local gastric irritation and systemic prostaglandin inhibition. They belong on the list of true shared effects, unlike physical dependence.

## title
Aspirin drug interaction

## question
Which drug interaction may occur when aspirin is given concomitantly with other drugs?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Potentiation of the anticoagulant effect of warfarin

## explanation_a
Correct. Aspirin potentiates warfarin's anticoagulant effect through two additive mechanisms: displacing warfarin from plasma protein binding, and independently impairing platelet aggregation via irreversible COX-1 inhibition. Together they raise bleeding risk beyond either drug alone.

## answer_b
Potentiation of the antihypertensive effect of beta-blockers

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Aspirin does not potentiate beta-blockers; if anything, NSAID-class drugs tend to blunt antihypertensive efficacy through reduced vascular prostacyclin, the opposite of potentiation. Beta-blockers are not the classic aspirin interaction being tested.

## answer_c
Potentiation of the coagulant action of vitamin K

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Vitamin K promotes coagulation, the opposite effect to what aspirin's antiplatelet action produces; aspirin does not potentiate a coagulant, pro-clotting action. This pairing misdescribes the direction of aspirin's own effect.

## answer_d
Potentiation of the diuretic effect of furosemide

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Aspirin does not potentiate furosemide's diuretic effect; NSAIDs as a class tend to blunt diuretic efficacy by inhibiting the renal prostaglandins that support natriuresis, again the opposite of potentiation.

## answer_e
Potentiation of the uricosuric effect of probenecid

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Low-dose aspirin actually antagonises uricosuric drugs like probenecid, competing for the same renal tubular transport mechanism, rather than potentiating their action. This is the opposite interaction to the one described.

## title
Aspirin's pharmacologic effect

## question
Which of the following is a pharmacologic effect of aspirin?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Anti-inflammatory, and anti-rheumatic effects

## explanation_a
Correct. At anti-inflammatory doses, aspirin's sustained cyclooxygenase inhibition gives it genuine anti-inflammatory and anti-rheumatic activity, distinct from its lower-dose antiplatelet and analgesic-antipyretic uses. This higher-dose regimen was historically used to arrest progressive joint destruction in acute rheumatic fever. It is the correct pairing of effects among the options listed here.

## answer_b
Promotion of platelet aggregation, which decrease bleeding tendency

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Aspirin inhibits, rather than promotes, platelet aggregation, by blocking thromboxane A2 synthesis, and this increases rather than decreases bleeding tendency. The option reverses both the direction of the platelet effect and its consequence.

## answer_c
Reduction of pain by stimulation of PGs synthesis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Aspirin reduces pain by inhibiting, not stimulating, prostaglandin synthesis. Prostaglandins sensitise nociceptors, so blocking their synthesis is exactly how aspirin relieves pain.

## answer_d
Central skeletal muscle relaxation

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Aspirin has no central skeletal muscle relaxant action; that effect belongs to a separate drug class (centrally acting muscle relaxants), unrelated to cyclooxygenase inhibition.

## answer_e
Reduction of blood pressure by diuretic effects

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Aspirin is not a diuretic and does not lower blood pressure through a diuretic mechanism. Any blood-pressure effect of NSAIDs as a class tends to work against antihypertensive therapy, not to lower pressure directly.

## title
Aspirin's therapeutic use in rheumatic fever

## question
Therapeutic uses of aspirin include:

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Prophylaxis of migraine.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Migraine prophylaxis is achieved with drugs such as beta-blockers, topiramate or specific anti-CGRP agents, not with aspirin as a preventive strategy. Aspirin can treat an acute migraine attack, but that is a different use from prophylaxis.

## answer_b
Treatment of hypertension with pregnancy.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Hypertension in pregnancy is managed with agents such as labetalol, methyldopa or nifedipine. Aspirin has a role in pre-eclampsia prevention at low dose in specific risk groups, but it is not a treatment for established hypertension.

## answer_c
Prevent the progress of joint destruction in rheumatic fever.

## explanation_c
Correct. At high, sustained anti-inflammatory doses, aspirin's cyclooxygenase inhibition genuinely reduces joint inflammation and helps prevent progressive joint destruction in acute rheumatic fever, a historic and still-recognised therapeutic use. This is a distinct application from aspirin's lower-dose antiplatelet or analgesic-antipyretic roles. The dose needed here is far higher than for simple pain relief.

## answer_d
Delayed labour

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Aspirin and other NSAIDs, by inhibiting prostaglandins that help initiate and sustain labour, can delay rather than assist labour; but 'delayed labour' as a therapeutic goal is not among aspirin's recognised uses here, and this option does not describe an intended use.

## answer_e
Treatment of thrombocytopenia

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Aspirin inhibits platelet function and can worsen bleeding tendency; it is not used to treat thrombocytopenia, and in a thrombocytopenic patient it would be relatively contraindicated because it compounds the bleeding risk.

## title
Ibuprofen's mechanism of action

## question
What is the mechanism of action of ibuprofen?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Non selective of COX enzyme inhibitor

## explanation_a
Correct. Ibuprofen blocks both the COX-1 and COX-2 isoforms without meaningful selectivity between them. This is why it produces both peripheral anti-inflammatory benefit and the gastric/antiplatelet effects tied to COX-1 inhibition. Its lack of isoform preference is what defines it as non-selective, unlike celecoxib.

## answer_b
Selective Cox-2 inhibitor

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Selective COX-2 inhibition describes drugs such as celecoxib, which spare COX-1-mediated gastric protection. Ibuprofen inhibits both isoforms rather than being selective for COX-2.

## answer_c
Phospholipase A2 inhibitor

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Phospholipase A2 inhibition is the mechanism of corticosteroids, acting one step upstream of cyclooxygenase by blocking arachidonic acid release itself. Ibuprofen acts downstream, on cyclooxygenase directly, not on phospholipase A2.

## answer_d
Lipooxygenase enzyme inhibitor

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Lipoxygenase is a separate branch of the arachidonic acid cascade, producing leukotrienes. Ibuprofen's cyclooxygenase-selective action leaves this parallel pathway untouched.

## answer_e
Kallikrein system inhibitor

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The kallikrein system generates bradykinin through a separate plasma protease cascade unrelated to arachidonic acid metabolism. Ibuprofen has no action on this system.

Ibuprofen does not reduce the synthesis of one of the following eicosanoids:

## subject

## status

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. TXA2 (thromboxane A2) is a cyclooxygenase product, synthesised via the COX pathway that ibuprofen inhibits. Its synthesis is reduced by ibuprofen, so it is not the exception.

## answer_b
PGE2

## explanation_b
Incorrect. PGE2 is likewise a cyclooxygenase product, central to fever and pain sensitisation, and its synthesis falls when ibuprofen blocks COX. It is a genuine target of ibuprofen's action.

PGF2a

LTB4

Correct. LTB4 (leukotriene B4) is made via the lipoxygenase pathway, a separate branch of arachidonic acid metabolism that cyclooxygenase inhibitors like ibuprofen do not touch. Its synthesis is unaffected by, and can even be relatively favoured by, COX-selective inhibition.

PGI2

## explanation_e
Incorrect. PGI2 (prostacyclin) is a cyclooxygenase product as well, and ibuprofen reduces its synthesis along with the other COX-pathway eicosanoids, making it part of the affected group rather than the exception.

NSAID proposed to act via COX-3 inhibition

## question
NSAID proposed to be acting via inhibition of COX-3 is:

## subject
pharm

## status

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Nimesulide

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Nimesulide is understood through conventional, preferentially COX-2-selective peripheral inhibition, not the COX-3 hypothesis. Its clinical profile (peripheral anti-inflammatory activity) does not match paracetamol's centrally selective one.

Paracetamol

Rofecoxib

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Rofecoxib is a selective COX-2 inhibitor, understood through standard peripheral cyclooxygenase pharmacology, not through a central COX-3 mechanism.

## answer_e
Aspirin

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Aspirin's mechanism is conventional, irreversible, non-selective peripheral COX-1/COX-2 inhibition. It is not the drug the COX-3 hypothesis was proposed to explain.

## title
Mechanism of aspirin's MI prophylaxis

## question
Aspirin is used in the prophylaxis of myocardial infarction because it results in:

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Inhibition of thromboxane synthetase

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Thromboxane synthetase is a distinct downstream enzyme from cyclooxygenase; aspirin's antiplatelet action works one step earlier, by inhibiting cyclooxygenase itself, not this separate synthetase.

## answer_b
Inhibition of cyclooxygenase in platelets

## explanation_b
Correct. Aspirin irreversibly inhibits cyclooxygenase in platelets, which cannot resynthesise the enzyme since they lack a nucleus. This permanently blocks thromboxane A2-driven platelet aggregation for that platelet's lifespan. The resulting antiplatelet effect is what reduces thrombotic risk in myocardial infarction prophylaxis.

## answer_c
Decreased serum lipids

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Aspirin has no meaningful lipid-lowering effect; its cardioprotective mechanism is antiplatelet, not related to serum lipid levels at all.

## answer_d
Coronary steal phenomenon

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Coronary steal is a phenomenon associated with certain vasodilators diverting blood flow away from ischemic myocardium; it is not how aspirin works, and is in fact an undesirable effect unrelated to aspirin's mechanism.

## answer_e
Coronary vasodilator

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Aspirin is not a direct coronary vasodilator; its protective effect in myocardial infarction prophylaxis comes from reduced platelet aggregation, not from dilating coronary vessels.

## question
