## title
Hinge joint identification: the ankle

## question
Which of the following is a hinge joint?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Shoulder joint

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The shoulder is a ball-and-socket synovial joint, allowing movement in many directions, not the single-plane motion of a hinge.

## answer_b
Wrist joint

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The wrist is an ellipsoid (condyloid) joint, which permits flexion, extension, abduction and adduction, more than a hinge allows.

## answer_c
Ankle joint

## explanation_c
Correct. The ankle is a synovial hinge joint formed between the tibia, fibula and talus, and like every hinge joint it moves around a single axis. That axis allows only dorsiflexion and plantarflexion, while side-to-side stability is provided by the medial deltoid ligament and the lateral ligament bands rather than by any bony freedom of movement. This restriction to one plane of motion is exactly what defines a hinge joint, and is why the ankle — unlike the ball-and-socket shoulder or the ellipsoid wrist — cannot abduct or rotate.

## answer_d
Interosseous membrane

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The interosseous membrane is a fibrous joint (a syndesmosis), not a synovial hinge, and permits no independent movement of its own.

## answer_e
Suture

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A suture is an immobile fibrous joint between skull bones, the opposite of a freely moving hinge.

Hinge joint identification: the elbow

## question
Which one of the following is considered a hinge joint?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner

## vignette

D

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A suture is an immobile fibrous joint, not a synovial hinge.

## answer_b
Interosseous membrane

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This is a fibrous syndesmosis joining the shafts of two bones, not a synovial hinge.

## answer_c
Wrist joint

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The wrist is an ellipsoid joint, permitting movement around two axes rather than the single axis of a hinge.

## answer_d
Elbow joint

## explanation_d
Correct. The elbow is classified as a synovial hinge joint, formed mainly between the trochlea of the humerus and the trochlear notch of the ulna, with the radial head contributing a separate small articulation. As a uniaxial hinge it permits flexion and extension only, and cannot perform abduction, adduction or independent rotation at the joint itself. This single-axis behaviour is the defining feature shared by every hinge joint, whether at the elbow, the ankle or the interphalangeal joints of the fingers.

## answer_e
Shoulder joint

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The shoulder is a synovial ball-and-socket joint, the most mobile joint in the body, not a hinge.

## title
Fibrous joint identification: sutures

## question
Which of the following joints is fibrous?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Ball and socket

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Ball-and-socket is a synovial joint type, freely movable, not fibrous.

## answer_b
Elbow joint

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The elbow is a synovial hinge joint, not a fibrous one.

## answer_c
Shoulder joint

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The shoulder is a synovial ball-and-socket joint.

## answer_d
Suture joints

## explanation_d
Correct. A fibrous joint unites two bones with fibrous tissue and allows no real movement, and sutures are one of its three named types, alongside gomphosis and syndesmosis. In a suture, the bones of the skull are held together by a thin sutural ligament, which is why the skull functions as a single rigid case despite being built from several separate bones. Sutures are also unusual among fibrous joints in that this ligament is gradually obliterated with age, unlike the periodontal ligament of a gomphosis or the interosseous ligament of a syndesmosis, both of which persist throughout life.

## answer_e
Inter tarsal joints

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The intertarsal joints of the foot are synovial plane joints, allowing gliding movement.

## title
What sutures are, beyond "fibrous"

## question
Sutures are best described as joints that…

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
connect bones of the skull

## explanation_a
Correct. A suture is one of the three named types of fibrous joint, and it is specifically the type that unites the bones of the skull with a thin sutural ligament sandwiched between them. Because that ligament is rigid and only very slightly compressible, sutures allow essentially no movement, which suits their role of holding the cranial vault together as a single protective case around the brain. This same sutural ligament is gradually obliterated with age, a fact that distinguishes sutures from the other two fibrous joint types, whose connecting ligaments persist for life.

Incorrect. The opposite is true: the sutural ligament of a suture is progressively obliterated (ossifies) with advancing age.

are a primary cartilaginous joint

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Sutures are fibrous joints, united by a ligament, not by cartilage.

## answer_d
are freely mobile

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Sutures permit essentially no movement; they are immobile fibrous joints, not freely mobile synovial ones.

## answer_e
present between the root of a tooth and its socket

## explanation_e
Incorrect. That description fits a gomphosis, a different named type of fibrous joint, not a suture.

## title
Gomphosis classification

## question
Gomphosis is:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Cartilaginous joint

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Gomphosis is not united by cartilage; it belongs to the fibrous joint group.

## answer_b
Fibrous joint

## explanation_b
Correct. Gomphosis is one of the three named types of fibrous joint, alongside suture and syndesmosis, and it describes a tooth root held in its bony socket by the periodontal ligament. Like every fibrous joint it allows no significant movement, its connecting tissue being a short, taut ligament rather than cartilage or a synovial cavity. This makes gomphosis the joint type examiners most often use to test whether a student can tell fibrous-joint subtypes apart, since a tooth-in-socket is such a distinctive, easily pictured example.

Diarthrosis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Diarthrosis is another name for a freely movable synovial joint, which a gomphosis, with essentially no movement, is not.

## answer_d
Synovial joint

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A synovial joint has a joint cavity and synovial fluid; a gomphosis has neither.

## answer_e
Polyaxial joint

## explanation_e
Incorrect. "Polyaxial" describes a synovial joint moving around several axes, such as the shoulder, not a fibrous joint like gomphosis.

## question
The type of the inferior tibiofibular joint is…

## subject

Syndesmosis

## title
Secondary cartilaginous joint: symphysis pubis

## question
Secondary cartilaginous joints……………….

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Ossify after birth

## explanation_a
Incorrect. It is the primary cartilaginous joint, such as the epiphyseal plate, that ossifies as growth completes; a secondary cartilaginous joint is permanent and does not ossify.

## answer_b
Have a wide range of movement

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A secondary cartilaginous joint allows only slight movement, far less than a synovial joint.

## answer_c
Are formed of hyaline cartilage

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Secondary cartilaginous joints are formed of fibrocartilage; hyaline cartilage is the tissue of a primary cartilaginous joint.

## answer_d
Have the symphysis pubis as an example

## explanation_d
Correct. A secondary cartilaginous joint (symphysis) unites two bones by fibrocartilage, lies in the midline, is permanent throughout life, and permits only a slight amount of movement, and the pubic symphysis is the standard example examiners quote. This is in contrast to a primary cartilaginous joint, which is hyaline, usually temporary, and ossifies once growth is complete. The intervertebral discs are the other commonly tested example of a secondary cartilaginous joint, sharing the same fibrocartilage, midline position and lifelong permanence as the pubic symphysis.

## answer_e
Present in the appendicular skeleton

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Both quoted examples, the pubic symphysis and the intervertebral discs, belong to the axial skeleton, not the appendicular skeleton.

## title
Synovial joints and free movement

## question
Synovial joints allow…….movement between their bony elements.

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
No

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A joint that allows no movement at all is fibrous or a primary cartilaginous joint, not synovial.

## answer_b
Limited

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Limited movement describes a secondary cartilaginous joint such as the pubic symphysis, not a synovial joint.

## answer_c
Sliding

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Gliding is only one of the several movements a synovial joint can perform, not a description of how much movement it allows overall.

## answer_d
Free

## explanation_d
Correct. A synovial joint is the freely mobile joint type, found mostly in the limbs, and its wide range of movement is made possible by a fibrous capsule enclosing a joint cavity, hyaline articular cartilage over the bone ends, and a synovial membrane that secretes lubricating synovial fluid into that cavity. This combination of a lubricated, largely frictionless cavity and smooth cartilage-covered surfaces is what lets synovial joints move so much more freely than a fibrous or cartilaginous joint ever could. The exact type of free movement — hinge, pivot, gliding or multi-axial — then depends on the shape of that particular joint's articular surfaces.

# Item

## id
QST-MANSAEP-JOINTS-AND-2ND-WEEK-EMBRYOLOGY-AEPVIP2-Q10

## title
Synovial membrane secretes synovial fluid

## question
The part of the synovial joint responsible for secretion of the synovial fluid is the…

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Capsule

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The fibrous capsule encloses and strengthens the joint, but it is the membrane lining its inner surface, not the capsule itself, that secretes fluid.

## answer_b
Intracapsular ligaments

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Ligaments inside the capsule add stability to the joint; they play no part in fluid secretion.

## answer_c
Synovial membrane

## explanation_c
Correct. The synovial membrane is a thin, moist, glistening layer lining the inside of the fibrous capsule and covering every internal surface of the joint except the articular cartilage itself, and it is this membrane that both secretes and reabsorbs the synovial fluid within the joint cavity. That fluid is pale yellow and viscous, much like egg albumin, and it lubricates and nourishes the avascular articular cartilage covering the bone ends. Without a functioning synovial membrane the joint cavity would have no lubricating fluid at all, and the cartilage surfaces would grind against one another with far more friction and wear.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The blastocele is the cavity that Heuser's membrane lines and converts into the primary yolk sac; the membrane lines it, but the yolk sac is the structure it comes to define.

## answer_b
Amniotic cavity

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The amniotic cavity's roof is formed by the amnioblast, a trophoblast-facing cell population, not by Heuser's membrane.

## answer_c
Extraembryonic coelom

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The extraembryonic coelom (chorionic cavity) forms later, around day thirteen, from spaces opening up in the extraembryonic mesoderm, not from Heuser's membrane.

## answer_d
Yolk sac

## explanation_d
Correct. ## answer_e
Decidua

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The decidua is maternal endometrial tissue; Heuser's membrane is an embryonic structure derived from the hypoblast, with no connection to it.

## question

## answer_a
Epiblast

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The epiblast is an inner-cell-mass derivative that goes on to form the embryo proper; it takes no part in eroding or adhering to the endometrium.

## answer_b
Embryoblast

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The embryoblast is the inner cell mass from which the epiblast and hypoblast arise; the outer trophoblast, not the embryoblast, does the work of implantation.

## answer_c
Blastocele

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The blastocele is simply the fluid-filled cavity within the blastocyst; it has no cells and cannot itself adhere to or penetrate anything.

## answer_d
Trophoblast

## explanation_d
Correct. It is the trophoblast, the outer cell layer of the blastocyst, that is responsible for both adhesion to and penetration into the endometrium during implantation. After the blastocyst's embryonic pole makes initial contact with the endometrial surface, the trophoblast there proliferates and differentiates into the outer syncytiotrophoblast, which produces the proteolytic enzymes that erode the endometrial epithelium and stroma, allowing the blastocyst to sink beneath the surface. The embryoblast, epiblast, hypoblast and blastocele, by contrast, are all inner structures of the blastocyst concerned with building the embryo itself, and play no direct part in the invasive process of implantation.

## answer_e
Hypoblast

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The hypoblast lines the developing yolk sac and takes no part in adhesion to, or penetration of, the endometrium.
