## title
Drug causing folate deficiency by inhibiting conjugases

## question
Which of the following drugs can cause folic acid deficiency by inhibiting the conjugases that break off folic acid from its food chelators?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Phenytoin

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Phenytoin causes folate deficiency by impairing intestinal absorption and inducing hepatic folate turnover, not by inhibiting conjugases.

## answer_b
Pyrimethamine

## explanation_b
Correct. Dietary folate is mostly bound as polyglutamates that intestinal brush-border conjugases must cleave to monoglutamate folate before absorption. Pyrimethamine, an antimalarial and DHFR inhibitor, additionally inhibits these conjugases, blunting the release of absorbable folate from food. This is a mechanism separate from — and additive to — its direct DHFR-inhibiting effect on folate metabolism.

## answer_c
Trimethoprim

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Trimethoprim is an antifolate that inhibits bacterial dihydrofolate reductase, a different mechanism from conjugase inhibition.

## answer_d
Desferroxamine

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Desferroxamine is an iron chelator used for iron toxicity/overload, unrelated to folate metabolism.

## answer_e
Cyanocobalamin

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Cyanocobalamin is a vitamin B12 preparation, unrelated to folate conjugase activity.

## title
Oral iron preparation causing GI upset

## question
A 40-year-old man with CA small intestine and Hb 9.2 g/dl was started on oral iron therapy. After a few days he complained of GI upset (nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea on and off). Which oral iron preparation was responsible for his problem?

## status

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Iron dextran is a parenteral preparation, not an oral one, so it cannot be the oral cause of this patient's GI symptoms.

## answer_b
Sodium ferric gluconate

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Sodium ferric gluconate is also parenteral, not oral.

Iron sucrose

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Iron sucrose is parenteral, given IV only.

Among the options listed, it is also the only oral preparation, since the other three are parenteral.

Fetal complication of maternal folate deficiency

## question
Deficiency of folic acid in a pregnant woman can result in which complication for the fetus?

## subject
pharm

## status

## vignette

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Congenital heart defects are linked to other exposures (e.g. certain teratogens, maternal diabetes), not specifically to folate deficiency.

Neural tube defects

## explanation_b
Correct. Folate is required for the rapid DNA synthesis and cell division of neurulation, when the neural tube closes in the first 3-4 weeks after conception. Incorrect. Cleft palate has multifactorial causes but is not the classic folate-deficiency-associated defect.

Down syndrome

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Down syndrome is a chromosomal abnormality (trisomy 21), unrelated to maternal folate status.

## title
Vitamin B12 general pharmacology

## question
Which of the following statements about vitamin B12 is correct?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Is normally absorbed in the upper small intestine

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Vitamin B12 is absorbed in the lower (terminal) ileum, via intrinsic factor receptors, not the upper small intestine.

## answer_b
Therapy by mouth is the first choice in pernicious anemia

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Oral B12 is not first-choice in pernicious anaemia, because the underlying defect (absent intrinsic factor) makes absorption, not dose, the limiting factor — parenteral B12 is used instead.

## answer_c
Is good trial therapy in undiagnosed anemias

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Giving B12 empirically in undiagnosed anaemia risks masking a folate-deficiency picture and delays proper workup; it is not recommended as a blind trial.

## answer_d
Its deficiency can lead to anemia and neurological symptoms

## explanation_d
Correct. Because B12 is required both for DNA synthesis (megaloblastic anaemia when deficient) and for myelin maintenance in the nervous system, its deficiency produces neurological symptoms (paraesthesia, subacute combined degeneration) in addition to anaemia. This dual haematological-and-neurological presentation distinguishes B12 deficiency from most other haematinic deficiencies. Recognising both facets is essential to avoid missing the neurological component if only the anaemia is treated.

## answer_e
Is the first choice for the treatment of aplastic anemia

## explanation_e
Incorrect. B12 is not first-choice for aplastic anaemia, which is a marrow-failure state better addressed with erythropoietin, immunosuppression, or transplant.

## subject

## title
Iron deficiency in a pregnant non-menstruating woman

## question
A 22-year-old pregnant woman at 14 weeks gestation has Hb 9 g/dL and microcytic hypochromic anaemia. She asks why she could have iron deficiency when she is no longer menstruating. Which is the best explanation?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Occult gastrointestinal blood loss

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Occult GI bleeding is not the expected explanation in an otherwise well pregnant woman; it is a less common cause that should not be the first assumption.

## answer_b
Expanded blood volume and iron transport to the fetus

## explanation_b
Correct. Pregnancy substantially increases iron requirements through two mechanisms: expansion of maternal blood volume, requiring more red cell mass, and active iron transport across the placenta to support fetal growth and iron storage. This increased-demand mechanism, not occult bleeding, hemolysis, or reduced absorption, is the expected and most common explanation for iron deficiency arising during pregnancy in an otherwise well woman.

Hemolysis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Hemolysis would cause a normocytic, not microcytic, anaemia pattern, and is not the typical mechanism here.

## answer_d
Folate deficiency

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Folate deficiency causes macrocytic, not microcytic hypochromic, anaemia.

## answer_e
Decreased iron absorption

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Iron absorption is not typically decreased in pregnancy; if anything, absorption efficiency increases to help meet the higher demand, though total needs still often outstrip diet.

## title
Most efficient treatment for pediatric moderate IDA

## question
A child of 3 years of age has clinical and laboratory signs of moderate iron deficiency anaemia. Choose the most efficient method of treatment:

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Parenteral iron preparations

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Parenteral iron is reserved for oral intolerance or urgent need, not first-line for moderate paediatric IDA.

## answer_b
B12 and folic acid supplements

## explanation_b
Incorrect. B12 and folic acid do not treat iron deficiency.

## answer_c
Oral iron preparations only until the normal Hb

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Stopping oral iron once haemoglobin normalises corrects the blood count but leaves iron stores still depleted.

## answer_d
Oral iron until the normal Hb is reached and additionally 2-3 months of prophylactic dose

## explanation_d
Correct. Correcting the haemoglobin level is only the first phase of treatment — once haemoglobin normalises, iron stores (ferritin, marrow iron) are still depleted and take additional time to rebuild. Oral iron therapy is therefore continued for a further 2-3 months at a prophylactic-equivalent dose after normal haemoglobin is reached, rather than stopping as soon as the blood count looks normal. This two-phase approach (correction, then repletion) is the most efficient strategy, more complete than diet changes alone and not usually requiring parenteral therapy.

## answer_e
Only diet changes with food rich in iron

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Diet changes alone are too slow and insufficient to correct an established moderate anaemia; they support, but do not replace, iron supplementation.

Correct. Incorrect. Black stool is an expected, harmless effect of unabsorbed iron and is not by itself a reason to discontinue therapy.

Metabolic alkalosis is the major feature of acute iron toxicity

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Metabolic acidosis, not alkalosis, is the major systemic feature of acute iron overdose toxicity, reflecting mitochondrial and cellular poisoning by free iron.
