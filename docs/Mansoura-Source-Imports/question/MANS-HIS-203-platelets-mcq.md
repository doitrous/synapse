## title
Light microscopic picture of platelets

## question
What is the light microscopic picture of platelets?

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Appear biconcave disc shaped

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Biconcavity is the erythrocyte's shape, not the platelet's. Platelets are oval-to-round fragments, not biconcave discs, and confusing the two shapes is a common mistake because both are anucleate.

## answer_b
Appear oval non-nucleated discs

## explanation_b
Correct. On light microscopy a platelet is a small, oval, non-nucleated disc, roughly 2-4 micrometres across. It is a cytoplasmic fragment budded from a megakaryocyte, so it never had a nucleus to lose, unlike the erythrocyte which extrudes one.

## answer_c
Irregular cell membrane with thick cell coat

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The platelet's plasma membrane is a regular lipid bilayer with a surface coat, not a distinctively thick or irregular one; irregularity of outline is not how platelets are identified under the light microscope.

## answer_d
Their inner part is transparent (hyalomere)

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The hyalomere (the pale peripheral zone) and granulomere (the dark central zone) are an electron-microscopic and special-stain distinction inside a single platelet, not what defines its light-microscopic outline; describing only the inner zone leaves out the platelet's overall appearance.

## answer_e
Their outer part is dark stained (granulomere)

## explanation_e
Incorrect. As with the hyalomere, the granulomere is one internal zone of the platelet, not a description of the whole cell's light-microscopic picture, which the oval non-nucleated disc already covers.

## title
Largest cell in thrombopoiesis

## question
The largest cell in thrombopoiesis is:

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Megakaryocyte

## explanation_a
Correct. The megakaryocyte, the mature stage of thrombopoiesis, is the largest cell in the bone marrow, up to 100 micrometres across. It reaches that size because it becomes polyploid through repeated endomitosis without cell division, which multiplies its DNA content and cytoplasmic volume many times over. Only after that growth does it fragment its cytoplasm into individual platelets.

## answer_b
Pro-megakaryocyte

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The pro-megakaryocyte precedes the megakaryocyte in the series and is smaller; size increases as the series progresses toward the fully polyploid megakaryocyte, not before it.

## answer_c
Megakaryoblast

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The megakaryoblast is the earliest recognisable precursor in the series and is smaller than the cells that follow it, well before the endomitotic enlargement that makes the megakaryocyte the largest.

## answer_d
Promyelocyte

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The promyelocyte belongs to the granulocyte series (granulopoiesis), not thrombopoiesis, and answering it here confuses the two myeloid lineages.

## answer_e
Proerythroblast

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The proerythroblast belongs to the erythroid series (erythropoiesis), not thrombopoiesis, and it is a small early precursor, not a large cell.

## title
Normal platelet life span

## question
What is the normal life span of platelets?

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
1-2 days

## explanation_a
Incorrect. 1-2 days is far shorter than a platelet's real circulating life span; a lifespan this short would make normal haemostasis impossible to sustain between marrow replenishment cycles.

## answer_b
7-10 days

## explanation_b
Correct. Platelets circulate for about 7-10 days before being removed, mainly by the spleen. This is far shorter than the erythrocyte's roughly 120-day lifespan, reflecting the platelet's role as a short-lived, rapidly replenished haemostatic fragment rather than a long-lived circulating cell.

## answer_c
30-35 days

## explanation_c
Incorrect. 30-35 days overstates the platelet's lifespan by roughly a factor of four; no blood cell type in this bank has that specific value as its correct lifespan.

## answer_d
1-2 months

## explanation_d
Incorrect. 1-2 months is closer to some white cell tissue lifespans than to the platelet's short circulating life; platelets do not persist in circulation this long.

## answer_e
5-10 months

## explanation_e
Incorrect. 5-10 months is an order of magnitude too long for any circulating blood cell in this series, platelet included.

## title
Normal platelet count

## question
Normal platelet count:

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
10000-15000/mm3

## explanation_a
Incorrect. 10,000-15,000/mm3 is roughly an order of magnitude below the true normal platelet range; a count this low would already be a significant thrombocytopenia.

## answer_b
150000-400000/mm3

## explanation_b
Correct. The normal platelet count is about 150,000-400,000/mm3 (commonly cited as roughly 300,000/mm3 as a working average). This range is what separates a normal count from thrombocytopenia below it and thrombocytosis above it.

## answer_c
4000-11000/mm3

## explanation_c
Incorrect. 4,000-11,000/mm3 is the normal range for total white blood cells, not platelets; the two counts are easy to confuse because both are reported per cubic millimetre.

## answer_d
4-5.5 million/mm3

## explanation_d
Incorrect. 4-5.5 million/mm3 is in the range of the red blood cell count, not the platelet count; platelets are far less numerous than erythrocytes.

## answer_e
4.5-6 million/mm3

## explanation_e
Incorrect. 4.5-6 million/mm3 is likewise an erythrocyte-range value, not a platelet count.

## title
Contents of platelet delta (dense) granules

## question
Delta granules of platelets contain:

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Lysosomes

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Lysosomes are their own separate organelle inside the platelet, not a content of the delta (dense) granule; they carry the platelet's own hydrolytic enzymes.

## answer_b
Platelet derived growth factor

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Platelet-derived growth factor is stored in the alpha granule, not the delta (dense) granule — the two granule types are a common paired distractor in this bank and carry different contents.

## answer_c
Mitochondria

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Mitochondria are separate organelles that supply the platelet's energy; they are not packaged inside a delta granule.

## answer_d
Serotonin

## explanation_d
Correct. The delta (dense) granule stores serotonin, along with ADP and calcium. It releases them on platelet activation, once the platelet has bound to exposed collagen at a site of vessel injury. That release recruits and aggregates further circulating platelets to build the platelet plug.

## answer_e
DNA

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Platelets are anucleate cytoplasmic fragments and contain no DNA at all, whether inside a granule or otherwise.

## title
Platelet development and the megakaryocyte nucleus

## question
Regarding platelet development:

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Stimulated by erythropoietin hormone

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Thrombopoiesis is stimulated mainly by thrombopoietin, not erythropoietin; erythropoietin drives the separate erythroid (red cell) series.

## answer_b
Megakaryoblasts have acidophilic granular cytoplasm

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The megakaryoblast, the earliest cell in the series, has basophilic (not acidophilic) and agranular (not granular) cytoplasm; granularity develops later, in the megakaryocyte itself.

## answer_c
Megakaryocyte is very small

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The megakaryocyte is the largest cell in the marrow, not a small one — the opposite of what this option states, and a direct contradiction of its own defining feature.

## answer_d
Megakaryocyte has a large lobulated nucleus

## explanation_d
Correct. The megakaryocyte's large, lobulated (multilobed) nucleus is one of its two defining features, alongside its very large polyploid cell size. Both arise from repeated endomitosis — DNA replication without cell division — which multiplies the nuclear DNA content into one large lobulated nucleus rather than several separate ones.

## answer_e
Pro-megakaryocytes are small with acidophilic non-granular cytoplasm

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Pro-megakaryocytes are larger than the megakaryoblast that precedes them, not small, and their cytoplasm is beginning to show granules as the series progresses toward the megakaryocyte, not remaining non-granular.

## title
Thrombocytes and blood clotting (past-exam block, دفعة 62)

## question
Which of the following blood cells play a role in blood clotting?

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Erythrocytes

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Erythrocytes carry oxygen and carbon dioxide; they are not the cell type responsible for the platelet plug and clot formation of haemostasis.

## answer_b
Monocytes

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Monocytes are phagocytic cells that become tissue macrophages; clotting is not their function.

## answer_c
Thrombocytes

## explanation_c
Correct. Thrombocytes (platelets) are the blood cell responsible for clotting. They aggregate at a site of vessel injury and activate, exposing a phospholipid surface. That surface, together with the contents released from their granules, drives the coagulation cascade forward to a stable clot.

## answer_d
Neutrophils

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Neutrophils are the first line of non-specific phagocytic defence against bacteria; they play no direct role in the clotting mechanism.

## answer_e
Basophils

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Basophils release histamine and heparin in inflammatory and allergic responses; heparin is in fact an anticoagulant, the opposite of a clotting role.

## title
The smallest blood cell (past-exam block, دفعة 63)

## question
The smallest blood cell is:

## subject
haem

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Red blood cell

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The erythrocyte, at about 7-7.5 micrometres, is larger than the platelet.

## answer_b
Small lymphocyte

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Even the small lymphocyte, the smallest nucleated leukocyte, is larger than an anucleate platelet fragment.

## answer_c
Platelet

## explanation_c
Correct. The platelet, at roughly 2-4 micrometres, is the smallest formed element of the blood, smaller than any leukocyte and smaller than the erythrocyte. It is this small because it is only a cytoplasmic fragment budded from a megakaryocyte, not a whole cell. That fragment nature, not a shrunken whole cell, is why it has no nucleus and no room for the organelles a full cell would carry.

## answer_d
Monocyte

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The monocyte is the largest leukocyte in a normal blood smear, the opposite extreme from the smallest blood cell asked for here.

## answer_e
Neutrophil

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The neutrophil, at 10-12 micrometres, is far larger than the platelet.
