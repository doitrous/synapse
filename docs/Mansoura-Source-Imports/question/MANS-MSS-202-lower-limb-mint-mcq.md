## title
Piriformis syndrome and spared sensation

## question
A 38-year-old male presents with right buttock pain radiating to the posterior thigh and lateral foot. Physical examination and imaging confirm piriformis syndrome causing sciatic nerve compression. When testing light touch sensation, which of the following areas would most likely remain intact?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Lateral aspect of the leg

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The lateral leg is supplied by the superficial peroneal nerve, itself a branch of the common peroneal division of the sciatic nerve. Compression of the sciatic nerve upstream would affect this territory, not spare it. It falls within the sciatic distribution, so it is not the area spared.

## answer_b
Dorsum of the foot

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The dorsum of the foot is mainly supplied by the superficial peroneal nerve, again a sciatic nerve branch. Like the lateral leg, this territory lies downstream of the compression, not outside it. Sensation here would be affected, not preserved.

## answer_c
Medial aspect of the leg

## explanation_c
Correct. The medial leg is supplied by the saphenous nerve, a cutaneous branch of the femoral nerve, which never joins the sciatic nerve at any point. Because piriformis syndrome compresses the sciatic nerve specifically, the saphenous nerve's territory is untouched, making the medial leg the one area of light-touch sensation that remains intact and a useful bedside discriminator from a more proximal (e.g. L4 root) lesion.

## answer_d
Posterior thigh

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The posterior thigh is supplied by the posterior cutaneous nerve of the thigh, which travels with the sciatic nerve out of the pelvis below piriformis. It is directly within the compressed nerve's territory. This is actually one of the areas expected to be affected, matching the vignette's own described pain radiation.

## answer_e
Web space between first and second toes

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The web space between the first and second toes is supplied by the deep peroneal nerve, another sciatic-derived branch. It lies within the sciatic distribution, downstream of the piriformis compression. Sensation there would be affected, not spared.

## title
Sciatic nerve location in the gluteal region

## question
A 55-year-old female undergoes hip surgery. During the procedure, the surgeon must identify the sciatic nerve to avoid its injury. When palpating its location in the gluteal region, it is typically found midway between the ischial tuberosity and which of the following landmarks?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Posterior superior iliac spine

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The posterior superior iliac spine is a landmark much higher on the pelvis, well above the gluteal region where the sciatic nerve is found. It is not the second point used to locate the nerve's midpoint course. This landmark is more relevant to identifying the sacroiliac joint.

## answer_b
Greater trochanter

## explanation_b
Correct. The sciatic nerve crosses the gluteal region roughly midway along the line joining the ischial tuberosity and the greater trochanter of the femur, two palpable bony points. This midpoint landmark is the reason intramuscular gluteal injections are placed in the upper outer quadrant of the buttock, away from this line, and is exactly what a surgeon uses to avoid the nerve during hip surgery.

## answer_c
Iliac tuberosity

## explanation_c
Incorrect. There is no standard landmark named 'iliac tuberosity' used for this purpose; the iliac tubercle is a different structure on the iliac crest, unrelated to sciatic nerve localisation in the gluteal region. It does not form part of the standard midpoint description.

## answer_d
Ischial spine

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The ischial spine is a separate, more medial and deeper landmark, relevant to the pudendal nerve's course rather than the sciatic nerve's surface projection. It is not one of the two points used to define the sciatic nerve's midpoint landmark.

## answer_e
Iliac crest

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The iliac crest is far superior to the gluteal region where the sciatic nerve is found, running along the top of the pelvis. It is not the companion landmark to the ischial tuberosity used for this purpose.

## title
Nerve injured in a waddling, pelvis-dropping gait

## question
A patient with a deep knife wound in the buttock walks with a waddling gait that is characterized by the pelvis falling toward one side at each step. Which of the following nerves is damaged?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Femoral nerve

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The femoral nerve supplies the anterior thigh (quadriceps, sartorius) and produces knee-extension weakness if injured, not a waddling gait from pelvic drop. It has no relationship to gluteus medius or minimus, the muscles responsible for pelvic stabilisation.

## answer_b
Inferior gluteal nerve

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The inferior gluteal nerve supplies gluteus maximus, the prime hip extensor. Its injury weakens hip extension (e.g. rising from sitting, climbing stairs) rather than pelvic stabilisation during single-leg stance. Gluteus maximus does not perform the pelvis-levelling role that produces this gait pattern.

## answer_c
Nerve to obturator internus

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The nerve to obturator internus supplies a small lateral rotator of the hip. Its injury would not produce a waddling, pelvis-dropping gait, since obturator internus plays no significant role in pelvic stabilisation during gait. This option targets a muscle with a minor, unrelated function.

## answer_d
Obturator nerve

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The obturator nerve supplies the medial (adductor) compartment of the thigh. Its injury weakens hip adduction, not pelvic stabilisation during stance, so it would not produce this waddling gait pattern. It belongs to a different functional group from the gluteal stabilisers.

## answer_e
Superior gluteal nerve

## explanation_e
Correct. The superior gluteal nerve supplies gluteus medius and minimus, which stabilise the pelvis during single-leg stance by pulling the ipsilateral hip down relative to the unsupported side. Injury to this nerve — as from a deep gluteal wound — weakens that stabilisation, so the contralateral pelvis drops instead on each step, producing the characteristic waddling (Trendelenburg) gait.

## title
Muscle damaged by a superior pubic ramus fracture

## question
Pelvic X-ray of a 15-year-old boy revealed fracture of the superior pubic ramus; which of the following muscles is more likely to be damaged?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Pectineus muscle

## explanation_a
Correct. Pectineus arises from the pecten pubis, a ridge that lies directly along the superior pubic ramus, so a fracture through that bone puts it at the most direct risk among the hip adductor group. The other adductors originate more from the pubic body or the ischiopubic ramus, sites further from this particular fracture.

## answer_b
Adductor longus

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Adductor longus arises from the body of the pubis, near the pubic symphysis, rather than from the superior ramus itself. It is a commonly recalled hip adductor, which is exactly why it is a tempting but incorrect default answer here. Its origin does not sit on the fractured bone.

## answer_c
Adductor brevis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Adductor brevis also arises from the body and inferior ramus of the pubis, not the superior ramus. Its origin site places it away from this particular fracture. It is therefore less directly at risk than pectineus.

## answer_d
Adductor magnus

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Adductor magnus has a broad origin from the ischiopubic ramus and ischial tuberosity, sites distinct from the superior pubic ramus. It is the largest adductor but not the one whose origin sits on the fractured bone described here.

## answer_e
Gracilis muscle

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Gracilis arises from the body and inferior ramus of the pubis, again not the superior ramus. It is a long, thin muscle crossing both the hip and knee, but its pelvic origin does not correspond to this fracture site.

## title
Strongest ligament preventing hip hyperextension

## question
Which one of the following ligaments is the strongest ligament in the body preventing hyperextension of the hip joint?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Pubofemoral ligament

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The pubofemoral ligament reinforces the hip joint inferiorly, mainly limiting excessive abduction and extension, but it is weaker than the iliofemoral ligament and is not credited as the body's strongest ligament. It plays a supporting, not primary, role in checking hyperextension.

## answer_b
Ischiofemoral ligament

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The ischiofemoral ligament reinforces the hip joint posteriorly, mainly limiting medial rotation and adduction. It is weaker than the iliofemoral ligament and is not the one specifically responsible for preventing hyperextension. Its orientation makes it better suited to resisting different movements.

## answer_c
Transverse acetabular ligament

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The transverse acetabular ligament bridges the acetabular notch, converting it into a foramen for vessels and the ligament of the head of femur, playing a structural rather than restraining role. It does not check hip hyperextension at all. It is a small, specialised structure, not one of the three main capsular ligaments.

## answer_d
Iliofemoral ligament

## explanation_d
Correct. The iliofemoral ligament, also called the ligament of Bigelow or the Y-ligament, runs from the anterior inferior iliac spine to the intertrochanteric line and is the strongest ligament in the entire human body. It becomes taut on hip extension, checking hyperextension and allowing the pelvis to lean backward over the femoral heads without sustained muscular effort, the basis of relaxed standing posture.

## answer_e
Labrum acetabular

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The acetabular labrum is a fibrocartilaginous rim that deepens the acetabulum and improves joint stability, but it is not a ligament and is not credited with preventing hyperextension specifically. Its role is increasing socket depth, a different mechanical function from a restraining ligament.

## title
Muscle supplied by the common peroneal division of the sciatic nerve

## question
Which muscle is supplied by the common peroneal division of the sciatic nerve?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Ischial part of adductor magnus

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The ischial part of adductor magnus is supplied by the tibial division of the sciatic nerve, the same division supplying most hamstrings. It follows the general pattern of tibial-division supply among the posterior/medial thigh muscles rather than the developmental exception.

## answer_b
Long head of biceps femoris

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The long head of biceps femoris is supplied by the tibial division, following the typical hamstring pattern. It is only its partner, the short head, that breaks from this pattern. Confusing the two heads of the same muscle is exactly the trap this question sets.

## answer_c
Semimembranosus

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Semimembranosus is supplied by the tibial division, like the rest of the hamstring group aside from the one exception. It does not receive any fibres from the common peroneal division.

## answer_d
Semitendinosus

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Semitendinosus is likewise supplied by the tibial division, following the general hamstring pattern. It is not the muscle that breaks from that pattern.

## answer_e
Short head of biceps femoris

## explanation_e
Correct. The short head of biceps femoris is the one hamstring supplied by the common peroneal division of the sciatic nerve rather than the tibial division, reflecting its developmental origin with the extensor (peroneal) compartment despite sitting anatomically among the flexor hamstrings. This means a common peroneal nerve injury can selectively weaken the short head while the rest of the hamstrings, tibial-supplied, remain normal.

## title
Muscle that unlocks the knee joint

## question
Which of the following muscles unlocks (initiate the flexion) the knee joint?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Tibialis anterior

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Tibialis anterior is a dorsiflexor and invertor of the ankle/foot, acting well below the knee joint. It has no role in the knee's screw-home locking mechanism. It is anatomically and functionally unrelated to unlocking the knee.

## answer_b
Tibialis posterior

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Tibialis posterior is an invertor and secondary plantar flexor of the ankle, also acting distal to the knee. Like tibialis anterior, it plays no part in the knee's locking or unlocking mechanism. It targets the ankle/subtalar joint instead.

## answer_c
Popliteus

## explanation_c
Correct. Popliteus, a small muscle in the floor of the popliteal fossa, laterally rotates the femur on the fixed tibia in weight-bearing, reversing the screw-home lock that stabilises the fully extended knee. This 'unlocking' action is a distinct mechanical step that must occur before the hamstrings can then drive flexion proper.

## answer_d
Peroneus tertius

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Peroneus tertius dorsiflexes and everts the foot at the ankle, a leg muscle acting distal to the knee joint. It has no mechanical relationship to the knee's locking mechanism. This option targets an unrelated joint.

## answer_e
Gastrocnemius

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Gastrocnemius is a powerful plantar flexor of the ankle and a weak flexor of the knee, but it is not the muscle credited with unlocking the extended, screw-home-locked knee. That specific unlocking role belongs to popliteus alone.

## title
Intracapsular structure of the knee joint

## question
Which one of the following structures is intracapsular at the knee joint?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Oblique popliteal ligament

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The oblique popliteal ligament is an extracapsular expansion of the semimembranosus tendon over the posterior knee, reinforcing the capsule from outside rather than lying within it. It does not cross the joint space itself. It is a capsular reinforcement, not an intracapsular structure.

## answer_b
Patellar ligament

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The patellar ligament is the extracapsular continuation of the quadriceps tendon below the patella, running anterior to the joint. It is not enclosed within the joint capsule. It is one of the classic extracapsular ligaments alongside the collaterals.

## answer_c
Medial and lateral cruciate ligaments

## explanation_c
Correct. The cruciate ligaments (anterior and posterior) are the knee's intracapsular ligaments, crossing within the joint space between the femoral and tibial condyles, though even they are technically extrasynovial, wrapped in a fold of synovium rather than sitting freely in the joint cavity. This intracapsular status is what makes a cruciate tear produce a haemarthrosis, blood within the joint capsule itself.

## answer_d
Medial and lateral collateral ligaments

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The medial and lateral collateral ligaments lie outside the capsule proper, blended with it medially or separate from it laterally. They are extracapsular, unlike the cruciate ligaments. Their injury more often produces localised swelling rather than a true joint effusion.

## answer_e
Tendon of quadratus femoris muscle

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Quadratus femoris is a hip, not knee, muscle, running from the ischial tuberosity to the femur to laterally rotate the hip. Its tendon has no relationship to the knee joint capsule at all. This option describes an entirely different joint.

## title
Nerve supplying the front of the thigh

## question
Which one of the following nerves supplies muscles of the front of the thigh?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Superior gluteal nerve

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The superior gluteal nerve supplies gluteus medius and minimus in the gluteal region, not the anterior thigh. Its territory is entirely posterior to the anterior thigh compartment. It plays no role in knee extension or hip flexion.

## answer_b
Inferior gluteal nerve

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The inferior gluteal nerve supplies gluteus maximus, again in the gluteal region rather than the anterior thigh. Like the superior gluteal nerve, its territory does not extend into the front-of-thigh muscles. It is responsible for hip extension, not knee extension.

## answer_c
Femoral nerve

## explanation_c
Correct. The femoral nerve enters the thigh lateral to the femoral artery and supplies the anterior compartment — quadriceps femoris, sartorius, iliacus and pectineus — the muscles responsible for knee extension and hip flexion. Its cutaneous territory (anterior/medial thigh, and via the saphenous branch, the medial leg) tracks the same anterior distribution.

## answer_d
Sciatic nerve

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The sciatic nerve supplies the posterior thigh compartment (the hamstrings) and, via its two divisions, essentially the entire leg and foot below the knee, but not the anterior thigh. The anterior compartment is femoral nerve territory specifically, a different nerve from a different part of the lumbosacral plexus.

## title
Nerve injury causing inability to stand on tiptoes

## question
A patient comes to you complaining of his inability to stand on his tiptoes. Which of the following nerve injuries is most likely to be involved?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Femoral nerve

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The femoral nerve supplies knee extension and hip flexion, not ankle plantarflexion. Its injury would weaken the ability to straighten the knee or climb stairs, not the ability to rise onto the toes. It has no relationship to gastrocnemius or soleus.

## answer_b
Tibial nerve

## explanation_b
Correct. The tibial nerve, the larger terminal division of the sciatic nerve, supplies gastrocnemius and soleus, the prime plantar flexors that raise the heel off the ground. Its injury weakens plantarflexion, making standing on tiptoes difficult or impossible — a bedside sign distinct from foot drop, which points instead to the peroneal nerve.

## answer_c
Common peroneal nerve

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The common peroneal nerve, once it divides into its deep and superficial branches, produces foot drop (loss of dorsiflexion) and eversion weakness, not loss of plantarflexion. It supplies the opposite functional group from the one needed to stand on tiptoes.

## answer_d
Deep peroneal nerve

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The deep peroneal nerve supplies the dorsiflexors (tibialis anterior and the toe extensors), producing foot drop if injured. It has no role in plantarflexion, so it would not explain difficulty standing on tiptoes.

## answer_e
Superficial peroneal nerve

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The superficial peroneal nerve supplies the evertors (peroneus longus and brevis) and is mainly sensory over the dorsum of the foot. It plays no role in plantarflexion and would not cause this presentation.

## title
Muscle causing eversion of the foot at the subtalar joint

## question
Which one of the following muscles causes eversion of the foot at the subtalar joint?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Tibialis Anterior

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Tibialis anterior dorsiflexes and inverts the foot, the opposite of eversion. Its tendon inserts on the medial side of the foot (first metatarsal/medial cuneiform), consistent with an inverting rather than everting action. It belongs to the anterior compartment, not the lateral (peroneal) compartment.

## answer_b
Extensor Digitorum Longus

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Extensor digitorum longus dorsiflexes the toes and assists ankle dorsiflexion, without a significant eversion action of its own. It lies in the anterior compartment alongside tibialis anterior. It is not one of the peroneal (everting) muscles.

## answer_c
Extensor Hallucis Longus

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Extensor hallucis longus extends the great toe and assists dorsiflexion, again an anterior-compartment muscle without a significant eversion role. It shares its compartment and general action with the other anterior-compartment muscles, not the lateral evertors.

## answer_d
Peroneus Longus

## explanation_d
Correct. Peroneus (fibularis) longus, together with peroneus brevis, everts the foot at the subtalar joint. Its tendon runs behind the lateral malleolus and crosses the sole to insert on the base of the first metatarsal and medial cuneiform, which also lets it help support the foot's transverse arch.

## answer_e
Gastrocnemius

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Gastrocnemius is a powerful plantar flexor of the ankle and a weak knee flexor, with no eversion action. It lies in the posterior compartment, functionally and anatomically distinct from the lateral peroneal muscles that produce eversion.

## title
Ligament stretched in a high-heel ankle twist

## question
A woman wearing high heels has fallen and twisted her ankle. Part of which ligament was most likely stretched?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Deltoid ligament

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The deltoid (medial) ligament is a single strong, fan-shaped band, and it is injured by the opposite mechanism, foot eversion, which is far less common than inversion. A high-heel ankle twist typically rolls the ankle into inversion, not eversion, so the deltoid ligament is not the structure at risk here.

## answer_b
Lateral ligament

## explanation_b
Correct. The lateral side of the ankle is reinforced by three separate, individually weaker bands (anterior talofibular, calcaneofibular and posterior talofibular ligaments), making it structurally weaker overall than the medial deltoid ligament. An inversion injury — the classic mechanism when a foot in a heeled shoe gives way sideways — stretches or tears this lateral complex, almost always the anterior talofibular ligament first.

## answer_c
Short plantar

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The short plantar (plantar calcaneocuboid) ligament supports the lateral longitudinal arch of the foot's sole, a structure unrelated to the ankle joint's own inversion/eversion stability. It is not the ligament classically stretched in this mechanism.

## answer_d
Long plantar

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The long plantar ligament also supports the longitudinal arch of the sole, running along the plantar surface of the foot rather than stabilising the ankle joint against inversion. It is a foot-arch ligament, not an ankle ligament.

## answer_e
Spring

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The spring (plantar calcaneonavicular) ligament supports the head of the talus and the medial longitudinal arch, a foot-arch structure rather than an ankle-stabilising ligament. It is not the structure classically injured in an inversion ankle sprain.
