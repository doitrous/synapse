## title
What Poiseuille's equation determines

## question
Which of the following is correct about Poiseuille's equation?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
It determines the factors affecting resistance of blood vessels.

## explanation_a
Correct. Poiseuille's equation is the physical relationship that determines the factors affecting the resistance a blood vessel offers to flow. It states that resistance varies directly with blood viscosity and vessel length, and inversely with the vessel radius raised to the fourth power. This is why radius, the one of the three determinants that changes physiologically, dominates changes in resistance.

## answer_b
Resistance is directly proportional to the radius of blood vessels.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Resistance is inversely, not directly, proportional to vessel radius — and specifically to the fourth power of radius, making the relationship far steeper than a simple inverse.

## answer_c
Resistance is inversely proportional to the length of the blood vessel.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Resistance is directly, not inversely, proportional to vessel length — a longer vessel offers more resistance, not less.

## answer_d
Blood viscosity doesn't affect resistance.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Blood viscosity is one of the three determinants in Poiseuille's equation and does affect resistance, varying directly with it.

## answer_e
It determines the factors affecting intraluminal pressure of blood vessels.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Poiseuille's equation determines the factors affecting vascular resistance and flow, not the factors affecting a vessel's own intraluminal pressure.

## title
A characteristic of turbulent blood flow

## question
Turbulent blood flow is characterized by which one of the following?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Blood flow is streamline.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Streamline (laminar) flow is the opposite pattern from turbulent flow, not a feature of it.

## answer_b
Eddy currents are present.

## explanation_b
Correct. Turbulent flow is disorganised flow in which eddy currents — swirling, cross-current movements of blood — replace the smooth, layered pattern of laminar flow. It arises once the Reynolds number rises high enough (chiefly through rising velocity or vessel diameter, or falling viscosity), and it is the eddy currents themselves that generate the vibrations detected clinically as murmurs and bruits. Streamline flow, silence, a low Reynolds number and a sub-critical velocity all instead describe laminar, not turbulent, flow.

## answer_c
It is silent.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Turbulent flow generates audible vibrations (murmurs and bruits); it is laminar flow that is silent.

## answer_d
Reynold's number is less than 2000.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A Reynolds number below roughly 2000 predicts laminar, not turbulent, flow — turbulence requires a HIGH Reynolds number.

## answer_e
Blood flow is below critical velocity.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Turbulence occurs ABOVE, not below, a vessel's critical velocity.

## title
The exception among peripheral-resistance facts

## question
As regards peripheral resistance, all the following are true except:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The main determinant factor is the change in diameter of arterioles.

## explanation_a
Incorrect (this statement is true). Arteriolar diameter is indeed the main determinant of peripheral resistance, since it is the one Poiseuille variable the body actively and rapidly controls.

## answer_b
Viscosity of blood is inversely related to peripheral resistance.

## explanation_b
Correct — this is the false statement (the exception). Blood viscosity is DIRECTLY, not inversely, related to peripheral resistance: a rise in viscosity raises resistance, and a fall in viscosity (as in anemia or hypoproteinemia) lowers it, exactly the opposite of an inverse relationship. This direct relationship is one of the three determinants named by Poiseuille's law, alongside vessel radius and length.

## answer_c
It decreases in anemia and hypoproteinemia.

## explanation_c
Incorrect (this statement is true). Anemia and hypoproteinemia both lower blood viscosity, and since viscosity is directly related to resistance, peripheral resistance falls in both conditions.

## answer_d
Increase in length of vessels, as in the systemic circulation, increases peripheral resistance.

## explanation_d
Incorrect (this statement is true). Vessel length is directly related to resistance by Poiseuille's law, so the longer total vessel length of the systemic circulation (compared with the pulmonary circulation) contributes to its higher resistance.

## title
How turbulent flow is detected

## question
Turbulent blood flow is detected by:

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Laplace's law.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Laplace's law relates wall tension to pressure and radius; it does not predict whether flow is laminar or turbulent.

## answer_b
Reynold's number.

## explanation_b
Correct. The Reynolds number is the dimensionless index used to detect and predict whether blood flow will be laminar or turbulent, rising with velocity, vessel diameter and blood density and falling with viscosity. A high Reynolds number (above roughly 3000) reliably predicts turbulence, while a low one (below roughly 2000) predicts laminar flow. None of the circulation's other named physical laws serves this specific predictive role.

## answer_c
Ohm's law.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Ohm's law (in its hemodynamic analogy, flow = pressure/resistance) describes the magnitude of flow, not whether that flow is laminar or turbulent.

## answer_d
Poiseuille's law.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Poiseuille's law describes the factors determining resistance and flow (viscosity, length, radius), not the laminar-versus-turbulent distinction.

The resistance vessels of the circulation

## question
Resistant vessels are:

## subject
cvs

## status

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Arteries mainly conduct blood under high pressure and contribute comparatively little variable resistance compared with arterioles.

## answer_b
Arterioles.

## explanation_b
Correct. Arterioles are the principal resistance vessels of the systemic circulation. Their small lumen combined with a well-developed smooth-muscle media lets them change vascular resistance far more than any other vessel type, and by Poiseuille's fourth-power radius relationship, small changes in arteriolar diameter produce large changes in resistance and flow. Incorrect. Capillaries are the site of exchange, with walls too thin and non-muscular to actively regulate resistance.

## answer_d
Veins.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Veins are thin-walled, highly compliant capacitance vessels that store blood volume; they are not the circulation's chief resistance vessels.

## title
Blood flow and arteriolar dilatation

## question
Concerning blood flow (F):

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
F = ΔP / r (radius).

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The flow equation is F = ΔP / R (resistance), not radius directly.

## answer_b
It decreases when ΔP increases.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Flow increases, not decreases, when the driving pressure gradient (ΔP) increases.

## answer_c
It increases when the resistance increases.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Flow decreases, not increases, when resistance increases — flow and resistance move in opposite directions in F = ΔP / R.

## answer_d
It increases due to arteriolar dilatation.

## explanation_d
Correct. Blood flow equals the pressure gradient divided by resistance (F = ΔP / R). Arteriolar dilatation sharply lowers resistance, since resistance falls with the fourth power of radius by Poiseuille's law, and a fall in R with ΔP unchanged raises F. This is the mechanism by which arteriolar dilatation increases local and downstream blood flow.

Velocity of blood flow at the vessel wall

The velocity of blood flow:

## subject

## title
The incorrect statement about turbulent flow

## question
Which of the following is incorrect concerning turbulent blood flow?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Turbulent flow is heard only in blood vessels.

## explanation_a
Correct — this is the incorrect statement. Turbulent-flow murmurs and bruits are audible over the chest and other body surfaces as sound conducted from the turbulent vessel, so the sound is not confined to ("heard only in") the blood vessel itself. The statement overstates where the turbulence is heard, and the other three options here all correctly describe turbulent flow.

## answer_b
If Reynold's number was more than 3000, turbulent flow will always occur.

## explanation_b
Incorrect (this statement is true). Above a Reynolds number of roughly 3000, turbulent flow reliably always occurs, which is the basis of the 3000 threshold this lecture teaches.

## answer_c
Above a critical velocity, blood flow becomes turbulent.

## explanation_c
Incorrect (this statement is true). Above a vessel's critical velocity — the velocity at which the Reynolds number crosses its upper threshold — flow reliably becomes turbulent.

## answer_d
Turbulence can create vibrations that can be detected as murmurs.

## explanation_d
Incorrect (this statement is true). The eddy currents of turbulent flow generate vibrations that are detected clinically as murmurs and bruits.

## topic
Physiology

## subtopic

## title
Greatest influence on blood flow velocity

## question
Which of the following factors has the greatest influence on blood flow velocity?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Blood viscosity.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Blood viscosity is a secondary influence on flow velocity compared with vessel diameter.

## answer_b
Vessel length.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Vessel length is essentially fixed and has little influence on flow velocity.

## answer_c
Vessel diameter.

## explanation_c
Correct. Vessel diameter has the greatest influence on blood flow velocity, because it sets the cross-sectional area available to flow. Across the circulation, velocity is lowest wherever total cross-sectional area is largest — the capillary bed — and highest where it is smallest, regardless of how narrow any single vessel of that type is. This cross-sectional-area effect dominates over viscosity, length or pressure in determining velocity.

## answer_d
Blood pressure.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Blood pressure influences the driving force for flow but has less direct influence on velocity than vessel diameter, which sets the cross-sectional area flow must pass through.

## title
Blood flow and vessel diameter

## question
Which of the following statements is true regarding blood flow and vessel diameter?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Blood flow is inversely proportional to vessel diameter.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Blood flow is directly, not inversely, proportional to vessel diameter — a wider vessel carries more flow, not less.

## answer_b
Blood flow is directly proportional to vessel diameter.

## explanation_b
Correct. Following the F = ΔP / R relationship and Poiseuille's fourth-power radius dependence of resistance, blood flow rises steeply as vessel diameter rises, since a wider vessel offers far less resistance. Blood flow is therefore directly proportional to vessel diameter. This holds across vessel types, not only in arteries.

## answer_c
Blood flow is not affected by vessel diameter.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Vessel diameter is one of the most powerful determinants of blood flow, through its effect on resistance.

## answer_d
Blood flow is only affected by vessel diameter in arteries.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The diameter-flow relationship applies across vessel types, not only in arteries.

## title
A cause of increased blood viscosity

## question
Which of the following conditions could increase blood viscosity?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Anemia.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Anemia lowers, rather than raises, blood viscosity, since it reduces the concentration of red blood cells.

## answer_b
Dehydration.

## explanation_b
Correct. Dehydration reduces the plasma (fluid) volume relative to the cellular volume of blood, effectively haemoconcentrating it. Because blood viscosity is set chiefly by haematocrit (red-cell concentration), this haemoconcentration raises viscosity. Anemia and hypoproteinemia instead lower the concentration of red cells or plasma protein and so lower viscosity, the opposite effect.

## answer_c
Low levels of cholesterol.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Low cholesterol is not a determinant of blood viscosity, which is set chiefly by haematocrit and plasma protein.

## answer_d
Vasodilation.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Vasodilation changes a vessel's diameter and resistance; it does not raise the viscosity of the blood itself.

## id
QST-MANSAEP-AEPPHYSIO17-AEPPHYSIO17-Q16

## title
Effect of increased resistance-vessel radius

## question
When the radius of the resistance vessels is increased, which of the following is increased?

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Systolic blood pressure.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Increasing resistance-vessel (arteriolar) radius lowers, rather than raises, resistance and so tends to lower rather than raise systolic pressure.

## answer_b
Diastolic blood pressure.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Increasing arteriolar radius lowers total peripheral resistance, and diastolic pressure — which depends heavily on peripheral resistance — falls rather than rises.

## answer_c
Viscosity of the blood.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Blood viscosity is unaffected by arteriolar radius; it is set by haematocrit and plasma protein, not vessel diameter.

## answer_d
Haematocrit.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Haematocrit is a property of the blood itself and is not changed by a change in resistance-vessel radius.

## answer_e
Capillary blood flow.

## explanation_e
Correct. When the radius of the resistance vessels (arterioles) increases, resistance falls sharply by Poiseuille's fourth-power relationship, and by F = ΔP / R, flow through that bed and into the downstream capillaries rises. Capillary blood flow is therefore what increases when resistance-vessel radius increases, not blood pressure, viscosity or haematocrit.
