## title
Muscle unaffected in a torn rotator cuff

## question
A patient has a torn rotator cuff of the shoulder joint as the result of an automobile accident. Which of the following muscle tendons is intact and has normal function?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Supraspinatus

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Supraspinatus is one of the four SITS muscles that make up the rotator cuff. Its tendon blends with the joint capsule, so it is directly at risk in a rotator cuff tear. It is, in fact, the tendon most commonly torn of the four.

## answer_b
Subscapularis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Subscapularis is a rotator cuff muscle, the medial rotator of the SITS group. Its tendon also blends with the capsule anteriorly. A rotator cuff tear can involve subscapularis along with the other three.

## answer_c
Teres major

## explanation_c
Correct. Teres major is not part of the rotator cuff, despite sitting right next to teres minor and sharing a medial-rotation action with subscapularis. Its tendon inserts on the intertubercular groove without blending into the joint capsule, so it stays structurally and functionally unaffected when the true cuff muscles (SITS) tear. This is the classic distractor pairing this question is built around.

## answer_d
Teres minor

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Teres minor is one of the four SITS muscles, a lateral rotator whose tendon blends with the capsule posteriorly. It is directly at risk in a rotator cuff tear, unlike its neighbour teres major.

## answer_e
Infraspinatus

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Infraspinatus is a rotator cuff muscle, the other lateral rotator of the SITS group. Its tendon also blends with the joint capsule. It is commonly involved in cuff tears alongside supraspinatus.

## title
Posterior cord — which nerve is not a branch

## question
The posterior cord (of brachial plexus) gives, all are correct EXCEPT:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Upper subscapular nerve

## explanation_a
Incorrect (i.e. this IS a posterior cord branch). The upper subscapular nerve is one of the posterior cord's five named branches, supplying part of subscapularis. It is a genuine posterior cord derivative, unlike the long thoracic nerve.

## answer_b
Long thoracic nerve

## explanation_b
Correct — this is the exception. The long thoracic nerve arises directly from the anterior rami of C5-C7, before the trunks or cords of the plexus have even formed. It supplies serratus anterior, a muscle relevant to scapular movement, which is exactly why it is easy to mistake for a cord-level branch. Its root-level origin, not cord-level origin, is what makes it the odd one out here.

## answer_c
Lower subscapular nerve

## explanation_c
Incorrect (i.e. this IS a posterior cord branch). The lower subscapular nerve is a genuine posterior cord branch, supplying subscapularis and teres major. Like the upper subscapular nerve, it forms after the cords, not before.

## answer_d
Axillary nerve

## explanation_d
Incorrect (i.e. this IS a posterior cord branch). The axillary nerve is one of the posterior cord's two terminal branches, alongside the radial nerve. It arises well after the cords have formed, unlike the long thoracic nerve.

## title
Cause of pectoralis minor paralysis

## question
A 27-year-old patient presents with an inability to draw the scapula forward and downward because of paralysis of the pectoralis minor. Which of the following would most likely be a cause of his condition?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Fracture of the clavicle

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A clavicle fracture can injure nearby neurovascular structures but is not pectoralis minor's attachment site. Pectoralis minor's insertion is the coracoid process specifically, not the clavicle. A clavicle fracture would not directly remove the muscle's mechanical leverage the way a coracoid fracture would.

## answer_b
Injury to the posterior cord of the brachial plexus

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Injury to the posterior cord would affect axillary, radial, thoracodorsal and subscapular nerve territory, not pectoralis minor. Pectoralis minor is supplied by the medial and lateral pectoral nerves, which arise from the medial and lateral cords instead. A posterior cord lesion is therefore the wrong category of injury for this vignette.

## answer_c
Fracture of the coracoid process

## explanation_c
Correct. Pectoralis minor's entire distal attachment is the coracoid process, so a fracture there removes the muscle's leverage even though the muscle fibres and their nerve supply remain intact. This is a structural (bony), not neural, cause of losing the specific action of drawing the scapula forward and downward. The vignette's phrasing (paralysis-like loss of a very specific movement, with no other deficit mentioned) is designed to point away from a nerve-injury answer toward this structural one.

## answer_d
Axillary nerve injury

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Axillary nerve injury weakens deltoid and teres minor, producing a flattened shoulder and lost abduction, not loss of pectoralis minor's scapular action. The axillary nerve has no relationship to pectoralis minor's nerve supply. This option describes an entirely different clinical picture.

## answer_e
Defects in the posterior wall of the axilla

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Defects in the posterior wall of the axilla would threaten structures related to teres major, latissimus dorsi and the subscapular vessels, which form that wall, not pectoralis minor, which lies more anteriorly. This option targets the wrong anatomical region for pectoralis minor's own attachment.

## title
Muscle paralysed after a fall during horseback riding

## question
A 21-year-old woman walks in with a shoulder and arm injury after falling during horseback riding. Examination indicates that she cannot adduct her arm because of paralysis of which of the following muscles?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Teres minor

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Teres minor is a lateral rotator of the shoulder, part of the rotator cuff, not a prime mover of adduction. Its paralysis would weaken lateral rotation, not the adduction described here. This option targets the wrong movement entirely.

## answer_b
Supraspinatus

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Supraspinatus initiates abduction, the opposite movement from adduction. Its paralysis would weaken the first 15 degrees of raising the arm, not the ability to bring it in toward the body. It is not the muscle this vignette is describing.

## answer_c
Latissimus dorsi

## explanation_c
Correct. Latissimus dorsi extends, adducts and medially rotates the arm, and it contributes to adduction more powerfully than its synergists such as pectoralis major or teres major. A vignette isolating loss specifically of adduction, without mentioning other deficits, points to latissimus dorsi paralysis, supplied by the thoracodorsal nerve.

## answer_d
Infraspinatus

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Infraspinatus is a lateral rotator, part of the rotator cuff, and plays no major role in adduction. Its paralysis would weaken lateral rotation, similar to teres minor. This does not match the isolated adduction loss described.

## answer_e
Serratus anterior

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Serratus anterior protracts and rotates the scapula upward; its paralysis produces a winged scapula, not loss of arm adduction. It acts on the scapula, not directly on the humerus at the shoulder joint. This option describes a completely different deformity.

## title
Nerve tested by the intrinsic hand L-position

## question
A physician asks the patient to assume the L-position with his hand, which involves flexion of the metacarpophalangeal joints and extension of the interphalangeal joints of the fingers. Which of the following nerves is being tested by this position?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Deep branch of radial nerve

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The deep branch of the radial nerve (posterior interosseous nerve) supplies the extensor muscles of the forearm, not the intrinsic hand muscles that produce this posture. Its loss produces finger-drop at the MCP joints, not the interossei/lumbrical action tested here. It is a forearm-level nerve, not a hand-level one.

## answer_b
Superficial branch of radial nerve

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The superficial branch of the radial nerve is purely sensory, supplying skin over the dorsum of the hand. It has no motor function and cannot be tested by any hand posture. This option is not even the right category of nerve for a motor test.

## answer_c
Recurrent branch of median nerve

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The recurrent branch of the median nerve supplies the thenar muscles (opposition, abduction, flexion of the thumb), not the interossei/lumbricals that produce MCP flexion with IP extension. It is a purely motor branch, but to a different muscle group than the one this posture tests.

## answer_d
Deep branch of ulnar nerve

## explanation_d
Correct. The deep (motor) branch of the ulnar nerve supplies the interossei and the medial two lumbricals, which together flex the metacarpophalangeal joints while extending the interphalangeal joints — the L-position. This posture is a bedside test specifically for this branch's intact function.

## answer_e
Superficial branch of ulnar nerve

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The superficial branch of the ulnar nerve is mainly sensory, supplying skin over the medial one and a half fingers, with a small motor contribution to palmaris brevis only. It does not produce the L-position; that is the deep branch's job.

## title
Muscle supplied by the ulnar nerve

## question
Which of the following muscle is supplied by the ulnar nerve?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Brachioradialis muscle

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Brachioradialis is supplied by the radial nerve, not the ulnar nerve. It is a forearm flexor with an unusual radial-nerve supply for a flexor, but it has no relation to the ulnar nerve's territory. This option is a different nerve's muscle entirely.

## answer_b
Adductor pollicis muscle

## explanation_b
Correct. Adductor pollicis is supplied by the deep (motor) branch of the ulnar nerve, making it the exception to the general rule that thenar/thumb muscles are median-supplied. It sits near the thenar eminence but is functionally grouped with the ulnar-innervated intrinsic hand muscles rather than the true thenar group.

## answer_c
Pronator teres muscle

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Pronator teres is supplied by the median nerve, one of the median nerve's forearm targets before it enters the carpal tunnel. It has no ulnar nerve contribution. This option belongs to the median, not ulnar, nerve's territory.

## answer_d
Anconeus muscle

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Anconeus is supplied by the radial nerve, as an extensor-compartment muscle near the elbow. It shares the radial nerve's territory with brachioradialis, not the ulnar nerve's. This option is unrelated to the ulnar nerve.

## answer_e
Opponens pollicis muscle

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Opponens pollicis is one of the true thenar muscles, supplied by the recurrent (motor) branch of the median nerve, not the ulnar nerve. It is exactly the kind of thenar muscle that adductor pollicis is the exception to. Confusing the two is the trap this option sets.

Nerve severed by a laceration at the base of the thumb

## question
A 10-year-old boy was running across a parking lot when he tripped and received lacerations on the base of his thumb from a broken glass bottle. On examination, his thumb was unable to oppose to his fingers, and the thumb also showed weakness when abducting and flexing. No sensory deficits were reported. What nerve was most likely severed?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Deep branch of radial nerve

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The deep branch of the radial nerve supplies forearm extensors, not the thenar muscles that oppose, abduct and flex the thumb. It would not produce this specific motor pattern. This option is the wrong nerve for the muscles described.

## answer_b
Superficial branch of radial nerve

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The superficial branch of the radial nerve is purely sensory to the dorsum of the hand. It has no motor supply to any thumb muscle. It cannot explain the thumb weakness in this vignette.

## answer_c
Median nerve

## explanation_c
Correct. The recurrent (motor) branch of the median nerve is exactly what has been severed here — it supplies opponens pollicis, abductor pollicis brevis and flexor pollicis brevis, so its injury weakens opposition, abduction and flexion of the thumb. Because this branch runs superficially at the base of the thenar eminence and is purely motor, a laceration there can sever it in isolation without touching the sensory fibres that travel more centrally in the palm — exactly matching the 'no sensory deficits' detail in the vignette.

Deep branch of ulnar nerve

Incorrect. The deep branch of the ulnar nerve supplies adductor pollicis, the interossei and the medial two lumbricals. It is also not the superficially-lacerated branch at the base of the thumb.

## answer_e
Superficial branch of ulnar nerve

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The superficial branch of the ulnar nerve is mainly sensory to the medial hand, with minimal motor function. It has no relationship to thumb opposition, abduction or flexion. This option does not fit either the motor or sensory findings described.

## title
Origin of the radial artery

## question
What is the origin of the radial artery?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Branch of axillary artery at the neck of the radius

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The axillary artery is a much more proximal segment, continuing as the brachial artery well before the level of the radial artery's origin. It does not itself give off the radial artery directly. The intervening brachial artery segment is the true immediate parent vessel.

## answer_b
Branch of ulnar artery at the neck of the radius

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The ulnar artery is the radial artery's sibling terminal branch, not its parent vessel. Both arise together from the brachial artery's bifurcation; the ulnar artery does not branch further into the radial artery.

## answer_c
Branch of brachial artery at the neck of the humerus

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The bifurcation occurs at the level of the radius, in the cubital fossa, not at the neck of the humerus, which is a more proximal, unrelated landmark near the shoulder. This option mismatches the correct parent vessel with the wrong bony landmark.

## answer_d
Branch of brachial artery at the neck of the radius

## explanation_d
Correct. The brachial artery terminates in the cubital fossa, roughly at the level of the neck of the radius, dividing into its two terminal branches: the radial artery and the ulnar artery. The radial artery continues the brachial artery's general line down the lateral forearm to the wrist.

## answer_e
Branch of subclavian artery at the neck of the humerus

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The subclavian artery is a far more proximal vessel, continuing as the axillary and then brachial artery long before reaching the level of the radius. It does not directly branch into the radial artery, and the neck of the humerus is also the wrong landmark for this bifurcation.

## title
Insertion of extensor carpi radialis longus

## question
What is the insertion of the extensor carpi radialis longus muscle?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
First metacarpal bone

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The first metacarpal is the thumb's own metacarpal, not the insertion of any radial wrist extensor. No wrist extensor of the ECRL/ECRB pair inserts there. This option belongs to a different muscle group (thumb muscles) entirely.

## answer_b
Second metacarpal bone

## explanation_b
Correct. Extensor carpi radialis longus inserts onto the base of the second metacarpal bone. It arises from the lateral supracondylar ridge of the humerus and, together with the other radial extensors, extends and abducts the wrist.

## answer_c
Third metacarpal bone

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The third metacarpal is the insertion of extensor carpi radialis brevis, ECRL's close synonym and near-neighbour, not ECRL itself. The two muscles are easily confused precisely because their insertions sit one bone apart. This option describes brevis, not longus.

## answer_d
Fourth metacarpal bone

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The fourth metacarpal is not the insertion of either radial extensor. It lies further ulnar-ward than either ECRL or ECRB reaches. This option does not correspond to any muscle in the ECRL/ECRB pair.

## answer_e

Incorrect. The fifth metacarpal is closer to extensor carpi ulnaris's territory, on the ulnar side of the wrist, not the radial extensors. ECRL, as its name suggests, inserts on the radial side of the hand. This option is anatomically on the wrong side of the wrist for ECRL.
