## label
Superior/inferior describes position along the body's craniocaudal axis; the cervical vertebrae are superior to the lumbar vertebrae

## id
CON-MSK-B96C71A410A851

## canonical_key
anatomy.terminology.superior-inferior-vertebral-levels

## aliases
Superior and inferior anatomical terms
Vertebral column craniocaudal order

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases


## definition
In standard anatomical position, superior and inferior describe a structure's position along the body's vertical (craniocaudal) axis: a structure is superior to another if it lies closer to the head, and inferior if it lies closer to the feet. Applied to the vertebral column, the cervical vertebrae (C1-C7) form the most superior segment, immediately below the skull, while the lumbar vertebrae (L1-L5) lie inferior to the thoracic vertebrae and superior only to the sacrum and coccyx -- so relative to the cervical vertebrae, the lumbar vertebrae are inferior, not medial, lateral, posterior or superior. Superior/inferior is one of three independent descriptive axes, alongside medial/lateral (distance from the midline) and anterior/posterior (front-to-back), and the three are never interchangeable.

## explicit_objective
State that superior/inferior describes position along the body's craniocaudal axis, and rank the cervical, thoracic, lumbar, sacral and coccygeal vertebral segments from most superior to most inferior.

## pitfalls
Confusing superior/inferior (craniocaudal axis) with medial/lateral (mediolateral axis) or anterior/posterior (anteroposterior axis) -- three separate, non-interchangeable descriptive pairs that a single question can test against each other as distractors.

## label
A labrum is a fibrocartilaginous rim that deepens a joint socket

## id
CON-MSK-653B012C055B5A

## canonical_key
anatomy.joints.labrum-general-definition

## aliases
Glenoid labrum
Acetabular labrum

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases


## definition
A labrum (Latin "lip") is a ring or rim of fibrocartilage attached to the bony margin of a shallow joint socket, extending it to increase both the depth of the socket and the surface area of contact with the opposing convex bone, thereby improving joint stability without materially restricting the joint's range of movement. The two named examples are the glenoid labrum of the shoulder (deepening the glenoid cavity for the head of the humerus) and the acetabular labrum of the hip (deepening the acetabulum for the head of the femur) -- the two joints whose bony sockets are shallowest relative to the size of the ball they receive, and so the two that most need the extra depth a labrum provides. A labrum is distinct from a meniscus (a wedge of fibrocartilage that improves congruity between two nearly flat surfaces, as at the knee), an intracapsular ligament, a tendon sheath or a bursa -- none of which deepens a socket.

## explicit_objective
Define the labrum as a fibrocartilaginous rim that deepens a shallow joint socket, and name the shoulder and hip as its two examples.

## pitfalls
Confusing the labrum with a meniscus: a labrum deepens a socket for a ball-shaped bone (shoulder, hip), while a meniscus improves fit between two flatter, sliding surfaces (knee).

## label
Blood sinusoids are wide, leaky capillaries and are numerous in the spleen

## id
CON-FND-6C6B1566BE4A7F

## canonical_key
anatomy.vascular.splenic-sinusoids

## aliases
Splenic sinusoids
Sinusoidal capillaries

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases


## definition
Blood sinusoids are a specialised, wide-bore type of capillary with a discontinuous or fenestrated endothelium and an incomplete basement membrane, allowing larger particles and even whole blood cells to pass between the blood and the surrounding tissue, unlike an ordinary continuous capillary, which restricts exchange to small molecules. Sinusoids are numerous specifically in organs that need this free exchange -- the spleen (where red-pulp sinusoids let macrophages test red-cell deformability and remove aged cells), the liver, bone marrow and some endocrine glands -- and their width and irregularity also make them a low-pressure blood reservoir. This sits alongside several other true/false vessel statements commonly paired with it: veins of the lower limb do have valves (to resist gravity), venae comitantes typically accompany small- and medium-sized arteries rather than standing alone as veins, blockage of an anastomotic artery is tolerated because of the alternative route the anastomosis provides, and the pulmonary vein carries oxygenated blood from the lungs directly to the left atrium, with no second, intervening capillary bed.

## explicit_objective
State that blood sinusoids are wide, discontinuous capillaries and name the spleen as an organ where they are numerous, distinguishing this from other true/false statements about veins, venae comitantes, anastomotic arteries and the pulmonary vein.

## pitfalls
Assuming all capillaries share one structure; sinusoids are a distinct, leakier subtype built for organs (spleen, liver, marrow) whose job requires exchanging cells or large particles, not just small solutes.

## label
Only four cranial nerves carry parasympathetic fibres: III, VII, IX and X

## id
CON-FND-B2AADF9AD08075

## canonical_key
anatomy.cranial-nerves.facial-nerve-parasympathetic

## aliases
Cranial nerve parasympathetic outflow
Facial nerve parasympathetic fibres

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases


## definition
Of the twelve cranial nerves, only four carry parasympathetic (autonomic, secretomotor) fibres: the oculomotor nerve (III, to ciliary and sphincter pupillae muscles for accommodation and pupillary constriction), the facial nerve (VII, to the lacrimal, submandibular and sublingual glands and the mucosa of the nose and palate), the glossopharyngeal nerve (IX, to the parotid gland), and the vagus nerve (X, to thoracic and most abdominal viscera). The facial nerve's parasympathetic fibres arise from the superior salivatory and lacrimal nuclei and travel via the nervus intermedius before synapsing in the pterygopalatine or submandibular ganglion. The trochlear (IV), trigeminal (V), abducent (VI) and vestibulocochlear (VIII) nerves carry no parasympathetic fibres of their own: the trigeminal is sensory, with a separate motor root to the muscles of mastication only, while IV, VI and VIII are purely sensory or purely motor to muscle.

## explicit_objective
Name the four cranial nerves that carry parasympathetic fibres (III, VII, IX, X) and identify the facial nerve as one of them.

## pitfalls
Crediting the trigeminal nerve with autonomic fibres because parasympathetic fibres travel alongside some of its branches (for example to the lacrimal gland) -- those fibres actually originate from cranial nerve VII and only share part of the trigeminal's course.

## id

## canonical_key
embryology.placenta.chorion-forms-placenta

## aliases

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases


## definition
The placenta forms from two contributions that fuse at the implantation site: a fetal part, the chorion frondosum (villous chorion), built from trophoblast plus the extraembryonic mesoderm lining it, and a maternal part, the decidua basalis, formed from the endometrium at the implantation site. Of the structures commonly listed alongside chorion, the amnion is a separate, thin, avascular membrane that forms the fluid-filled sac around the embryo and takes no part in placenta formation; the yolk sac and allantois are separate extraembryonic membranes with their own roles (early nutrition and primordial germ cell origin, and urachus/umbilical vessel formation respectively) and likewise contribute nothing to the placental disc; and the hypoblast is an early bilaminar-disc layer that gives rise to the yolk sac lining, one step further removed still. Of the five listed options, only the chorion is a direct structural component of the placenta.

## explicit_objective
State that the chorion is the fetal contribution to the placenta, and distinguish it from the other extraembryonic membranes (amnion, yolk sac, allantois) by their separate roles.

## pitfalls
Confusing the four extraembryonic membranes by function -- only the chorion forms placenta; the amnion forms the fluid sac; the yolk sac contributes to the primitive gut and germ cells; the allantois contributes to the urachus and umbilical vessels.

## id

## canonical_key
embryology.gut.primitive-gut-from-yolk-sac

## aliases

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases


## definition
Following lateral and cephalocaudal folding of the trilaminar embryonic disc in the fourth week, part of the endoderm-lined yolk sac cavity is incorporated into the body of the embryo as a continuous tube: the primitive gut. The primitive gut is divided into foregut, midgut and hindgut, each retaining the endodermal lining derived from the yolk sac and going on to form the epithelial lining and glandular derivatives of the alimentary tract, while the muscular and connective-tissue coats come from surrounding splanchnic mesoderm. ## explicit_objective
State that the primitive gut develops from the yolk sac's endodermal lining, incorporated by folding, and distinguish this from the connecting stalk, chorion, allantois and amnion.

## pitfalls
Crediting the allantois (an outpouching of the yolk sac that becomes the urachus) with forming the gut itself, rather than the yolk sac's own endodermal lining, which is incorporated wholesale into the gut tube during folding.

## label
Ovum maturation (completion of meiosis II) occurs only at fertilization, not at ovulation

## id
CON-DEV-36A4C79042DAAA

## canonical_key
embryology.gametogenesis.ovum-maturation-completes-at-fertilization

## aliases
Meiosis II arrest and completion
Secondary oocyte metaphase II arrest

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases


## definition
Oogenesis arrests twice before completion. The primary oocyte, formed before birth, is arrested in prophase of meiosis I until ovulation, when the luteinising-hormone surge triggers completion of meiosis I, producing a secondary oocyte and the first polar body. The secondary oocyte then begins meiosis II but arrests again, in metaphase II, and is ovulated in this arrested state -- it is this secondary oocyte, not a fully mature ovum, that is released at ovulation and that a sperm normally penetrates. Meiosis II is completed, producing the mature ovum and the second polar body, only if and when a sperm fertilises the secondary oocyte; without fertilisation the secondary oocyte degenerates still arrested in metaphase II. So ovulation, cleavage and puberty are each events that occur before or independently of the completion of ovum maturation, and only fertilisation supplies the trigger that finishes meiosis II.

## explicit_objective
State that ovum maturation (completion of meiosis II) is triggered by fertilization, not ovulation, and describe the secondary oocyte's metaphase II arrest.

## pitfalls
Assuming ovulation releases a mature ovum; what is actually released at ovulation is a secondary oocyte arrested in metaphase II, and true maturation waits for fertilisation.

## definition
Gastrulation -- during which the primitive streak forms at the caudal end of the bilaminar embryonic disc and the primitive node (Hensen's node) forms at its cranial end -- begins early in the third week of development (around day 15-16), converting the bilaminar disc into a trilaminar disc with three germ layers. This places the primitive node's appearance after the first week (fertilisation, cleavage, blastocyst formation) and the second week (implantation, bilaminar disc formation), and before the fourth week (folding, early organogenesis) -- so of the five weeks listed, only the third is correct.

## explicit_objective
State that the primitive node appears in the third week of development, at the start of gastrulation.

## pitfalls
Losing track of the week-by-week timeline; "week 1 = one layer becoming a blastocyst, week 2 = two layers (bilaminar disc), week 3 = three layers (trilaminar disc, via the primitive streak/node)" anchors the primitive node's appearance correctly to week 3.

## arabic_label

## definition
Human chorionic gonadotrophin (hCG) is a glycoprotein hormone secreted by the syncytiotrophoblast, the outer, multinucleated, invasive layer of the trophoblast that lacks distinct cell boundaries and lies in direct contact with maternal blood once lacunae form within it. hCG maintains the corpus luteum's progesterone (and oestrogen) output through the early weeks of pregnancy, before the placenta itself takes over steroid production, and it is the hormone detected by urine and serum pregnancy tests. The cytotrophoblast, by contrast, is the inner, mononuclear, mitotically active layer that generates new cells which fuse into the syncytiotrophoblast, but it does not itself secrete hCG; fetal endothelial cells and extraembryonic mesoderm are structural/vascular components of the chorionic villi with no comparable endocrine role; and decidua basalis is the maternal contribution to the placenta, not fetal trophoblast tissue at all.

## explicit_objective
State that hCG is secreted by the syncytiotrophoblast, and distinguish the syncytiotrophoblast (secretes hCG, contacts maternal blood) from the cytotrophoblast (divides, supplies new cells).

## pitfalls
Swapping cytotrophoblast and syncytiotrophoblast -- the cytotrophoblast divides and supplies new cells, while only the syncytiotrophoblast, the layer that actually contacts maternal blood, secretes hCG.

The epidermis is keratinized stratified squamous epithelium

## arabic_label

## definition
The epidermis, the epithelial (ectodermal) component of the skin, is keratinized stratified squamous epithelium: "stratified" because it is many cell layers thick, "squamous" because its most superficial cells are flattened, and "keratinized" because those superficial cells lose their nuclei and organelles and fill instead with the tough, water-resistant protein keratin, forming a dead, protective outer layer (the stratum corneum) that is continuously shed and replaced from below. This keratinized surface distinguishes the epidermis from non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium, which lines moist internal surfaces subject to abrasion but not to the drying/protective demands of an exposed body surface -- the oesophagus, vagina and cornea are the classic examples of the non-keratinized type. Simple squamous, simple columnar and pseudostratified columnar epithelia are all single-layered (or, for pseudostratified, only apparently multilayered) and lack the many-layered, abrasion-resistant structure the epidermis needs.

## explicit_objective
Identify the epidermis as keratinized stratified squamous epithelium, and distinguish keratinized from non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium by site and function.

## pitfalls
Forgetting the keratinized/non-keratinized distinction within stratified squamous epithelium -- both are multilayered with a flattened surface, but only a surface exposed to air, like skin, keratinizes; internal, moist stratified squamous surfaces (oesophagus, vagina, cornea) do not.

Mast cells secrete histamine and heparin on degranulation

## id

## arabic_label

## definition
The mast cell is a connective-tissue cell, derived from a bone-marrow progenitor distinct from the basophil despite the two cells' similar function and appearance, whose cytoplasm is packed with metachromatic (toluidine-blue-staining) secretory granules. On stimulation -- classically by cross-linking of surface-bound IgE by an allergen, in a type I hypersensitivity reaction -- the mast cell degranulates, releasing histamine (which increases vascular permeability and causes smooth-muscle contraction and itching), heparin (an anticoagulant, and the substance responsible for the granules' metachromasia), and other mediators such as leukotrienes and eosinophil chemotactic factor. This central role in immediate (type I) hypersensitivity -- urticaria, allergic rhinitis, and anaphylaxis at its most severe -- is what makes the mast cell, rather than another connective-tissue cell, secrete histamine; matrix, steroids, antibodies and collagen fibres are each products of other, different cells (ground substance generally, the adrenal cortex, the plasma cell, and the fibroblast respectively).

## explicit_objective
Identify the mast cell as the connective-tissue cell that secretes histamine and heparin on degranulation, and distinguish it from the plasma cell (antibodies) and fibroblast (collagen).

## pitfalls
Confusing the mast cell with the plasma cell (secretes antibodies) or the fibroblast (secretes collagen) -- the mast cell's distinguishing products are histamine and heparin, released by degranulation rather than continuous synthesis and export.

## label
The Golgi apparatus is the secretory apparatus of the cell

## id
CON-FND-5CF66C0A999E21

## canonical_key
histology.organelles.golgi-is-secretory-apparatus

## aliases
Golgi apparatus function
Cell secretory apparatus

## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases


## definition
Among the cell's membranous organelles, the Golgi apparatus is specifically the one responsible for the final modification, concentration, packaging and dispatch of secretory products -- which is why it is described as the secretory apparatus of the cell. It receives newly synthesised protein from the rough endoplasmic reticulum at its cis (entry) face, chemically modifies it (glycosylation and related processing) as it passes through its stacked cisternae, and buds off the finished product from its trans (exit) face as secretory vesicles ready for release, or as primary lysosomes. This is distinct from the roles of the other membranous organelles: lysosomes digest material rather than secreting it outward; rough endoplasmic reticulum is the site of initial protein synthesis and segregation, one step upstream of the Golgi; smooth endoplasmic reticulum is chiefly concerned with lipid/steroid synthesis and detoxification, not export packaging; and mitochondria generate ATP rather than handling secretory protein at all.

## explicit_objective
Identify the Golgi apparatus as the cell's secretory apparatus, and place it downstream of rough endoplasmic reticulum in the synthesis-to-secretion pathway.

## pitfalls
Crediting rough endoplasmic reticulum with the packaging step -- rER synthesises and segregates the protein, but it is the Golgi apparatus, one step downstream, that concentrates, modifies and packages it for secretion.
