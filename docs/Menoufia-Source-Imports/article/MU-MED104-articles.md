## summary
Five MFM42Support-family exam facts share one department source and one theme — extracellular-matrix/mineral biochemistry gone wrong or misunderstood: a copper-transport defect (Menkes disease) that starves the collagen/elastin cross-linking enzyme lysyl oxidase; a fibrillin-1 defect (Marfan syndrome) that removes the scaffold elastin is deposited on; the pH-dependence of calcium solubility and absorption in the gut; which fraction of plasma calcium is actually physiologically active; and the four criteria the department's own book gives for classifying vitamin D as a hormone rather than a plain vitamin.

## sections
### Definition
Menkes disease is an X-linked defect of intestinal copper absorption and transport, so tissue copper — and every enzyme that depends on it — runs short. Marfan syndrome is an autosomal dominant defect of a different extracellular-matrix protein, fibrillin-1, the glycoprotein scaffold elastin is normally deposited onto. Calcium absorption is the uptake of dietary calcium across the gut wall, which first requires the mineral to be kept in solution, and once absorbed, only about half of plasma calcium is in the free, ionized form that is actually physiologically active. Vitamin D is classically taught as a vitamin, but the department book lists it as a prohormone: cholecalciferol made in skin, activated in two further steps, and acting through mechanisms that match a hormone far more than a dietary micronutrient.

### Mechanism
Collagen and elastin gain their mechanical strength from covalent cross-links formed after secretion, when lysyl oxidase — a copper-dependent enzyme — oxidatively deaminates lysine and hydroxylysine residues to reactive aldehydes that then condense into cross-links. Menkes disease knocks out this step by starving the enzyme of its copper cofactor, producing defective cross-linking of both collagen and elastin together, not a defect of either protein's initial synthesis. Fibrillin-1, by contrast, is not a cross-linking enzyme but a structural glycoprotein secreted by fibroblasts that forms the microfibril scaffold onto which elastin is deposited in big arteries, periosteum and the eye's suspensory ligament; a fibrillin-1 mutation in Marfan syndrome leaves elastin without a proper scaffold in exactly those tissues.

Calcium in the gut lumen must stay ionised and soluble to be absorbed. A low, acidic pH keeps calcium salts in solution and favours absorption; a higher, more alkaline pH, and binding agents such as oxalate, phytate and unabsorbed fatty acids, precipitate calcium as insoluble salts and block it. Absorption is also driven physiologically by dietary calcium load, vitamin D (calcitriol) and parathyroid hormone. Once in the plasma, calcium exists in three fractions: about 45% bound to albumin (inactive), about 5% diffusible but complexed to citrate (still inactive), and about 50% free and ionized — only this ionized fraction is physiologically active, which is why a fall in it specifically, not in total plasma calcium, precipitates tetany.

Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) is made in skin from 7-dehydrocholesterol under ultraviolet light, then hydroxylated in the liver to 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 and in the kidney to the active 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3 (calcitriol). Calcitriol then acts through a nuclear receptor, structurally related to the steroid-hormone receptor family, on distant target organs.

### Key determinants
What makes Menkes disease a cross-linking disorder rather than a synthesis disorder is that copper acts on lysyl oxidase, an enzyme working after collagen and elastin chains are already made and secreted — contrast this with vitamin C, whose lack (scurvy) instead blocks the earlier hydroxylation step, before secretion. Marfan syndrome, in turn, is neither a collagen nor an elastin defect itself but a fibrillin-1 scaffold defect — the same "extracellular matrix protein gone wrong" family as Menkes disease and scurvy, but a third distinct protein and mechanism.

What determines whether intestinal calcium stays soluble is pH (low favours solubility), the presence of competing anions (oxalate, phytate) that precipitate it, and unabsorbed fatty acids that saponify it — all independent of, and additional to, the hormonal drivers (vitamin D, PTH). Separately, once calcium is in the plasma, what determines whether it is physiologically active is not its total concentration but which fraction it is in: protein-bound and non-ionizable-diffusible calcium are both inactive, and only the ionizable diffusible fraction (about 50%) does the physiological work.

What makes vitamin D count as a hormone, on the department's own list, is: (1) a steroid-like cyclopentanoperhydrophenanthrene ring and nuclear-receptor action; (2) endogenous synthesis in skin, not dependence on diet; (3) feedback-regulated activation of both its active forms; and (4) definite, distant target organs (intestine, bone, kidney). Being present in dietary food is not on this list and is not, by itself, a reason to call it a hormone.

### Clinical significance
Menkes disease presents with brittle, kinky hair, lax skin and vessels, and progressive neurological deterioration from infancy — the connective-tissue and neurological pictures both trace back to the same copper-dependent enzymes running short. Marfan syndrome instead presents with lens dislocation, tall stature with long digits and joint hyperextensibility, and — most dangerously — progressive dilation of the ascending aorta from a weakened aortic media, all tissues where fibrillin-1's elastin scaffold is normally load-bearing.

Achlorhydria (loss of gastric acid) impairs calcium absorption precisely because it removes the acidic environment calcium solubility depends on, and this is the same logic tested when a question asks what favours calcium solubility in the gut. Separately, a change in plasma protein (as in albuminuria) shifts the protein-bound, already-inactive fraction of calcium and so causes no tetany, while a rise in plasma phosphate precipitates calcium out of the active, ionized fraction specifically and does — which is why the ionized fraction, not the total, is what a clinician (and an exam) tracks.

Recognising vitamin D as a hormone — rather than "just a vitamin" — is what explains why deficiency (or excess) produces effects that look endocrine (calcium/phosphate derangement, secondary hyperparathyroidism) rather than a typical vitamin-deficiency picture, and why its regulation sits alongside two protein hormones, parathyroid hormone and calcitonin, in calcium homeostasis.

## summary
A neurone carries several distinct classes of ion channel, each opened by a different stimulus and doing a different job; the axon's own action potential is generated and conducted specifically by the voltage-gated class.

## sections
### Definition
An ion channel is classified by what opens it. Leakage (non-gated) channels are always open at a low rate. Ligand-gated channels open when a chemical ligand binds a receptor, as at a synapse. Mechanically-gated channels open with physical deformation of the membrane, as at a mechanoreceptor. Voltage-gated channels open when the membrane potential itself crosses a threshold.

### Mechanism
The axon's resting potential is set by leakage channels and the sodium-potassium pump. When the membrane depolarises past threshold at the axon hillock, voltage-gated sodium channels open, driving the rising, depolarising phase of the action potential as sodium rushes in. As these close, voltage-gated potassium channels open and drive the falling, repolarising phase as potassium leaves. This sequential opening and closing of voltage-gated channels, propagating point by point, is what carries the impulse the length of the axon.

### Key determinants
What locates a channel type is where on the neurone it sits and what it responds to: a synaptic receptor channel on the same cell is ligand-gated, not voltage-gated, because it answers to a neurotransmitter rather than to membrane potential; a stretch receptor's channel is mechanically-gated because it answers to deformation. The axon membrane along its conducting length is dominated by the voltage-gated class precisely because propagating an all-or-none impulse requires a channel that responds to the potential change the impulse itself creates.

### Clinical significance
Local anaesthetics and agents such as tetrodotoxin act by blocking voltage-gated sodium channels specifically, silencing conduction along the axon without touching the ligand-gated channels at the synapse beyond it — which is why a nerve block anaesthetises a region without needing to act at every synapse in it.

## summary
Two bone-pathology facts from the same MSK2 end-module paper: giant cell tumour of bone sits in its own "locally malignant" behaviour class, distinct from both benign and frankly malignant primary bone tumours, while a sequestrum is the dead, avascular fragment of bone left behind when osteomyelitis cuts off the local blood supply.

## sections
### Definition
Giant cell tumour of bone (osteoclastoma) is a primary bone tumour of unknown histogenetic origin. A sequestrum is a dead (avascular) fragment of bone produced in the course of osteomyelitis.

### Mechanism
Giant cell tumour arises typically in the epiphysis of adults aged 20-50 (male predominance), classically around the knee, distal radius or sacrum. It expands the epiphysis with multiple blood-filled cysts, thinning the cortex to produce the "soap-bubble" X-ray appearance; microscopically it shows numerous multinucleated giant cells evenly distributed against a background of plump mononuclear cells, with areas of haemorrhage.

In acute osteomyelitis, a subperiosteal abscess elevates the periosteum and cuts off the blood supply to the underlying cortex; the resulting dead bone is the sequestrum, gradually walled off from living bone by granulation tissue. The elevated periosteum simultaneously lays down new living bone around it — the involucrum — so the two structures, one dead and one newly formed, appear together on the same radiograph.

### Key determinants
The department's own primary-bone-tumour classification table places tumours in three behaviour columns — benign, locally malignant, malignant — by histologic type. Giant cell tumour and adamantinoma sit in the middle, "locally malignant," column: locally aggressive and prone to recurrence after curettage, but not metastasising the way a true sarcoma (osteosarcoma, in the malignant column) does. This is why it should be called neither simply "benign" nor simply "malignant."

A sequestrum is avascular, so on X-ray it appears denser (more radio-opaque) than the living, remodelling bone around it — the opposite of what impaired blood supply usually produces in soft tissue. Its dead, walled-off nature (isolated from both the bloodstream and systemic antibiotics) is what makes it a nidus for chronic, relapsing infection until it is surgically removed.

### Clinical significance
An osteolytic epiphyseal mass with numerous evenly distributed multinucleated giant cells on biopsy in a 20-50-year-old points to giant cell tumour, and its locally malignant behaviour (rather than benign or frankly malignant) is what should guide counselling on recurrence risk after curettage.

X-ray evidence of a dense, separated bone fragment (sequestrum) alongside a periosteal reaction (involucrum) in a patient with chronic bone pain and discharging sinuses points to chronic osteomyelitis, and the presence of a sequestrum specifically predicts that antibiotics alone will not clear the infection — the dead bone requires surgical removal (sequestrectomy).

## summary
Three related facts from the same MED104 Biochemistry department-book pages: the five taught physiological functions of calcium (and why "coenzyme of a kinase" is not among them), vitamin D's bone-specific action of stimulating mineral deposition, and calcitonin's opposite-to-PTH action of suppressing bone resorption to lower plasma calcium.

## sections
### Definition
Calcium's department-taught functions span structural (bone and teeth), electrical (nerve impulses, neuromuscular excitability), contractile (muscle contraction), haemostatic (blood and milk clotting) and enzymatic/endocrine (activating lipase and phosphorylase; regulating hormone secretion such as insulin) roles. Two hormones then act on bone to move calcium in opposite directions: vitamin D (calcitriol) stimulates deposition of calcium and phosphate into bone (mineralization), while calcitonin inhibits mobilization of calcium out of bone (resorption).

### Mechanism
Calcium's five taught functions are not interchangeable with every calcium-dependent process found elsewhere in physiology — the department's own list names lipase and phosphorylase as the enzymes calcium activates, not a kinase, which is exactly the distinction a "which of these is incorrect" question is built to test.

Vitamin D raises plasma calcium and phosphate through three target organs: intestine (induces a calcium-binding protein that increases absorption), kidney (increases tubular reabsorption) and bone (stimulates deposition of calcium and phosphate, i.e. mineralization — supplying and driving the mineral that osteoblasts lay down as new bone matrix). Calcitonin acts on one target, bone, and in the opposite direction to parathyroid hormone: by inhibiting mobilization (resorption) of calcium from bone, it lowers rather than raises plasma calcium.

### Key determinants
What separates a true calcium function from a plausible-sounding distractor is the department's own specific list — bone/teeth constituent, nerve/neuromuscular/contractile regulation, clotting, lipase/phosphorylase activation, hormone-secretion regulation — with no kinase-coenzyme role among them.

What separates vitamin D's bone action from calcitonin's is direction: vitamin D deposits mineral into bone (mineralization, working with osteoblasts), while calcitonin suppresses mineral being taken out of bone (resorption, opposing osteoclast activity) — both end up raising or lowering plasma calcium, but by acting on bone in opposite directions.

### Clinical significance
Recognising calcium's true functional list matters for interpreting a deficiency or excess state — a nerve-impulse, clotting, or bone-mineralization abnormality points to a calcium-mediated process, while attributing a kinase-coenzyme role to calcium is a plausible-sounding but untaught distractor.

Vitamin D deficiency or excess produces effects at all three of its target organs (intestine, kidney, bone) together, while calcitonin's clinical role is narrower — mainly relevant to its bone-resorption-suppressing action, which is why it is used therapeutically to lower plasma calcium in hypercalcaemia and to slow bone resorption in some metabolic bone diseases.

## summary
Three structural facts, one anatomy/histology grouping: which tissue forms each wall of the inguinal canal, why articular cartilage can only grow by interstitial (not appositional) growth, and what a Haversian system (osteon) is organized around.

## sections
### Definition
The inguinal canal has four walls, each formed by a different abdominal-wall layer: an aponeurotic anterior wall, a fascial/tendinous posterior wall, a muscular roof and a ligamentous floor. Hyaline cartilage grows by two distinct processes — interstitial (chondrocyte division and matrix synthesis from within) and appositional (new chondroblasts differentiating from the perichondrium) — but articular cartilage, lacking a perichondrium on its joint surface, can only use the first. A Haversian system (osteon) is the structural unit of compact bone: concentric bone lamellae organized around a central, blood-vessel-carrying canal.

### Mechanism
The inguinal canal's roof is formed by the arching fibers of internal oblique and transversus abdominis as they arch over the canal's contents before inserting into the conjoint tendon; its floor is the inguinal ligament (the rolled-under lower border of the external oblique aponeurosis), reinforced medially by the lacunar ligament. This muscular-roof/ligamentous-floor arrangement, together with an aponeurotic anterior wall and fascial/tendinous posterior wall, is what lets the canal serve as a controlled route for the spermatic cord (or round ligament) through an otherwise continuous abdominal wall.

Articular cartilage and the epiphyseal growth plates of long bones are the two sites named for continuing interstitial growth (chondrocyte division and matrix synthesis from within) beyond the earliest stages of cartilage formation, precisely because neither has a perichondrium — the source of the chondrogenic cells that appositional growth depends on.

A Haversian system's concentric lamellae, each a ring of osteocytes in lacunae connected by canaliculi, are built around a central Haversian canal that itself carries loose connective tissue rich in blood vessels, lymphatics and nerves, lined by osteogenic cells and osteoblasts. Transverse Volkmann's canals connect adjacent Haversian systems to each other and to the periosteum and marrow cavity, but they are not what the osteon's own lamellae are organized around.

### Key determinants
Naming the roof requires naming a muscle layer (arching internal oblique/transversus abdominis fibers); naming the floor requires naming a ligament (inguinal/lacunar) — the anterior (aponeurosis) and posterior (fascia transversalis/conjoint tendon) walls are neither.

What makes articular cartilage's growth mode interstitial rather than appositional is simply the absence of a perichondrium at the joint surface — appositional growth is not wrong in general for cartilage, only for the specific, perichondrium-free surfaces a question about a joint is asking about.

What makes a structure a Haversian system rather than a Volkmann's canal is orientation and role: Haversian canals run longitudinally and are what the concentric lamellae are built around; Volkmann's canals run transversely/obliquely and connect Haversian systems to each other, sharing the same vascular contents but not the same organizing role.

### Clinical significance
Correctly locating the roof and floor of the inguinal canal is what underlies surgical hernia repair, since indirect and direct inguinal hernias each exploit a different weak point in this four-wall arrangement.

Recognising that articular cartilage repairs itself (when it does) only through interstitial growth from surviving chondrocytes — since it cannot recruit new appositional growth from a perichondrium it does not have — is part of why articular cartilage damage (as in osteoarthritis) heals so poorly compared with perichondrium-covered cartilage elsewhere.

A bone biopsy question describing lamellae organized around a central, vascular canal is describing a normal Haversian system, the everyday structural unit examined whenever compact bone histology or a metabolic bone disease affecting osteon structure is being taught.

## summary
Three muscle-ultrastructure facts from the same MED104 Physiology/Histology department-book pages: how a sarcomere is defined (the distance between two Z lines), what replaces T tubules in smooth muscle (caveolae), and why ATP depletion after death causes rigor mortis.

## sections
### Definition
A sarcomere is the functional contractile unit of skeletal muscle, defined as the distance between two successive Z lines. Smooth muscle has no T-tubule system at all; in its place, subsarcolemmal caveolae serve the equivalent conducting role. Rigor mortis is the postmortem stiffening of muscle caused by depletion of ATP after death.

### Mechanism
Each sarcomere spans one entire A band (formed by overlapping thick and thin filaments) plus half of the I band on either side, with the Z line marking its own boundary at the centre of each I band; when a muscle contracts, thin filaments slide further onto thick filaments, the sarcomere shortens, and its Z lines move closer together while the A band's own length stays fixed.

Caveolae are vesicular invaginations of the smooth muscle sarcolemma, clustered just beneath the membrane, which the histology department book states function like T tubules in conducting the contraction impulse and, together with the cell's poorly developed sarcoplasmic reticulum, help regulate cytoplasmic calcium — this is necessary because smooth muscle draws about 90% of its contractile calcium from the extracellular fluid rather than an internal store the way skeletal muscle's triad system does.

ATP is required for the power stroke of contraction, but also, separately, to break the actin-myosin cross bridge so the myosin head can detach and reset. After death, ATP production stops and calcium pumps fail, leaving actin binding sites exposed; myosin cross bridges attach to these sites but, without ATP, cannot detach, so the muscle becomes rigid until the bound proteins themselves deteriorate.

### Key determinants
The sarcomere is measured Z-line to Z-line, not A-band, H-zone, I-band or M-line to its counterpart — the Z line is the only landmark that sits at the sarcomere's own boundary.

What identifies caveolae as the T-tubule equivalent, rather than dense bodies, attachment plaques, desmin or gap junctions, is their conducting role: dense bodies and attachment plaques anchor actin (the Z-line-equivalent role), desmin is a cytoskeletal filament protein, and gap junctions couple adjacent cells electrically — only caveolae are named as functioning like T tubules in conducting the impulse into the cell.

What determines rigor mortis's mechanism is that ATP is needed for cross-bridge detachment, not formation — the muscle is not failing to contract, it is failing to relax, because bridges that already formed cannot let go.

### Clinical significance
Sarcomere length is the physiological basis of the length-tension relationship in muscle, and abnormal sarcomere structure or spacing underlies several inherited myopathies.

Smooth muscle's caveolae-based, T-tubule-free calcium handling is part of why smooth muscle contraction is slower and more sustained (tonic) than skeletal muscle's fast, phasic, T-tubule-triggered contraction.

Rigor mortis's timing (onset within hours, resolution once muscle proteins deteriorate) is used forensically to estimate time since death, and understanding its ATP-depletion mechanism is what explains both why it develops and why it eventually passes.
