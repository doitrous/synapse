## title
Roof of the inguinal canal

## question
A 54 years old male suffered from inguinal hernia that passes through the inguinal canal. Which one of the following forms the roof of this structure?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Arching fibers

## explanation_a
Correct. The roof of the inguinal canal is formed by the arching fibers of the internal oblique and transversus abdominis muscles as they arch over the canal's contents before inserting into the conjoint tendon medially. This muscular roof is what allows the canal to squeeze onto its contents during raised intra-abdominal pressure, one of the mechanisms protecting against hernia. It is anatomically distinct from the canal's floor, which is ligamentous rather than muscular.

## answer_b
Conjoint tendon

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The conjoint tendon (the fused medial insertion of internal oblique and transversus abdominis) reinforces the posterior wall of the canal, not the roof — it is the medial end-point the arching roof fibers insert into, not the roof itself.

## answer_c
External oblique

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The aponeurosis of the external oblique muscle forms the anterior wall of the inguinal canal, reinforced laterally by fibers of internal oblique; it plays no part in forming the roof.

## answer_d
Fascia transversalis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Fascia transversalis forms the posterior wall of the inguinal canal (reinforced medially by the conjoint tendon), which is why a direct inguinal hernia — pushing through a weak posterior wall — is described as passing through fascia transversalis, not the roof.

## answer_e
Inguinal ligament

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The inguinal ligament (the rolled-under lower border of the external oblique aponeurosis) forms the floor of the inguinal canal, reinforced medially by the lacunar ligament — the opposite wall from the roof this question asks about.

## title
Physiologically active form of plasma calcium

## question
The only physiologically active form of calcium is:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Ionizable diffusible

## explanation_a
Correct. About 50% of plasma calcium exists as the ionizable, diffusible fraction, and this is the only physiologically active form. It is this fraction that nerve and muscle excitability and the coagulation cascade actually respond to, and it is a fall in this fraction specifically, not in total plasma calcium, that precipitates tetany.

## answer_b
Ionizable non diffusible

## explanation_b
Incorrect. "Ionizable non-diffusible" is not one of the department's named plasma calcium fractions — being ionizable and diffusible go together for the active fraction; a non-diffusible fraction (bound to plasma proteins, mainly albumin) is instead described as non-ionized, not ionizable.

## answer_c
Non ionizable diffusible

## explanation_c
Incorrect. About 5% of plasma calcium is diffusible but complexed to anions such as citrate, making it non-ionizable and therefore still physiologically inactive — being diffusible alone is not enough for activity if the calcium is not free (ionized).

## answer_d
Non ionizable non diffusible

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The non-diffusible, non-ionized fraction (about 45% of plasma calcium) is calcium bound to plasma proteins, mainly albumin; being bound to a large protein keeps it both non-diffusible and physiologically inactive.

## answer_e
Total calcium

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Total plasma calcium lumps together all three fractions — protein-bound (inactive), non-ionizable diffusible (inactive) and ionizable diffusible (active) — so a normal total can coexist with an abnormal active fraction, which is exactly why total calcium alone is not the answer to "which form is active."

## title
Vitamin D's action on bone in remodeling

## question
In bone remodling, vitamin D,

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
stimulate osteoclasts

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Stimulating osteoclasts describes a bone-resorbing action, which is not vitamin D's own taught bone action; the department book's own wording for vitamin D's effect on bone is that it "stimulates deposition of Ca2+ and phosphate" (mineralization), an osteoblast-mediated, bone-forming action, not an osteoclast-driven resorptive one.

## answer_b
promotes bone resorption

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Promoting bone resorption is the opposite of what the department book teaches for vitamin D's bone action — vitamin D stimulates deposition of calcium and phosphate into bone (mineralization), not their release from it.

## answer_c
increases Ca excretion

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Vitamin D increases, not decreases, renal tubular reabsorption of calcium (i.e. it decreases urinary calcium excretion), as one of its three coordinated target-organ actions alongside intestine and bone.

## answer_d
stimulate osteoblasts

## explanation_d
Correct. The department book states that vitamin D stimulates deposition of calcium and phosphate in bone and teeth, i.e. bone mineralization — an action carried out by osteoblasts, the bone-forming cells that lay down and mineralize new matrix. This is one of vitamin D's three coordinated actions (intestine, kidney, bone) that together raise plasma calcium and phosphate.

## answer_e
inhibit calcium absorption

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Vitamin D increases, not inhibits, intestinal calcium absorption — it induces synthesis of a calcium-binding protein in the intestine that increases calcium uptake, the opposite of this option's claim.

## title
Gene defect causing Marfan syndrome

## question
Marfan syndrome is caused by defect in:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
fibronectin

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Fibronectin is a different extracellular-matrix glycoprotein involved chiefly in cell adhesion to the matrix; it is not the protein whose gene is mutated in Marfan syndrome.

## answer_b
collagen

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Collagen defects underlie other connective-tissue disorders (such as some forms of Ehlers-Danlos syndrome or osteogenesis imperfecta), not Marfan syndrome, which is instead a fibrillin-1 gene defect.

## answer_c
laminin

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Laminin is a basement-membrane glycoprotein, structurally and functionally unrelated to the microfibril scaffold fibrillin forms; it is not implicated in Marfan syndrome.

## answer_d
fibrillin

## explanation_d
Correct. Marfan syndrome is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by mutations in the fibrillin-1 gene. Fibrillin is a glycoprotein secreted by fibroblasts that forms the microfibril scaffold onto which elastin is deposited in big arteries, the periosteum and the eye's suspensory ligament, which is why its defect produces the syndrome's characteristic cardiovascular (aortic dilation), skeletal (arachnodactyly, joint hyperextensibility) and ocular (lens dislocation) features.

## answer_e
elastine

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Elastin itself is the protein deposited onto the fibrillin scaffold, not the protein that is genetically defective in Marfan syndrome — the defect is one step upstream, in fibrillin, the scaffold elastin depends on.

## title
Type and growth of articular cartilage

## question
An old aged female complains of pain in the knee joints. X-RAY reveals damage of articular cartilage. What is the type of cartilage in this joint & how does it grow:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
yellow elastic cartilage & grows by appositional growth

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Articular cartilage is hyaline, not yellow elastic cartilage (found instead in structures like the epiglottis and external ear); and hyaline articular cartilage grows by interstitial, not appositional, growth, so both parts of this option are wrong.

## answer_b
hyaline cartilage & grows by appositional growth

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Articular cartilage is correctly identified as hyaline here, but its growth mode is wrong: because articular cartilage has no perichondrium on its free (joint) surface, it cannot use appositional growth (which depends on chondrogenic cells differentiating from the perichondrium) — it grows only interstitially.

## answer_c
hyaline cartilage & grows by interstitial growth

## explanation_c
Correct. Knee joint articular cartilage is hyaline cartilage. Hyaline cartilage normally grows by two processes, interstitial and appositional, but articular cartilage specifically has no perichondrium covering its joint surface, so it continues to grow only by interstitial growth — division of pre-existing chondrocytes and matrix synthesis from within.

## answer_d
fibro cartilage & grows by interstitial growth

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Articular cartilage in a synovial joint like the knee is hyaline, not fibrocartilage (which is instead found in structures like the intervertebral discs and menisci); the growth mode named, interstitial, is correct for articular cartilage, but the cartilage type is wrong.

## answer_e
fibro cartilage & grows by appositional growth

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Both parts are wrong: knee joint articular cartilage is hyaline, not fibrocartilage, and hyaline articular cartilage grows by interstitial growth, not appositional growth, since it lacks the perichondrium appositional growth requires.

## title
Correct statement about Haversian systems

## question
Bone biopsy obtained at the autopsy of a 68-years-old man femur is examined by light microscopy. Which one of the following is a correct statement concerning Haversian systems (osteons)?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
are found in fetal skeletons

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Haversian systems are the structural unit of mature compact bone, not of the fetal skeleton, which is built initially from woven bone that is later remodelled into the lamellar, Haversian architecture of adult compact bone.

## answer_b
are found in spongy bone

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Haversian systems are the structural unit of compact (dense) bone, found for example in the shafts of long bones; spongy (cancellous) bone instead consists of trabeculae without the same concentric-lamellae-around-a-canal organisation.

## answer_c
develop around a Volkman's canal

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A Volkmann's canal is a transverse or oblique canal that connects adjacent Haversian systems to each other and to the periosteum and marrow cavity — Haversian systems connect to Volkmann's canals, but their own concentric lamellae are organized around their own (Haversian) canal, not around a Volkmann's canal.

## answer_d
develop around a blood vessel

## explanation_d
Correct. A Haversian system (osteon) is built of 4-20 concentric lamellae of osteocytes in their lacunae, arranged around a central Haversian canal. That canal contains loose connective tissue rich in blood vessels (and lymphatics and nerves), so the osteon develops around, and its lamellae are organized concentrically around, this central blood-vessel-carrying canal.

## answer_e
develop around a Sharpey's fiber

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Sharpey's fibers are collagen fibers that anchor the periosteum (or tendons/ligaments) to the outer circumferential lamellae of bone, not the structure a Haversian system's own concentric lamellae are organized around.

## title
Cause of rigor mortis stiffness (Group 1 wording)

## question
A cadaver discovered two days after death showing Rigor Mortis Signs in the form of Stiffening of the joints and muscles. What is the cause of the stifness?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Increased Acetyl choline release

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Acetylcholine release triggers excitation-contraction coupling during life, but it is not what maintains rigor mortis after death — the stiffness of rigor mortis comes from ATP-depleted cross bridges that cannot detach, not from ongoing neurotransmitter release, which stops with death.

## answer_b
Increased cross-bridge duration

## explanation_b
Correct. After death, ATP is depleted, so the calcium pumps stop working and the actin-myosin cross bridges that form can no longer detach — they remain bound for an abnormally long, effectively indefinite duration instead of the brief, rapidly cycling attachment-detachment of normal contraction. This increased (permanent) cross-bridge duration is what produces the sustained muscle rigidity of rigor mortis.

## answer_c
Increased extracellular calcium

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Extracellular calcium concentration is not what drives rigor mortis; the relevant failure is intracellular — calcium pumps stop working after ATP is depleted, leaving cytoplasmic calcium elevated and actin sites exposed, but the option's framing ("increased extracellular calcium") does not describe this mechanism.

## answer_d
Increased muscle cell excitability

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Rigor mortis is not a state of increased muscle cell excitability — the cell's electrical excitability is lost entirely after death; the stiffness is a purely mechanical, ATP-dependent cross-bridge phenomenon, not an excitability change.

## answer_e
Increased muscle adenosine triphosphate

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Rigor mortis results from decreased, not increased, ATP — it is precisely the depletion of ATP after death that prevents cross-bridge detachment and causes the sustained rigidity.
