## title
Anatomical relation of lumbar to cervical vertebrae (inferior)

## question
What is the correct anatomical term that describes the relationship of the lumbar vertebrae to the cervical vertebrae?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Medial

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Medial describes distance from the midline in the coronal plane, a different axis from the head-to-foot position being asked about here.

## answer_b
Lateral

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Lateral, like medial, describes the mediolateral axis, not the craniocaudal (head-to-foot) axis that relates the lumbar and cervical vertebrae to each other.

## answer_c
Posterior

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Posterior describes the anteroposterior (front-to-back) axis, a third axis unrelated to the vertebral column's head-to-foot ordering.

## answer_d
Superior

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Superior would describe the lumbar vertebrae as closer to the head than the cervical vertebrae, the opposite of their actual relationship.

## answer_e
Inferior

## explanation_e
Correct. Superior and inferior describe position along the body's craniocaudal axis: the cervical vertebrae (C1-C7) form the most superior spinal segment, immediately below the skull, while the lumbar vertebrae (L1-L5) lie inferior to the thoracic vertebrae and superior only to the sacrum and coccyx. Relative to the cervical vertebrae, then, the lumbar vertebrae are inferior -- closer to the feet along the vertebral column's own craniocaudal sequence. This is a pure application of the standard anatomical-position vocabulary, independent of the medial/lateral and anterior/posterior axes offered as distractors.

## title
Muscle role that prevents unwanted movement at intermediate joints (synergist)

## question
Which term of the followings describes the muscles that contract to prevent the unwanted movements at the intermediate joints?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Prime mover

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The prime mover (agonist) is the muscle that initiates and maintains the movement itself, not the muscle that cancels unwanted side-effects at other joints it crosses.

## answer_b
Agonist

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Agonist is another name for the prime mover, the muscle producing the movement, not the one preventing unwanted movement elsewhere.

## answer_c
Antagonist

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The antagonist opposes the prime mover's own action at the joint being moved; it does not specifically address unwanted movement at intermediate joints the prime mover crosses.

## answer_d
Synergist

## explanation_d
Correct. The synergist contracts to cancel out unwanted movements that the prime mover would otherwise produce at other, intermediate joints it crosses on its way to the joint that matters, so that the prime mover's action there becomes maximal. A classic example is the wrist extensors acting as synergists during finger flexion, preventing the long flexor tendons from also flexing the wrist as they cross it. This is distinct from the fixator, which stabilises the prime mover's own origin rather than an intermediate joint.

## answer_e
Fixator

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The fixator (stabiliser) fixes the origin of the prime mover, or stabilises the joint the prime mover acts on, so its pull has something to work against -- a related but distinct job from the synergist's role at intermediate joints.

## title
Structures that are eight in number (carpal bones)

## question
Which of the followings are eight (8) in number?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Carpal bones

## explanation_a
Correct. The carpus (wrist) is built of eight carpal bones in two rows of four: proximally the scaphoid, lunate, triquetrum and pisiform, and distally the trapezium, trapezoid, capitate and hamate. A hand skeleton listed with only seven bones in this group is short one -- the commonest distractor against the correct count of eight. This count is fixed and does not vary the way, for example, the number of ribs or vertebral segments can show minor normal variation.

## answer_b
Metatarsal bones

## explanation_b
Incorrect. There are five metatarsal bones, one for each digit of the foot, not eight.

## answer_c
Tarsal bones

## explanation_c
Incorrect. There are seven tarsal bones (talus, calcaneus, navicular, cuboid, three cuneiforms), not eight.

## answer_d
Cervical vertebrae

## explanation_d
Incorrect. There are seven cervical vertebrae (C1-C7), a number many students confuse with the eight carpal bones precisely because both are commonly tested landmark counts.

## answer_e
Sacral vertebrae

## explanation_e
Incorrect. There are five sacral vertebrae before they fuse into the single sacrum, not eight.

## title
Fibrocartilaginous lip that deepens a joint socket (labrum)

## question
Which of the followings describes the fibrocartilaginous lip that extends from the edge of a joint socket to deepen it?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Intracapsular ligament

## explanation_a
Incorrect. An intracapsular ligament is a ligament lying within the joint capsule (such as the cruciate ligaments of the knee), not a rim of fibrocartilage deepening a socket.

## answer_b
Tendon sheath

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A tendon sheath is a synovial-lined tube that reduces friction on a tendon running past a joint; it plays no role in deepening a bony socket.

## answer_c
Meniscus

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A meniscus is a wedge of fibrocartilage that improves congruity between two nearly flat articulating surfaces (as at the knee), a different job from deepening a socket for a ball-shaped bone.

## answer_d
Labrum

## explanation_d
Correct. A labrum (Latin 'lip') is a ring of fibrocartilage attached to the bony margin of a shallow joint socket, extending it to increase both the depth of the socket and the contact area with the opposing convex bone. The two named examples are the glenoid labrum of the shoulder and the acetabular labrum of the hip, the two joints whose bony sockets are shallowest relative to the ball they receive. This improves stability while preserving the joint's mobility, unlike a meniscus, ligament, tendon sheath or bursa, none of which deepens a socket.

## answer_e
Bursa

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A bursa is a fluid-filled sac that reduces friction between a tendon or muscle and adjacent bone or skin; it does not deepen a joint socket.

## title
Correct statement about blood vessels (splenic sinusoids)

## question
As regard blood vessels, which of the following statements is correct?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Veins of the lower limb have no valves

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Veins of the lower limb are richly supplied with valves, precisely because they must resist gravity to return blood to the heart; the opposite statement is true.

## answer_b
Venae commitantes accompany medium-sized veins

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Venae comitantes (paired veins) typically accompany small- and medium-sized arteries, not veins in isolation, running alongside the artery within a shared fascial sheath.

## answer_c
Blockage of anastomotic arteries causes severe tissue damage

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Blockage of an anastomotic artery is characteristically well tolerated because the anastomosis provides an alternative route for blood flow, unlike blockage of an end-artery, which does cause severe tissue damage.

## answer_d
Pulmonary vein is interposed between two capillary beds

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The pulmonary vein carries oxygenated blood directly from the lung capillaries to the left atrium, with no second, intervening capillary bed -- that two-bed-in-series arrangement is instead the defining feature of a portal circulation.

## answer_e
Blood sinusoids are numerous in the spleen

## explanation_e
Correct. Blood sinusoids are a wide-bore, discontinuous- or fenestrated-endothelium capillary type that allows free passage of larger particles and even whole blood cells, unlike an ordinary continuous capillary. They are numerous specifically in organs that need this free exchange -- the spleen (where red-pulp sinusoids let macrophages test red-cell deformability and remove aged cells), the liver and the bone marrow -- and their width also makes them a low-pressure blood reservoir.

## title
Enlarged lymph node group after a breast abscess (axillary)

## question
A 31-year-old female patient had a breast abscess. A few days later, the lymph nodes in her armpit were enlarged. Which one of these groups of lymph nodes was affected?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Cervical

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The cervical lymph nodes drain the head and neck, not the breast, and are not located in the armpit.

## answer_b
Axillary

## explanation_b
Correct. Approximately 75% of breast lymph drains to the axillary lymph nodes, especially the pectoral group, which is precisely why an infective or malignant breast process classically presents with palpable, enlarged nodes in the armpit. This makes the axillary group the first and most important site to examine clinically whenever a breast pathology, infective or neoplastic, is suspected.

## answer_c
Inguinal

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The inguinal lymph nodes drain the lower limb, perineum and lower anal canal, not the breast, and lie in the groin rather than the armpit.

## answer_d
Supraclavicular

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The supraclavicular nodes (Virchow's node on the left being classically linked to abdominal malignancy) lie above the clavicle, not in the armpit, and are not the primary drainage site for the breast.

## answer_e
Mediastinal

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The mediastinal lymph nodes lie within the thoracic cavity and drain thoracic viscera; they are not palpable in the armpit and are not the breast's chief lymphatic drainage.

## title
Cranial nerve carrying parasympathetic fibres (facial, VII)

## question
Which of the following cranial nerves carry parasympathetic fibers?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
IV (trochlear)

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The trochlear nerve is purely motor, supplying only superior oblique; it carries no parasympathetic fibres.

## answer_b
V (trigeminal)

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The trigeminal nerve is chiefly sensory, with a separate motor root to the muscles of mastication only; the parasympathetic fibres that travel alongside some of its branches actually originate from cranial nerve VII and merely share part of its course.

## answer_c
VI (abducent)

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The abducent nerve is purely motor, supplying only lateral rectus; it carries no parasympathetic fibres.

## answer_d
VII (facial)

## explanation_d
Correct. Of the twelve cranial nerves, only four carry parasympathetic fibres: III, VII, IX and X. The facial nerve's parasympathetic fibres arise from the superior salivatory and lacrimal nuclei and travel via the nervus intermedius before synapsing in the pterygopalatine or submandibular ganglion, ultimately supplying the lacrimal, submandibular and sublingual glands and the mucosa of the nose and palate.

## answer_e
VIII (vestibulocochlear)

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The vestibulocochlear nerve is purely sensory, carrying hearing and balance information; it carries no parasympathetic fibres.

Which one of the following contributes to the formation of the placenta?

## subject

## vignette

Chorion

Correct. Allantois

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The allantois contributes to the urachus and, via its vessels, to the umbilical arteries and vein, but it is not itself a structural component of the placental disc.

## answer_e
Hypoblast

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The hypoblast is an early bilaminar-disc layer that gives rise to the yolk sac lining, one step further removed from placenta formation still.

Origin of the primitive gut (yolk sac)

## question
The primitive gut is developed from which one of the followings?

## subject
dev

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Connecting stalk

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The connecting stalk becomes the umbilical cord's connective-tissue core, not the gut.

## answer_b
Chorion frondosum

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The chorion frondosum contributes to the placenta, not to the gut tube.

## answer_c
Yolk sac

## explanation_c
Correct. Following lateral and cephalocaudal folding of the trilaminar embryonic disc in the fourth week, part of the endoderm-lined yolk sac cavity is incorporated into the body of the embryo as a continuous tube: the primitive gut. The primitive gut divides into foregut, midgut and hindgut, each retaining the endodermal lining derived from the yolk sac and going on to form the epithelial lining and glandular derivatives of the alimentary tract, while surrounding splanchnic mesoderm supplies the muscular and connective-tissue coats.

## answer_d
Allantois

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The allantois contributes to the urachus and umbilical vessels, not to the gut tube itself.

## answer_e
Amnion

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The amnion forms the fluid-filled amniotic sac around the embryo; it does not form the gut.

## title
Characteristic feature of placenta accreta (villous invasion of myometrium)

## question
Placenta accreta is characterized by which one of the followings?

## subject
dev

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Separation of the placenta from the uterine wall

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Premature separation of an otherwise normally attached placenta from the uterine wall describes placental abruption, a different condition from accreta.

## answer_b
Invasion of chorionic villi into the myometrium

## explanation_b
Correct. Placenta accreta is an abnormality of placental attachment in which chorionic villi attach directly to, or invade into, the myometrium, because the decidua basalis is deficient or absent at the implantation site, rather than remaining confined to a normal decidual plane. This depth-of-invasion abnormality is why the placenta cannot separate normally at delivery, carrying a high risk of massive postpartum haemorrhage.

## answer_c
Small size of the placenta

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Placenta accreta is not defined by placental size; abnormal size is a separate finding (as in a small placenta from placental insufficiency) unrelated to the depth of villous invasion.

## answer_d
Placenta is empty in the middle

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A placenta 'empty in the middle' describes placenta fenestrata (a central defect), a structural variant distinct from accreta's abnormal depth of invasion.

## answer_e
Presents in the lower segment of the uterus

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Presenting in the lower uterine segment, over or near the internal os, describes placenta praevia, a positional abnormality distinct from accreta, though the two conditions can coexist.

## title
When ovum maturation is completed (fertilization)

## question
The completion of ovum maturation occurs after which one of the followings?

## subject
dev

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Birth

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Birth occurs long before ovum maturation is completed; primary oocytes remain arrested in meiotic prophase from before birth until much later.

## answer_b
Cleavage

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Cleavage (the early mitotic divisions of the zygote) happens after fertilization has already triggered completion of meiosis II, so it follows rather than causes ovum maturation.

## answer_c
Fertilization

## explanation_c
Correct. Oogenesis arrests twice. The primary oocyte stays arrested in prophase of meiosis I until ovulation, when the LH surge triggers completion of meiosis I, producing a secondary oocyte that immediately arrests again, this time in metaphase II. It is this secondary oocyte, not a mature ovum, that is ovulated and penetrated by a sperm; meiosis II, and so true ovum maturation, is completed only if and when fertilization actually occurs.

## answer_d
Ovulation

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Ovulation releases the secondary oocyte still arrested in metaphase II, not a mature ovum -- true maturation is completed only afterward, at fertilization.

## answer_e
Puberty

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Puberty marks the resumption of cyclical ovulation, not the completion of any individual oocyte's maturation.

Fifth

Incorrect. By week 5, early organogenesis is already underway, well after the primitive node appeared in week 3.

The human chorionic gonadotrophin hormone is released from which one of the followings?

Syncytiotrophoblast

Correct. Human chorionic gonadotrophin (hCG) is a glycoprotein hormone secreted by the syncytiotrophoblast, the outer, multinucleated, invasive layer of the trophoblast that lies in direct contact with maternal blood once lacunae form within it. hCG maintains the corpus luteum's progesterone output through the early weeks of pregnancy, before the placenta itself takes over, and is the hormone detected by pregnancy tests.

Fetal endothelial cells

Extraembryonic mesoderm

## answer_e
Decidua basalis

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Decidua basalis is the maternal contribution to the placenta, formed from the endometrium; it is not fetal trophoblast tissue and does not secrete hCG.

## title
Character of the outer nuclear membrane (continuous with RER)

## question
Which of the following is a character of the outer nuclear membrane?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Continues with SER

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The outer nuclear membrane is continuous with rough, not smooth, endoplasmic reticulum -- both surfaces specifically carry bound ribosomes, a feature rough ER has and smooth ER lacks.

## answer_b
Shows chromatin granules

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Chromatin granules lie within the nucleus, associated with the inner nuclear membrane and the nuclear lamina, not scattered on the outer nuclear membrane's own surface.

## answer_c
Continues with RER

## explanation_c
Correct. The outer nuclear membrane is directly continuous with the membrane of rough endoplasmic reticulum, and both are studded on their cytoplasm-facing surface with bound ribosomes for the same reason -- rER is, structurally, an extension of the nucleus's own outer membrane out into the cytoplasm. This continuity is why polyribosomes are seen on the outer nuclear envelope as well as on the rER proper.

## answer_d
Appears acidophilic

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Acidophilia (staining pink/eosinophilic) is a property of protein-poor, lipid- or mitochondria-rich cytoplasm; the ribosome-studded outer nuclear membrane instead contributes to basophilia, not acidophilia.

## answer_e
Has no pores

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The nuclear envelope, including the region around the outer membrane, is perforated by numerous nuclear pores that regulate traffic between nucleus and cytoplasm; it is not pore-free.

## title
True statement about chromatin (euchromatin is active)

## question
Which of the following is true concerning chromatin?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
It is acidophilic

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Chromatin is basophilic, not acidophilic, because of the acidic phosphate groups of its nucleic acid.

## answer_b
Euchromatin is active

## explanation_b
Correct. Chromatin exists in two forms: euchromatin, the extended, uncoiled form that is transcriptionally active, predominating in metabolically active cells and giving a pale, vesicular nucleus; and heterochromatin, the coiled, condensed, inactive form, predominating in quiescent cells such as the small lymphocyte and giving a dense, dark nucleus. Because euchromatin is loosely packed, RNA polymerase and transcription factors can access its DNA freely, which is exactly what makes it the transcriptionally active form. A cell that is very metabolically active, synthesising large amounts of protein, therefore has a nucleus that looks pale and vesicular under the microscope, dominated by euchromatin rather than the dense, dark heterochromatin of a quiescent cell.

## answer_c
Heterochromatin is active

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Heterochromatin is the condensed, transcriptionally inactive form of chromatin -- the opposite of the correct statement about euchromatin.

## answer_d
It is attached to the outer nuclear membrane

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Chromatin is associated with the inner nuclear membrane and nuclear lamina, not the outer nuclear membrane, which instead carries ribosomes and is continuous with rough ER.

## answer_e
It is lipid in nature

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Chromatin is DNA complexed with histone and non-histone proteins, not a lipid.

Non-membranous organelles (microfilaments)

## question
Which of the following are non-membranous organelles?

## subject
fnd

## status

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer

## answer_a
Mitochondria

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Mitochondria are bounded by a double membrane (outer and inner, the inner folded into cristae); they are a membranous organelle.

Microfilaments

## explanation_b
Correct. Organelles are classified by whether they have a limiting membrane. Lysosomes

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Lysosomes are bounded by a single limiting membrane; they are a membranous organelle.

## answer_e
Crystals

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Crystals, like glycogen, are a cell inclusion rather than an organelle, and so fall outside the membranous/non-membranous organelle classification altogether.

## title
Epithelium type forming the epidermis (keratinized stratified squamous)

## question
What type of epithelium forms the epidermis of skin?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Simple squamous epithelium

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Simple squamous epithelium is a single layer of flattened cells, far too thin to protect an exposed body surface from abrasion; it lines structures such as blood vessels and alveoli instead.

## answer_b
Simple columnar epithelium

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Simple columnar epithelium is a single layer of tall cells, typically an absorptive or secretory lining (such as the gut), not a multilayered protective covering like skin.

## answer_c
Keratinized stratified squamous epithelium

## explanation_c
Correct. The epidermis is keratinized stratified squamous epithelium: many cell layers thick, flattened at the surface, and 'keratinized' because those surface cells lose their nuclei and organelles and fill with the tough, water-resistant protein keratin, forming a dead, protective outer layer that is continuously shed and replaced from below. This keratinized surface is what distinguishes it from non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium, which lines moist internal surfaces such as the oesophagus, vagina and cornea.

## answer_d
Non keratinized stratified squamous epithelium

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium shares the many-layered, flattened-surface structure but its surface cells stay alive and nucleated; it lines moist internal surfaces (oesophagus, vagina, cornea), not the exposed, keratinizing surface of the skin.

## answer_e
Pseudostratified columnar epithelium

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Pseudostratified columnar epithelium is a single true layer of cells of varying height that appears multilayered; it lines structures such as the trachea, not the skin.

## answer_a

## explanation_a

## answer_b
Attached to SER

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Ribosomes attach to rough, not smooth, endoplasmic reticulum -- smooth ER is defined precisely by the absence of bound ribosomes on its surface.

## answer_c
Formed of 2 subunits

## explanation_c
Correct. A ribosome is a non-membranous particle of ribosomal RNA and protein, assembled in the nucleolus as a large and a small subunit of unequal size; the two subunits return to the cytoplasm separately and join one another, held together by threading messenger RNA, only during active protein synthesis. Acidophilic organelles

## answer_e
Surrounded by a double membrane

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A ribosome has no membrane at all, let alone a double one; it is one of only two classes of non-membranous organelle, alongside the cytoskeleton.

## title
True statement about membranous organelles (Golgi is the secretory apparatus)

## question
Which of the following is true concerning membranous organelles?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Lysosomes are concerned with protein synthesis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Lysosomes digest material (via their acid hydrolase enzymes); protein synthesis is instead the role of ribosomes and rough endoplasmic reticulum.

## answer_b
Rough Endoplasmic Reticulum is the digestive apparatus of the cell

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Rough endoplasmic reticulum is the site of protein synthesis and segregation, one step upstream of the Golgi; digestion is the role of lysosomes, not rER.

## answer_c
Golgi is the secretory apparatus of the cell

## explanation_c
Correct. Among the cell's membranous organelles, the Golgi apparatus is specifically the one responsible for the final modification, concentration, packaging and dispatch of secretory products -- receiving newly synthesised protein from rough ER at its cis face, modifying it through its stacked cisternae, and budding off finished secretory vesicles or primary lysosomes from its trans face -- which is why it is described as the secretory apparatus of the cell. This places the Golgi one step downstream of rough endoplasmic reticulum, which synthesises and segregates the protein but does not itself modify or package it for export. A transfer vesicle arriving at the Golgi's cis face is therefore Golgi input, not Golgi product -- the products, secretory vesicles and primary lysosomes, leave only from the trans face.

## answer_d
Smooth Endoplasmic Reticulum is the powerhouse of the cell

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Smooth endoplasmic reticulum is chiefly concerned with lipid/steroid synthesis and drug detoxification, not energy production; the mitochondrion, not smooth ER, is the cell's powerhouse.

## answer_e
Mitochondria are concerned with lipid synthesis

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Mitochondria are chiefly concerned with generating ATP through oxidative phosphorylation, not lipid synthesis, which is instead a smooth endoplasmic reticulum function.
