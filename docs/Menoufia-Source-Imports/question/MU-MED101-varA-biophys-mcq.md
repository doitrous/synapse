# Item

## title
Which of the following is not a cause of metabolic acidosis

## question
Which of the following is not a cause of metabolic acidosis:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Vomiting

## explanation_a
Correct. Vomiting empties the stomach of hydrochloric acid, and the resulting loss of hydrogen ion, together with the renal contraction-alkalosis response to the accompanying volume depletion, drives the blood toward metabolic alkalosis rather than acidosis. This is the opposite direction from every other option listed, each of which is a genuine cause of metabolic acidosis. Because vomiting is therefore not a cause of metabolic acidosis, it is the correct answer to this 'which is NOT a cause' question.

## answer_b
Muscular exercise

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Strenuous muscular exercise generates lactic acid faster than it can be cleared, producing lactic acidosis, a recognised cause of metabolic acidosis.

## answer_c
Diarrhea

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Diarrhoea washes bicarbonate-rich intestinal fluid out of the body, producing a normal-anion-gap (hyperchloremic) metabolic acidosis.

## answer_d
Renal failure

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Failing kidneys cannot excrete the daily fixed-acid load or reclaim filtered bicarbonate normally, so retained acid produces metabolic acidosis.

## answer_e
Ketosis

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Accumulating ketoacids (acetoacetate, beta-hydroxybutyrate) in uncontrolled diabetes or starvation is a classic high-anion-gap cause of metabolic acidosis.

QST-MUMED101-VARABIOPHYS-Q38

## title
Inhibition of respiration as a feature of an acid-base disturbance

## question
Inhibition of respiration is a feature of:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
respiratory acidosis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Respiratory acidosis is itself caused by hypoventilation (inhibited respiration is the primary problem, not a compensatory feature).

## answer_b
compensated respiratory acidosis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A primary respiratory acidosis is compensated by the kidney retaining bicarbonate, not by any further change in ventilation.

## answer_c
compensated metabolic alkalosis

## explanation_c
Correct. Metabolic alkalosis (raised pH and raised bicarbonate) is compensated by the respiratory centre reducing ventilation rate and depth -- hypoventilation, i.e. inhibition of respiration -- which retains carbon dioxide. The retained CO2 raises carbonic acid and hydrogen ion concentration, pulling the pH back toward normal without correcting the underlying bicarbonate excess. This hypoventilatory response is exactly why compensated metabolic alkalosis, among the five options, is the one in which inhibited respiration is a defining feature.

## answer_d
compensated metabolic acidosis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Metabolic acidosis is compensated by increased, not inhibited, ventilation (hyperventilation blowing off CO2).

## answer_e
metabolic alkalosis

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Uncompensated metabolic alkalosis is the primary disturbance itself, before any respiratory response has occurred; the inhibited-respiration compensation only becomes a feature once compensation is under way.

## title
True statement regarding sodium chloride solution

## question
Which of the following is True regarding sodium chloride solution.

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Solute particles are more than 200 nm in diameter

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Particles more than roughly 200 nanometres in diameter describe a coarse suspension, not a true solution such as sodium chloride, and are large enough to be visible and to settle out rather than remain dissolved.

## answer_b
Can pass through semipermeable membrane

## explanation_b
Correct. Sodium chloride dissolves into hydrated ions under about 1 nanometre in diameter -- so small that, unlike colloidal particles, they pass freely through both filter paper and a semipermeable (dialysis) membrane. This free passage through a semipermeable membrane is exactly the property used to define a true (crystalloid) solution and to separate it experimentally from a colloid, whose larger particles are retained by such a membrane even though they still pass filter paper. Being able to cross a semipermeable membrane is therefore the single defining physical property that makes sodium chloride solution a true solution rather than a colloid or suspension.

## answer_c
Cannot pass through filter paper

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Filter paper retains only the coarsest suspension particles; a true solution such as sodium chloride passes through filter paper just as freely as it passes through a semipermeable membrane.

## answer_d
Can be seen by ordinary microscope

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Dissolved sodium and chloride ions are far too small to be resolved by an ordinary light microscope, which can only visualise particles down to roughly the size of a bacterium or larger.

## answer_e
Solute particles range between 1-200 nm in diameter

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A particle diameter of roughly 1-200 nanometres is the classic range given for a colloid, not a true solution; true-solution particles such as dissolved Na+ and Cl- are smaller than about 1 nanometre.

## title
Carbohydrate with no nutritional value

## question
Which out of the following is a carbohydrate with no nutritional value?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
glycogen

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Glycogen is joined by alpha-1,4 (with alpha-1,6 branch) glycosidic bonds, which human amylase readily hydrolyses to glucose, giving it full nutritional value as the body's own storage carbohydrate.

## answer_b
Starch

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Starch's alpha-1,4/alpha-1,6 bonds are digested by salivary and pancreatic amylase into absorbable glucose, making it a major dietary source of calories.

## answer_c
Dextrin

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Dextrins are partial amylase-digestion products of starch, still built of alpha-linked glucose and further digested to glucose, so they retain nutritional value.

## answer_d
Cellulose

## explanation_d
Correct. Cellulose is built from D-glucose units joined exclusively by beta-1,4-glycosidic bonds, and humans lack the cellulase enzyme needed to hydrolyse that bond. Because it passes through the human gastrointestinal tract completely undigested, cellulose yields no absorbable monosaccharide and therefore contributes no calories or nutritional value, unlike starch and glycogen, whose alpha-linked bonds are cleaved efficiently by human amylase. Cellulose instead functions in the human diet purely as insoluble dietary fibre, adding bulk and aiding transit rather than being absorbed as a nutrient.

## answer_e
Lactose

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Lactose is digested by lactase (in those who express it) into absorbable galactose and glucose, giving it nutritional value.

## title
Epimer of glucose

## question
Epimer of glucose is

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Fructose

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Fructose shares glucose's molecular formula but differs in functional group (ketone vs aldehyde), making it glucose's aldose-ketose isomer, not an epimer.

## answer_b
Mannose

## explanation_b
Correct. Mannose and glucose share the same molecular formula and differ in configuration only at carbon 2, making mannose the C2 epimer of glucose -- the same kind of single-carbon relationship by which galactose is glucose's C4 epimer. An epimer is defined precisely by this one-carbon difference at a non-anomeric position, distinguishing it from an anomer (differs only at the anomeric carbon) or an aldose-ketose isomer (differs in functional group, like fructose). Mannose is therefore the correct answer because it is the specific sugar related to glucose by exactly this one-carbon epimeric relationship.

## answer_c
Ribose

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Ribose is a five-carbon aldopentose, a different chain length from six-carbon glucose, so the epimer relationship (which requires the same molecular formula) does not apply.

## answer_d
Glyceraldhyde

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Glyceraldehyde is the simplest three-carbon aldose, far shorter than six-carbon glucose, so it cannot be an epimer of it.

## answer_e
Xylose

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Xylose is a five-carbon aldopentose, a different chain length from glucose, so it is not an epimer of glucose.

Keratan sulfate

## answer_e
Dermatan sulphate

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Dermatan sulphate is a structural glycosaminoglycan of skin and blood vessels; it does not stimulate lipoprotein lipase.

## title
Gradual change in specific rotation of a sugar solution

## question
. . . . . . . is gradual change in specific rotation of optical active substance when for a time.

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
mutarotation

## explanation_a
Correct. Mutarotation is the slow, spontaneous change in optical rotation shown by a freshly dissolved reducing sugar as its open-chain form cyclises and interconverts between the alpha and beta anomeric ring forms until an equilibrium mixture is reached. This is exactly the gradual, time-dependent change in specific rotation the question describes, distinguishing mutarotation from optical activity in general (which is present throughout, not a changing property) and from racemisation (formation of an equal D/L mixture, a different phenomenon). Mutarotation is therefore the correct term for this specific gradual equilibration process.

## answer_b
optical activity

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Optical activity is simply the general property of rotating plane-polarised light; it does not itself describe a gradual change over time.

## answer_c
isomerism

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Isomerism is a structural relationship between two different compounds, not a time-dependent change within one dissolved compound.

## answer_d
sugar reducing power

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Sugar reducing power refers to a free anomeric hydroxyl's ability to reduce an oxidising agent (as in Benedict's test), an unrelated chemical property.

## answer_e
racemic mixture

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A racemic mixture is an equal mixture of D and L enantiomers with zero net optical rotation, a static end-state rather than the gradual process being described.

This is exactly why 'collection of nerve cells outside CNS' is the correct, defining answer.

Incorrect. Option D correctly defines an autonomic ganglion, so 'none of the above' is not correct.

Incorrect. Since options A, B and D are each false, 'all of the above' cannot be correct.

Nature of osmosis

## question
Osmosis:

## subject

## vignette

## correct_answer

## answer_a
Occurs against concentration gradient

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Osmosis moves water down its own concentration gradient (from a region of lower solute/higher water concentration to higher solute/lower water concentration), not against a gradient.

## answer_b
Needs a carrier

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Osmosis requires no carrier protein; water moves directly across the membrane (through the lipid bilayer and via aquaporin channels) rather than being carried by a transporter.

## answer_c
Needs energy

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Osmosis requires no metabolic energy input; it is driven entirely by the water concentration gradient itself.

## answer_d
Is a passive process

## explanation_d
Correct. Osmosis is the net movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane from a region of lower solute concentration to one of higher solute concentration, driven by the water concentration gradient itself. It requires no carrier protein and no metabolic energy, making it a passive process, unlike the active transport of solutes. This passive, energy-independent, carrier-free character is exactly what makes option D the correct description of osmosis.

## answer_e
is a movement of electrolyte

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Osmosis is specifically the movement of water (the solvent), not of an electrolyte (a dissolved solute); electrolyte movement across a membrane is a separate transport process.
