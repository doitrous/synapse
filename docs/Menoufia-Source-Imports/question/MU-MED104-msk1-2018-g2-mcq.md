## title
Floor of the inguinal canal

## question
A 54 years old male suffered from a scrotal swelling that increases with cough and sneezing. On examination, it was diagnosed as inguinal hernia that passes through the inguinal canal. Which one of the following forms the floor of this structure?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Arching fibers

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The arching fibers of internal oblique and transversus abdominis form the roof of the inguinal canal, not the floor — the opposite wall from the one this question asks about.

## answer_b
Conjoint tendon

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The conjoint tendon reinforces the posterior wall of the inguinal canal medially, not the floor.

## answer_c
Fascia transversalis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Fascia transversalis forms the posterior wall of the inguinal canal (reinforced medially by the conjoint tendon); it is not the floor.

## answer_d
Inguinal ligament

## explanation_d
Correct. The floor of the inguinal canal is the inguinal ligament — the rolled-under lower border of the external oblique aponeurosis, stretching from the anterior superior iliac spine to the pubic tubercle — reinforced medially by the lacunar ligament. This ligamentous floor is the wall the canal's contents rest on as they pass from the deep to the superficial inguinal ring.

## answer_e
Internal oblique

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Medial fibers of internal oblique reinforce the anterior wall of the inguinal canal (alongside the external oblique aponeurosis), not the floor.

## question

## subject

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Promoting bone resorption is parathyroid hormone's action (which mobilises calcium from bone to raise plasma calcium), not calcitonin's — calcitonin's action on bone is the reverse.

## answer_e

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Inhibiting calcium absorption describes an intestinal action, not calcitonin's taught bone action; calcitonin's role as taught is specifically on bone (suppressing resorption), not on intestinal absorption.

## question
Menkes disease is caused by defect in:

## subject
fnd

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Fibronectin is a distinct extracellular-matrix adhesion glycoprotein, unrelated to the copper-transport/lysyl-oxidase pathway defective in Menkes disease.

## answer_b
collagen

## explanation_b
Correct. Menkes disease is a copper-transport defect that starves the copper-dependent enzyme lysyl oxidase, which is responsible for cross-linking collagen (and, by the same chemistry, elastin) after secretion. ## answer_e
elastine

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Elastin is, like collagen, a substrate that lysyl oxidase normally cross-links, and both are affected by the enzyme's copper starvation in Menkes disease — but as printed and keyed on this paper, the tested answer names collagen rather than elastin as the affected protein.

## title
Definition of the sarcomere

## question
The sarcomere is the distance between two successive:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
A bands

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The A band is the dark band formed by overlapping thick and thin filaments; a sarcomere includes one entire A band (plus half of the I band on each side), but the sarcomere is not defined as the distance between two A bands.

## answer_b
H zones

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The H zone is the lighter central region of the A band where only thick filaments lie (no overlap); it is a sub-region within a single sarcomere's A band, not the landmark whose repeat distance defines a sarcomere.

## answer_c
I bands

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The I band is the light band of thin filaments only, bisected by the Z line; a sarcomere is built from half an I band on each side of its A band, but is not itself measured as the distance between two I bands.

## answer_d
M lines

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The M line is the midline of the A band (and of the whole sarcomere), not the boundary marker between successive sarcomeres — a sarcomere runs Z-line to Z-line with the M line at its centre, not M-line to M-line by definition.

## answer_e
Z lines

## explanation_e
Correct. The sarcomere, the functional contractile unit of the skeletal muscle fiber, is defined as the distance between two successive Z lines — spanning one complete A band plus half of the I band on each side. As a muscle contracts, Z lines move closer together while the A band's length stays constant, which is why Z-line spacing (not any other band's) is what measures sarcomere shortening.

## title
T-tubule equivalent in smooth muscle cells

## question
In smooth muscle cells, which of the following represents T- tubules?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Attachment plaques

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Attachment plaques are small, darkly staining patches on the sarcolemma where dense bodies containing alpha-actinin anchor actin filaments — they play the anchoring role the Z line plays in skeletal muscle, not the impulse-conducting role of T tubules.

## answer_b
Caveolae

## explanation_b
Correct. Smooth muscle has no true T-tubule system. The histology department book states that subsarcolemmal caveolae — vesicular invaginations of the sarcolemma — function like T tubules in conducting the contraction impulse, and, together with the poorly developed sarcoplasmic reticulum, help regulate calcium flow.

## answer_c
Dense bodies

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Dense bodies are cytoplasmic (and membrane-attached) structures that anchor actin filaments in smooth muscle, functionally analogous to the Z line's anchoring role — not a T-tubule equivalent for impulse conduction.

## answer_d
Desmin

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Desmin is a cytoskeletal intermediate filament protein that helps link dense bodies together and to the cytoskeleton; it is a structural scaffolding protein, not a membrane structure that conducts the contraction impulse.

## answer_e
Gap junctions

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Gap junctions (nexuses) electrically couple adjacent smooth muscle cells, allowing impulse spread from cell to cell — a different function from a T tubule's role of conducting the impulse into the interior of a single cell.

## title
Cause of rigor mortis stiffness (Group 2 wording)

## question
A cadaver discovered two days after death showing Rigor Mortis Signs in the form of Stiffening of the joints and muscles. What is the cause of the stifness?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
ATP, which is necessary for the detachment of cross bridges, is not being formed.

## explanation_a
Correct. ATP is required for the myosin cross bridge to detach from actin after the power stroke, not only to form it. After death, ATP production stops, so cross bridges that have already attached to exposed actin sites (with calcium pumps also failing) cannot release — this failure of ATP-dependent detachment, not formation, is what produces the sustained rigidity of rigor mortis.

## answer_b
ATP, which is necessary for formation of cross bridges, continues to be formed after death.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. ATP production stops after death rather than continuing — this option's premise (ATP continuing to be formed) is the opposite of what actually happens and does not explain rigidity in any case, since it is detachment, not formation, that ATP is needed for in this mechanism.

## answer_c
ATP, which is necessary for the formation of cross bridges, is not being formed.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This option correctly notes that ATP is not being formed after death but wrongly claims ATP is needed for cross-bridge formation — cross bridges still form (myosin still binds the exposed actin sites); it is their detachment, not their formation, that specifically requires ATP and fails.

## answer_d
Deterioration of muscle proteins prevents detachment of cross bridges.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Deterioration of muscle proteins is what eventually resolves rigor mortis (by breaking down the bound cross bridges), not what causes it in the first place — the cause is ATP depletion preventing detachment, occurring well before protein deterioration begins.

## answer_e
Deterioration of muscle proteins causes detachment of cross bridges.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Deterioration of muscle proteins, when it does occur, breaks down (releases) cross bridges and so resolves rigor mortis rather than causing detachment as part of the stiffening process — this option both mistimes and reverses the role of protein deterioration.
