# Item

QST-MUMED101-HISTO-Q01

## title
Which of these is not a cell inclusion (microtubules)

## question
Which of the following is NOT considered an inclusion?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Pigment

## explanation_a
Incorrect (it is an inclusion). Pigment granules (such as lipofuscin or melanin) are classic cytoplasmic inclusions — non-living, often temporary accumulations of material.

## answer_b
Glycogen

## explanation_b
Incorrect (it is an inclusion). Glycogen, stored as cytoplasmic granules, is a metabolic inclusion, present or absent depending on the cell's nutritional state.

## answer_c
Lipid

## explanation_c
Incorrect (it is an inclusion). Lipid droplets are likewise a stored, non-membrane-bound inclusion, most obvious in adipocytes but present transiently in many other cell types.

## answer_d
Secretory granules

## explanation_d
Incorrect (it is an inclusion). Secretory granules, membrane-bound stores of a cell's secretory product awaiting release, are also classified as inclusions.

## answer_e
microtubules

## explanation_e
Correct. Microtubules are permanent, living components of the cytoskeleton, built from tubulin and present in essentially every cell to provide structural support, intracellular transport tracks and the machinery of mitosis — the opposite of an inclusion. Cell inclusions, by definition, are non-living, often transient or metabolically variable accumulations of material (pigments, stored nutrients such as glycogen and lipid, or secretory products) rather than permanent organelles or cytoskeletal elements. Because microtubules are a structural, functional, everyday component of the cell rather than a stored or accumulated substance, they are the one listed item that is not an inclusion.

## subject

Incorrect (it does stain fat). Sudan black B is likewise a lipid-soluble dye, staining fat (and other lipids, including myelin) black.

## title
Most important and essential pigment in the body (hemoglobin)

## question
Which is the most important and essential pigment in the body?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
carotene

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Carotene is a dietary, fat-soluble exogenous pigment that gives a yellow tinge to fat and skin; it plays no essential physiological role of its own beyond being a vitamin A precursor.

## answer_b
melanin

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Melanin is an important protective pigment against ultraviolet light, but the body can survive, and function, entirely without it (as in albinism).

## answer_c
hemoglobin

## explanation_c
Correct. Hemoglobin, the iron-containing respiratory pigment packed into red blood cells, is essential rather than merely important: it is the molecule that actually carries oxygen from the lungs to every tissue and carbon dioxide back again, and its complete absence is incompatible with life. This distinguishes it from the other pigments listed, which are protective, diagnostic or incidental rather than directly life-sustaining in the way oxygen transport is.

## answer_d
carbon particles

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Carbon (anthracotic) particles are an exogenous inhaled pigment, most often seen in lung and lymph-node macrophages; they have no physiological function and are simply inert accumulated debris.

## answer_e
bilirubin

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Bilirubin is a breakdown product of hemoglobin catabolism, useful diagnostically (its level indicates liver and hemolytic status) but not itself an essential functional pigment.

## title
Organelles that together form lysosomes (rER and Golgi)

## question
Which of the following two organelles share in the formation of lysosomes?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
rER & SER

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The smooth endoplasmic reticulum (SER) does not synthesise the acid hydrolase enzymes packaged into lysosomes; that is the job of the rough ER (rER), so this pairing is wrong on its second component.

## answer_b
SER & Golgi

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The rough (not smooth) ER is the site of hydrolytic-enzyme synthesis; SER is not involved in the classic lysosome-formation pathway.

## answer_c
rER & Golgi

## explanation_c
Correct. Lysosome formation begins with the rough endoplasmic reticulum (rER), whose ribosomes synthesise the acid hydrolase enzymes destined for the lysosome; these enzymes are then transported to the Golgi apparatus, where they are further processed, tagged (with mannose-6-phosphate) for lysosomal targeting, and packaged into the primary lysosomes that bud off from the trans face of the Golgi. Both organelles are therefore necessary, sequential partners in the pathway, which is why the pair rER and Golgi, rather than either alone, is the correct answer.

## answer_d
rER & Cell membrane

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The cell membrane is not part of the classical biosynthetic pathway that produces primary lysosomes; it becomes involved later, in endocytosis, when it forms the endosomes that primary lysosomes go on to fuse with.

## answer_e
Golgi & Cell membrane

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Golgi is correctly one partner, but the cell membrane is not the organelle that synthesises the lysosomal enzymes — that role belongs to rER.

## title
Stage of duplication of DNA and centrioles (S phase)

## question
In which of the following stages the duplication of DNA and centrioles has been occurred?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
GI- stage of inter phase

## explanation_a
Incorrect. G1 (Gap 1) is the phase of cell growth and preparation before DNA synthesis begins; DNA has not yet been duplicated during G1.

## answer_b
Metaphase of mitosis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Metaphase is a stage of mitosis, well after interphase, when the already-duplicated chromosomes are aligned at the metaphase plate — replication has long since finished by this point.

## answer_c
S- stage of inter phase

## explanation_c
Correct. The S (synthesis) phase of interphase is specifically when the cell replicates its DNA content, from the normal diploid amount to double, and duplicates its centrioles (each existing centriole nucleating a new daughter centriole nearby), so that by the end of S phase the cell has both duplicated chromosomes (as sister chromatids) and duplicated centrosomes ready for mitosis. This is why S phase is the defining, name-giving event of interphase's middle stage, bracketed by the G1 and G2 gap phases on either side.

## answer_d
G2 - stage of inter phase

## explanation_d
Incorrect. G2 (Gap 2) is the phase after DNA synthesis is already complete, during which the cell checks its replicated DNA and prepares for mitosis — duplication itself has already occurred by G2, not during it.

## answer_e
Prophase of mitosis

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Prophase is the first stage of mitosis, occurring after interphase (and therefore after S phase) is complete, when the already-duplicated chromosomes begin to condense.

## id
QST-MUMED101-HISTO-Q19

## title
Feature distinguishing mitosis from meiosis II (S phase)

## question
How to differentiate between mitosis and meiosis II?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Crossing over occurs in meiosis Il, not in mitosis.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Crossing over (exchange of segments between homologous chromosomes) occurs during meiosis I, in prophase I, not during meiosis II; by meiosis II no homologous pairs remain to cross over, so this statement misplaces the event.

## answer_b
S phase precedes mitosis but not meiosis II.

## explanation_b
Correct. An S phase, with full DNA replication, precedes mitosis (as the S phase of the interphase that comes immediately before it), but meiosis II is not preceded by its own S phase — the DNA was already replicated once, before meiosis I began, and that same replicated DNA content (as sister chromatids) is simply carried through meiosis I into meiosis II without being copied again. This absence of a second, intervening S phase is precisely what allows meiosis II to reduce the chromatid number without further duplicating DNA, and is the cleanest single feature that distinguishes it from an ordinary mitotic division, which is always preceded by its own S phase.

## answer_c
Separation of the chromatids occurs in mitosis, not in meiosis II.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Separation of sister chromatids happens in both mitosis (at anaphase) and meiosis II (at anaphase II); it is not a feature unique to mitosis, so it does not distinguish the two.

## answer_d
Pairing of the homologus chromosomes distinguishes meiosis II but not mitosis.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Pairing of homologous chromosomes (synapsis) is a feature of meiosis I, not meiosis II; by the time meiosis II occurs, homologous pairs have already been separated, so this statement misassigns the pairing event to the wrong division.

## title
Site where ribosomes are formed (nucleoli)

## question
Where are ribosomes formed?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Cytoskeleton

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The cytoskeleton (microtubules, microfilaments and intermediate filaments) provides structural support and tracks for movement; it has no role in ribosome synthesis.

## answer_b
Peroxisome

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Peroxisomes are membrane-bound organelles specialised for oxidative reactions (such as fatty-acid beta-oxidation and breakdown of hydrogen peroxide); they play no part in making ribosomes.

## answer_c
Microfilaments

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Microfilaments, the actin-based component of the cytoskeleton, are involved in cell shape and movement, not ribosome production.

## answer_d
Centrioles

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Centrioles organise the mitotic spindle and form the basal bodies of cilia; they have no role in ribosome assembly.

## answer_e
Nucleoli

## explanation_e
Correct. The nucleolus is the site within the nucleus where ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes are transcribed and where rRNA is assembled with ribosomal proteins (imported from the cytoplasm) to form the large and small ribosomal subunits. These subunits are then exported separately through the nuclear pores into the cytoplasm, where they come together on messenger RNA to form a complete, functional ribosome. A cell's nucleolar size and prominence correlates directly with how much protein synthesis it is doing, which is why highly secretory or rapidly dividing cells typically have large, obvious nucleoli.

## title
Best cell type for studying lysosomes (macrophages)

## question
Which one of the following cells would be best for the study of lysosomes?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
fibroblasts

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Fibroblasts are chiefly synthetic cells (producing collagen and ground substance); they are not a tissue notably rich in lysosomes.

## answer_b
macrophages

## explanation_b
Correct. Macrophages are professional phagocytes, and their central job — engulfing and digesting microorganisms, debris and worn-out cells — depends on an unusually large and active population of lysosomes to break down whatever the cell has phagocytosed. This makes macrophages the classic, textbook cell type used to study lysosomal structure and function, since their lysosomes are both numerous and easily demonstrated (for example by acid phosphatase histochemistry).

## answer_c
mast cells

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Mast cells are characterised by prominent metachromatic secretory granules containing histamine and heparin, not by a distinctively large lysosomal population.

## answer_d
mesenchymal cells

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Mesenchymal cells are relatively undifferentiated stem-like cells; they are not notable for a rich lysosomal apparatus.

## answer_e
fat cells

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Fat (adipose) cells are dominated by a single large lipid droplet with the rest of the cytoplasm and organelles compressed to a thin rim; they are not the cell type chosen to study lysosomes.

Its trans face is called the forming face.

## explanation_b

Its cisternae are interconnected.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The Golgi cisternae are typically described as a series of separate, stacked, flattened sacs rather than as a single interconnected system; material moves between them via vesicular transport (or cisternal maturation) rather than through direct continuity.
