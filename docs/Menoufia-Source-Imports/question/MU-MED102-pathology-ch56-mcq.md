## title
Route of spread, infiltrating breast carcinoma with axillary metastasis

## question
A 35-year-old female presented with a mass in the right breast adherent to the underlying structures, with enlarged firm axillary lymph nodes. Biopsy from the breast mass revealed malignant epithelial cells and biopsy from the enlarged lymph node showed the same neoplastic growth. What is the route of malignant spread in this case?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Blood spread

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Blood spread alone does not explain the tumour being adherent to the underlying chest-wall structures, which reflects direct local extension.

## answer_b
Direct and blood spread

## explanation_b
Incorrect. There is no evidence here of a distant, blood-borne metastasis; the two sites involved (the breast itself and the draining axillary node) are exactly what direct-plus-lymphatic spread produces.

## answer_c
Direct spread

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Direct spread alone explains the fixation to underlying structures but not the separate, biopsy-proven tumour in the axillary lymph node.

## answer_d
Direct and lymphatic spread

## explanation_d
Correct. The mass is adherent to (fixed to) the underlying structures, showing direct local extension, and the enlarged axillary lymph node biopsy-proven to contain the same tumour shows lymphatic spread to the regional nodes draining the breast. Breast carcinoma classically spreads by this direct-plus-lymphatic combination before later blood-borne or other spread supervenes. Both criteria are explicitly satisfied by the vignette: fixation (direct) and nodal metastasis (lymphatic).

## answer_e
Direct and transcoelomic spread

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Transcoelomic spread applies to organs covered by serous membranes seeding a body cavity (e.g. ovary into the peritoneum), not to a breast mass with axillary nodal disease.

## title
Step of extracellular matrix invasion

## question
One of the following is a step included in the invasion of extracellular matrix by a malignant tumour cell:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Cell-cell detachment

## explanation_a
Correct. Local invasion of the extracellular matrix begins with cell-cell detachment, the loss of adhesion molecules such as E-cadherin that frees an individual tumour cell from its neighbours. Only after this detachment can the cell attach to and degrade the matrix and migrate through it. It is therefore the first step of the local invasion cascade, occurring before the cell ever reaches a vessel.

## answer_b
Dissemination in circulation

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Dissemination in circulation is a later step, occurring after the tumour cell has already invaded through the matrix and entered a vessel (intravasated).

## answer_c
Extravasation

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Extravasation (exiting the vessel at a distant site) is a step of distant metastatic spread, occurring well after local matrix invasion.

## answer_d
Homing

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Homing (localising to a specific distant organ) is one of the final steps of the metastatic cascade, not a step of local extracellular matrix invasion.

## answer_e
Intravasation

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Intravasation (entry into a vessel) happens after the cell has already invaded through the matrix, not as part of that invasion step itself.

Exception among benign-dangerous tumours

## question
The following list are benign dangerous tumours except:

## explanation_a
Incorrect (this is a true benign-dangerous example, not the exception). A benign ureteric tumour is dangerous because it can obstruct urine flow despite being histologically benign.

## answer_b
Benign tumours of oesophagus

## explanation_b
Incorrect (true benign-dangerous example). A benign oesophageal tumour can obstruct the narrow oesophageal lumen and threaten swallowing/nutrition.

The other four options (ureteric, oesophageal, cardiac and pituitary tumours) are all dangerous by virtue of their site despite being histologically benign. Lipoma's danger, unlike theirs, does not come from location.

## title
Autopsy finding in a trained marathon runner

## question
A 35-year-old marathon runner died in a motor vehicle accident while driving to work. At autopsy, which of the following features could be identified through gross and microscopic examination of the organs?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Atrophy of the skeletal muscle

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A trained runner's skeletal muscle is expected to be hypertrophied (from training), not atrophied.

## answer_b
Pathologic hyperplasia of the heart

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The runner's cardiac change from training is physiologic, not pathologic, and cardiomyocytes cannot undergo hyperplasia.

## answer_c
Physiologic hypertrophy of the heart

## explanation_c
Correct. Sustained endurance training drives physiologic hypertrophy of the heart — an adaptive, reversible enlargement of cardiomyocytes in response to the normal physiological demand of regular exercise, distinct from the pathologic hypertrophy seen in chronic hypertension or valve disease. This is the expected, benign autopsy finding in a trained athlete's heart and should not be mistaken for occult cardiac disease. The same physiologic-adaptation principle also explains the runner's hypertrophied (not atrophied) skeletal muscle.

## answer_d
Physiologic hyperplasia of the heart

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Cardiomyocytes are permanent, post-mitotic cells and cannot undergo hyperplasia; the training-induced change is hypertrophy.

## answer_e
Pathologic hyperplasia of the skeletal muscle

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The skeletal muscle change from training is physiologic hypertrophy (larger fibres), not pathologic hyperplasia.

## question
Which type of atrophy occurs in poliomyelitis patients?

## explanation_a

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Disuse atrophy occurs with immobilisation of a muscle whose nerve supply is intact, which is not the mechanism in poliomyelitis.

Neuropathic

## explanation_c
Correct. Poliovirus destroys anterior horn motor neurons in the spinal cord; the skeletal muscle fibres innervated by those neurons then lose their nerve supply and undergo neuropathic (denervation) atrophy. ## explanation_e
Incorrect. Vascular atrophy follows reduced blood supply (ischaemia), not motor neuron destruction as in poliomyelitis.

## title
Left ventricular finding in uncontrolled hypertension

## question
A 55-year-old female has suffered from hypertension for 10 years. She is on antihypertensive drugs, but her blood pressure remains uncontrolled. Which of the following is an expected finding in her left ventricle?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Adaptive hypertrophy

## explanation_a
Correct. Ten years of uncontrolled hypertension imposes sustained pressure overload on the left ventricle, which responds with adaptive hypertrophy — myocyte enlargement that increases wall thickness and contractile force to cope with the raised afterload, the same pathologic-pressure-overload response seen with aortic stenosis. Because cardiomyocytes are permanent cells, this is enlargement of existing cells, not an increase in cell number. Persisting uncontrolled hypertension is exactly the chronic stimulus that drives this adaptation.

Aging atrophy

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Aging atrophy is an unrelated, generalized age-related process, not the specific pressure-overload response expected here.

Compensatory hypertrophy

## explanation_c

## answer_d
Hormonal hyperplasia

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Hormonal hyperplasia describes a hormone-driven increase in cell number in a proliferation-capable tissue (e.g. breast), not the cardiomyocyte hypertrophic response to pressure overload.

## answer_e
Physiological hypertrophy

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Physiological hypertrophy describes the reversible, exercise-driven cardiac adaptation of a trained athlete, not the pathologic adaptation to chronic uncontrolled hypertension.

## title
Exception among examples of metaplasia

## question
Which of the following conditions is not an example of metaplasia?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Adenoacanthoma

## explanation_a
Incorrect (this IS a true metaplasia example). Adenoacanthoma shows squamous metaplasia within an adenocarcinoma.

## answer_b
Barrett's oesophagus

## explanation_b
Incorrect (true metaplasia example). Barrett's oesophagus is squamous-to-intestinal (columnar) metaplasia of the distal oesophagus.

## answer_c
Congenital pyloric stenosis

## explanation_c
Correct. Correct. Standard microscopic criteria of malignancy — nuclear pleomorphism, hyperchromatism, increased and abnormal mitoses, loss of normal cell polarity — correlate well with aggressive behaviour in conventional carcinomas such as breast carcinoma, making them reliable grading features there. This reliability is the exception, not the rule, among the tumour types listed, all of which (clear cell renal cell carcinoma, papillary thyroid carcinoma, and neuroendocrine tumours of appendix or pancreas) are well-recognised exceptions where these same criteria do not reliably predict clinical behaviour. Neuroendocrine tumor of appendix

## explanation_d

## answer_e
Neuroendocrine tumor of pancreas

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A pancreatic neuroendocrine tumour similarly does not reliably follow standard cytological malignancy criteria; tumour-specific grading systems are used instead.
