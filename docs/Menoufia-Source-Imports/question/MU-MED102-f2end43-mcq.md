## title
Bacterial size range

## question
The size of bacteria range from...to...

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
0.2-8um.

## explanation_a
Correct. The smallest bacteria, such as Mycoplasma, approach about 0.2 micrometres, and the largest rod-shaped forms reach about 8 micrometres, so the accepted teaching range for the group as a whole is roughly 0.2 to 8 micrometres. This spans the vast majority of medically important bacteria seen on light microscopy. The unit is micrometres, not millimetres.

## answer_b
0.3-8um.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Starting the range at 0.3 micrometres excludes the smallest organisms, such as Mycoplasma species, which lack a cell wall and can be smaller than 0.3 micrometres. The correct lower bound is about 0.2 micrometres. This distractor is close to the true value but shifts the boundary too high.

## answer_c
0.2-8 mm.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Writing the range in millimetres rather than micrometres is a thousand-fold unit error that would make bacteria visible to the naked eye, which they are not except for a few unusual giant species. Ordinary bacteria require light or electron microscopy to be seen. The digits 0.2-8 are correct, but the unit is wrong.

## answer_d
0.4-8um.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Starting the range at 0.4 micrometres again excludes the smallest bacteria near the true 0.2 micrometre lower limit. This distractor narrows the accepted range without a teaching basis for doing so. The correct lower bound remains 0.2 micrometres.

## answer_e
2-8um.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A range of 2-8 micrometres omits the many smaller bacteria, cocci and small bacilli included in standard teaching, that fall well under 2 micrometres. The true lower bound is about 0.2 micrometres, not 2. This option describes only the larger end of the bacterial size spectrum.

## title
Gram-positive cell wall composition

## question
50% of cell wall of Gram positive bacteria is formed of:

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Glycolipid.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Glycolipid is not a major named component of the Gram-positive cell wall; the wall is instead dominated by peptidoglycan and teichoic acid. This option does not correspond to a component this exam's own key credits with the 50% figure. It is a plausible-sounding but unsupported distractor.

## answer_b
Lipopolysaccrides.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS, endotoxin) is a Gram-negative outer membrane component, not a Gram-positive cell wall component. Gram-positive bacteria lack an outer membrane entirely, so they carry no LPS. This option tests whether a student confuses Gram-positive and Gram-negative wall architecture.

## answer_c
Outer membrane.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The outer membrane is a Gram-negative structure, absent from Gram-positive bacteria, which instead have a single thick peptidoglycan-rich wall external to the cytoplasmic membrane. This option again swaps Gram-negative anatomy into a Gram-positive question. It does not answer what makes up half the Gram-positive wall.

## answer_d
Peptidoglycan.

## explanation_d
Correct. Peptidoglycan alone accounts for roughly half the mass of the thick Gram-positive cell wall, with teichoic and lipoteichoic acid contributing most of the remainder. This is the specific compositional fact the item asks for. The wall's multilayered peptidoglycan sheet is what gives it both rigidity and this characteristic proportion.

## answer_e
Peptidoglycan and techoic acid.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. This option combines peptidoglycan with teichoic acid, which together would account for more, not exactly, the 50% figure the item asks about. The question specifically isolates the peptidoglycan-only fraction. Adding teichoic acid overstates what the 50% figure refers to.

## title
Streptodornase enzyme action

## question
Choose the best description of Streptodornase enzyme action

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
DNAase enzymes.

## explanation_a
Correct. Streptodornase is streptococcal deoxyribonuclease, a DNAase enzyme that depolymerises the DNA accumulating in purulent exudate from lysed leukocytes. This reduces the viscosity of pus and helps the organism spread through tissue. It is one of several streptococcal spreading-factor enzymes.

## answer_b
Fibrinolysin.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Fibrinolysin describes streptokinase's action, a different streptococcal spreading-factor enzyme that lyses fibrin clots rather than degrading DNA. The similar names of streptodornase and streptokinase make this a common point of confusion. Only streptokinase acts on fibrin.

## answer_c
Haemolysins.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Haemolysins (such as streptolysin O and S) lyse red blood cells and other host cells, a different mechanism entirely from DNA depolymerisation. Streptodornase does not lyse cell membranes. This option names a genuine streptococcal virulence factor, just not the one this stem describes.

## answer_d
Protease.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A general protease degrades proteins, not DNA, so it does not describe streptodornase's specific DNAase activity. Streptodornase's substrate is nucleic acid, not protein. This distractor tests whether a student conflates all secreted bacterial enzymes as proteases.

## answer_e
Superantigen.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A superantigen (such as streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxins) triggers massive, non-specific T-cell activation and cytokine release, an immunological mechanism unrelated to DNA breakdown. Streptodornase's action is enzymatic degradation of DNA, not immune stimulation. These are two distinct classes of streptococcal virulence factor.

## title
Bacterial growth curve, rapid growth phase

## question
In bacterial growth curve the phase of rapid growth is known as:

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Decline phase.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The decline (death) phase is when nutrient depletion and toxic waste accumulation cause the death rate to exceed the division rate, so the population falls. This is the opposite of rapid growth. It is the final phase of the curve, not the phase of fastest growth.

## answer_b
Lag phase.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The lag phase is a period of adaptation to the new medium, during which bacteria synthesise enzymes and other machinery but do not yet divide at a rapid rate. Growth is minimal or absent here. Rapid growth follows the lag phase, it does not define it.

## answer_c
Log phase.

## explanation_c
Correct. The log (exponential) phase is the phase of rapid, regular growth, in which the population doubles at the maximal rate the medium and conditions allow. Bacteria are most metabolically active here, which is also why this phase is when antibiotics targeting active division are most effective. It is bounded by the lag phase before it and the stationary phase after.

## answer_d
Multiplication phase.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. "Multiplication phase" is not one of the four standard named phases of the bacterial growth curve, which are lag, log (exponential), stationary and decline. This option is a plausible-sounding but non-standard term. The correct name for the rapid-growth phase is the log or exponential phase.

## answer_e
Stationary phase.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The stationary phase is when the growth rate equals the death rate and the population plateaus at its largest size, not when it grows fastest. A large population size is a different property from a rapid growth rate. Only the log phase is defined by rapid growth.

Has outer membrane.

Has Thin peptidoglycan layer.

## title
Bacterial capsule function

## question
Which of the following is the bacterial Capsule function?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Antiphagocytic

## explanation_a
Correct. The capsule's primary virulence function is antiphagocytic: it coats the bacterial surface and interferes with recognition and engulfment by host phagocytes, letting the organism survive longer in tissue. This is why encapsulated strains of a species are typically more virulent than unencapsulated ones. It is a passive, structural form of immune evasion rather than a secreted toxin.

## answer_b
It acts as exotoxin.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. An exotoxin is a secreted protein toxin, a different category of virulence factor from the structural, surface-coating capsule. The capsule itself is not secreted and does not act as a toxin. This option conflates two distinct bacterial virulence mechanisms.

## answer_c
It destroys host tissues.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Direct tissue destruction is typically the role of secreted enzymes and toxins (such as hyaluronidase or exotoxins), not the capsule, which instead protects the bacterium rather than actively damaging host tissue. The capsule's role is defensive immune evasion. This option describes a different class of virulence factor.

## answer_d
It inhibits cell wall.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The capsule sits external to the cell wall and does not inhibit cell wall synthesis; that is instead the mechanism of certain antibiotics, such as beta-lactams. This option confuses a bacterial structure's own function with a pharmacological mechanism. The capsule's function is protective, not inhibitory toward the bacterium's own wall.

## answer_e
It is an endotoxin.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Endotoxin refers specifically to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in the Gram-negative outer membrane, a chemically and functionally distinct structure from the capsule. The capsule is not itself a toxin of any kind. This option mislabels the capsule using terminology that belongs to a different bacterial component.

## title
Virulence factor for bacterial adherence

## question
Select one important virulence factor help in bacterial adherence

## subject
inf

## status

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Cell wall.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The cell wall is a general structural component present in essentially all bacteria and is not itself the specialised adherence structure the item is asking about. Its main roles are shape and osmotic protection. Adherence is instead mediated by specific surface appendages such as pili.

## answer_b
Enzymes.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Secreted enzymes typically aid tissue invasion or nutrient acquisition, such as hyaluronidase breaking down connective tissue, rather than mediating initial attachment to host cells. This option names a genuine virulence factor category, just not the adherence-specific one asked for. Pili are the structure specifically dedicated to adherence.

## answer_c
Exotoxins.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Exotoxins are secreted proteins that damage host cells or disrupt their function; they act after colonisation has occurred rather than mediating the initial adherence step. This option describes a later-stage virulence mechanism, not an adherence factor. Adherence itself is a pili-mediated event.

## answer_d
Flagella.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Flagella are the organ of bacterial motility, propelling the organism through liquid, a different function from adherence. Motility can help a bacterium reach a surface, but the structure that then anchors it there is the pilus, not the flagellum. This option confuses two distinct bacterial surface appendages.

## answer_e
Pili.

## explanation_e
Correct. Ordinary (common) pili are short, hair-like appendages whose main function is adhesion of the bacterium to host cells or surfaces, an essential first step in colonisation and infection. This is distinct from the sex pilus, a longer structure used for conjugative gene transfer. Pili are the structure most specifically associated with bacterial adherence.

## title
Antibiotic acting on bacterial cell wall

## question
The antibiotic acting on bacterial cell wall is:

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Aminoglycoside.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Aminoglycosides act on the bacterial 30S ribosomal subunit, inhibiting protein synthesis, not the cell wall. This is a different antibiotic class and mechanism from the beta-lactams. Confusing ribosome-targeting and wall-targeting antibiotic classes is the main trap here.

## answer_b
Cephalosporin.

## explanation_b
Correct. Cephalosporins are beta-lactam antibiotics that bind penicillin-binding proteins and block the transpeptidation (cross-linking) step of peptidoglycan assembly, weakening the cell wall until the cell lyses under its own osmotic pressure. This is the same general cell-wall-active target as penicillins. Among the listed options, only cephalosporin acts on the cell wall.

## answer_c
Ciprofloxacin.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Ciprofloxacin, a fluoroquinolone, inhibits DNA gyrase (topoisomerase II) and topoisomerase IV, blocking bacterial DNA replication, not cell wall synthesis. This is a distinct mechanism from beta-lactam antibiotics. It targets the bacterial genome, not the wall.

## explanation_a
Correct. Ritodrine.

## subject

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Calcium metabolism is unrelated to hemochromatosis, which is specifically a disorder of iron handling. This option names a different metabolic element entirely. It does not describe the pathology this condition is defined by.

## answer_b
Hematin.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Hematin is a breakdown product related to haemoglobin degradation, not the element hemochromatosis is classified by. This option names a related but distinct biochemical entity. Iron, not hematin specifically, is the disorder's defining element.

## answer_c
Haemosidrin.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Haemosiderin is the storage form iron is deposited as within tissues in hemochromatosis, the visible pigment on histology, but it is not itself the metabolic element the disorder is classified by; that element is iron. This option names the storage pigment rather than the underlying metabolic disorder's own element. Confusing the storage form with the element itself is the main trap in this item.

## answer_d
iron.

## explanation_d
Correct. Hemochromatosis is a disorder of iron metabolism in which excessive iron absorption (primary/hereditary hemochromatosis) or excessive iron intake (secondary, transfusion-related) leads to iron deposition, largely as haemosiderin, in the liver, pancreas, heart, joints and skin, producing organ damage over time. Iron is the correct underlying element the disorder is classified by. This distinguishes the disorder itself from haemosiderin, its storage form.

## answer_e
Melanin.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Melanin metabolism is unrelated to hemochromatosis; disorders of melanin relate to pigmentation conditions, not iron overload. This option names an entirely different pigment/metabolic pathway. It does not describe hemochromatosis's defining element.

## title
Bone marrow response to acute hemorrhage

## question
A 20 years old male had an accident a weak ago. He suffered severe hemorrhage from multiple deep wounds that he lost about 1 litter of blood. What is the characteristic finding in his bone marrow?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Aging atrophy.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Aging atrophy is a physiological, generalized shrinkage process associated with senility, not a response to acute blood loss. This option names an unrelated cause of atrophy. It does not describe the bone marrow's response to hemorrhage.

## answer_b
Compensatory atrophy.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. "Compensatory atrophy" combines the correct trigger word (compensatory) with the wrong direction of change (atrophy, a decrease); hemorrhage triggers an increase, hyperplasia, not a decrease, in marrow cellularity. This is a tempting distractor because it shares the word "compensatory" with the correct answer. Only the direction of change (hyperplasia vs atrophy) distinguishes it from the right answer.

## answer_c
Compensatory hyperplasia.

## explanation_c
Correct. After acute, significant blood loss, the bone marrow responds with compensatory hyperplasia: erythropoietin drives an increase in the number of haematopoietic (chiefly erythroid) precursor cells to replace the lost red cell mass, a physiological, reversible adaptive growth response. This is distinct from atrophy (a decrease in cell size or number) and from irritation hyperplasia (a response to chronic physical or chemical irritation rather than to blood loss). Compensatory hyperplasia is the correct bone marrow response to this vignette's acute hemorrhage.

## answer_d
Irritation hyperplasia.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Irritation hyperplasia is a response to chronic physical or chemical irritation (such as recurrent friction on skin), a different trigger from the acute blood loss described in this vignette. This option names hyperplasia with the wrong underlying stimulus. The marrow's response to hemorrhage is specifically compensatory, driven by erythropoietin, not by irritation.

## answer_e
Physiological hypertrophy.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Physiological hypertrophy describes an increase in cell size (such as skeletal muscle with exercise or the uterus in pregnancy), not an increase in cell number, which is what the marrow's response to hemorrhage involves. Hyperplasia, not hypertrophy, is the correct process here, since new precursor cells are generated rather than existing cells simply enlarging. This option names the wrong type of adaptive growth.

Cachexia and growth disturbance

## question
Which of the following growth disturbances is most likely to occur in patients suffering from cachexia?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Atrophy.

## explanation_a
Correct. Pathological generalized atrophy is driven by decreased anabolism (chronic malnutrition, starvation) or increased catabolism, including advanced malignancy's cachexia. Malignant cachexia, marked loss of fat and lean body mass, weakness and anaemia, results from cytokines produced by the tumour and host, producing this generalized, whole-body wasting. Atrophy is the correct growth disturbance most associated with cachexia among the five options.

## answer_b
Dysplasia.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Dysplasia describes disordered, abnormal cellular maturation, typically a premalignant epithelial change, an entirely different process from the whole-body tissue wasting seen in cachexia. This option names an unrelated growth disturbance. It does not describe cachexia's characteristic effect.

## answer_c
Hyperplasia.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Hyperplasia describes an increase in cell number, the opposite direction of change from the tissue loss and wasting characteristic of cachexia. This option reverses the correct direction of change. Cachexia is a wasting, not a growth-promoting, process.

## answer_d
Hypertrophy.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Hypertrophy describes an increase in cell size, again the opposite direction from cachexia's characteristic loss of fat and lean body mass. This option, like hyperplasia, describes tissue growth rather than the wasting cachexia produces. It does not match the catabolic state described.

## answer_e
Metaplasia.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Metaplasia describes the reversible replacement of one differentiated cell type by another, typically in response to chronic irritation, an unrelated process to the generalized wasting of cachexia. This option names a different adaptive cellular change entirely. It does not describe cachexia's characteristic systemic effect.
