## title
The anatomical plane dividing the body into equal right and left halves

## question
Which one of the following is the anatomical plane that divides the body into equal right and left halves?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Coronal

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A coronal (frontal) plane is a vertical plane running side to side, dividing the body into anterior and posterior parts, not right and left halves.

## answer_b
Median

## explanation_b
Correct. The median (midsagittal) plane is the single vertical anteroposterior plane that runs through the exact midline of the body, dividing it into equal right and left halves. It is the reference plane from which lateral and medial are measured, and every other sagittal plane (parasagittal) is defined only by its offset from this one central plane. Because it passes through the body's true centre, it is the only sagittal-type plane that produces two mirror-image halves rather than two unequal portions.

## answer_c
Transverse

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A transverse (horizontal) plane divides the body into upper (superior) and lower (inferior) parts, at whatever level it is taken, not into right and left halves.

## answer_d
Parasagittal

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A parasagittal plane is any plane parallel to the median plane but offset from the true midline; by definition it produces two unequal portions, not equal right and left halves.

## answer_e
Frontal

## explanation_e
Incorrect. "Frontal" is simply another name for the coronal plane, so it shares the same problem as option A: it divides the body into anterior and posterior parts, not right and left halves.

## title
Relationship of the radius to the ulna

## question
Which term of the following describes the relationship of the radius to the ulna?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Proximal

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Proximal describes position along a limb's long axis (closer to the trunk), not the side-to-side relationship between the two forearm bones.

## answer_b
Distal

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Distal, like proximal, describes position along the limb's long axis (further from the trunk), not a side-to-side relationship.

## answer_c
Medial

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Medial would describe the ulna's position relative to the radius, not the radius's position relative to the ulna -- the two terms are opposites and are not interchangeable.

## answer_d
Lateral

## explanation_d
Correct. In the standard anatomical position, with the body erect and the palms facing forward, the radius is the lateral bone of the forearm and the ulna is the medial bone. This relationship is fixed by convention to the anatomical position and does not change with pronation or supination, even though the radius physically crosses over the ulna during those movements. "Lateral" and "medial" describe this side-to-side relationship specifically, distinguishing it from the length-axis terms proximal/distal and the front-to-back terms anterior/posterior.

## answer_e
Anterior

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Anterior describes a front-to-back relationship, which does not apply to two bones lying side by side in the same coronal plane of the forearm.

## title
Moving the sole of the foot away from the median plane

## question
Moving the sole of the foot away from the median plane is called which one of the following?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Extension

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Extension is a straightening movement at a joint (increasing the joint angle), not a description of which way the sole of the foot faces.

## answer_b
Abduction

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Abduction is movement of a whole limb away from the body's midline; it is a distinct named movement from the specific foot movement of eversion, even though both involve moving "away" from a reference line.

## answer_c
Adduction

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Adduction is movement of a limb toward the midline, the opposite of abduction, and is not the term used for the sole-of-foot movement described here.

## answer_d
Eversion

## explanation_d
Correct. Eversion turns the sole of the foot to face laterally, away from the median plane, and occurs mainly at the subtalar and transverse tarsal joints, produced chiefly by peroneus (fibularis) longus and brevis. It is the direct opposite of inversion. Ankle sprains occur far more often in forced inversion than in forced eversion, because the lateral ankle ligaments are weaker than the strong deltoid (medial) ligament that resists eversion.

## answer_e
Inversion

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Inversion is the opposite movement, turning the sole to face medially (toward the median plane), produced chiefly by tibialis anterior and posterior -- the reverse of what the question describes.

## title
The body region with no deep fascia

## question
Which of the following regions doesn't contain deep fascia?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Thigh

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The thigh has a well-developed deep fascia (the fascia lata), which invests the thigh muscles as a tight fibrous stocking.

## answer_b
Sole of the foot

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The sole of the foot has a thickened deep fascia, the plantar aponeurosis, which supports the longitudinal arch of the foot.

## answer_c
Face

## explanation_c
Correct. The face is the classic exception among named body regions: it has only superficial fascia, with the muscles of facial expression lying directly within it and no deep fascial layer separating them from the skin. This absence is exactly why facial muscles, unlike limb muscles, can insert directly into the skin and produce fine movements of expression, rather than acting only on bone or joints through a fascial sheath. It is also why facial infections and swelling spread relatively superficially rather than being contained by a deep fascial compartment.

## answer_d
Arm

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The arm has a deep fascia (the brachial fascia) investing its muscle compartments, continuous with the fascia of the forearm and shoulder.

## answer_e
Ankle

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The ankle region has deep fascia, thickened locally into the flexor, extensor and peroneal retinacula that hold the crossing tendons in place.

## title
Muscle attachment as a thin sheet of fibrous tissue

## question
When the attachment of a muscle is in the form of a thin sheet of fibrous tissue, it is called which one of the following?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Tendon

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A tendon is a cord-like fibrous attachment, concentrated into a rope-like band rather than spread out as a thin sheet.

## answer_b
Aponeurosis

## explanation_b
Correct. An aponeurosis is a thin, broad, flattened sheet of dense fibrous tissue that attaches a muscle to bone or to other fascia, typically where a wide muscle (such as the external oblique or the epicranial muscles) needs a wide area of attachment rather than a single concentrated point. It differs from a tendon only in shape, not in tissue type -- both are dense regular fibrous connective tissue -- and the distinction is purely about whether the attachment is sheet-like or cord-like.

## answer_c
Retinaculum

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A retinaculum is a fibrous band that retains and holds tendons in place as they cross a joint (such as the flexor retinaculum at the wrist); it is not itself a muscle's primary attachment.

## answer_d
Raphe

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A raphe is a fibrous seam formed where the fasciae of two muscles meet and interdigitate in the midline (such as the pharyngeal or perineal raphe), not a broad sheet-like attachment of a single muscle.

## answer_e
Fleshy

## explanation_e
Incorrect. "Fleshy" describes a muscle attaching directly by its muscle fibres without any intervening fibrous tissue at all, which is the opposite of the fibrous-sheet attachment the question describes.

## title
Which bone is a short (miniature) long bone

## question
Which of the following is considered a short long bone?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Carpal bones

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The carpal bones are classed as short bones (a compact shell around cancellous bone, roughly cuboidal in shape), not as any type of long bone -- they have no shaft (diaphysis) at all.

## answer_b
Phalanges

## explanation_b
Correct. A miniature (short) long bone -- the metacarpals, metatarsals and phalanges -- has a diaphysis like a typical long bone, but only one epiphysis, at one end, with the other end fusing to the shaft without its own separate ossification centre. This single-epiphysis pattern is exactly what separates a miniature long bone from a typical long bone (which has an epiphysis at each end) while still keeping it in the long-bone family rather than the short-bone family.

## answer_c
Clavicle

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The clavicle is classed as a modified (atypical) long bone for different reasons entirely -- it has no medullary cavity and ossifies partly intramembranously -- not because it is a miniature long bone with one epiphysis.

## answer_d
Radius

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The radius is a typical long bone, with a diaphysis and a separately-ossifying epiphysis at each end (proximal and distal), which is the opposite of the single-epiphysis miniature pattern.

## answer_e
Ulna

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The ulna, like the radius, is a typical long bone with an epiphysis at each end, not a miniature long bone.

## title
Structure responsible for growth of a long bone in thickness

## question
The structure responsible for the growth of a long bone in thickness is which one of the following?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Epiphysis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The epiphysis is the bone end itself, the site where the epiphyseal plate is located; it is not the tissue layer responsible for adding new bone width.

## answer_b
Epiphyseal plate

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The epiphyseal (growth) plate is responsible for growth in length, not width -- it is the site of endochondral ossification that lengthens the shaft, a separate mechanism entirely from width growth.

## answer_c
Diaphysis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The diaphysis is the shaft of the bone, the structure that grows, not the tissue layer that does the growing; naming it does not answer which structure drives appositional width growth.

## answer_d
Periosteum

## explanation_d
Correct. The periosteum is the fibrous membrane covering the outer surface of a long bone, and its deep, osteogenic layer lays down new bone by appositional growth, which is what widens the shaft. This runs in parallel with, and independently of, length growth at the epiphyseal plate -- a fracture through the epiphyseal plate can stunt a bone's eventual length while its width continues to increase normally, because the periosteum is unaffected. Endosteal resorption on the inner surface enlarges the medullary cavity as the periosteum adds bone outside, keeping the cortical wall a roughly constant thickness as the whole bone widens.

## answer_e
Endosteum

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The endosteum lines the internal (medullary) surface of the bone and is mainly resorptive during growth, hollowing out the marrow cavity as the shaft widens from the outside -- it is not the source of the new bone that increases the shaft's outer width.

## title
Identifying a synovial joint

## question
Which of the following is a synovial joint?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Intervertebral discs

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The intervertebral discs form a secondary cartilaginous joint (a symphysis), not a synovial joint -- they lack a joint cavity and are joined by fibrocartilage.

## answer_b
Intercarpal joints

## explanation_b
Correct. The intercarpal joints, between adjacent carpal bones, are synovial plane joints: freely mobile, with a joint cavity and capsule, and their bone ends covered by hyaline articular cartilage. This is exactly the structural definition that separates a synovial joint from the cartilaginous and fibrous joints listed alongside it in this question -- the presence of a true joint cavity, not simply the fact that two bones meet.

## answer_c
Middle radioulnar joint

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The middle radioulnar region is spanned by the interosseous membrane, a fibrous syndesmosis, not a synovial joint (the proximal and distal radioulnar joints, at the ends of the forearm, are synovial pivot joints, but the shaft-level connection is not).

## answer_d
Epiphyseal plate

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The epiphyseal plate is a primary cartilaginous joint (a synchondrosis), made of hyaline cartilage, allowing bone growth rather than movement -- it has no joint cavity.

## answer_e
Inferior tibiofibular joint

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The inferior tibiofibular joint is a fibrous syndesmosis, held by an interosseous ligament rather than possessing a synovial cavity.

Joint type that allows rotation

## question
Which of the following joints allow rotation?

## subject

## status

QST-MUMED101-ANSLBL-ANSWERLABELED-Q37

## title
Cells that cannot divide

## question
Which of the following cells cannot divide?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Pancreatic cells

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Pancreatic cells are classed as stable (quiescent) cells: they do not divide continuously under normal conditions, but they retain the capacity to re-enter the cell cycle and proliferate when needed, unlike a truly permanent cell.

## answer_b
Liver cells

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Liver cells are the classic example of a stable/quiescent cell population -- normally non-dividing, but capable of substantial regeneration and division after injury (such as partial hepatectomy), which is the opposite of "cannot divide."

## answer_c
Endocrine gland cells

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Endocrine gland cells, like liver cells, are stable cells that can re-enter the cell cycle and divide when stimulated, not permanently non-dividing cells.

## answer_d
Nerve cells

## explanation_d
Correct. Cells are classified by renewal capacity into labile (continuously dividing), stable/quiescent (normally non-dividing but able to re-enter the cycle), and permanent cells, which have permanently exited the cell cycle after full differentiation. Mature nerve cells are the classic permanent cell: once fully differentiated, they cannot divide again, which is why neuronal loss (for example after a stroke or spinal cord injury) is not repaired by division of the remaining neurons.

## answer_e
Epithelial cells

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Most epithelial cells are labile cells, among the most actively and continuously dividing cell populations in the body (for example the epidermis and gut lining), the opposite of cells that cannot divide.
