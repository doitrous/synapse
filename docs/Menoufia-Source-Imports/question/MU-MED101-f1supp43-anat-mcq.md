# Item

## id
QST-MUMED101-ANAT-Q01

## title
Movement lost when the shoulder cannot move forward (protraction)

## question
A patient cannot move the shoulder anteriorly, which one of the following movements is lost?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Protrusion

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Protrusion is not the standard term used for scapular/shoulder-girdle movement in this direction; the exam and standard anatomical usage reserve it for the mandible.

## answer_b
Retrusion

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Retrusion, like protrusion, is a term used for the mandible moving backward, not for the shoulder girdle.

## answer_c
Opposition

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Opposition is the thumb pulp meeting the pulp of another digit; it has nothing to do with moving the shoulder forward.

## answer_d
Protraction

## explanation_d
Correct. Protraction is the forward gliding movement of the scapula around the chest wall, carrying the shoulder anteriorly, produced chiefly by serratus anterior and pectoralis minor. Losing the ability to move the shoulder anteriorly is therefore a loss of protraction specifically, distinguishing it from retraction (the opposite, posterior-pulling movement) and from elevation/depression, which move the scapula up or down rather than forward or back. The clinical picture that most classically produces this loss is long thoracic nerve palsy, which paralyses serratus anterior and also causes a winged scapula.

## answer_e
Retraction

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Retraction is the opposite movement, pulling the shoulder girdle backward toward the midline, produced by the rhomboids and middle trapezius; losing it would prevent backward movement, not forward movement.

# Item

## id
QST-MUMED101-ANAT-Q02

## title
Movement lost when the thumb cannot meet the other fingers (opposition)

## question
A patient cannot move the thumb to meet each of the other four fingers, which one of the following movements is lost?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Abduction

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Abduction of the thumb moves it away from the palm in a plane perpendicular to it; it does not by itself bring the thumb pulp to meet the other digits.

## answer_b
Adduction

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Adduction brings the thumb back toward the index finger in the plane of the palm; it is one component of opposition but not the composite movement being described.

## answer_c
Opposition

## explanation_c
Correct. Opposition is the composite movement, unique to the thumb, that combines abduction, flexion and medial rotation at the carpometacarpal joint so the thumb pulp can meet the pulp of each of the other four fingers in turn. It is what makes precision (pinch) grip possible, and its motor supply is the median nerve via the muscles of the thenar eminence, chiefly opponens pollicis. Loss of opposition, as in low median nerve palsy, is disabling because it removes the hand's pincer function even though the individual movements of flexion, abduction and adduction may each still be intact on their own.

## answer_d
Reposition

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Reposition is the return movement from opposition back to the resting, unopposed position of the thumb — the opposite of what is being lost here.

## answer_e
Flexion

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Flexion alone bends the thumb across the palm without the rotation component; flexion is only one part of the composite movement of opposition, not opposition itself.

QST-MUMED101-ANAT-Q03

## title
Term correctly describing supination

## question
Which one of the following terms is correct regarding supination?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Abduction

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Abduction moves a limb away from the midline in a coronal plane; supination is a rotational movement of the forearm, not a movement away from the midline.

## answer_b
Adduction

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Adduction moves a limb toward the midline; it does not describe the rotation of the radius that occurs in supination.

## answer_c
Lateral rotation

## explanation_c
Correct. Supination turns the palm to face anteriorly (in the anatomical position) or upward, and it is achieved by lateral (external) rotation of the radius around the ulna at the proximal and distal radio-ulnar joints, carrying the radius from its pronated, crossed position back to lie parallel with the ulna. Biceps brachii is the most powerful supinator when the elbow is flexed, assisted by supinator itself at any elbow angle. Describing supination as a lateral rotation of the radius is the anatomically precise way to classify what is otherwise a named, special movement of the forearm.

## answer_d
Medial rotation

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Medial rotation of the radius is what produces pronation, turning the palm to face posteriorly or downward — the opposite of supination.

## answer_e
Circumduction

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Circumduction is a compound, circular sequence of flexion-abduction-extension-adduction seen at ball-and-socket or condyloid joints; it is not the movement being asked about here.

## title
Term for the sole of the foot facing outward (eversion)

## question
Which one of the following terms is correct if sole of the foot faces outward or laterally?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Dorsiflexion

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Dorsiflexion moves the whole foot upward at the ankle joint, bringing the dorsum of the foot toward the shin; it does not turn the sole outward.

## answer_b
Eversion

## explanation_b
Correct. Eversion turns the sole of the foot to face outward (laterally), occurring mainly at the subtalar and transverse tarsal joints, produced chiefly by the peroneus (fibularis) longus and brevis muscles. It is the direct opposite of inversion, and the two are frequently combined with dorsiflexion or plantarflexion in the compound movements of pronation and supination of the foot. Ankle sprains most often occur in forced inversion rather than eversion, because the lateral ligaments resisting inversion are weaker than the strong deltoid ligament resisting eversion.

## answer_c
Inversion

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Inversion is the opposite movement, turning the sole to face inward (medially), produced chiefly by tibialis anterior and tibialis posterior.

## answer_d
Plantarflexion

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Plantarflexion points the foot downward at the ankle joint, away from the shin; it does not turn the sole to face laterally.

## answer_e
Lateral rotation

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Lateral rotation is a term used for whole-limb rotation around a long axis (as at the hip or shoulder), not for the specific foot movement of turning the sole outward.

## title
Term for limb movement away from the midline (abduction)

## question
Movement of the limb away from the midline of the body is called which one of the followings?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Abduction

## explanation_a
Correct. Abduction is defined as movement of a limb, or part of a limb, away from the midline of the body (or, for digits, away from the axis of the hand or foot), typically occurring in the coronal plane at joints such as the shoulder and hip. It is paired with, and is the direct opposite of, adduction, which returns the limb toward the midline. This midline-referenced definition is the standard anatomical convention used consistently across all limb movement terminology.

## answer_b
Protraction

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Protraction describes a forward-gliding movement, most often of the mandible or the scapula, not movement of a limb away from the midline.

Flexion

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Flexion decreases the angle at a joint, usually moving a part anteriorly (or posteriorly at the knee); it is not defined by distance from the midline.

## answer_d
Extension

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Extension increases the angle at a joint, the opposite of flexion; like flexion, it is not a midline-referenced movement.

## answer_e
Adduction

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Adduction is the opposite of abduction — movement of a limb toward the midline of the body — not away from it.

## title
Movements performed by the wrist joint

## question
Which of the following movements are done by the wrist joint:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Flexion

## explanation_a
Incorrect on its own. Flexion is indeed one movement the wrist performs, but it is not the only one, so it is not the single best answer when a more complete option is available.

## answer_b
Extension

## explanation_b
Incorrect on its own. Extension is also a genuine wrist movement, but again only one of several, so it under-states what the joint can do.

## answer_c
Adduction

## explanation_c
Incorrect on its own. Adduction (ulnar deviation) is a real wrist movement, but selecting it alone omits the other movements the joint performs.

## answer_d
Abduction

## explanation_d
Incorrect on its own. Abduction (radial deviation) is likewise a genuine wrist movement, but naming it alone is incomplete.

## answer_e
All of the above

## explanation_e
Correct. The wrist (radiocarpal) joint is a condyloid (ellipsoid) synovial joint, and condyloid joints characteristically allow two degrees of freedom: flexion-extension and abduction-adduction, plus circumduction as their combination. All four listed movements — flexion, extension, adduction (ulnar deviation) and abduction (radial deviation) — are therefore genuinely performed at the wrist, which is exactly why "all of the above" is the single best answer rather than any one movement alone.

QST-MUMED101-ANAT-Q05

## title
Bone developing by intramembranous ossification (skull vault)

## question
Which of the following bones develops by intra-membranous ossification?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Skull vault

## explanation_a
Correct. The flat bones of the skull vault (the roof bones, such as the frontal and parietal bones) ossify by the intramembranous route: mesenchymal cells condense directly into osteoblasts within a fibrous membrane, without an intervening cartilage model. This is why the vault bones can grow rapidly to accommodate the expanding brain and why the fontanelles (unossified membrane) persist at birth between adjacent vault bones. It contrasts with endochondral ossification, which first lays down a hyaline cartilage model that is later replaced by bone, as happens in essentially every long bone of the limbs.

## answer_b
Radius

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The radius is a typical long bone of the limb and ossifies endochondrally, from a cartilage model, not intramembranously.

## answer_c
Carpal bones

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The carpal bones are short bones that also ossify endochondrally from cartilage models, each developing its own secondary ossification centre after birth.

## answer_d
Metacarpals

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The metacarpals are miniature long bones and, like other limb bones, ossify endochondrally rather than intramembranously.

## answer_e
Phalanges

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The phalanges are likewise miniature long bones that ossify endochondrally, each from its own cartilage model.

QST-MUMED101-ANAT-Q06

## title
Structure responsible for a long bone growing in width (periosteum)

## question
Growth of bone in width is due to activity of which one of the following?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Epiphysis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The epiphysis is the bony end of a long bone; it is not itself the tissue layer responsible for widening the shaft.

## answer_b
Diaphysis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The diaphysis is the shaft of the long bone — the structure that gets wider, not the tissue that makes it widen.

## answer_c
Periosteum

## explanation_c
Correct. A long bone widens by appositional growth: osteoblasts in the deep, osteogenic layer of the periosteum lay down new bone on the outer surface of the shaft, while osteoclasts on the inner (endosteal) surface resorb bone to enlarge the medullary cavity, together increasing the bone's diameter without a cartilage intermediate. This periosteal widening happens throughout growth alongside, but independently of, the epiphyseal-plate lengthening mechanism, and it continues in a slower form throughout adult life as part of normal bone remodelling.

## answer_d
Metaphysis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The metaphysis is the transitional, actively remodelling zone of the shaft just deep to the epiphyseal plate; it is where longitudinal growth is converted into mature bone, not the site responsible for widening.

## answer_e
Epiphyseal cartilage

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The epiphyseal cartilage (growth plate) is responsible for growth in length, not in width — that is precisely the distinction this question and its paired question are testing.

# Item

## id
QST-MUMED101-ANAT-Q07

## title
Structure responsible for a long bone growing in length (epiphyseal cartilage)

## question
Growth of bone in length is due to activity of which one of the following?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Epiphysis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The epiphysis is the ossified end of the bone on the far side of the growth plate; it does not itself generate new length.

## answer_b
Metaphysis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The metaphysis is where cartilage produced by the growth plate is being converted into bone; it is the site of ossification of new length, not the site that generates it.

## answer_c
Periosteum

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The periosteum is responsible for growth in width by appositional new bone formation, not for growth in length.

## answer_d
Diaphysis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The diaphysis is the shaft that is being lengthened; it is not itself the source of new growth.

## answer_e
Epiphyseal cartilage

## explanation_e
Correct. A long bone lengthens at its epiphyseal (growth) plate of hyaline cartilage, where chondrocytes proliferate and undergo an ordered sequence of columnar arrangement, hypertrophy and calcification before being replaced by bone at the metaphysis — a process called endochondral ossification. The epiphyseal plate at one end of a bone typically closes (ossifies) earlier than the plate at the other end, and the end that keeps growing longer, because its plate closes later, is called the growing end of that bone. Growth in length stops permanently once both epiphyseal plates fuse with the diaphysis at the end of puberty, under hormonal control that occurs about two years earlier in females than in males.
