## title
Active methionine (SAM) function

## question
Active methionine (SAM) acts as:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Sulphate donor

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Sulphate-donor duty belongs to PAPS (3'-phosphoadenosine-5'-phosphosulphate), not SAM. Both are adenine-nucleotide activated carriers, but they carry different groups.

## answer_b
Methyl donor

## explanation_b
Correct. S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), often called "active methionine", is the cell's universal methyl donor. Its reactive sulfonium centre makes the attached methyl group easy to transfer, and methyltransferases use it to methylate DNA, RNA, proteins, phospholipids and small molecules such as noradrenaline, releasing S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH) as a by-product.

## answer_c
Hydrogen donor

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Hydrogen-carrier duty belongs to NAD+, FAD and NADP+, not SAM, which carries a methyl group instead.

## answer_d
Oxygen donor

## explanation_d
Incorrect. SAM has no oxygen-donor role in normal human biochemistry; its defining function is methyl-group transfer.

## title
DNA melting temperature and GC content

## question
The melting temperature of DNA is increased by its:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
A and T content

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A-T base pairs are held by only two hydrogen bonds, so higher A-T content lowers, not raises, the melting temperature relative to an equally long GC-rich region.

## answer_b
G and C content

## explanation_b
Correct. A guanine-cytosine base pair is held by three hydrogen bonds, one more than an adenine-thymine pair's two, so a DNA region richer in G and C requires more thermal energy -- a higher melting temperature -- to denature. This is why GC-rich primers and templates are harder to denature in PCR, a practical consequence of the same hydrogen-bond count.

## answer_c
Sugar content

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Every DNA molecule has the same deoxyribose sugar in its backbone, so sugar content does not vary between DNA samples and cannot explain melting-temperature differences.

## answer_d
Phosphate content

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Every DNA molecule has the same phosphate backbone chemistry, so phosphate content does not vary between DNA samples and cannot explain melting-temperature differences.

## title
DNA replication is semiconservative

## question
Which of the following statements about DNA replication is NOT correct?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Unwinding of the DNA molecule occurs as hydrogen bonds break

## explanation_a
Incorrect (i.e. this statement is true). Unwinding the double helix does happen as the hydrogen bonds between the two strands break, allowing each strand to serve as a template.

## answer_b
The process is known as semiconservative DNA replication because one original strand is conserved in the new DNA molecule

## explanation_b

Nucleosomes and transcription regulation

## question
How does nucleosome formation on genomic DNA affect the initiation and/or elongation phases of transcription?

## subject

B

## answer_a
Nucleosomes inhibit access of enzymes involved in all phases of transcription

## explanation_a

## answer_b
Nucleosomes recruit histone and DNA modifying enzymes, and the actions of these recruited enzymes affect the access of transcription proteins to DNA

## explanation_b
Correct. Nucleosome formation recruits histone-modifying enzymes (e.g. histone acetyltransferases/deacetylases) and DNA-modifying enzymes (e.g. DNA methyltransferases); it is the actions of those recruited enzymes -- loosening or tightening chromatin -- that determine how accessible the DNA is to the transcription machinery.
