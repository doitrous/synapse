## title
Site of protraction and retraction

## question
Which of the following is a site for protraction and retraction movements?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Elbow

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The elbow is a hinge joint permitting flexion and extension, not the protraction/retraction pair.

## answer_b
Foot

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The foot is the site of inversion and eversion (tested separately in this same exam), not protraction/retraction.

## answer_c
Mandible

## explanation_c
Correct. Protraction and retraction are temporomandibular-joint movements: protraction glides the mandible forward, retraction pulls it back. These movements let the lower jaw slide horizontally beneath the upper jaw, distinct from the joint's separate opening/closing (depression/elevation) movements. Loss of smooth protraction/retraction is a recognised finding in temporomandibular joint dysfunction.

## answer_d
Thumb

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The thumb's characteristic movement is opposition, not protraction/retraction.

## title
Origin of the epiphysis

## question
The epiphysis of a bone develops from which of the following?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Periosteum

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Periosteum covers the shaft's external surface and contributes to appositional bone growth, not to the epiphysis's own ossification.

## answer_b
Primary center

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The primary ossification center forms the diaphysis (shaft), not the epiphysis.

## answer_c
Secondary centers

## explanation_c
Correct. Secondary ossification centres appear in the cartilaginous epiphyses after birth and form each epiphysis. As they expand they leave behind an epiphyseal plate of growth cartilage between epiphysis and metaphysis. Articular cartilage covering the joint surface is the one cartilage in this region never replaced by bone, even into adulthood.

## answer_d
Upper epiphyseal plate of cartilage

## explanation_d
Incorrect. "Upper epiphyseal plate of cartilage" names a structure (the growth plate itself), not the ossification centre that gives rise to the epiphysis.

## title
Naming a muscle by its tendon-fibre arrangement

## question
Which of the following is the term for a muscle in which the tendon receives muscle fibres from one side?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Bipennate

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A bipennate muscle's tendon receives fibres from both sides of a central tendon, not one side only.

## answer_b
Multipennate

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A multipennate muscle's tendon receives fibres from several directions onto a branching tendon, not one side only.

## answer_c
Unipennate

## explanation_c
Correct. Pennate muscles are classed by how their fascicles meet the tendon. A unipennate muscle's fascicles approach the tendon from one side only, at an angle, resembling one side of a feather. This one-sided arrangement packs more fibres into a given muscle volume than a parallel-fibred muscle, at some cost to the total range of shortening.

## answer_d
Circumpinnate

## explanation_d
Incorrect. "Circumpinnate" is not a recognised pennate-muscle classification; it is a distractor term, not a real category.

## title
Territory drained by the right lymphatic duct

## question
The right lymphatic duct drains lymph from which of the following parts?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Abdomen

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The abdomen drains via the thoracic duct (through the cisterna chyli), not the right lymphatic duct.

## answer_b
Left lower limb

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The left lower limb drains via the thoracic duct, the larger of the two terminal lymph ducts.

## answer_c
Left upper limb

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The left upper limb also drains via the thoracic duct, not the right lymphatic duct.

## answer_d
Right side of the thorax

## explanation_d
Correct. The right lymphatic duct drains one quadrant of the body -- the right side of the head, neck, thorax and right upper limb. The thoracic duct, the larger of the two terminal lymph ducts, drains the other three quadrants. The right lymphatic duct empties into the venous system at the right venous angle, near the junction of the right subclavian and internal jugular veins.

## title
Cell junction allowing molecular passage

## question
Which of the following is the type of cell junction that allows the passage of molecules between adjacent epithelial cells?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Gap junction

## explanation_a
Correct. The gap junction is the only one of the four lateral junctions through which anything actually passes: paired channels, each built of six symmetrical transmembrane protein subunits, carry ions, small molecules and (between muscle cells) electrical impulses directly between adjacent cells. It is not part of the junctional complex, which is built from the other three junctions instead.

## answer_b
Macula adherens

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The macula adherens (desmosome) is a calcium-dependent anchoring junction that provides mechanical strength; it does not allow molecules to pass between cells.

## answer_c
Zonula adherens

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The zonula adherens is an adhesion belt anchoring the actin cytoskeleton; it seals cells together rather than permitting molecular passage between them.

## answer_d
Zonula occludens

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The zonula occludens (tight junction) actually fuses the adjacent membranes to seal the intercellular space, specifically preventing free passage between cells.

## title
What pinocytosis takes into the cell

## question
Which of the following can be taken in by the cell membrane through the process of pinocytosis?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Fluid particles

## explanation_a
Correct. The membrane takes material in by phagocytosis (large solid particles), pinocytosis (fluid) or receptor-mediated endocytosis (specific molecules), and expels material by exocytosis. Pinocytosis, literally "cell drinking", is specifically the fluid-uptake route.

## answer_b
Old organelle

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Old organelles are broken down internally (autophagy, forming autolysosomes), not taken in from outside the cell by pinocytosis.

## answer_c
Solid particles

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Solid particles are taken in by phagocytosis, a distinct process from the fluid uptake of pinocytosis.

## answer_d
Food particles

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Discrete food particles are engulfed by phagocytosis, not pinocytosis, which specifically takes in fluid.

## title
Cause of RER basophilia

## question
Rough endoplasmic reticulum is basophilic due to the presence of which of the following?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Acidic protein

## explanation_a
Incorrect. "Acidic protein" is not the specific structure responsible for RER's staining property -- ribosomal RNA is what carries the negative charge that binds basic dyes.

## answer_b
Lysosomes

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Lysosomes are unrelated organelles, not attached to or responsible for rough ER's basophilia.

## answer_c
Protein

## explanation_c
Incorrect. "Protein" alone is too non-specific; it is the ribosome's RNA content, not protein generically, that produces the basophilic staining.

## answer_d
Ribosomes

## explanation_d
Correct. Free ribosomes make protein the cell keeps for itself, while attached ribosomes, studded on the rough ER's cytoplasmic face, make protein for export. Both are composed largely of ribosomal RNA, whose phosphate backbone carries a strong negative charge. That negative charge is what binds basic dyes such as haematoxylin, producing the basophilic staining of ribosome-rich cytoplasm and rough ER.
