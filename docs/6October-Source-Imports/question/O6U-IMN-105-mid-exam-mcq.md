## title
Why hypoglycemia is dangerous

## question
Why is hypoglycemia dangerous?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
It affects the glucose supply to the brain

## explanation_a
Correct. The brain relies almost entirely on a continuous supply of blood glucose (it cannot use fatty acids and only switches to ketone bodies after prolonged fasting), so hypoglycemia is dangerous specifically because it threatens that glucose supply -- producing neuroglycopenic symptoms such as confusion, seizures or coma if severe or prolonged. This is why prompt correction of hypoglycemia is a clinical priority, to protect ongoing cerebral glucose delivery.

## answer_b
It is irreversible

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Hypoglycemia is not itself irreversible; it is corrected by restoring blood glucose (via counter-regulatory hormones or glucose administration), though prolonged severe episodes can cause lasting brain injury.

## answer_c
It leads to glucosuria

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Glucosuria requires hyperglycemia exceeding the renal threshold, the opposite of hypoglycemia's low blood glucose.

## answer_d
It produces hemolysis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Hypoglycemia does not produce hemolysis; that is unrelated to the danger being tested here.

## title
Complex I components

## question
In the electron transport chain, which of the following is not a component of Complex I?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Seven iron sulfur centers

## explanation_a
Incorrect (i.e. this is a component of Complex I). Complex I is built from roughly seven iron-sulfur centres that relay electrons toward coenzyme Q.

## answer_b
Many polypeptides

## explanation_b
Incorrect (i.e. this is a component of Complex I). Complex I is a large, multi-subunit assembly built from many polypeptides.

## answer_c
NADH dehydrogenase

## explanation_c
Incorrect (i.e. this is a component of Complex I). NADH dehydrogenase (NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase) is another name for Complex I itself, which accepts electrons from NADH.

## answer_d
Succinate dehydrogenase

## explanation_d
Correct. Succinate dehydrogenase is a separate complex (Complex II) that accepts electrons from FADH2 generated in the TCA cycle -- it is not a component of Complex I, even though both ultimately feed electrons into the same respiratory chain. Recognising that succinate dehydrogenase is a separate complex is a common exam discriminator between the two respiratory-chain entry points.

## title
Glucosuria definition

## question
Which of the following is the best definition of Glucosuria?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
It means Diabetes mellitus

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Glucosuria is a urinary finding, not a synonym for diabetes mellitus -- diabetes mellitus is a disease that commonly causes glucosuria, but glucosuria itself is defined independently of that diagnosis.

## answer_b
It is the presence of detectable amount of glucose in urine

## explanation_b
Correct. Glucosuria is defined as the presence of a detectable amount of glucose in urine, occurring once plasma glucose filtered at the glomerulus exceeds the renal tubular reabsorptive capacity. This renal-threshold-based definition applies regardless of the underlying cause, whether diabetic or otherwise.

## answer_c
It is the presence of fructose and glucose in urine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Glucosuria is defined by glucose in urine specifically, not a combination with fructose.

## answer_d
It means presence of less than 25 mg/dl of glucose in urine

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Glucosuria is not bounded by a specific low threshold such as "less than 25 mg/dl" -- it is simply detectable glucose in urine, at levels that in practice are well above such a low figure once the renal threshold is exceeded.

## title
TCA enzyme that is also an ETC member

## question
Which of the following enzymes of the TCA cycle is a member of the electron transport chain?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Isocitrate dehydrogenase

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Isocitrate dehydrogenase catalyses a TCA-cycle oxidation step but is a soluble mitochondrial matrix enzyme, not embedded in the inner membrane as an ETC complex.

## answer_b
Alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase catalyses a TCA-cycle oxidation step but, like isocitrate dehydrogenase, is a matrix enzyme, not an ETC complex.

## answer_c
Succinate thiokinase

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Succinate thiokinase (succinyl-CoA synthetase) generates GTP/ATP in the TCA cycle via substrate-level phosphorylation, but is not part of the electron transport chain.

## answer_d
Succinate dehydrogenase

## explanation_d
Correct. Succinate dehydrogenase is unique among TCA-cycle enzymes in being embedded directly in the inner mitochondrial membrane and doubling as Complex II of the electron transport chain, oxidising succinate to fumarate while passing electrons (via FAD) into the chain at coenzyme Q. No other TCA enzyme sits directly in the inner mitochondrial membrane as part of a respiratory complex the way succinate dehydrogenase does.

## title
Hormone that is NOT anti-insulin

## question
Which of the following hormones is not anti-insulin?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Parathormone

## explanation_a
Correct. Parathormone (PTH) regulates plasma calcium and phosphate (via bone resorption, renal calcium reabsorption and vitamin D activation), not glucose homeostasis -- it has no anti-insulin (counter-regulatory) role, unlike the other three options. Keeping the glucose-regulating and calcium-regulating hormone families separate avoids misattributing parathormone's effects to blood glucose control.

## answer_b
Adrenaline

## explanation_b
Incorrect (i.e. this is anti-insulin). Adrenaline is a genuine counter-regulatory hormone, raising blood glucose via glycogenolysis, opposing insulin's effect.

## answer_c
Growth hormone

## explanation_c
Incorrect (i.e. this is anti-insulin). Growth hormone genuinely is anti-insulin: it reduces peripheral glucose uptake and promotes lipolysis, raising blood glucose over time.

## answer_d
Glucagon

## explanation_d
Incorrect (i.e. this is anti-insulin). Glucagon is a genuine counter-regulatory hormone, stimulating glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis to raise blood glucose, directly opposing insulin.
