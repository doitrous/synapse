Neutrophil nuclear shape

## question
Neutrophil nucleus is:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

C

Unilobed

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A single unlobed nucleus describes a monocyte or a lymphocyte, not a neutrophil.

## answer_b

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A bilobed nucleus is the eosinophil's (and, less obviously, the basophil's) identifying feature, not the neutrophil's.

Multilobed

Incorrect. Multilobed is one of the listed options, so "none of the above" is wrong.

## subject

Incorrect (i.e. this statement is true). A reticulocyte is by definition the immature stage between the last nucleated erythroblast and the mature erythrocyte.

## explanation_b
Correct — this is the false statement, so it is the EXCEPT answer. Reticulocytes normally make up about 1% (not about 10%) of the total circulating erythrocyte count. ## answer_d
Reticulocytosis occurs in haemorrhage or high altitude

## explanation_d
Incorrect (i.e. this statement is true). Haemorrhage and high altitude both drive an appropriately increased marrow output of reticulocytes, i.e. reticulocytosis.

## title
Erythrocyte structural adaptations

## question
Regarding adaptation of erythrocytes:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Absence of nucleus & organelles

## explanation_a
Incorrect on its own — true, but not the fullest answer. Losing the nucleus and organelles is one real adaptation (it frees up room for haemoglobin), but the question asks for all adaptations at once.

## answer_b
The biconcave disc-shape of RBCs

## explanation_b
Incorrect on its own — true, but not the fullest answer. The biconcave disc shape increases the erythrocyte's surface area relative to its volume, but it is only one of the listed adaptations.

## answer_c
Cell membrane is flexible

## explanation_c
Incorrect on its own — true, but not the fullest answer. A flexible membrane lets the erythrocyte deform to pass through capillaries narrower than its own resting diameter, but it too is only one adaptation.

## answer_d
All of the above

## explanation_d
Correct. All three are genuine, independently examinable adaptations of the mature erythrocyte: it has no nucleus or organelles, giving it more room for haemoglobin; it is shaped as a biconcave disc, which increases its surface-area-to-volume ratio for gas exchange; and its membrane is flexible enough to deform through capillaries narrower than the cell's own resting diameter. Together they explain how a cell built purely to carry oxygen is also built to survive squeezing through the smallest vessels in the body.

Erythrocyte circulating lifespan

## question
The life span of an erythrocyte in the circulation is:

## subject

## status

Granular leukocytes differ in:

## subject

## title
Which maturation stage is mainly acidophilic

## question
Which of the following cells have mainly acidophilic cytoplasm?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer

## answer_a
Proerythroblast

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The proerythroblast is the earliest stage, with deeply basophilic (ribosome-rich) cytoplasm, not acidophilic.

## answer_b
Erythroblast

## explanation_b
Incorrect. "Erythroblast" alone is too general — cytoplasmic staining shifts across the erythroblast series from basophilic toward acidophilic, so the stage matters.

## answer_c
Orthochromatophilic erythroblast

## explanation_c
Correct. The orthochromatophilic erythroblast is the last nucleated stage before the reticulocyte, and by this stage haemoglobin has almost fully replaced the ribosomal machinery that made the earlier stages basophilic. Its cytoplasm therefore stains mainly acidophilic — the name "orthochromatophilic" literally marks it as having reached the normal (haemoglobin-dominant) staining pattern of a mature red cell's precursor.

## answer_d
Basophilic erythroblast

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The basophilic erythroblast is named for the opposite property — deeply basophilic cytoplasm from its abundant ribosomes, not acidophilic staining.

## title
Definition of leukocytosis

## question
Leukocytosis is:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
An increase of WBCs count

## explanation_a
Correct. Leukocytosis is a raised total white-cell (leukocyte) count on the CBC, above the normal range of roughly 4,000-10,000 cells/mm3, most often from infection or malignancy. It is a CBC finding, not a clinical diagnosis on its own — the specific cause still has to be worked out from the rest of the clinical picture.

## answer_b
An increase of RBCs count

## explanation_b
Incorrect. An increase in the red-cell count is polycythaemia, not leukocytosis.

## answer_c
A decrease of WBCs count

## title
Monocyte identifying feature

## question
Monocytes are characterized by one of the following:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Granular cytoplasm

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Monocytes are agranular — they carry no specific granules, unlike the neutrophils, eosinophils and basophils.

## answer_b
Phagocytic function

## explanation_b
Correct. The monocyte's defining function is phagocytosis: it circulates briefly in an agranular, kidney-nucleated form before migrating into tissues and differentiating into a macrophage, which then phagocytoses pathogens and debris. This phagocytic role is the feature that most reliably characterises the monocyte-macrophage line.

## answer_c
The mother cell of lymphocytes

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Monocytes are a separate, myeloid-lineage cell; they are not the precursor of lymphocytes, which arise from their own lymphoid stem-cell lineage.

## answer_d
Show negative golgi image

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A prominent (not negative) Golgi image is typical of an actively secreting protein-forming cell such as a plasma cell, not a defining feature used to characterise monocytes.
