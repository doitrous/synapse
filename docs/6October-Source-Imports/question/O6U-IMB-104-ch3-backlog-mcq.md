## title
Function of the TATA sequence in eukaryotes

## question
In eukaryotes, the TATA sequence functions as:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
an indication of the starting point for replication.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Origins of replication are marked by distinct origin-recognition sequences, not by the TATA box, which belongs to transcriptional promoters.

## answer_b
an indication of the starting point for transcription.

## explanation_b
Correct. The TATA box is a core promoter element found roughly 25-30 base pairs upstream of the transcription start site in many eukaryotic genes. It is recognised by the TATA-binding protein (TBP), which nucleates assembly of the general transcription factors and RNA polymerase II, positioning the polymerase to begin transcription at the correct nucleotide.

## answer_c
a termination signal for transcription.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Transcription termination in eukaryotes is signalled by sequences near the 3' end of the gene (linked to cleavage/polyadenylation), not by the TATA box, which sits upstream at the start of the gene.

## answer_d
an indication of the starting point for translation.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Translation begins at the AUG start codon on the mature mRNA, not at the TATA box, which acts before transcription even begins.

## title
What mRNA is complementary to

## question
mRNA is complementary to the nucleotide sequence of:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Coding strand

## explanation_a
Incorrect. mRNA has essentially the same sequence as the coding (sense) strand, not its complement — the coding strand and the mRNA differ only in that T is replaced by U.

## answer_b
Ribosomal RNA

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Ribosomal RNA is a separate RNA species that forms part of the ribosome; mRNA is not synthesised as its complement.

## answer_c
tRNA

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Transfer RNA is a separate class of RNA involved in translation, not the strand RNA polymerase reads during transcription.

## answer_d
Template strand

## explanation_d
Correct. RNA polymerase reads the template (antisense) strand of DNA 3' to 5' and synthesises mRNA complementary to it, 5' to 3'. Because the template strand is itself complementary to the coding strand, the resulting mRNA ends up matching the coding strand's sequence (with U in place of T).

## title
Role of the sigma factor

## question
The role of the sigma factor in bacterial RNA polymerase is:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

B

to catalyze RNA synthesis:

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Catalysing the actual RNA polymerisation reaction is carried out by the core enzyme's catalytic subunits, not by sigma.

## answer_b
to position RNA polymerase correctly on the template DNA.

## explanation_b
Correct. Sigma factor is the subunit that joins the bacterial RNA polymerase core enzyme to form the holoenzyme, and its job is to recognise the promoter sequence and correctly position the polymerase there so transcription starts at the right nucleotide. ## explanation_c
Incorrect. Termination of bacterial transcription is signalled by rho factor or by intrinsic hairpin structures, not by sigma, which acts at initiation.

## answer_d
to unwind the DNA template

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Local unwinding of the DNA template at the promoter follows sigma-directed positioning, but sigma itself is a positioning/recognition factor, not the unwinding activity.

## question
Processing of mRNA does not include:

## subject

Correct. "Trimming" is not one of the three standard mRNA processing steps taught for eukaryotic pre-mRNA — those are capping, splicing, and polyadenylation. It is the odd term out among the four options, which is why processing does not include it.

## title
True statement about RNA synthesis (I)

## question
Which of the following is true of RNA synthesis (transcription)?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
RNA synthesis is always in the 5' - 3' direction.

## explanation_a
Correct. Like DNA polymerase, RNA polymerase can only extend a chain from a free 3'-OH end, so RNA synthesis always proceeds 5' to 3'. This holds for every RNA polymerase and every gene transcribed.

## answer_b
RNA polymerase needs a primer to initiate transcription.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Unlike DNA polymerase, RNA polymerase can initiate synthesis de novo and does not require a primer to start transcription.

## answer_c
The sequence of the mRNA is complementary to the sequence of coding strand

## explanation_c
Incorrect. mRNA is complementary to the template strand, not the coding strand — mRNA actually matches the coding strand's sequence (with U replacing T).

## answer_d
The RNA polymerase utilizes ribonucleoside triphosphates (ATP, GTP, CTP and TTP)

## explanation_d
Incorrect. RNA is built from ribonucleoside triphosphates ATP, GTP, CTP and UTP; "TTP" (thymidine triphosphate) is a DNA precursor, not one of RNA's building blocks, since RNA uses uracil instead of thymine.

Inhibitor of prokaryotic RNA polymerase-promoter binding

## question
Binding of the prokaryotic DNA dependent RNA polymerase to promoter site is inhibited by

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Rifampicin

## explanation_a
Correct. Rifampicin binds the beta subunit of bacterial DNA-dependent RNA polymerase and blocks initiation of transcription, which is why it is used clinically as an antimycobacterial (e.g. in tuberculosis and meningococcal prophylaxis regimens).

## answer_b
Tetracycline

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Tetracycline inhibits bacterial translation by blocking aminoacyl-tRNA binding to the ribosomal A site, not RNA polymerase binding to the promoter.

## answer_c
Puromycin

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Puromycin inhibits translation by mimicking aminoacyl-tRNA and causing premature chain termination on the ribosome; it does not act on RNA polymerase.

## answer_d
Streptomycin

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Streptomycin inhibits bacterial translation by binding the 30S ribosomal subunit and causing misreading of mRNA; it is not a transcription inhibitor.

## title
True statement about RNA synthesis (II)

## question
Which of the following is true about RNA synthesis

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Synthesis of RNA is always in the 5' to 3' direction

## explanation_a
Correct. As with DNA polymerase, RNA polymerase extends the growing chain only from a free 3'-OH end. This chemistry means RNA synthesis is always 5' to 3', for every gene and every RNA polymerase. No exception to this directionality is known.

## answer_b
RNA polymerase requires a primer for initiating transcription

## explanation_b
Incorrect. RNA polymerase can initiate transcription de novo, without needing a primer — this is one of the features that distinguishes it from DNA polymerase.

## answer_c
U is inserted opposite T in transcription

## explanation_c
Incorrect. During transcription, uracil is inserted opposite adenine on the template strand, not opposite thymine; a template thymine directs insertion of adenine in the RNA, not uracil.

## answer_d
New nucleotides are added on the 2'-OH of the ribose sugar.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. New nucleotides are joined onto the free 3'-OH of the ribose sugar at the growing end of the chain, not the 2'-OH; the 3'-OH is what forms the new phosphodiester bond.

## title
Calculating processed mRNA coding-region length

## question
A gene encodes a protein with 150 amino acids. There is one intron of 1000bps, a 5'- untranslated region of 100bp, and a 3'-untranslated region of 200bp. In the final processed mRNA, how many bases lie between the start AUG codon and the final termination codon?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
1750

## explanation_a
Incorrect. 1750 corresponds to including the intron (1000bp) and the UTRs in the count, but the intron is removed during splicing and the UTRs lie outside the coding region, so neither belongs between the start and stop codons.

## answer_b
750

## explanation_b
Incorrect. 750 does not correspond to the coding-region length implied by 150 encoded amino acids; it overcounts relative to the open reading frame.

## answer_c
650

## explanation_c
Incorrect. 650 likewise does not match the coding sequence length implied by a 150-amino-acid protein.

## answer_d
450

## explanation_d
Correct. The intron and the two untranslated regions lie outside (or are spliced out of) the coding sequence, so they do not count toward the distance between the start and stop codons. The coding region itself is set by the protein length: 150 codons at 3 bases each gives the open reading frame's span, which this item's key rounds to 450 bases.

## title
mRNA features that protect against degradation

## question
What sequence feature of mature mRNAs listed below is thought to protect mRNAs from degradation?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
3' Poly (A)n tail

## explanation_a
Incorrect alone. The 3' poly(A) tail does protect mRNA from 3'-to-5' exonuclease degradation, but the 5' cap contributes equivalent protection at the other end, so option A alone is incomplete.

## answer_b
5' G cap

## explanation_b
Incorrect alone. The 5' cap does protect the transcript from 5'-to-3' exonuclease degradation, but the poly(A) tail contributes the same kind of protection at the 3' end, so option B alone is incomplete.

## answer_c
Introns

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Introns are removed during splicing and are not retained in mature mRNA at all, so they cannot be a protective feature of the mature transcript.

## answer_d
Both a and b

## explanation_d
Correct. Together, the 5' cap and the 3' poly(A) tail shield the two ends of a mature mRNA from exonuclease attack. Each structure guards one end of the molecule, so neither alone gives the full picture. This dual protection is a major reason processed mRNAs are far more stable than an unprotected, unprocessed transcript would be.

## title
Bond linking the 5' cap to mRNA

## question
The bond connecting the initiation nucleotide of the mRNA with the 5'-G Cap structure is a:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A 3'-5' phosphodiester bridge is the standard linkage between successive nucleotides within an RNA or DNA chain, not the unusual bond joining the cap to the first transcribed nucleotide.

## answer_b
5'-5' triphosphate bridge

## explanation_b
Correct. The 7-methylguanosine cap is attached to the first transcribed nucleotide through an unusual 5'-5' triphosphate bridge, rather than the standard 3'-5' phosphodiester linkage used elsewhere in the chain. 3'-3' triphosphate bridge

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The bridge is 5'-5', not 3'-3'; the cap's guanosine is joined through its 5' carbon, not its 3' carbon.

## answer_d
3'-5' triphosphate bridge

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The linkage is a triphosphate bridge, but it connects the 5' carbons on both sides, not a 3' carbon to a 5' carbon.

## title
Name for the accessory proteins RNA polymerases require to bind promoters

## question
Eukaryotic RNA polymerases all have a requirement for a large variety of accessory proteins to enable them to bind promoters and form physiologically relevant transcription complexes; these proteins are termed

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Basal or general transcription factors

## explanation_a
Correct. The proteins that all eukaryotic RNA polymerases need in order to bind promoters and assemble a functional initiation complex are called basal (general) transcription factors — for RNA polymerase II these include TFIIA, TFIIB, TFIID (containing TBP), TFIIE, TFIIF and TFIIH. They are required at essentially every promoter, unlike gene-specific regulatory proteins.

## answer_b
Activators

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Activators are gene-specific regulatory proteins that increase transcription at particular promoters; they are not the universally required, basal machinery this question describes.

## answer_c
Accessory factors

## explanation_c
Incorrect. "Accessory factors" is not the standard term used for this universally required initiation machinery; the standard term is general/basal transcription factors.

## answer_d
Elongation factors

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Elongation factors act after initiation, helping RNA polymerase move along the gene body, not to help it first bind the promoter.
