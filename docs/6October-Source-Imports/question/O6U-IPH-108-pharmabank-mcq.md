## question

## subject

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Exocrine glands carry the M3 muscarinic receptor subtype, not M2.

## answer_b
Heart

## explanation_b
Correct. M2 muscarinic receptors are located in the heart, where they are Gi-protein coupled and slow the heart rate by decreasing intracellular cAMP. Exocrine glands, vascular endothelium and smooth muscle instead carry the Gq-coupled M3 receptor subtype, which signals through IP3/DAG rather than cAMP. This M2/M3 split by tissue and signalling pathway is why the two receptor subtypes are tested separately.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Vascular endothelium carries the M3 muscarinic receptor subtype, which triggers nitric oxide release, not M2.

## answer_d
Smooth muscle

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Smooth muscle carries the M3 muscarinic receptor subtype, which mediates contraction, not M2.

## title
Why acetylcholine is unsuitable for clinical use

## question
Acetylcholine is nearly not used in clinical practice because:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
It is very costly

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Cost is not the reason acetylcholine is avoided clinically; its near-instant hydrolysis is.

## answer_b
The doses required are very high

## explanation_b
Incorrect as the single best answer here. High doses would indeed be needed for any sustained effect, but that requirement is itself a direct consequence of the rapid hydrolysis printed as this question's correct answer, not a separate root cause.

## answer_c
It is highly toxic

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Acetylcholine is not characteristically described as highly toxic; its clinical unsuitability instead comes from its extremely short duration of action.

## answer_d
It is ineffective orally

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Oral ineffectiveness is not the reason cited; acetylcholine is in any case not given orally at all, since it would be hydrolysed and poorly absorbed regardless of route.

## answer_e
It is very rapidly hydrolyzed

## explanation_e
Correct. Acetylcholine is hydrolysed almost instantly by acetylcholinesterase and plasma cholinesterase once it is given, so its duration of action is extremely short. Combined with its lack of receptor selectivity for muscarinic versus nicotinic sites, this means the doses needed to sustain any therapeutic effect would be impractically high. This is why more selective, longer-acting cholinomimetics such as carbachol, bethanechol or pilocarpine are used clinically instead of acetylcholine itself.

## title
M2 blockade does not directly relax smooth muscle

## question
The atropine blocking action on the M2 receptors can induce all of the following effects except:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
CNS stimulation

## explanation_a
Incorrect. CNS stimulation is a genuine effect linkable to atropine's blockade of central muscarinic receptors including M2, so this is not the exception.

## answer_b
Tachycardia

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Tachycardia from blocking the cardiac M2 receptor is a genuine, classic atropine effect, so this is not the exception.

## answer_c
Smooth muscle relaxation

## explanation_c
Correct. Smooth muscle relaxation from atropine results mainly from blocking the Gq-coupled M3 receptor on smooth muscle, not the Gi-coupled M2 receptor that atropine also blocks in the heart and at some presynaptic sites. M2 blockade instead accounts for CNS effects (atropine is a tertiary amine that crosses the blood-brain barrier), tachycardia (removing the M2-mediated brake on the SA node), and, by blocking presynaptic M2 autoreceptors that normally inhibit further acetylcholine release, an increase in acetylcholine release. Because the question asks for the exception, smooth muscle relaxation is the one effect that belongs to M3, not M2, blockade.

## answer_d
Bradycardia

## explanation_d
Incorrect. At very low doses, atropine can paradoxically cause transient bradycardia via partial presynaptic M2 blockade before the dominant tachycardic effect appears, so this remains a recognised, if minor, part of the drug's M2-linked profile, not the exception the question asks for.

## answer_e
Increase Ach release

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Increased acetylcholine release from blocking presynaptic M2 autoreceptors is a genuine, recognised effect, so this is not the exception.

## question
In children, the most dangerous effects of anticholinergic drugs Is

## status

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Cycloplegia is a genuine anticholinergic effect but is not the most dangerous one in children.

## answer_b
Bradycardia

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Anticholinergic drugs cause tachycardia, not bradycardia, by removing vagal restraint on the heart.

## answer_c
Tachycardia

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Tachycardia is a genuine anticholinergic effect but is not the most dangerous one in children, unlike the impaired heat dissipation that causes hyperthermia.

## answer_d
Hyperthermia

## explanation_d
Correct. Anticholinergic drugs block sweating by blocking muscarinic receptors on eccrine sweat glands, which impairs a child's ability to dissipate heat through evaporation; combined with children's relatively larger surface-area-to-mass ratio, this can produce dangerous, rapidly progressive hyperthermia that is the most feared anticholinergic toxicity in this age group. Cycloplegia, tachycardia and dry mucous membranes (contributing to a sensation of dehydration) are genuine anticholinergic effects but are not the most dangerous. Bradycardia is not an anticholinergic effect at all, since blocking muscarinic tone on the heart produces tachycardia, not bradycardia.

## answer_e
Dehydration

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Dry mucous membranes are a genuine anticholinergic effect, but hyperthermia from impaired sweating is the more dangerous complication in a child.
