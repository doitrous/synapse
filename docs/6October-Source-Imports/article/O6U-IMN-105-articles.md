## summary
Hypoglycemia is dangerous chiefly because the brain depends on a continuous glucose supply,
this course sets its diagnostic threshold below 45 mg/dl, and glucosuria is simply detectable
urinary glucose once the renal threshold is exceeded.

## sections
### Definition
The brain relies almost entirely on blood glucose for energy and cannot use fatty acids, so
hypoglycemia threatens it specifically -- this is why hypoglycemia is dangerous, rather than
being irreversible or itself producing glucosuria or hemolysis. This course diagnoses
hypoglycemia at a blood glucose level below 45 mg/dl. Glucosuria is defined as the presence of
a detectable amount of glucose in urine, occurring once filtered glucose exceeds the renal
tubular reabsorptive capacity (roughly 180 mg/dl plasma glucose) -- it is a urinary finding,
not a synonym for diabetes mellitus.

### Mechanism
Below the hypoglycemic threshold, counter-regulatory hormones (glucagon, adrenaline, cortisol,
growth hormone) are triggered to raise blood glucose, but if the fall is severe or prolonged
enough, neuroglycopenic symptoms (confusion, seizures, coma) follow from inadequate cerebral
glucose delivery. Glucosuria develops once the renal threshold is exceeded because the
proximal tubule's glucose transporters (SGLT2 mainly) become saturated and can no longer
reabsorb all the filtered glucose.

### Key determinants
"Brain glucose supply" is the specific danger tested for hypoglycemia, not a generic or
unrelated consequence. This course's numeric cutoff (<45 mg/dl) is the value to reach for on
this specific exam, distinguishing it from higher distractor values that sit near or within
the normal range. "Detectable glucose in urine" -- nothing more specific -- is glucosuria's
full definition; it is a finding, not a disease name.

### Clinical significance
Recognising hypoglycemia's danger as specifically cerebral (rather than vague or unrelated)
guides the urgency of correction in clinical practice; glucosuria is a practical bedside/
laboratory clue that plasma glucose has exceeded the renal threshold, most commonly from
diabetes mellitus but not defined by it.

## summary
Succinate dehydrogenase is the single TCA-cycle enzyme that also functions as electron
transport chain Complex II, while Complex I is built from NADH dehydrogenase, iron-sulfur
centres and multiple polypeptides -- and does not include succinate dehydrogenase.

## sections
### Definition
Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase/NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase) accepts electrons from NADH via
many polypeptide subunits and roughly seven iron-sulfur centres, passing them to coenzyme Q.
Succinate dehydrogenase is a separate complex (Complex II), unique among TCA-cycle enzymes for
being embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane: it oxidises succinate to fumarate in the
TCA cycle while passing the electrons gained (via FAD) directly into the respiratory chain.

### Mechanism
Both complexes ultimately reduce coenzyme Q, but from different electron donors (NADH for
Complex I, FADH2 generated at succinate dehydrogenase for Complex II), and only Complex I
pumps protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane as electrons pass through it --
Complex II does not contribute to the proton gradient directly, one reason its dual TCA/ETC
role is distinctive rather than typical.

### Key determinants
Succinate dehydrogenase is the answer whenever a question asks which TCA enzyme also belongs
to the ETC, or which ETC complex is not present in Complex I specifically -- other TCA
enzymes (isocitrate dehydrogenase, alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, pyruvate carboxylase)
are not ETC complexes at all.

### Clinical significance
Succinate dehydrogenase mutations (in the SDH gene family) are associated with hereditary
paraganglioma/phaeochromocytoma syndromes, reflecting the enzyme's central role bridging two
major energy-metabolism pathways.

## summary
Adrenaline, growth hormone, glucagon and cortisol are the classic anti-insulin (counter-
regulatory) hormones that raise blood glucose; parathormone is not one of them, since its job
is calcium and phosphate regulation, not glucose homeostasis.

## sections
### Definition
The counter-regulatory (anti-insulin) hormones -- adrenaline, growth hormone, glucagon and
cortisol -- all act to raise blood glucose, directly opposing insulin's glucose-lowering
effect, and are mobilised during hypoglycemia or stress. Parathormone (PTH) regulates plasma
calcium and phosphate instead, via bone resorption, renal calcium reabsorption and vitamin D
activation -- it has no role in glucose counter-regulation, making it the odd one out among a
list of otherwise-genuine anti-insulin hormones.

### Mechanism
Each true anti-insulin hormone raises glucose by a different route: adrenaline and glucagon
stimulate glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis acutely; growth hormone reduces peripheral
glucose uptake and promotes lipolysis over a longer time course; cortisol promotes
gluconeogenesis and insulin resistance. Parathormone's signalling (via PTH receptors on bone
and kidney) touches none of these glucose-regulating pathways.

### Key determinants
When a question lists parathormone alongside genuine anti-insulin hormones and asks which one
does NOT raise glucose, parathormone is reliably the answer -- its physiological remit is
calcium/phosphate, not glucose.

### Clinical significance
Mistaking parathormone for a glucose-regulating hormone would misdirect a differential for
either hypoglycemia (whose true counter-regulatory hormones are adrenaline, growth hormone,
glucagon and cortisol) or hypercalcemia (where PTH is the relevant hormone) -- keeping the two
hormone families separate avoids that error.
