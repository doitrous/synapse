## summary
Neutrophils, eosinophils and basophils are the granular leukocytes, told apart by three
criteria held at once: nuclear shape, relative percentage, and the staining reaction of
their specific granules. Each has a distinctive, commonly-confused pair of features.

## sections
### Definition
The neutrophil's single nucleus is divided into three to five lobes ("polymorphonuclear").
The eosinophil's nucleus is bilobed, and its specific granules are acidophilic (eosin pink)
with a dense crystalline core visible by electron microscopy -- the only granulocyte whose
granules have this internal crystalline structure. The basophil's nucleus is bilobed or
S-shaped and usually obscured by its own coarse, deeply basophilic (metachromatic) granules
-- never simply "central and rounded". Normal relative percentages run neutrophils (the
majority of leukocytes) > eosinophils (about 1-4%) > basophils (about 0.5%, the rarest).

### Mechanism
Each granulocyte's specific (secondary) granules carry a distinct chemical payload that
determines both its staining behaviour and its function: neutrophil granules carry
antimicrobial enzymes and stain neutrally; eosinophil granules carry major basic protein in
a crystalline core and stain acidophilic, suited to killing large parasites; basophil (and
mast cell) granules carry histamine and heparin and stain metachromatically with toluidine
blue, suited to an allergic/inflammatory role. Nuclear shape is an independent identifying
feature layered on top of granule staining, not derived from it.

### Key determinants
A granulocyte is named from its specific granules' staining reaction (neutrophilic,
eosinophilic/acidophilic, or basophilic/metachromatic), and its nuclear shape is a second,
independent identifying feature -- both must be checked together, since granule staining can
be hard to judge on a poorly-fixed film while nuclear shape usually remains clear.

### Clinical significance
Because eosinophils and basophils are both minority granulocytes with lobed/segmented
nuclei, exam distractors commonly swap their percentages, their granule contents, or assign
one's crystalline-core feature to the other. Fixing "crystalline core = eosinophil only" and
"metachromatic granules obscuring the nucleus = basophil" as the two granulocytes' single
most distinctive feature avoids that swap.

## published_summary

## published_sections

## hold_these
The neutrophil nucleus is multilobed (3-5 lobes), not simply segmented into two.
Only the eosinophil's specific granules have a crystalline core; its nucleus is bilobed.
The basophil nucleus is bilobed/S-shaped and typically obscured by granules, not "central and rounded".
Granulocytes differ in nuclear shape, relative percentage, and granule staining reaction, all three together.

## lose_the_mark
Assigning the crystalline-core granule feature to neutrophils or basophils instead of eosinophils.
Reading a granulocyte's nucleus as "central and rounded" -- that description fits a monocyte or lymphocyte, not any granulocyte.
Treating percentage alone as sufficient to identify a granulocyte without checking nuclear shape and granule staining.

## summary
Across the erythroblast maturation series, cell size falls, the nucleus condenses and is
extruded (never lobulated), and ribosome-rich basophilic cytoplasm gives way to
haemoglobin-rich acidophilic cytoplasm -- the orthochromatophilic erythroblast is the stage
that reaches that acidophilic end point. Circulating reticulocytes, the last stage before a
mature erythrocyte, normally make up about 1% of total erythrocytes.

## sections
### Definition
The erythroblast series runs proerythroblast -> basophilic erythroblast ->
polychromatophilic erythroblast -> orthochromatophilic erythroblast -> reticulocyte ->
mature erythrocyte. The orthochromatophilic erythroblast is the last nucleated stage, and
the first to show mainly acidophilic cytoplasm, because haemoglobin has by then almost
fully replaced the ribosomal machinery. Reticulocytes are anucleate but still carry residual
ribosomal RNA, and normally make up about 1% (not 10%) of the circulating erythrocyte count.

### Mechanism
Three changes proceed in parallel across the series: cell size decreases; the nucleus
becomes progressively smaller and more condensed (heterochromatic) until it is extruded
whole from the cell at the orthochromatophilic stage -- an erythroid nucleus is never
lobulated, since lobulation is a granulocyte-only nuclear change; and cytoplasmic basophilia
(from ribosomes making globin) falls as haemoglobin accumulates and acidophilia rises.

### Key determinants
Cytoplasmic tinctorial state (basophilic -> polychromatophilic -> acidophilic/
orthochromatophilic) is the fastest way to place a given erythroblast in the series on a
stained film, since it tracks haemoglobin content directly. Nuclear condensation tracks the
same maturation but is judged separately, and its endpoint (extrusion) is a discrete event,
not a gradual lobulation.

### Clinical significance
An elevated reticulocyte count (reticulocytosis) signals an appropriately increased marrow
response to anaemia or hypoxia; a normal resting reticulocyte count of about 1% is the
baseline against which that response is measured, so overstating the normal baseline (e.g.
reading "about 10%" as normal) would make a genuine reticulocytosis look unremarkable.

## published_summary

## published_sections

## hold_these
Reticulocytes are normally about 1% of total erythrocytes, not about 10%.
The orthochromatophilic erythroblast is the maturation stage with mainly acidophilic cytoplasm.
RBC maturation condenses and extrudes the nucleus -- it never lobulates it.
Cytoplasmic basophilia falls and haemoglobin/acidophilia rises across the erythroblast series.

## lose_the_mark
Naming the basophilic erythroblast when asked which stage has acidophilic cytoplasm -- basophilic erythroblast is named for the opposite staining.
Reading "10% of total erythrocytes" as the normal reticulocyte baseline.
Assuming the erythroid nucleus lobulates by analogy with the granulocyte series -- it condenses and is extruded whole instead.

## summary
Bone marrow stroma is not passive scaffolding: reticular cells stimulate haemopoietic stem
cells to differentiate, and fat cells serve as the marrow's local fuel reserve. The
myeloblast, the earliest recognisable granulocyte precursor, carries no specific granules
yet.

## sections
### Definition
The bone marrow stroma is a supportive framework of reticular cells (secreting the
reticular fibre meshwork), fat cells, macrophages and blood vessels, within which
haemopoietic stem cells proliferate and differentiate. The myeloblast is the earliest
granulocyte-series cell recognisable as such: a large cell with a pale, finely stippled
nucleus, prominent nucleoli, and a narrow basophilic cytoplasmic rim carrying no specific
granules -- those appear only from the promyelocyte stage onward.

### Mechanism
Reticular cells provide the local growth-factor signalling that stimulates stem-cell
proliferation and differentiation into the various blood lines, functionally distinct from
their structural role of secreting reticular fibres. Fat cells store lipid that is drawn on
as fuel for the metabolically demanding process of hemopoiesis happening around them, and
the marrow's balance of red (active, haemopoietic) versus yellow (inactive, fat-replaced)
marrow shifts with the body's ongoing demand for new blood cells.

### Key determinants
Distinguishing a myeloblast from a promyelocyte on a marrow film turns on granules: the
myeloblast has none, the promyelocyte has large non-specific azurophilic granules -- one
stage's worth of difference that is easy to test directly.

### Clinical significance
A marrow biopsy's ratio of active red marrow to fat-replaced yellow marrow, and the
proportion of blast forms present, are both read from these same structural and functional
roles -- an expanded blast population with reduced maturation is the histological signature
several marrow disorders share.

## published_summary

## published_sections

## hold_these
Bone marrow reticular cells stimulate haemopoietic stem cells to form blood cells.
Bone marrow fat cells are the local fuel reserve for hemopoiesis.
The myeloblast is the earliest recognisable granulocyte precursor and has no specific granules.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing the reticular cell's stem-cell-stimulating function with its structural role of secreting reticular fibres, as if only one of the two were true.
Confusing the myeloblast (agranular) with the promyelocyte (large azurophilic granules) as "the" granule-free stage.

## summary
Monocytes are agranular, phagocytic myeloid cells, a separate lineage from lymphocytes. Both
T- and B-lymphocyte stem cells originate in the bone marrow, though T-cell precursors
complete their maturation in the thymus.

## sections
### Definition
The monocyte is the largest normal circulating leukocyte: agranular (no specific granules),
with a large kidney- or horseshoe-shaped nucleus and abundant pale cytoplasm, defined
functionally by phagocytosis and by its short circulating life before migrating into
tissues to become a macrophage. T- and B-lymphocytes both trace back to a haemopoietic stem
cell that originates in the bone marrow; B-lymphocytes mature there, while T-lymphocyte
precursors leave while still immature and complete maturation in the thymus.

### Mechanism
Monocytes and lymphocytes are both agranular mononuclear leukocytes on a stained film, which
is why they are commonly confused with each other -- but they arise from separate stem-cell
lineages (myeloid for the monocyte, lymphoid for the lymphocyte), not one from the other.
The site named for a lymphocyte's origin (bone marrow, for the stem cell) and the site named
for its later maturation (thymus, for T-cells specifically) are two different facts that
answer two different exam questions.

### Key determinants
Whether a question asks for the stem cell's origin (bone marrow, for both T- and
B-lymphocytes) or a specific lineage's site of later maturation (thymus, for T-cells only)
determines which answer is correct -- reading the question's exact phrasing matters here.

### Clinical significance
Mistaking the monocyte for a lymphocyte precursor, or the thymus for the origin rather than
the maturation site of T-cells, are both errors that would misattribute where an
immunodeficiency or a leukaemia's abnormal cell population originates.

## published_summary

## published_sections

## hold_these
Monocytes are agranular and phagocytic, and belong to the myeloid lineage.
Monocytes are not the mother cell of lymphocytes.
T- and B-lymphocyte stem cells both originate in the bone marrow.

## lose_the_mark
Calling the monocyte "the mother cell of lymphocytes" from surface resemblance on a film.
Answering "Thymus" for where lymphocyte stem cells originate, instead of where T-cells later mature.
