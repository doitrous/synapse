## summary
Cholinesterase inhibitors split by reversibility (neostigmine, physostigmine, pyridostigmine, edrophonium reversible; echothiophate, isoflurophate irreversible) and, independently, by whether their structure -- lipid-soluble tertiary amine versus charged quaternary compound -- lets them cross the blood-brain barrier, penetrate the cornea, and be absorbed orally. Three commonly-confused non-effects of cholinomimetics round out this article: glaucoma is treated, not caused, by a cholinomimetic eye drop; hyperglycemia is not a cholinomimetic adverse effect; and skeletal-muscle weakness is a nicotinic, not muscarinic, finding.

## sections
### Definition
Cholinesterase inhibitors are indirect-acting cholinomimetics: rather than binding and activating the receptor themselves, they inhibit acetylcholinesterase, letting endogenously-released acetylcholine accumulate and act longer at its own muscarinic and nicotinic receptors.

### Mechanism
Reversibility and CNS penetration are independent properties. Physostigmine is a lipid-soluble tertiary amine, reversible, and crosses the blood-brain barrier, giving it central as well as peripheral action -- useful for reversing the anticholinergic toxidrome. Neostigmine, pyridostigmine and edrophonium are reversible but charged quaternary compounds, poorly and unreliably absorbed orally, excluded from the CNS and cornea, restricting their action to the periphery. Echothiophate is irreversible yet also charged and CNS-excluded, which is exactly why it is safe as a topical glaucoma drop; isoflurophate, though also irreversible, is lipid-soluble and does cross the blood-brain barrier. Separately, glaucoma is the condition a cholinomimetic eye drop treats (by increasing aqueous outflow), not an adverse effect of one; cholinomimetics slow rather than speed the heart and do not raise blood glucose, so hyperglycemia is not one of their effects; and skeletal-muscle weakness under cholinergic excess follows from nicotinic, not muscarinic, receptor overstimulation at the neuromuscular junction.

### Key determinants
A cholinesterase inhibitor's molecular charge (quaternary ammonium versus lipid-soluble tertiary amine) is the single feature that predicts oral absorption, corneal penetration and CNS access, independent of whether its enzyme binding is reversible or irreversible.

### Clinical significance
Choosing a CNS-penetrant inhibitor (physostigmine) for a central toxidrome, or a CNS-excluded one (echothiophate, neostigmine) for a purely peripheral or topical indication, depends entirely on getting this structure/permeability classification right.

## summary
Atropine's muscarinic blockade produces a broad, internally-consistent effect profile: tachycardia with little direct effect on blood pressure (and no ability to block a beta-adrenergic response like epinephrine's); relaxation of visceral smooth muscle (but not laryngeal skeletal muscle) and the classic mydriasis/raised-IOP/cycloplegia ocular triad; oral absorption and CNS penetration rather than injection-only, CNS-excluded pharmacokinetics; and smooth-muscle relaxation mediated mainly by M3, not M2, receptor blockade. Several other drug classes (antihistamines, opioids, tricyclics, antiarrhythmics) carry atropine-like side effects, while a purpose-built antimuscarinic, not atropine itself, is preferred for Parkinsonism. Finally, several antimuscarinic facts run counter to a naive guess: asthma is not a contraindication, short duration is not a disadvantage for incontinence therapy, hyoscine is more (not less) potent than atropine as an antisecretory, and pediatric hyperthermia from impaired sweating is the most dangerous anticholinergic effect in children.

## sections
### Definition
Atropine is the prototypical muscarinic antagonist; its effect profile across the heart, smooth muscle, eye, glands and CNS follows directly and consistently from blocking muscarinic receptor subtypes throughout the body.

### Mechanism
Removing the vagal M2 brake on the SA/AV node produces tachycardia with little direct vascular effect, since resistance vessels lack significant direct parasympathetic innervation; this M2 blockade is a separate receptor system from the beta-adrenergic receptors epinephrine acts on, so atropine cannot block epinephrine's cardiac stimulation, and it is the wrong drug for a tachyarrhythmia. Blocking the Gq-coupled M3 receptor on visceral smooth muscle (not the Gi-coupled M2 receptor) is what relaxes gut, ureteric and bronchial smooth muscle and produces the ocular triad; laryngeal muscle, being skeletal and nicotinic-controlled, is unaffected. As a lipid-soluble tertiary amine, atropine is orally absorbed, CNS-penetrant, and even systemically absorbed after topical ocular instillation. Pre-anesthetic atropine is given deliberately to dry secretions, not for its incidental mydriasis, which can hinder pupil-based anesthetic-depth monitoring. Beyond atropine itself, antihistamines, opioids, tricyclics and antiarrhythmics carry atropine-like side effects as part of their own pharmacology, while purpose-built antimuscarinics (not atropine) are preferred for chronic Parkinsonism therapy. Hyoscine's central penetration makes it more sedating, more antiemetic and, contrary to a naive guess, more potent as an antisecretory than atropine.

### Key determinants
Which muscarinic receptor subtype (M2 versus M3) and which tissue (cardiac, smooth muscle, glandular, ocular, skeletal) a given atropine effect acts through determines whether that effect is genuine, and whether a related drug or condition is a true match, mismatch, contraindication or special risk.

### Clinical significance
Mismatching atropine to a tachyarrhythmia, assuming its mydriasis is a therapeutic pre-anesthetic goal, misreading its M2/M3 mechanism, missing an atropine-like drug interaction, or missing the pediatric hyperthermia risk each carries real clinical consequences.

## summary
Two drugs anchor a set of commonly-tested indication-matching trivia. Neostigmine is mismatched to hair tonic (a pilocarpine use) and to urinary incontinence (it treats the opposite problem, atonic urinary retention). Pilocarpine, a direct-acting muscarinic agonist, genuinely lowers intraocular pressure in glaucoma and is used topically as a hair tonic, and, as a pure agonist, is the one drug among common atropine-adjacent teaching examples that lacks any muscarinic-blocking activity.

## sections
### Definition
Beyond a drug's core mechanism, exam-style "indication matching" questions test whether a specific clinical use is genuinely associated with a drug or is a plausible-sounding mismatch.

### Mechanism
Neostigmine's real indications (postoperative GI/bladder motility, neuromuscular blockade reversal, myasthenia gravis) do not include hair tonic (pilocarpine's minor topical use) or incontinence (neostigmine increases detrusor tone, treating retention, the opposite bladder problem). Pilocarpine's ciliary-muscle contraction opens the trabecular meshwork, increasing aqueous outflow and lowering intraocular pressure, and its local cholinergic stimulation is separately used as a scalp hair tonic; because it is a pure agonist with no blocking activity, it is the drug that lacks muscarinic-blocking activity among a set that otherwise includes chlorpromazine, scopolamine, pancuronium and meperidine, each of which carries some genuine antimuscarinic or atropine-like activity.

### Key determinants
Whether a drug increases or decreases detrusor tone determines whether it treats incontinence or retention; whether a drug is an agonist or an antagonist at the muscarinic receptor determines whether it can be described as having "blocking activity" at all.

### Clinical significance
Confusing neostigmine's bladder-emptying (retention) indication with incontinence, or missing that pilocarpine is a pure agonist, leads directly to a wrong drug choice.

## published_summary

## published_sections

## hold_these
Neostigmine is not used as a hair tonic (pilocarpine is); neostigmine is not used for urinary incontinence (it treats atonic urinary retention instead).
Pilocarpine lowers IOP in glaucoma and is used as a hair tonic.
Pilocarpine is a pure muscarinic agonist, lacking any muscarinic-blocking activity, unlike chlorpromazine, scopolamine, pancuronium or meperidine.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming any drug that raises bladder tone treats every bladder-emptying problem, including incontinence.
Assuming pilocarpine, grouped with atropine-adjacent examples, has some blocking activity of its own.
