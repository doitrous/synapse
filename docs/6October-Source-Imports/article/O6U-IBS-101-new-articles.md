## summary
Four foundational cytology and histology facts a first-year body-structure exam tests directly: which germ layers give rise to epithelium, how a compound gland's branched duct sets it apart from a simple gland, how peripheral membrane proteins differ functionally from integral ones, and how cigarette smoke damages the cilia of the respiratory lining.

## sections
### Definition
Epithelial tissue is not confined to a single embryonic origin: surface and glandular epithelia arise from ectoderm (skin, oral and anal canal linings), endoderm (the gut tube and its glandular derivatives) and mesoderm (endothelium and mesothelium). A compound gland is an exocrine gland whose duct branches before reaching its secretory units, as opposed to a simple gland's single unbranched duct. A peripheral membrane protein sits at one surface of the plasma membrane rather than embedded in the lipid bilayer, unlike an integral protein. Cigarette smoke damages the cilia of the respiratory tract's pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium.

### Mechanism
Each germ layer contributes epithelium by its own developmental route -- ectoderm folding to form skin and the gut's terminal linings, endoderm forming the gut tube proper, mesoderm forming vascular endothelium and body-cavity mesothelium. A gland's duct branches or stays single depending on how many secretory acini or tubules feed into it during development, the pancreatic and major salivary glands being classic branched (compound) examples. A membrane protein's attachment mode -- embedded (integral) versus surface-bound (peripheral) -- determines its accessible role: integral proteins commonly transport substances across the bilayer, peripheral proteins commonly act as surface enzymes or receptors. Cigarette smoke's chemical constituents damage the ciliary apparatus directly, disabling the mucociliary escalator that normally sweeps trapped particles and pathogens out of the airway.

### Key determinants
Whether an epithelium is ectodermal, endodermal or mesodermal is determined by anatomical site, not by a single universal rule -- always check the specific epithelium named, rather than assuming ectoderm because skin is the most familiar example. Simple-versus-compound gland classification is determined specifically by duct branching, a separate axis from the exocrine/endocrine/mixed classification by presence of a duct at all. Integral-versus-peripheral protein classification is determined by whether the protein is embedded in the bilayer, which in turn predicts its typical functional role (transport versus enzyme/receptor). The degree of ciliary damage from cigarette smoke is determined by exposure -- chronic exposure can progress from ciliary loss to epithelial metaplasia, a related but distinct injury.

### Clinical significance
Confusing a germ-layer origin, a gland's duct classification, a membrane protein's structural class, or the specific histological injury from smoking are all classic sources of lost exam marks precisely because each has a same-topic, easily-confused near-neighbour (a different germ layer; the exocrine/endocrine axis; the transport-role protein; epithelial metaplasia). Recognising the specific distinguishing criterion in each case -- site of origin, duct branching, embedding, and mechanism of injury -- is what separates a student who has memorised isolated facts from one who understands the underlying classification logic each fact belongs to.

## summary
Eight anatomical-terminology and regional-anatomy facts a first-year body-structure exam tests directly: the direction pronation and inversion turn the palm and sole, what "caudal" means, why the head alone lacks direct spinal-nerve supply, why the pulmonary veins are the exception among veins, why the vermiform appendix is not part of the small intestine, where protraction/retraction occurs, and how a unipennate muscle is defined.

## sections
### Definition
Pronation carries the palm from anterior to posterior by rotating the radius across the ulna; inversion carries the sole of the foot to face medially at the subtalar joint. Caudal means toward the tail/foot end of the body axis. The head is the one major body region supplied by cranial rather than spinal nerves. The pulmonary veins are the only postnatal veins carrying oxygenated blood. The vermiform appendix is a large-intestine (caecal) structure, not part of the small intestine. Protraction and retraction are temporomandibular-joint movements. A unipennate muscle's fascicles approach its tendon from one side only.

### Mechanism
Pronation and inversion are named from the anatomical-position starting reference: pronation's radial rotation moves the palm 180 degrees from anterior to posterior, and inversion's subtalar tilt moves the sole from its resting downward orientation to face the opposite limb. Caudal, cranial, medial, lateral, proximal and distal together form one coordinate system built on the anatomical position, each axis measured from a different reference (the median plane for medial/lateral, the limb root for proximal/distal, the body's long axis for cranial/caudal). Spinal nerves arise segmentally from the cord and supply the trunk and limbs via the brachial and lumbosacral plexuses; the head instead receives its nerve supply from the twelve cranial nerves arising directly from the brainstem. Every vein carries blood toward the heart regardless of oxygen content, so the pulmonary veins' oxygenated cargo reflects what has already happened upstream in the lungs, not a general property of veins. The appendix arises embryologically and anatomically as an outgrowth of the caecum, the first part of the large intestine, not as a continuation of the ileum. Protraction/retraction and pennate muscle architecture (unipennate/bipennate/multipennate) are both structural classifications read directly off the joint or muscle's geometry.

### Key determinants
The joint involved is what separates pronation/inversion (and protraction/retraction) from one another -- forearm, subtalar joint and temporomandibular joint respectively -- so naming the wrong joint is the commonest error, not misunderstanding the movement itself. A directional term is determined by which axis and which reference point it is measured against, not by treating it as a named region. The head/spinal-nerve distinction is determined by embryological origin of the nerve supply (cranial nerves from the brainstem versus spinal nerves from the cord), with the genuine exception that upper cervical spinal nerves reach a strip of posterior scalp skin. Whether a vein carries oxygenated or deoxygenated blood is determined by its position in the circulation (pulmonary versus systemic), not by any inherent property of "vein" as a vessel type. Pennate-muscle classification is determined by how many sides/directions the fascicles approach the tendon from.

### Clinical significance
These are the terms and classifications a clinician uses to describe every subsequent anatomical structure precisely -- an examiner who cannot correctly apply pronation/inversion, the directional terms, or the vein/nerve exceptions cannot describe a clinical finding's location or a surgical approach unambiguously. The appendix's true attachment to the caecum, not the ileum, is directly relevant to appendicitis's presentation and the surgical anatomy of appendicectomy. The mandible's protraction/retraction and jaw-muscle architecture are directly examined in temporomandibular joint assessment.

## hold_these
Pronation carries the palm from anterior to posterior; inversion carries the sole to face medially -- different joints, do not conflate them.
Caudal means toward the tail/foot end of the body axis, a direction, not a fixed region.
The head is supplied by cranial nerves, not spinal nerves, as the printed-exam rule (with a genuine cervical-spinal-nerve nuance for posterior scalp skin).
The pulmonary veins are the one postnatal exception to "veins carry deoxygenated blood".
The vermiform appendix is a large-intestine (caecal) structure, not part of the small intestine.
Protraction/retraction is a temporomandibular-joint movement pair.
A unipennate muscle's fascicles approach the tendon from one side only.

## lose_the_mark
Describing pronation or inversion without stating the anatomical-position starting reference.
Treating "caudal" as a body region rather than a direction along the body axis.
Stating the head has no spinal-nerve supply at all without noting the upper-cervical posterior-scalp exception.
Forgetting the fetal umbilical vein is a second (prenatal) exception to "veins carry deoxygenated blood".
Assuming any structure attached to the intestine is automatically "part of the small intestine".
Picking a limb joint instead of the mandible for protraction/retraction.
Confusing unipennate with bipennate/multipennate, which involve fibres from more than one side/direction.
