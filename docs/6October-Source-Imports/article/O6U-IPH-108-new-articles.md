## summary
The parenteral and topical/mucosal routes each trade off speed, control and hazard
differently. IV gives complete, instant bioavailability but risks extravasation necrosis,
thrombophlebitis and air embolism. IM suits oily depot preparations at the cost of more
pain than SC. SC gives steady absorption from a small volume but cannot take irritants or
large volumes. Transdermal gives slow sustained delivery, unsuitable for an acute attack.
Nasal delivery works for small peptides (calcitonin, vasopressin) but not large molecules
like heparin, and a decongestant must alternate nostrils.

## sections
### Definition
Parenteral routes bypass the gastrointestinal tract and deliver a drug by injection (IV,
IM, SC) or across a barrier (transdermal, nasal). Each route's absorption speed, achievable
volume, and hazard profile follow from the anatomy and physiology of the tissue it uses.

### Mechanism
IV delivery enters the bloodstream directly, so its hazards (extravasation, phlebitis, air
embolism) are technique/anatomy hazards of the infusion itself, not the drug. IM's richer
muscular blood supply gives faster absorption than SC but more injection pain; its larger
tissue bed tolerates oily depot suspensions that release slowly. SC's poorer blood supply
gives slow, steady absorption but cannot dilute an irritant drug or accommodate a large
volume the way IM or IV can. Transdermal absorption across intact skin is inherently slow,
which rules it out whenever fast relief of an acute attack is needed (nitroglycerin patch
vs sublingual tablet). Nasal mucosa absorbs small peptides/proteins (calcitonin,
vasopressin, GnRH analogues) directly into circulation but cannot pass a large, charged
molecule like heparin; a topical decongestant is applied one nostril at a time to avoid
risking total bilateral obstruction.

### Key determinants
Route choice is driven by required onset speed, tolerated volume, irritancy of the drug,
and whether sustained or immediate action is needed.

### Clinical significance
Choosing the wrong route for a given clinical need -- e.g. transdermal nitroglycerin for an
acute angina attack, or SC injection of an irritant drug -- either fails to relieve the
patient in time or causes preventable local tissue injury.

## published_summary

## published_sections

## hold_these
IV: complete bioavailability, hazards are extravasation necrosis, thrombophlebitis, air embolism.
IM: more painful than SC but suited to oily depot preparations and larger volumes.
SC: steady absorption from a small volume; cannot take irritants or large volumes.
Transdermal: slow sustained delivery, unsuitable for an acute attack needing fast relief.
Nasal: works for small peptides (calcitonin, vasopressin); heparin cannot cross nasal mucosa; decongestants go one nostril at a time.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming SC and IM tolerate the same drugs/volumes.
Assuming any peptide can be given intranasally regardless of size/charge.
Assuming a transdermal patch can substitute for a sublingual tablet in an acute attack.

## summary
Dosage-form names encode both physical form and intended use: a liniment (alcoholic,
rubbed in) differs from a lotion (aqueous, not rubbed); a gel best covers a large skin
area; a spinhaler delivers dry powder, not liquid aerosol; an ampoule is single-dose; a
spansule is sustained-release; a sublingual tablet is friable for rapid disintegration.
Inhalation devices split into pressurized metered dose inhalers (MDI), which still contain
propellant/excipients alongside the drug, and dry powder inhalers (turbohaler, spinhaler,
discus, rotahaler), activated by the patient's own breath rather than a propellant.

## sections
### Definition
A dosage form's name specifies both its physical composition and its route/technique of
use; confusing similarly-sounding forms (liniment/lotion, spansule/sugar-coated tablet) is
a common source of error.

### Mechanism
Liniment's alcohol base and rubbing-in application both counter-irritate and aid skin
penetration; lotion's aqueous base is applied without rubbing. Gel's spreadable,
non-greasy texture suits large-area application better than cream or ointment. An MDI uses
a pressurized propellant to aerosolise a metered dose per actuation, so its canister always
contains more than the active drug alone; a dry powder inhaler instead relies on the
patient's inspiratory effort to disperse a dry powder, with no propellant.

### Key determinants
Physical form (liquid/solid/powder), base (aqueous/alcoholic/oily), and activation
mechanism (propellant versus inspiratory effort) are the deciding features distinguishing
these dosage forms and devices.

### Clinical significance
Selecting the wrong dosage form (e.g. a lotion instead of a liniment when rubbing-in is
needed, or expecting an MDI to be propellant-free) leads to reduced efficacy or a
device/technique mismatch with the patient's needs.

## published_summary

## published_sections

## hold_these
Liniment is alcoholic and rubbed in; lotion is aqueous and not rubbed.
Gel best covers a very large area of skin.
A sublingual tablet is friable, for rapid buccal disintegration.
MDI is pressurized, portable, NOT a dry powder inhaler, and contains more than the active drug alone.

## lose_the_mark
Treating liniment and lotion as interchangeable liquid topical forms.
Assuming an MDI is a dry powder inhaler or drug-only.

## summary
Natural-product drug classes are grouped and named by structure: alkaloids are basic,
nitrogenous, "-ine"-named, plant-derived and water-insoluble as free bases (formulated as
water-soluble salts such as atropine sulfate); glycosides (digoxin, ouabain, salicin,
rutin) attach a sugar to a non-sugar aglycone; resins (cholestyramine) and volatile oils
(spasmolytic/expectorant/carminative/counter-irritant, not laxative) are separate classes;
astringents act by combined vasoconstriction and surface protein denaturation. Not every
drug taught alongside these classes is itself plant-derived (adrenaline is not), and
alkaloid toxicity (e.g. aconitine, fatal without any anatomical lesion) is classically
treated with tannin-rich heavy tea, which precipitates the alkaloid.

## sections
### Definition
Pharmacognosy classifies natural-product drugs by chemical structure -- alkaloid,
glycoside, resin, volatile oil, tannin -- each with characteristic properties and clinical
uses distinct from the others.

### Mechanism
Alkaloids' free-base water-insolubility is why they are dispensed as salts (atropine
sulfate). Tannins precipitate alkaloids, the basis of heavy tea as a simple antidote for
alkaloid toxicity (not for iron/heavy-metal toxicity or colic). A glycoside's sugar-plus-
aglycone structure defines the class regardless of the aglycone's own pharmacological
effect; insulin, a peptide hormone, has no such structure and is not a glycoside. An
astringent's dual vasoconstrictor/protein-denaturing action is what shrinks and toughens
exposed tissue and controls minor bleeding/oozing.

### Key determinants
Chemical structure (nitrogenous base, sugar-aglycone bond, resinous or oily character)
determines class membership; biological origin (plant vs synthetic/endogenous) is a
separate axis, illustrated by adrenaline's exclusion from "plant origin" despite sharing a
teaching set with true plant alkaloids.

### Clinical significance
Recognising these classes correctly guides both formulation choices (salt forms for
water-solubility) and toxicity management (tannin/heavy tea for alkaloid poisoning
specifically).

## published_summary

## published_sections

## hold_these
Alkaloids: basic, nitrogenous, "-ine"-named, plant-derived, water-insoluble as free base; heavy tea (tannin) treats alkaloid toxicity; aconitine overdose can be fatal with no anatomical lesion.
Glycosides: digoxin, ouabain, salicin, rutin -- not insulin.
Cholestyramine is a resin.
Volatile oils: spasmolytic/expectorant/carminative/counter-irritant, not laxative.
Adrenaline is not plant-derived.
Astringent = vasoconstrictor + protein-denaturing action.

## lose_the_mark
Treating dopamine as an alkaloid.
Assuming any drug in this teaching set is plant-derived by default.
Confusing astringent action with emollient (soothing) action.

## summary
Four practical pharmacy topics: narcotic (controlled-drug) prescriptions require special
safeguards, including the dose written out in words, no refills, and a special
prescription form; a multidose eye drop bottle is discarded 4 weeks after opening
regardless of its printed expiry date; atropine sulfate, the clinically used salt of the
alkaloid atropine, is water-soluble unlike the free base; and a drug's dosage form does not
by itself determine local versus systemic action -- a sublingual nitroglycerin tablet acts
systemically despite its oral-looking form, unlike a topical lotion, paste, lozenge or gel.

## sections
### Definition
Pharmacy practice topics cover the regulatory, dispensing and formulation rules that
govern how a drug reaches and is used safely by a patient, distinct from its
pharmacological mechanism.

### Mechanism
Narcotic prescription safeguards (words not numerals, no refill, dedicated form) exist
specifically to prevent forgery and diversion of controlled substances. The 4-week discard
rule for opened eye drops exists because opening breaks the sterile seal and exposes the
solution to repeated contamination that the unopened-bottle expiry date does not account
for. Salt formation (atropine to atropine sulfate) converts a water-insoluble free base
into a soluble, clinically formulable compound. Whether a preparation acts locally or
systemically depends on where and how it is absorbed, not its tablet/lotion/gel form as
such -- sublingual placement of a nitroglycerin tablet is what makes it systemic.

### Key determinants
Regulatory category (controlled vs ordinary drug), time since a container was opened, salt
form versus free base, and site/route of absorption each independently determine the
practical rule that applies.

### Clinical significance
Getting any of these four rules wrong -- refilling a narcotic prescription, keeping an
opened eye drop bottle past 4 weeks, assuming a free-base alkaloid is water-soluble, or
assuming a tablet always acts locally -- creates a real safety or diversion risk in
practice.

## published_summary

## published_sections

## hold_these
Narcotic prescriptions: dose written in words, no refills, dedicated form, not OTC-only, not sedative/hypnotic-only.
Opened multidose eye drops: discard after 4 weeks regardless of printed expiry.
Atropine sulfate is water-soluble; the free-base alkaloid is not.
Nitroglycerin (sublingual) tablets act systemically, unlike local preparations such as boric acid lotion, toothpaste, lozenges or oral gel.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming a narcotic prescription can be refilled like an ordinary one.
Assuming an eye drop bottle is safe until its printed expiry date regardless of when it was opened.
Assuming "tablet" always means local, or "lotion/gel" always means local, without checking the drug's actual absorption route.
