## summary
Two acid-base facts a first-year body-function exam tests directly: the normal arterial blood pH range, and why severe muscular exercise pushes blood pH toward a metabolic, not respiratory, acidosis.

## sections
### Definition
Human arterial blood pH is tightly regulated within 7.35-7.45. Severe or prolonged muscular exercise produces a metabolic (not respiratory) acidosis by generating lactic acid faster than it can be buffered or cleared.

### Mechanism
The bicarbonate buffer system, together with respiratory (CO2 excretion rate) and renal (bicarbonate reabsorption/acid excretion) compensation, holds blood pH within its narrow normal range against everyday acid and base loads. Severe exercise outpaces oxidative metabolism's oxygen supply, so exercising muscle shifts to anaerobic glycolysis and produces lactic acid faster than buffering and clearance can keep up; the resulting pH fall is driven by this fixed (non-volatile) acid load, which is what makes it a metabolic rather than a respiratory acidosis. The raised ventilation seen during exercise is the respiratory system's compensatory response to that acid load (blowing off CO2 to help restore pH), not the cause of the acidosis.

### Key determinants
A blood pH value is classed as acidaemia below 7.35 and alkalaemia above 7.45, against the fixed normal range. Whether an acidosis is metabolic or respiratory is determined by its primary cause -- a fixed/non-volatile acid load (metabolic) versus CO2 retention from inadequate ventilation (respiratory) -- not by whether breathing rate happens to be elevated, since a raised respiratory rate can equally be the compensation for a metabolic problem.

### Clinical significance
The normal arterial pH range (7.35-7.45) is the reference every acid-base disturbance in clinical medicine is read against, and it is a distinct fact from the general-chemistry definition of "basic" (any pH above 7) that a first-year student is also expected to know -- conflating the two is a common and costly exam error. Correctly attributing exercise-induced acidosis to its metabolic (lactic-acid) mechanism, rather than to the visibly raised breathing rate, is the same reasoning skill needed later to distinguish primary respiratory acidosis from a metabolic acidosis with respiratory compensation in a sick patient.

## summary
Three autonomic-pharmacology facts a first-year body-function exam tests directly: what atropine treats and how, what prostigmine does as an anticholinesterase, and why a sympathomimetic -- not a parasympathomimetic -- relieves bronchial asthma.

## sections
### Definition
Atropine is a muscarinic (antimuscarinic) receptor antagonist used to relax spasmodic visceral smooth-muscle contractions. Prostigmine (neostigmine) is an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor that increases gastric secretion, among other parasympathomimetic effects. A sympathomimetic (beta-2 adrenergic agonist), not a parasympathomimetic, is the drug class used to relieve bronchial asthma.

### Mechanism
Atropine blocks parasympathetic muscarinic tone on visceral smooth muscle, relaxing spasmodic contractions of the gut and biliary/renal tract, and by the same mechanism blocks vagal muscarinic tone on the SA node (raising heart rate) and on the iris sphincter (dilating the pupil). Prostigmine inhibits acetylcholinesterase, so acetylcholine persists longer at cholinergic synapses and muscarinic receptors; the resulting enhanced parasympathetic tone increases gastric acid and enzyme secretion, contracts the bladder detrusor and constricts the pupil -- the mirror image of atropine's effects. Bronchial asthma narrows the airway by bronchial smooth-muscle contraction; sympathomimetics relax that smooth muscle via beta-2 adrenergic receptors, producing bronchodilation, while a parasympathomimetic would instead worsen the bronchoconstriction.

### Key determinants
Whether a drug is antimuscarinic (atropine) or anticholinesterase (prostigmine) determines the direction of its effect on every muscarinic target organ at once (heart rate, pupil, bladder, gut, glands) -- knowing the drug class lets a student predict the whole effect profile rather than memorising organs one at a time. The choice of sympathomimetic over parasympathomimetic for asthma is determined by which autonomic branch relaxes versus contracts bronchial smooth muscle, not by a general "calming" intuition about the parasympathetic system.

### Clinical significance
Atropine's heart-rate-raising and pupil-dilating effects are why it is used for bradycardia and pre-anaesthetic pupil dilation as well as for smooth-muscle spasm, and why an exam question testing "atropine's use" often carries a distractor describing the opposite (rate-lowering) effect. Prostigmine's cholinergic effects are exploited therapeutically in myasthenia gravis and post-operative ileus/urinary retention. Correctly identifying the sympathomimetic (not parasympathomimetic) class for asthma is the same reasoning a clinician uses at the bedside when choosing a bronchodilator.

Picking a parasympathomimetic for asthma by pattern-matching "narrowing of the bronchi" to a generic muscarinic answer.
