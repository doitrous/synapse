## summary
A nucleoside is a base joined to a sugar; adding phosphate makes it a nucleotide, and removing
phosphate reverses that. The sugar-phosphate backbone (linked by phosphodiester bonds) is
distinct from the bases it carries, which pair across the double helix by hydrogen bonds in a
strictly complementary, antiparallel fashion that follows Chargaff's rule (%A=%T, %G=%C) --
a rule that does not apply to single-stranded RNA. Melting temperature is the point at which
half of that helical pairing is lost.

## sections
### Definition
A nucleoside is a purine or pyrimidine base joined to a pentose sugar; adding one or more
phosphate groups converts it into a nucleotide, and removing the phosphate converts a
nucleotide back into a nucleoside. Chains of nucleotides are linked into the nucleic acid
backbone by phosphodiester bonds between the 3' carbon of one sugar and the 5' phosphate of
the next, with the bases projecting outward rather than forming part of the backbone. Across
the DNA double helix, complementary bases pair by hydrogen bonds (A-T, G-C), and the two
antiparallel strands are fully complementary -- one strand's sequence determines the other's.

### Mechanism
Because A pairs only with T and G pairs only with C, double-stranded DNA obeys Chargaff's
rule: %A=%T and %G=%C, so knowing one base's percentage fixes its pairing partner's
percentage. RNA, being typically single stranded, has no obligate partner strand enforcing
this equality, so its base percentages need not satisfy Chargaff's rule. Melting temperature
(Tm) is the temperature at which half of the helical base pairing has denatured into single
strands -- a physical unwinding, not a chemical hydrolysis of the molecule into its nucleotide
building blocks.

### Key determinants
Sort "sugar+base" (nucleoside) from "sugar+base+phosphate" (nucleotide) by the presence or
absence of phosphate. Sort "backbone" (sugar-phosphate, joined by phosphodiester bonds) from
"base pairing" (hydrogen bonds between strands) as two entirely different bond types serving
two different structural roles. Apply Chargaff's rule (%A=%T, %G=%C) only to double-stranded
DNA, never to single-stranded RNA.

### Clinical significance
Confusing DNA "melting" (a reversible physical denaturation used diagnostically and in PCR) with
irreversible chemical hydrolysis is a common exam trap. GC-rich DNA regions, which form three
hydrogen bonds per base pair instead of two, resist melting at a higher temperature -- a
principle exploited directly in PCR primer design and in interpreting a sample's melting curve.

## summary
cAMP is degraded by phosphodiesterase (not "phosphomonoesterase"), and Coenzyme A's specific
job is carrying acyl groups, not hydrogen. mRNA carries the genetic code to the ribosome,
where a codon on mRNA pairs with the matching anticodon on a tRNA that is charged with only
its own one specific amino acid -- every tRNA sharing a conserved 3'-terminal CCA sequence and
a cloverleaf fold of about three loops, not five. AMP is a purine nucleotide; UTP is the
pyrimidine one that acts as a high-energy intermediate. Histone H1 is a linker histone outside
the core nucleosome, and histones generally are basic proteins rich in lysine and arginine.

## sections
### Definition
cAMP, formed from ATP by adenylyl cyclase, is degraded to 5'-AMP by cAMP phosphodiesterase (a
name reflecting the phosphodiester bond within its cyclic structure, not "phosphomonoesterase").
Coenzyme A carries acyl groups via a high-energy thioester bond at its terminal thiol, distinct
from the hydrogen-carrier role of NAD+/FAD/NADP+. Messenger RNA (mRNA) carries the genetic code
from nuclear DNA to the ribosome; there, a three-base codon on the mRNA pairs with the
complementary anticodon on a transfer RNA (tRNA) that has been charged, by its own specific
aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase, with only the one amino acid matching that anticodon.

### Mechanism
Mature tRNA folds into a cloverleaf with an acceptor stem and roughly three main loops (D-loop,
anticodon loop, TψC loop), and every tRNA -- whatever its internal sequence -- ends in a
conserved, unpaired CCA sequence at its 3' terminus, the site of amino acid attachment. Among
the nucleotides, AMP (containing adenine) is a purine nucleotide, while UMP, CMP and TMP
(uracil, cytosine, thymine) are pyrimidine nucleotides; UTP is the pyrimidine nucleotide that
serves as a high-energy intermediate, most notably forming UDP-glucose for glycogen synthesis.
In chromatin, the core nucleosome octamer is built from H2A, H2B, H3 and H4; histone H1 is a
separate linker histone outside that core, and histones generally are basic, lysine/arginine-
rich proteins that bind DNA's negatively charged phosphate backbone electrostatically.

### Key determinants
Sort SAM (methyl), PAPS (sulphate), NAD+/FAD/NADP+ (hydrogen) and CoA (acyl) by what each one
specifically carries. Codon = mRNA, anticodon = tRNA -- never the reverse. A tRNA carries one
amino acid, not any of the 20. AMP is the purine among {AMP, UMP, CMP, TMP}; UTP is the
pyrimidine high-energy intermediate among {ATP, UTP, UDP-Glc, CMP}. H1 sits outside the core
nucleosome octamer.

### Clinical significance
cAMP-phosphodiesterase inhibitors (e.g. in some bronchodilator and cardiac drug classes) work
by preserving cAMP signalling; recognising phosphodiesterase (not "phosphomonoesterase") as
the correct degrading enzyme avoids a common naming trap. Histone-DNA electrostatic binding via
lysine/arginine residues underlies chromatin compaction, relevant to understanding how
histone-modifying drugs and epigenetic mechanisms alter gene accessibility.
