## summary
Several adenine-nucleotide derivatives act as activated group-transfer coenzymes: SAM carries
a methyl group, PAPS carries an activated sulphate group, and NAD+/FAD/NADP+ carry hydrogen
-- but coenzyme A, despite the family resemblance, carries acyl groups instead.

## sections
### Definition
S-adenosylmethionine (SAM, "active methionine") is the cell's methyl donor, formed from
methionine and ATP and used by methyltransferases across DNA, RNA, protein and small-molecule
substrates. 3'-Phosphoadenosine-5'-phosphosulphate (PAPS) is the parallel activated-sulphate
carrier, used by sulfotransferases in phase II conjugation and glycosaminoglycan sulphation.
NAD+, FAD and NADP+ are the classic hydrogen-carrier coenzymes; coenzyme A is not one of them
-- its job is carrying acyl groups on its terminal thiol.

### Mechanism
Each of these coenzymes is built around an adenine nucleotide but is chemically specialised
for one kind of group transfer: SAM's reactive sulfonium centre makes its methyl group easy to
transfer; PAPS's high-energy sulphate-phosphate bond drives sulphate transfer; NAD(P)/FAD's
redox-active ring systems accept and donate hydride/hydrogen equivalents; CoA's thiol forms a
high-energy thioester with an acyl group.

### Key determinants
The fastest way to sort these coenzymes on an exam is by what they carry: methyl (SAM),
sulphate (PAPS), hydrogen (NAD+, FAD, NADP+), or acyl (CoA) -- memorising this one-word
mapping avoids the common trap of grouping CoA with the other adenine-nucleotide coenzymes as
if it too carried hydrogen.

### Clinical significance
SAM-dependent methylation (DNA methylation, catecholamine synthesis) and PAPS-dependent
sulphation (drug and steroid conjugation) are both central to normal metabolism and
xenobiotic clearance; a NAD+/FAD/NADP+ hydrogen-carrier deficiency (e.g. in niacin
deficiency, pellagra) impairs the electron transport chain and multiple dehydrogenase
reactions system-wide.

## summary
DNA and RNA differ in their pyrimidine base (thymine vs uracil) and so in their nucleotide
inventory (dUMP is not a DNA nucleotide); tRNA's D-arm carries the unusual base
dihydrouracil; GC-rich DNA melts at a higher temperature than AT-rich DNA; and gout is a
crystal arthropathy driven by increased plasma uric acid, the end product of human purine
catabolism.

## sections
### Definition
DNA's four deoxyribonucleotides are dAMP, dGMP, dCMP and dTMP -- dUMP is excluded because DNA
uses thymine, not uracil, as its pyrimidine (though dUMP is a transient intermediate en route
to dTMP via thymidylate synthase). RNA, conversely, uses uracil rather than thymine. Mature
tRNA's cloverleaf structure carries several unusual, post-transcriptionally modified bases,
including dihydrouracil concentrated in the D-arm. Uric acid is the terminal product of
purine base catabolism in humans (who lack uricase), and its accumulation above the plasma
solubility limit defines the hyperuricemia of gout.

### Mechanism
Thymidylate synthase methylates dUMP to dTMP using a folate-derived one-carbon donor,
explaining why dUMP is a real cellular intermediate even though it never appears in finished
DNA. tRNA's D-arm dihydrouracil arises from post-transcriptional reduction of a uracil ring
double bond. Purine catabolism proceeds through hypoxanthine and xanthine to uric acid via
xanthine oxidase, the enzyme targeted therapeutically (allopurinol) to lower uric acid in
gout.

### Key determinants
"Does this nucleotide contain thymine or uracil" sorts DNA from RNA nucleotides in one step.
Recognising dihydrouracil specifically as the D-arm's hallmark base (as opposed to the T-psi-C
arm's thymine-pseudouridine-cytosine) avoids mixing up tRNA's two most commonly tested arms.
Uric acid, not urea or creatinine, is the specific marker raised in gout.

### Clinical significance
Confusing dUMP's transient catabolic role with a place in finished DNA, or confusing gout's
marker (uric acid) with unrelated nitrogen-waste products (urea, creatinine), are both common
exam traps; xanthine oxidase inhibitors (allopurinol) exploit the purine-to-uric-acid pathway
directly to treat gout. Higher GC content raising a DNA region's melting temperature underlies
PCR primer-design rules of thumb and helps explain why GC-rich genomic regions can be harder
to sequence or amplify.

## summary
Not all DNA is the standard right-handed B-form double helix: Z-DNA is a left-handed variant
favoured by alternating purine-pyrimidine sequences, and mitochondrial DNA is a distinct,
double-stranded circular molecule structurally more like bacterial DNA than the linear
nuclear chromosomes.

## sections
### Definition
B-DNA is the standard, physiological right-handed double helix. A-DNA is a shorter, wider
right-handed form seen under dehydration and in RNA-DNA hybrids. Z-DNA is the exception to
the right-handed rule -- a left-handed helix favoured by alternating GC-rich sequences, with
a proposed role in gene regulation. Mitochondrial DNA is a separate, small, double-stranded
circular molecule (not linear like nuclear chromosomes), maternally inherited and making up
roughly 0.3-1% of total cellular DNA.

### Mechanism
Z-DNA's left-handed zigzag backbone arises from alternating syn/anti glycosidic bond
conformations along a purine-pyrimidine repeat, a structural transition that can be induced
by negative supercoiling or specific sequence context. Mitochondrial DNA's circularity and
double-strandedness reflect its evolutionary origin from an endosymbiotic bacterium, and its
maternal-only inheritance follows from sperm mitochondria being eliminated after
fertilisation.

### Key determinants
"Left-handed" is the single fastest identifier of Z-DNA among the helical forms. For
mitochondrial DNA, the pairing to remember is "double-stranded AND circular" -- not single-
stranded, and not linear.

### Clinical significance
Mitochondrial DNA mutations, inherited exclusively through the maternal line, are associated
with myopathies and neurological disorders reflecting the high energy demand of muscle and
neural tissue -- a distinct inheritance pattern from nuclear (Mendelian) genetic disease.

## published_sections

## summary
DNA replication is semiconservative, relieved of supercoiling by topoisomerases, and
completed on the lagging strand through Okazaki fragments joined by DNA ligase -- while
chromosome ends (telomeres) need the eukaryote-specific enzyme telomerase, absent from
circular prokaryotic chromosomes.

## sections
### Definition
Replication is semiconservative: each daughter duplex retains one original parental strand
paired with one newly synthesised strand. Topoisomerases relieve the positive supercoiling
generated ahead of the fork by transiently cutting and resealing DNA strands. The lagging
strand is synthesised discontinuously as Okazaki fragments, each starting with its own RNA
primer; DNA ligase seals the nicks left once those primers are removed and replaced with DNA.
The telomere is the repetitive DNA sequence capping linear eukaryotic chromosome ends;
telomerase, a eukaryote-only reverse transcriptase carrying its own RNA template, extends
telomeres to solve the end-replication problem -- prokaryotes, having circular chromosomes
with no ends, use neither telomeres nor telomerase.

### Mechanism
Helicase unwinds the parental duplex at the fork; topoisomerase relieves the resulting
supercoiling further ahead; primase lays an RNA primer on each new strand start; DNA
polymerase III (prokaryotes) or the replicative polymerases (eukaryotes) extend from each
primer 5' to 3', continuously on the leading strand and in short Okazaki-fragment bursts on
the lagging strand; DNA polymerase I removes the RNA primers and fills the gaps with DNA; DNA
ligase seals the final nick. Telomerase uses its internal RNA template as a guide to add new
telomeric repeats to the 3' overhang, giving the lagging-strand machinery room to prime and
complete the very end of the chromosome.

### Key determinants
"One parental strand per daughter duplex" is the semiconservative model in one phrase.
Distinguishing topoisomerase (relieves supercoiling by cutting/resealing) from helicase
(unwinds the duplex) avoids a very common mix-up. Telomerase's three defining features --
eukaryote-only, own RNA template, reverse-transcriptase activity -- do NOT include "elongates
the leading strand"; it addresses the lagging strand's end-replication problem specifically.

### Clinical significance
Progressive telomere shortening with each replication cycle (absent active telomerase) limits
normal somatic cell division (replicative senescence); many cancers reactivate telomerase to
escape this limit, making telomerase a subject of oncology research interest.

## published_sections

## summary
Transcription builds mRNA complementary to the DNA template strand, starting at a promoter,
ending (in prokaryotes) at a rho-dependent or rho-independent signal, carried out in
eukaryotes by RNA polymerase II for mRNA specifically, and followed by spliceosome-mediated
removal of introns; actinomycin D inhibits the whole process by intercalating into DNA.

## sections
### Definition
mRNA is synthesised complementary to one strand of DNA (the template strand), 5' to 3',
matching the non-template (coding) strand's sequence with uracil for thymine. A promoter is
the specific DNA sequence to which RNA polymerase binds to begin transcription. In
prokaryotes, rho-dependent termination relies on the rho factor tracking the nascent
transcript to a paused polymerase and unwinding the RNA-DNA hybrid. In eukaryotes, RNA
polymerase II specifically synthesises mRNA (I makes the large rRNAs, III makes tRNA and
other small RNAs). Splicing removes introns and joins exons, carried out by the spliceosome, a
five-snRNP ribonucleoprotein complex. Actinomycin D intercalates into GC-rich DNA and blocks
RNA polymerase movement, inhibiting transcription.

### Mechanism
General transcription factors assemble at the promoter to position RNA polymerase correctly
before elongation begins. Rho factor, an ATP-dependent helicase-like protein, physically
displaces RNA polymerase from rho-dependent terminators. The spliceosome recognises intron-
exon boundary sequences and catalyses two sequential transesterification reactions to excise
each intron and ligate the flanking exons.

### Key determinants
"Complementary to the template, sequence-matching the coding strand" resolves most mRNA-DNA
relationship questions. Remembering "I = rRNA, II = mRNA, III = tRNA/small RNA" sorts the
three eukaryotic RNA polymerases instantly. "Introns out, exons joined" is the one-line
definition of splicing.

### Clinical significance
Actinomycin D's transcription-blocking mechanism underlies both its experimental use
(distinguishing transcription-dependent from -independent processes) and its clinical use as
an antineoplastic agent; alpha-amanitin's selective inhibition of RNA polymerase II underlies
death-cap mushroom poisoning's severe hepatotoxicity.

Translation and point mutation: codons, tRNA, and the missense/silent distinction

## summary
Translation happens in the cytoplasm, begins at an AUG codon and ends at one of three stop
codons, with tRNA delivering amino acids matched to each mRNA codon; a point mutation's
biological consequence -- silent, missense (as in sickle cell disease), nonsense or frame-
shift -- depends on how it changes the resulting codon.

## sections
### Definition
Translation, unlike transcription/splicing/replication, does not occur in the eukaryotic
nucleus -- it requires cytoplasmic ribosomes. AUG is both the codon for methionine and the
chain-initiating codon; UAA, UAG and UGA are the three chain-terminating codons. tRNA
transfers amino acids from the cytosol to the ribosome, matched to mRNA codons via its
anticodon. A codon is the three-base mRNA sequence specifying one amino acid or a stop signal.
Sickle cell anaemia arises from a missense mutation (glutamic acid to valine at beta-globin
codon 6); a silent mutation, by contrast, leaves the encoded protein's amino acid sequence
unchanged because of the genetic code's degeneracy.

### Mechanism
Ribosomes assemble at an AUG start codon (recognised via the ribosome binding site/Kozak
context) and translate in that reading frame until a stop codon is reached, at which point
release factors (not a tRNA) terminate the chain. Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases charge each tRNA
with its matching amino acid before delivery to the ribosome. A point mutation's effect
depends on whether the new codon: still specifies the same amino acid (silent, due to code
degeneracy), specifies a different amino acid (missense, as in sickle cell disease),
specifies a premature stop (nonsense), or shifts the entire downstream reading frame
(insertion/deletion of 1-2 bases).

### Key determinants
"No ribosomes in the nucleus" is the fastest way to place translation outside the nuclear
steps of gene expression. AUG starts, UAA/UAG/UGA stop -- memorising all three stop codons
(not just one) avoids undercounting. Sorting a mutation as silent/missense/nonsense/frame-
shift hinges on exactly what happens to the resulting codon and reading frame.

### Clinical significance
Sickle cell disease is the classic missense-mutation exemplar, its single amino-acid swap
enough to make deoxygenated haemoglobin S polymerise and distort red cells; recognising
"amino acid sequence unchanged" as the hallmark of a silent mutation prevents assuming every
DNA-level change alters the protein.

## published_summary

## published_sections

## hold_these

AUG is the chain-initiating codon; UAA, UAG and UGA are the three chain-terminating codons.
tRNA carries amino acids from the cytosol to the ribosome.
Sickle cell anaemia is caused by a missense mutation.
A silent mutation does not change the encoded protein's amino acid sequence.
A codon is the three-base mRNA sequence specifying one amino acid.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming translation happens in the nucleus because DNA does.
Naming only one stop codon instead of all three.
Calling the sickle-cell mutation "nonsense" instead of "missense".

## summary
Gene expression is regulated at multiple levels beyond transcription initiation itself:
nucleosomes recruit chromatin-modifying enzymes rather than blocking access outright, the
5'UTR carries regulatory sequence between the mRNA cap and the start codon, and microRNAs
silence (not enhance) their target mRNAs.

## sections
### Definition
Nucleosome formation on genomic DNA affects transcription indirectly: nucleosomes recruit
histone- and DNA-modifying enzymes, and it is those enzymes' activity that determines
chromatin accessibility to the transcription machinery. The 5'UTR is the sequence spanning
from the mRNA's 5' 7-methylguanosine cap to the AUG start codon -- transcribed but not
translated, and often carrying elements that tune translation efficiency. MicroRNAs are small
non-coding RNAs that mediate RNA silencing: a single miRNA can bind and regulate several
hundred target mRNAs, decreasing rather than increasing their expression.

### Mechanism
Recruited histone acetyltransferases/deacetylases and DNA methyltransferases open or close
chromatin around a nucleosome depending on which modification is added, making the
nucleosome's net effect on transcription context-dependent rather than a simple on/off block.
miRNAs base-pair (often imperfectly) with complementary sequence in a target mRNA's
untranslated regions, recruiting the RNA-induced silencing complex to repress translation or
promote mRNA degradation.

### Key determinants
Nucleosomes act through the enzymes they recruit, not by blocking access unconditionally.
5'UTR sits before the start codon (as opposed to the 3'UTR, which follows the stop codon).
miRNA regulation is characteristically repressive -- an option describing a miRNA "increasing"
target expression is the wrong direction.

### Clinical significance
Aberrant chromatin remodelling and miRNA dysregulation are both implicated in cancer biology
(oncogenic or tumour-suppressor miRNAs, epigenetic silencing of tumour suppressors via
chromatin modification), making these regulatory layers active areas of therapeutic interest.
