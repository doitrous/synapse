## summary
Dopamine's receptor selectivity is dose-dependent (D1 renal/mesenteric/coronary vasodilation at low dose); isoprenaline's pure beta-agonism supports heart block; norepinephrine's pressor mechanism includes reflex bradycardia, not positive chronotropism. Selective alpha-1 agonists (phenylephrine, midodrine) cause vasoconstriction and mydriasis without bronchodilation or cycloplegia; pseudoephedrine and ritodrine are purpose-built sympathomimetic decongestant/tocolytic agents; epinephrine is never given orally and is unsuitable for acute congestive glaucoma; brimonidine is preferred to epinephrine in chronic simple glaucoma. Clonidine is a centrally-acting alpha-2 agonist, not a peripheral sympathomimetic, used for urgency hypertension and carrying its own withdrawal-hypertension risk that methyldopa and reserpine, despite their own distinct toxicities, do not share.

## sections
### Definition
This article covers the tail of the adrenergic-agonist/sympathomimetic pharmacology taught in the pharmabank/pharmabank2 clusters, plus a new section on adrenergic-neuron-blocking antihypertensives (clonidine, methyldopa, reserpine).

### Mechanism
Catecholamine receptor selectivity (dopaminergic D1, beta-1, beta-2, alpha-1, alpha-2) determines each agonist's dominant clinical effect and, for dopamine specifically, shifts with dose. Alpha-1 agonists act on vascular and iris-dilator smooth muscle (vasoconstriction, mydriasis) but have no beta-2 bronchodilator or antimuscarinic-ciliary action, so they neither bronchodilate nor cause cycloplegia. Clonidine's central alpha-2-autoreceptor action suppresses sympathetic outflow, the opposite pharmacologic role from a peripheral sympathomimetic, and its abrupt withdrawal releases that suppressed outflow, producing rebound hypertension -- a mechanism methyldopa and reserpine, acting differently (a false-neurotransmitter prodrug and a catecholamine-store depleter respectively), do not share.

### Key determinants
Whether a fact concerns dopamine's dose-band, an alpha-1 agonist's selective smooth-muscle action, epinephrine's route/indication limits, or clonidine's central mechanism versus methyldopa's/reserpine's own toxicities determines which specific drug and receptor family the fact applies to.

### Clinical significance
Confusing dopamine's dose-dependent receptor shift, prescribing an alpha-1 agonist expecting bronchodilation, giving epinephrine orally or in acute angle-closure glaucoma, misclassifying clonidine as a peripheral sympathomimetic, or assuming methyldopa/reserpine share clonidine's withdrawal-hypertension risk each carry real clinical consequences.

## hold_these
Low-dose dopamine causes D1-mediated renal/mesenteric/coronary vasodilation; intermediate dose is beta-1 inotropic; high dose is alpha-1 vasoconstrictor.
Isoprenaline (pure beta-agonist) supports heart block; norepinephrine's net heart-rate effect is reflex bradycardia despite direct beta-1 chronotropy.
Selective alpha-1 agonists (midodrine) cause vasoconstriction/mydriasis without bronchodilation/cycloplegia; midodrine is preferred for chronic orthostatic hypotension.
Pseudoephedrine is the most common nasal decongestant; ritodrine is the classic beta-2-selective tocolytic.
Epinephrine is never given orally; it is unsuitable for acute congestive (angle-closure) glaucoma but useful in chronic simple (open-angle) glaucoma; brimonidine (alpha-2) is preferred there over epinephrine.
Clonidine is centrally-acting (alpha-2), NOT a peripheral sympathomimetic; its primary systemic use is urgency hypertension; abrupt cessation causes rebound hypertension.
Methyldopa (hemolytic anemia, hepatitis, SLE-like syndrome, galactorrhoea) and reserpine (peptic ulcer aggravation, depression/suicide, breast changes, pseudotolerance) each carry distinct toxicities but NOT clonidine's withdrawal-hypertension risk.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming any catecholamine is interchangeable regardless of dose or receptor selectivity.
Assuming an alpha-1 agonist can substitute for a beta-2 agonist bronchodilator.
Assuming epinephrine is safe by any route or in any glaucoma subtype.
Grouping clonidine with peripheral sympathomimetics, or assuming methyldopa/reserpine share its withdrawal syndrome.

## summary
Atenolol (hydrophilic, renal, long half-life), metoprolol (lipophilic, hepatic, short half-life) and esmolol (esterase-cleared, ultra-short) illustrate the lipophilic/hydrophilic pharmacokinetic classification, which also determines CNS-effect risk (depression). Membrane-stabilizing activity (propranolol) and intrinsic sympathomimetic activity (contraindicated in angina) are distinct ancillary properties; nebivolol's vasodilation is nitric-oxide-mediated, unlike carvedilol's alpha-1-blockade route. Beta-1 blockade lowers heart rate/contractility/renin (not lipolysis, not hypertension); beta-2 blockade causes hyperkalaemia and bronchoconstriction (propranolol worst) plus decreased portal flow/thyroxine activation/aqueous formation. Beta blockers have declined as first-line uncomplicated-hypertension monotherapy (weaker stroke/MI protection) though their mechanism is mainly renin inhibition; they are drugs of choice for glaucoma, migraine, tremor, variceal-bleed prophylaxis and situational anxiety (not generalized social anxiety disorder) but are contraindicated in a pheochromocytoma-related hypertensive crisis, in vasospastic angina, and in AF with heart failure; only four agents (metoprolol, bisoprolol, carvedilol, nebivolol) have heart-failure mortality evidence, with defined starting prerequisites; timolol treats glaucoma via beta-2 blockade; and beta blockers carry three distinct potentially fatal drug interactions.

## sections
### Definition
This article covers the comprehensive beta-blocker pharmacology tested across pp.184-213 of the source: pharmacokinetic classification, ancillary (non-receptor) properties, beta-1-versus-beta-2 receptor-subtype physiology, and the full span of clinical indications and contraindications.

### Mechanism
A beta blocker's lipophilicity determines blood-brain-barrier penetration, elimination route (hepatic vs renal), half-life, and CNS-effect risk. Ancillary properties (membrane stabilization, intrinsic sympathomimetic activity, nitric-oxide-mediated vasodilation) are independent of, and layered onto, the shared beta-receptor-blockade mechanism, each with its own clinical consequence. Beta-1 blockade acts on the heart and juxtaglomerular apparatus (chronotropy, inotropy, renin); beta-2 blockade acts on bronchial smooth muscle, splanchnic/peripheral vasculature, the thyroid, the eye, and cellular potassium handling -- distinguishing which receptor subtype mediates which action, and in which direction, is the recurring exam theme. Clinical indications and contraindications each trace back to one of these same mechanisms: renin inhibition (hypertension), oxygen-demand reduction (angina, MI), evidence-based mortality reduction in a specific heart-failure agent subset, or unopposed-alpha-action/negative-inotropy risk (pheochromocytoma, vasospastic angina, decompensated heart-failure AF).

### Key determinants
Whether a fact concerns pharmacokinetics (lipophilic/hydrophilic), an ancillary (non-receptor) property, beta-1-versus-beta-2 receptor-subtype physiology, or a specific clinical indication/contraindication determines which drug and which mechanism the fact applies to.

### Clinical significance
Selecting the wrong beta blocker for asthma (nonselective vs cardioselective), for angina (ISA-carrying vs not), for heart failure (untested vs evidence-based agent, or too early vs once stabilised), or for a pheochromocytoma/vasospastic-angina patient (beta blocker before/instead of alpha or calcium-channel blockade) each carries real clinical consequences, as does missing a fatal drug interaction (sympathomimetic, oral hypoglycaemic, verapamil).

## hold_these
Atenolol: hydrophilic, renal elimination, long half-life, no BBB crossing. Metoprolol: lipophilic, hepatic elimination, SHORT half-life, crosses BBB. Esmolol: esterase-cleared, ultra-short, no BBB crossing. Hydrophilic agents cause less CNS depression than lipophilic ones.
Membrane-stabilizing activity = propranolol; intrinsic sympathomimetic (partial-agonist) activity is contraindicated in angina. Nebivolol vasodilates via nitric oxide (not alpha-1 blockade like carvedilol). Propranolol decreases lignocaine metabolism via decreased hepatic blood flow, NOT enzyme inhibition.
Beta-1 blockade actions: negative chronotropy/inotropy, decreased renin/angiotensin II/salt-water retention -- NOT lipolysis, NOT hypertension. Beta-2 blockade: bronchoconstriction (worst with propranolol), decreased portal flow/thyroxine activation/aqueous formation, HYPERKALAEMIA (not hypokalaemia).
Beta blockers declined as uncomplicated-HTN monotherapy (weaker stroke/MI protection); antihypertensive mechanism = renin inhibition. Indicated: glaucoma, migraine ppx, tremor, variceal-bleed ppx, situational anxiety (NOT generalized social anxiety disorder). Contraindicated: pheochromocytoma crisis (alpha blockers preferred), vasospastic angina, AF with heart failure.
Heart-failure agents with mortality evidence: metoprolol, bisoprolol, carvedilol, nebivolol (NOT propranolol); prerequisites: no fluid retention + on ACE inhibitor + no recent IV inotropes.
Angina benefit = decreased myocardial O2 demand (not increased supply). MI benefit = pain relief + limited infarct size + decreased remodelling. Timolol treats glaucoma via beta-2 blockade lowering aqueous formation.
Fatal interactions: nonselective sympathomimetics (unopposed alpha action), oral hypoglycaemics (silent hypoglycaemia), verapamil (additive cardiac depression).

## lose_the_mark
Assuming all beta blockers share the same pharmacokinetic (lipophilic/hydrophilic) profile.
Conflating membrane-stabilizing activity with intrinsic sympathomimetic activity.
Assuming lipolysis is beta-1-mediated, or that beta-2 blockade lowers (rather than raises) potassium.
Assuming any beta blocker is safe in a pheochromocytoma crisis, vasospastic angina, or decompensated-heart-failure AF.
Assuming any beta blocker is interchangeable for heart failure, or starting one before the patient is stabilised.

## summary
Prazosin is indicated for hypertension, BPH and Raynaud's disease, not social anxiety disorder; labetalol's combined alpha/beta action gives it a pregnancy-hypertension indication distinct from the pure-alpha-blocker-class labelling of phenoxybenzamine (pheochromocytoma), phentolamine (intraoperative hypertension) and tamsulosin (BPH). Alpha blockade relaxes bladder-neck, vas deferens, iris-dilator and vascular smooth muscle, but not bronchial smooth muscle. Tamsulosin's alpha-1A selectivity and alpha-2-autoreceptor sparing give it minimal reflex tachycardia; labetalol avoids reflex tachycardia through its added beta-blocking action. Alpha-blockade vasodilation is balanced across both arteries and veins, underlying prazosin's first-dose hypotension; floppy iris syndrome is a distinct, direct iris-alpha-1-blockade effect, not a consequence of that systemic vasodilation.

## sections
### Definition
This article covers the alpha-blocker pharmacology tested at pp.215-223 of the source, the final section of the pharma MCQs bank: indications, smooth-muscle selectivity, reflex-tachycardia profile across agents, and prazosin's specific adverse-effect mechanisms.

### Mechanism
Alpha-1 receptor blockade relaxes smooth muscle wherever alpha-1 receptors mediate contraction (vessels, bladder neck, vas deferens, iris dilator), producing vasodilation, improved urinary flow, and pupil-sparing, but has no action on bronchial smooth muscle, which is controlled by parasympathetic and beta-2, not alpha, receptors. Reflex tachycardia follows from the degree of hypotension an alpha blocker produces (via baroreceptor reflex) and from whether alpha-2 autoreceptor feedback (which normally limits norepinephrine release) is spared or also blocked -- explaining why tamsulosin (alpha-1A-selective, alpha-2-sparing) causes the least, and why labetalol avoids it altogether through its own added beta blockade. Floppy iris syndrome is a distinct, iris-specific alpha-1A-blockade effect rather than a downstream consequence of an alpha blocker's systemic vasodilation.

### Key determinants
Whether a fact concerns a labelled clinical indication, a smooth-muscle-relaxation site, a reflex-tachycardia comparison across agents, or a distinct iris-specific adverse effect determines which drug and which mechanism the fact applies to.

### Clinical significance
Prescribing an alpha blocker for the wrong indication (e.g. assuming any alpha-blocker-class label applies uniformly across labetalol, tamsulosin, phenoxybenzamine and phentolamine), missing that reflex tachycardia risk differs materially between a nonselective and an alpha-1A-selective agent, or failing to anticipate floppy iris syndrome before cataract surgery in a patient on tamsulosin or another alpha-1 blocker, each carry real clinical consequences.
