## summary
An infarct's colour follows its tissue's vascular architecture: red where a dual or
collateral supply lets blood re-enter (loose tissues like lung), pale where a single end-
artery is cut with no collateral (solid organs like heart, kidney, spleen). Congestion
(passive, impaired venous drainage) and hyperemia (active, increased arterial inflow) are
the two mechanisms by which an organ ends up with excess blood, and exam distractors
commonly swap their definitions.

## sections
### Definition
An infarct is an area of ischaemic necrosis from occlusion of either the arterial supply or
venous drainage of a tissue. Red (haemorrhagic) infarction happens in loose, dual-supplied
tissue such as lung; pale (anaemic) infarction happens in solid, end-artery-supplied organs
such as heart, kidney and spleen. Congestion is passive venous/capillary dilatation from
impaired drainage; hyperemia is active arteriolar dilatation increasing arterial inflow.

### Mechanism
A tissue's blood supply determines an infarct's colour because it determines whether blood
can physically re-enter the dead zone: a dual supply (bronchial + pulmonary arteries in the
lung) lets blood seep back in, giving a red infarct; a single end-artery supply, once cut,
leaves nothing to refill the zone, giving a pale infarct. Congestion and hyperemia both
raise an organ's blood content but through opposite vessels and opposite direction of
change: congestion is downstream (venous outflow blocked), hyperemia is upstream (arterial
inflow increased).

### Key determinants
Whether an infarct will be red or pale is determined by the tissue's vascular architecture
(dual/collateral versus single end-artery), not by tissue firmness alone. Whether an organ's
excess blood is congestion or hyperemia is determined by which side of the capillary bed
dilated and why (impaired outflow versus increased inflow).

### Clinical significance
Recognising red-versus-pale infarction correctly matters because the two patterns point to
different underlying vascular lesions (dual-supply organ versus end-artery organ), and
recognising congestion-versus-hyperemia correctly distinguishes a chronic venous-outflow
problem (e.g. right heart failure) from an acute active-inflammatory process.

## published_summary

## published_sections

## hold_these
Red infarction occurs in loose, dual-supplied tissue (e.g. lung); pale infarction occurs in solid, end-artery organs (heart, kidney, spleen).
Congestion is a passive process from impaired venous drainage; hyperemia is an active process from increased arterial inflow.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming "loose tissue" always means red infarct regardless of blood supply.
Swapping congestion's and hyperemia's mechanisms in a distractor.

## summary
Virchow's triad -- endothelial injury, abnormal flow, hypercoagulability -- names the three
factors that predispose to thrombosis. A deep-leg-vein thromboembolus commonly lodges in a
peripheral pulmonary arterial branch, accounting for about 95% of pulmonary emboli. Four
Starling forces determine fluid movement across the capillary wall.

## sections
### Definition
Virchow's triad: endothelial injury, abnormal blood flow (stasis/turbulence) and
hypercoagulability, are the three factors predisposing to thrombus formation. A
thromboembolus from the deep leg veins commonly travels to and lodges in a peripheral
pulmonary arterial branch (pulmonary embolism), accounting for the great majority (about
95%) of pulmonary emboli. Four Starling forces (capillary hydrostatic pressure, plasma
colloid osmotic pressure, interstitial hydrostatic pressure, interstitial colloid osmotic
pressure) determine capillary fluid movement.

### Mechanism
Each arm of Virchow's triad independently raises thrombosis risk by a different route:
injury exposes thrombogenic subendothelial collagen, abnormal flow lets clotting factors
concentrate rather than being washed away, and hypercoagulability tips the balance of
pro- versus anti-coagulant factors. A detached venous thrombus embolises through the venous
system, right heart, and into the pulmonary arterial tree, lodging where the vessel narrows
past the embolus's own diameter.

### Key determinants
Which of the three Virchow's-triad factors dominates in a given clinical scenario (surgery
and immobility favour stasis; catheters and vasculitis favour endothelial injury; cancer and
pregnancy favour hypercoagulability) shapes where and how a thrombus forms. Which Starling
force is deranged (raised hydrostatic pressure in heart failure, lowered plasma oncotic
pressure in hypoalbuminaemia) shapes which clinical cause of edema is present.

### Clinical significance
A deep vein thrombosis is clinically important chiefly because of its embolic potential to
the lungs; recognising the ~95% figure underlines why DVT prophylaxis is a pulmonary-
embolism-prevention measure, not merely a leg-symptom one.

## published_summary

## published_sections

## hold_these

Four Starling forces determine capillary fluid movement.

## lose_the_mark
Naming only two of Virchow's three factors.
Assuming pulmonary emboli originate evenly from multiple sources rather than overwhelmingly from deep leg veins.

## summary
Edema is excess fluid in the interstitium or a body cavity. Gangrene is necrosis with
superadded bacterial putrefaction, classically by clostridia. Chronic hepatic venous
congestion gives the liver a characteristic mottled "nutmeg" cut surface.

## sections
### Definition
Edema: increased interstitial fluid, or an excessive body-cavity fluid accumulation.
Gangrene: necrosis with superadded putrefaction from bacterial activity (classically
clostridial), producing gas and toxins. Nutmeg liver: the mottled cut-surface pattern of
chronic hepatic venous congestion, most often from right-sided heart failure.

### Mechanism
Edema follows any imbalance among the Starling forces. Gangrene follows plain necrosis being
colonised by putrefactive bacteria, distinguishing it from sterile necrosis. The nutmeg
pattern arises because centrilobular hepatocytes, furthest from the portal blood supply, are
most exposed to the back-pressure of chronic venous congestion and appear darker/congested
against paler periportal zones.

### Key determinants
Whether necrosis is called gangrene depends specifically on the presence of superadded
bacterial putrefaction, not on the extent or cause of the underlying necrosis. Whether liver
congestion shows the nutmeg pattern depends on it being chronic (allowing the centrilobular/
periportal contrast to develop), not acute.

### Clinical significance
The nutmeg liver is a classic autopsy/imaging sign of long-standing right heart failure;
recognising gangrene's bacterial-putrefaction requirement (versus plain ischaemic necrosis)
guides whether antimicrobial and surgical debridement management is needed.

## published_summary

## published_sections

## hold_these

## lose_the_mark

Expecting the nutmeg pattern in acute, rather than chronic, hepatic congestion.

## summary
Hepatic schistosomiasis causes periportal fibrosis and portal hypertension -- distinct from
true cirrhosis -- which can predispose to pulmonary bilharziasis and hypersplenism. Urinary
bladder bilharziasis classically produces squamous (not adenocarcinoma) cancer, sandy
patches more often than the colon, and a precancerous cystitis glandularis (unlike cystitis
cystica), most commonly at the trigone.

## sections
### Definition
Hepatic bilharziasis produces periportal ("pipe-stem") fibrosis and portal hypertension, not
true cirrhosis. That portal hypertension can predispose to pulmonary bilharziasis (via
portosystemic shunting) and hypersplenism (via splenomegaly). Urinary bladder bilharziasis
classically predisposes to squamous cell carcinoma (not adenocarcinoma), shows sandy patches
more often than the colon does, most commonly affects the trigone, and can produce cystitis
glandularis (precancerous) as distinct from cystitis cystica (not precancerous).

### Mechanism
Schistosome ova deposited in tissue provoke a granulomatous reaction and fibrosis wherever
they lodge -- periportal in the liver, submucosal in the bladder and colon. In the liver this
presinusoidal fibrosis raises portal pressure without the nodular regeneration that defines
cirrhosis. In the bladder, chronic irritation from ova and secondary infection drives
squamous metaplasia of the normally transitional urothelium, which is why the resulting
cancer is classically squamous rather than the adenocarcinoma or transitional-cell
carcinoma more typical of non-bilharzial bladder cancer.

### Key determinants
Whether hepatic schistosomal fibrosis is called cirrhosis is determined by whether nodular
regeneration is present (it is not, in schistosomiasis) -- fibrosis alone does not make
cirrhosis. Whether a bladder metaplastic lesion is precancerous is determined by which named
variant it is (glandularis: yes; cystica: no), not by the general category "cystitis X".

### Clinical significance
Recognising bilharzial bladder cancer as classically squamous (not adenocarcinoma) changes
which histology a clinician expects and screens for in an endemic population; recognising
schistosomal liver fibrosis as distinct from cirrhosis avoids inappropriate cirrhosis-
specific management (e.g. hepatocellular-carcinoma surveillance protocols built for
nodular-regenerative cirrhosis) being applied to a different fibrotic pattern.

## published_summary

## published_sections

## hold_these

Bilharzial bladder cancer is classically squamous cell carcinoma; sandy patches favour the bladder over the colon.
Cystitis glandularis is precancerous; cystitis cystica is not.
Hepatic schistosomiasis causes periportal fibrosis, not true cirrhosis.
The bladder trigone is the commonest site affected in bilharzial cystitis.

## lose_the_mark
Defaulting to "adenocarcinoma" for bilharzial bladder or colon cancer.
Calling schistosomal liver fibrosis "cirrhosis" because both involve chronic fibrosis.
Assuming cystitis glandularis and cystitis cystica carry the same cancer risk.

## summary
Leprosy's clinical spectrum is set by cell-mediated immunity: lepromatous leprosy lacks it
(negative lepromin test, more severe), tuberculoid leprosy has it (positive lepromin test,
milder). Syphilis progresses primary (chancre) -> secondary -> tertiary (gummas, aortitis)
over years, not weeks. Actinomycosis is bacterial, not fungal, despite its name. Toxemia,
bacteremia and septicemia name three distinct blood states, and pyemic abscesses (from
septic emboli) are acute.

## sections
### Definition
Lepromatous leprosy: poor/absent T-cell immunity, negative lepromin test, more severe/
disseminated. Tuberculoid leprosy: strong T-cell immunity, positive lepromin test, milder
but more locally destructive. Syphilis: primary (chancre, at inoculation site) -> secondary
-> tertiary (gummas; aortitis of the ascending aorta/arch; occurs years, not weeks, after
infection). Actinomycosis: bacterial (Actinomyces species), not fungal, despite branching-
filament, fungus-like morphology. Toxemia: circulating toxins. Bacteremia: mere bacterial
presence in blood. Septicemia: actively multiplying, toxin-producing organisms in blood.
Pyemia: septic emboli seeding acute abscesses at distant sites.

### Mechanism
Leprosy's two poles reflect the host's own immune response to M. leprae, not differences in
the organism itself -- a strong cellular response (tuberculoid) contains the organism at the
cost of local tissue damage from that same immune reaction; a weak response (lepromatous)
allows disseminated bacillary growth with less local immune-mediated damage but far more
overall disease burden. Syphilis's stages reflect the host-pathogen interaction evolving over
years as Treponema pallidum persists untreated. The toxemia/bacteremia/septicemia distinction
turns on what, specifically, is circulating (toxins alone, organisms merely present, or
organisms actively multiplying and producing toxins).

### Key determinants
Distinguishing lepromatous from tuberculoid leprosy is determined by the lepromin skin test
result and by disease extent/severity, not by which sounds more "controlled". Distinguishing
the three blood-borne infection terms is determined by exactly what is circulating and
whether it is multiplying, a precise definitional distinction the exam tests directly.

### Clinical significance
Lepromin-test status and syphilis-stage timing both guide clinical staging and treatment
urgency. Correctly distinguishing bacteremia from septicemia matters clinically because
septicemia (with active multiplication and toxin production) is the more dangerous state
requiring more urgent treatment, even though "bacteremia" is sometimes loosely used to mean
either.

## published_summary

## published_sections

## hold_these
Lepromatous leprosy lacks T-cell immunity and gives a negative lepromin test; tuberculoid leprosy is the opposite and is the milder form.
The chancre is a primary-syphilis lesion; tertiary syphilis occurs years, not weeks, after infection.
Tertiary syphilis produces gummas and classically damages the ascending aorta/arch.
Actinomycosis is bacterial, not fungal.
Bacteremia is mere bacterial presence in blood, distinct from septicemia's active multiplication and toxin production.
Pyemic abscesses, from septic emboli, are acute lesions.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming "lepromatous" is the milder leprosy form because it sounds more organised.
Confusing bacteremia with septicemia.
Expecting tertiary syphilis on the same short timescale as primary/secondary disease.

## summary
Neoplasia's basic vocabulary is best learned as compare-contrast pairs: dysplasia versus
metaplasia, choristoma versus hamartoma, grading versus staging, and benign-slow versus
malignant-rapid growth -- each pair is a classic exam swap. A carcinoma is specifically a
malignant epithelial tumour.

## sections
### Definition
Dysplasia: disordered growth/maturation within one tissue type (loss of cell uniformity and
architectural orientation). Metaplasia: replacement of one differentiated tissue type by
another. Choristoma: normal tissue in an abnormal site. Hamartoma: disorganised native
tissue in its normal site. Grading: a tumour's degree of differentiation. Staging: a
tumour's extent of spread. Malignant tumours typically grow rapidly; benign tumours
typically grow slowly. A carcinoma is a malignant tumour of epithelial cell origin.

### Mechanism
Each pair names two related-sounding but genuinely different concepts, and the exam tests
whether the student has kept the definitions attached to the right term rather than
memorising the definitions in isolation. Dysplasia and metaplasia are both disorders of
growth, but one stays within a tissue type (dysplasia) and one crosses to a different tissue
type (metaplasia). Choristoma and hamartoma are both benign developmental anomalies of
otherwise-normal tissue, but one is in the wrong place (choristoma) and one is disorganised
in the right place (hamartoma). Grading and staging both classify a malignant tumour but
along different axes (cellular appearance versus anatomic spread).

### Key determinants
For each pair, the deciding question is specific: for dysplasia/metaplasia, "does the tissue
type change, or does it stay the same tissue with disordered architecture?" For choristoma/
hamartoma, "right tissue wrong site, or right site wrong organisation?" For grading/staging,
"differentiation, or extent of spread?"

### Clinical significance
Grading and staging together determine treatment and prognosis for a malignant tumour, so
keeping the two separate is not merely a vocabulary exercise. Recognising dysplasia (as
opposed to metaplasia) in a biopsy has direct premalignant-surveillance implications, since
dysplasia is a step further along the path to malignancy than simple metaplasia.

## published_summary

## published_sections

## hold_these
Dysplasia is disordered growth within one tissue type; metaplasia is replacement by a different differentiated tissue.
A choristoma is normal tissue in an abnormal site; a hamartoma is disorganised native tissue in its normal site.
Grading measures differentiation; staging measures extent of spread.
Malignant tumours typically grow rapidly; benign tumours typically grow slowly.
A carcinoma is a malignant tumour of epithelial cell origin.

## lose_the_mark
Swapping dysplasia's and metaplasia's definitions.
Swapping choristoma's and hamartoma's definitions.
Swapping grading's and staging's definitions.
Calling a carcinoma benign.
