## label
Removal of RNA primers in eukaryotic replication is not carried out by DNA polymerase ε

## canonical_key
replication.dna-polymerase-epsilon.not-primer-removal

## definition
Eukaryotic RNA primer removal is carried out by RNase H1, which degrades most of the RNA primer, and FEN1 (flap endonuclease 1), which trims the last ribonucleotide, not by DNA polymerase ε. Polymerase ε is instead a high-fidelity, proofreading-capable enzyme associated with continuous strand synthesis. Attributing primer excision to polymerase ε is a common exam trap that confuses the enzymes responsible for synthesis with those responsible for primer removal.

## explicit_objective
State that DNA polymerase ε does not carry out RNA primer removal in eukaryotic replication.

## pitfalls
Assuming any DNA polymerase directly excises RNA primers; in eukaryotes this is RNase H1/FEN1's job, not a polymerase's.

## label
At the replication fork, the leading strand is synthesized continuously and the lagging strand discontinuously

## canonical_key
replication.strands.leading-continuous-lagging-discontinuous

## definition
Because DNA polymerase can only extend 5' to 3' but the two template strands run antiparallel, only one new strand (the leading strand) can be built continuously toward the fork from a single primer. Primase synthesizes the short RNA primer required to initiate DNA synthesis

## canonical_key
replication.primer.primase-synthesizes-short-rna-primer

## definition
Because DNA polymerases cannot start a new chain de novo and can only extend an existing 3'-OH end, primase first lays down a short RNA molecule that base-pairs with the template strand. DNA polymerase then extends from this RNA primer's 3' end. In bacteria this is the enzyme DnaG; in eukaryotes it is the primase activity of the polymerase α-primase complex. The RNA primer is later excised and replaced with DNA.

## explicit_objective
State that primase synthesizes the short RNA primer required to initiate DNA synthesis, and that this primer is RNA, not DNA or protein.

## pitfalls
Crediting RNA polymerase (rather than the dedicated enzyme primase) with making the replication primer, or thinking the primer itself is made of DNA or protein rather than RNA.

## label
DNA excision repair does not require topoisomerase

## canonical_key
dnarepair.enzymes.topoisomerase-not-required

## definition
Standard excision-repair pathways need an endonuclease to cut out the damaged stretch, an exonuclease to trim it away, and a repair DNA polymerase to fill the resulting gap before ligase seals the nick. Topoisomerase, which relieves the supercoiling generated by unwinding large stretches of DNA (as at a replication fork), is not part of this core repair toolkit because a short excision-repair patch does not generate that kind of topological strain.

## explicit_objective
Identify that topoisomerase is not one of the core enzymes required for excision-repair of DNA.

## pitfalls
Assuming every enzyme involved in replication (including topoisomerase) is also required for DNA repair -- repair's enzyme set is narrower.

## label
DNA polymerase I removes RNA primers during prokaryotic replication

## canonical_key
replication.prokaryotic-primer-removal.dna-polymerase-i

## definition
DNA polymerase I has a 5'-to-3' exonuclease activity that removes the RNA primers laid down by primase, and it simultaneously fills the resulting gaps with DNA using its polymerase activity -- the nick-translation step of prokaryotic replication. DNA ligase then seals the remaining nick between the new DNA and the adjacent fragment. Polymerase III, by contrast, is the main enzyme that extends the primer with new DNA, not the one that removes it.

## explicit_objective
State that DNA polymerase I removes RNA primers during prokaryotic replication.

## pitfalls
Confusing polymerase I (primer removal and gap-filling) with polymerase III (the main replicative enzyme) in prokaryotic replication.

## label
Eukaryotes use different enzymes for leading- and lagging-strand synthesis, unlike prokaryotes

## canonical_key
replication.eukaryote-vs-prokaryote.different-enzymes-leading-lagging

## definition
Prokaryotes use one polymerase, DNA polymerase III, for both the leading and lagging strands. Eukaryotes instead use separate polymerases (classically ε- and δ-associated activities) for the two strands. This division of labour between different enzymes for the two strands is a genuine mechanistic difference between eukaryotic and prokaryotic replication; both systems remain semi-discontinuous, and both use RNA (not DNA) primers.

## explicit_objective
Identify that eukaryotes, unlike prokaryotes, use different enzymes for leading- versus lagging-strand synthesis.

## pitfalls
Assuming the eukaryote/prokaryote difference lies in primer chemistry or continuity of synthesis -- both use RNA primers and both are semi-discontinuous; the real difference is which enzyme handles which strand.
