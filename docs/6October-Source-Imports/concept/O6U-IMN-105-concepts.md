## label
Hypoglycemia is dangerous mainly because it deprives the brain of its glucose supply

## canonical_key
metabolism.hypoglycemia.danger-is-brain-glucose-deprivation

## definition
Unlike most tissues, the brain relies almost entirely on a continuous supply of blood glucose
for its energy needs (it cannot use fatty acids and only switches to ketone bodies after
prolonged fasting). Hypoglycemia is dangerous specifically because a fall in blood glucose
threatens this glucose supply to the brain, producing neuroglycopenic symptoms (confusion,
seizures, coma) if severe or prolonged -- it is not itself irreversible, does not cause
glucosuria (which requires hyperglycemia), and does not produce hemolysis.

## explicit_objective
State that hypoglycemia is dangerous primarily because it threatens the brain's glucose
supply.

## pitfalls
Picking a distractor describing a different, unrelated consequence (irreversibility,
glucosuria, hemolysis) -- the specific danger tested here is brain glucose deprivation.

## label
Glucosuria is the presence of a detectable amount of glucose in urine

## canonical_key
metabolism.glucosuria.definition

## definition
Glucosuria is defined simply as the presence of a detectable amount of glucose in the urine,
occurring once the plasma glucose concentration filtered at the glomerulus exceeds the renal
tubular reabsorptive capacity (the renal threshold, roughly 180 mg/dl). It is not itself a
synonym for diabetes mellitus (a broader disease that can cause glucosuria but is not defined
by it), does not involve fructose, and is not bounded by a specific low threshold such as
"less than 25 mg/dl".

## explicit_objective
Define glucosuria as detectable glucose in the urine, distinguishing it from the disease
(diabetes mellitus) that commonly causes it.

## pitfalls
Equating glucosuria directly with "diabetes mellitus" -- glucosuria is a urinary finding that
diabetes mellitus commonly causes, not a synonym for the disease itself.

## label
Complex I of the electron transport chain does not contain succinate dehydrogenase

## canonical_key
etc.complex-i.excludes-succinate-dehydrogenase

## definition
Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase/NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase) accepts electrons from NADH and
is built from many polypeptide subunits and roughly seven iron-sulfur centres that relay
electrons to coenzyme Q. Succinate dehydrogenase is a separate complex (Complex II) that
accepts electrons from FADH2 generated in the TCA cycle -- it is not a component of Complex I,
even though both ultimately feed electrons into the same respiratory chain.

## explicit_objective
State that succinate dehydrogenase is not a component of Complex I, since it constitutes the
separate Complex II.

## pitfalls
Assuming every TCA/ETC-linked enzyme belongs to Complex I -- succinate dehydrogenase's home is
Complex II, distinct from Complex I's NADH dehydrogenase, iron-sulfur centres and polypeptide
subunits.

## label
Succinate dehydrogenase is the TCA-cycle enzyme that is also a member of the electron
transport chain

## canonical_key
etc.tca-integration.succinate-dehydrogenase-dual-role

## definition
Succinate dehydrogenase is unique among TCA-cycle enzymes in being embedded directly in the
inner mitochondrial membrane and doubling as Complex II of the electron transport chain: it
oxidises succinate to fumarate in the TCA cycle while simultaneously passing the electrons
gained (via FAD) into the respiratory chain at coenzyme Q. Isocitrate dehydrogenase, alpha-
ketoglutarate dehydrogenase and pyruvate carboxylase are TCA-related (or anaplerotic) enzymes
but are not themselves ETC complexes.

## explicit_objective
Identify succinate dehydrogenase as the TCA-cycle enzyme that also functions as ETC Complex
II.

## pitfalls
Picking any other TCA enzyme by association -- succinate dehydrogenase specifically is the
one physically embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane as an ETC complex.

## label
Parathormone is not an anti-insulin (counter-regulatory) hormone

## canonical_key
endocrine.anti-insulin-hormones.parathormone-excluded

## definition
The classic anti-insulin (counter-regulatory) hormones -- adrenaline, growth hormone,
glucagon and cortisol -- all raise blood glucose, directly opposing insulin's glucose-
lowering action. Parathormone (PTH) is not among them: its physiological role is regulating
plasma calcium and phosphate (via bone resorption, renal calcium reabsorption and activation
of vitamin D), not glucose homeostasis, so it is the odd one out when the other three options
are all genuine counter-regulatory hormones.

## explicit_objective
State that parathormone is not an anti-insulin hormone, distinguishing its calcium-regulating
role from the glucose-raising role of adrenaline, growth hormone and glucagon.

## pitfalls
Selecting growth hormone as "not anti-insulin" because a Google Forms respondent's own
recorded answer picked it -- growth hormone genuinely is anti-insulin (it raises blood
glucose); the respondent's selection was wrong, and this record follows the hand-circled,
fact-checked key (parathormone) instead, per the standing rule against keying from a
respondent's own mark.
