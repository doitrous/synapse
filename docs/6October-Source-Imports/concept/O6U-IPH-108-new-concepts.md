## label
The intravenous route gives complete, instant bioavailability but its main hazards are extravasation necrosis, thrombophlebitis and air embolism

## canonical_key
teaching.pharma.routes.iv-hazards-and-uses

## definition
The intravenous (IV) route delivers a drug directly into the systemic circulation,
bypassing absorption entirely, which makes it the route of choice for irritant drugs
(diluted in the bloodstream rather than concentrated at a tissue site) and for large
volumes such as saline or glucose infusions. Its principal hazards follow directly from
this direct-to-bloodstream delivery: extravasation (the needle or cannula slipping outside
the vein) causes local tissue necrosis because the full, undiluted drug concentration is
released into surrounding tissue instead of being carried away by flowing blood;
thrombophlebitis follows a catheter left in place for a prolonged time as the vein wall is
irritated; and air embolism is a risk specific to injections given above the level of the
heart, where air can be drawn into the circulation instead of being vented.

## explicit_objective
State why extravasation outside the vein causes local necrosis by the IV route, and name
the other two classic hazards (thrombophlebitis, air embolism) with the condition that
provokes each.

## label
The intramuscular route is more painful than SC injection but suits oily depot preparations and moderate volumes that SC cannot take

## canonical_key
teaching.pharma.routes.im-depot-and-limits

## definition
Intramuscular (IM) injection deposits the drug into muscle tissue, which is more richly
vascularised than the subcutaneous layer and so absorbs faster, but the injection itself
is more painful than a subcutaneous one because muscle has denser nerve supply and the
needle must pass through more tissue. Muscle's rich blood supply and its ability to hold a
larger, oily depot make IM the route of choice for oily suspensions designed to release a
drug slowly over time (a depot preparation) -- the oil phase resists rapid absorption,
giving prolonged action from a single injection. IM also tolerates a larger injected volume
than SC and is the standard example of a parenteral (injected, non-enteral) route.

## explicit_objective
State that IM injection is more painful than SC but is preferred for oily depot
preparations because muscle's blood supply and tissue bulk support slow, sustained release.

hydrocortisone, are instead given by another parenteral route.

## label
Nasal delivery works for small systemic drugs like calcitonin and vasopressin but not for a large molecule like heparin, and a decongestant must alternate nostrils rather than treat both at once

## canonical_key
teaching.pharma.routes.nasal-limits

## definition
The nasal mucosa can absorb small peptide and protein drugs directly into the systemic
circulation, avoiding both the gut and first-pass metabolism -- calcitonin, vasopressin and
gonadotrophin-releasing hormone analogues are all given this way. Heparin, a large,

given intranasally. Separately, a topical nasal decongestant should be applied to one side
of the nose at a time rather than both sides simultaneously, because treating both nostrils
at once risks complete bilateral nasal obstruction if the mucosa reacts, leaving the
patient unable to breathe through the nose at all.

## explicit_objective
Name heparin as the systemic drug that cannot be given intranasally (unlike calcitonin,
vasopressin, GnRH analogues), and state that a nasal decongestant is applied to one nostril
at a time, not both simultaneously.

## label
Liniment, gel, ointment, spansule, spinhaler and sublingual tablet are distinguished by physical form and intended use, not interchangeable dosage-form names

## canonical_key
teaching.pharma.dosage-forms.topical-and-terminology

## definition
Several pharmaceutical dosage-form names describe distinct physical preparations that are
easy to confuse. A liniment is an alcoholic solution intended for topical use and applied
with rubbing, which both counter-irritates and helps penetration -- unlike a lotion (an
aqueous solution applied without rubbing). A gel is the dosage form best suited to covering
a very large area of skin because of its spreadability and non-greasy after-feel, compared
with a cream or ointment. Among delivery-device and formulation terms: a spinhaler is a
device for inhaling a powder (not a liquid aerosol), an ampoule is a single-dose (not
multi-dose) parenteral container, a spansule is a sustained-release capsule (not a
sugar-coated tablet for taste-masking), and a sublingual tablet is specifically a friable
tablet designed for rapid disintegration under the tongue for fast buccal absorption.

## explicit_objective
Distinguish liniment (alcoholic, rubbed in) from lotion (aqueous, not rubbed), identify gel
as best for large skin areas, and correctly define spinhaler, ampoule, spansule and
sublingual tablet against their common confusions.

## label
A metered dose inhaler is pressurized and delivers a fixed dose per puff but is not itself a dry powder inhaler and is not the active drug alone

## canonical_key
teaching.pharma.dosage-forms.inhaler-devices

## definition
Inhaled drugs reach the lung through devices that differ in mechanism. A metered dose
inhaler (MDI) is a pressurized, portable device that delivers a fixed dose of drug per
actuation ("puff"), but the canister also contains a propellant and other excipients
alongside the active drug -- it does not contain only the active drug. Dry powder inhalers
(turbohaler, spinhaler, discus, rotahaler) deliver the drug as a dry powder activated by
the patient's own inspiratory effort rather than a propellant; an MDI is specifically NOT a
dry powder inhaler, since it uses a pressurized propellant instead.

## explicit_objective
Classify the metered dose inhaler as pressurized, portable and NOT a dry powder inhaler,
and state that its canister contains excipients/propellant in addition to the active drug.

used for local action at the site of application. A nitroglycerin tablet, despite looking
like an ordinary oral solid dosage form, is placed sublingually specifically so the drug is
absorbed into the systemic circulation to relieve angina throughout the coronary
circulation -- it is not intended for local action in the mouth, making it the exception
among locally-acting preparations in this comparison.

## explicit_objective
Identify nitroglycerin (sublingual) tablets as acting systemically, in contrast with a set
of preparations (boric acid lotion, toothpaste, lozenges, oral gel) that act locally.
