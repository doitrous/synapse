## summary
Drug-receptor responses depend on binding forces, affinity, efficacy, intrinsic activity, and the mechanism by which agonists and antagonists alter signalling.
## sections
### Definition
Pharmacodynamics links drug-receptor binding to biological response, therapeutic effectiveness, and toxicity.

### Mechanism
Ionic, hydrogen, and covalent bonds differ in strength and reversibility. Agonists activate receptors, inverse agonists reduce constitutive activity, and antagonists oppose agonist responses through competitive, noncompetitive, physiological, pharmacological, or chemical mechanisms.

### Key determinants
- An ionic bond forms through attraction between opposite electrical charges.
- An ionic drug-receptor bond can form between a positively charged drug and a negatively charged amino-acid side chain.
- A covalent bond is the strongest common type of drug-receptor bond.
- The superglue analogy describes covalent drug-receptor binding.
- Hydrogen bonding occurs over a short distance between suitable donor and acceptor groups.
- Hydrogen bonds are relatively easy to break and reform.
- Acetylcholine binds the anionic site of the nicotinic receptor through an ionic interaction.
- Hydrogen-bond geometry can contribute to drug selectivity for receptors.
- Recovery after irreversible covalent receptor binding may require synthesis of new receptors.
- Organophosphorus compounds form a covalent bond with cholinesterase.
- Omeprazole forms a covalent bond with the gastric hydrogen-potassium ATPase proton pump.
- Ionic binding can hold a drug firmly while still allowing reversible detachment.
- A full agonist can produce the maximum possible response, or Emax.
- A full agonist has high efficacy.
- Morphine acts as a full agonist at opioid receptors in this comparison.
- A partial agonist produces a submaximal response even when it occupies all available receptors.
- A partial agonist can antagonise a full agonist when both compete for the same receptor population.
- Nalorphine is classified as a partial agonist at opioid receptors in this comparison.
- A partial agonist has receptor affinity but lower efficacy than a full agonist.
- A superagonist produces a greater response than the natural endogenous agonist.
- Fentanyl is classified as a superagonist in this source comparison.
- Isoprenaline is classified as a superagonist at adrenergic receptors in this source comparison.
- Carbachol is classified as a superagonist at muscarinic receptors in this source comparison.
- Co-agonism requires two agonists working together within the same receptor system.
- NMDA receptor activation requires glutamate and glycine acting as co-agonists.
- An inverse agonist produces an effect opposite to that of an agonist.
- An inverse agonist reduces constitutive receptor activity below baseline.
- Losartan can act as an inverse agonist at AT1 receptors.
- Angiotensin II causes vasoconstriction and increases arterial pressure.
- An inverse agonist both opposes agonist action and reduces constitutive activity below baseline.
- Affinity is the ability of a drug to bind to its receptor.
- Maximal efficacy describes the largest response a drug can produce regardless of dose.
- Receptor affinity helps determine how many drug-receptor complexes form at a given drug concentration.
- Therapeutic index relates a drug’s desired therapeutic effect to its toxic effect.
- When a lower dose produces the same response, that drug is more potent.
- Pindolol behaves as a partial agonist, stimulating beta receptors alone but opposing stronger beta agonists.
- A narrow therapeutic index means the therapeutic dose approaches the toxic dose.
- An agonist binds a receptor and produces a response that mimics its endogenous ligand.
- A competitive antagonist increases the agonist ED50 without reducing maximal efficacy when sufficient agonist is available.
- Relative potency cannot be inferred solely from whether drugs are partial or full agonists.
- Isoproterenol is classified as a full agonist in the cardiac beta-receptor comparison.
- An antagonist has receptor affinity but no efficacy.
- A noncompetitive antagonist decreases the maximum response to an agonist.
- Terbutaline bronchodilation physiologically antagonises leukotriene-mediated bronchoconstriction.
- A drug that directly interacts with atracurium and reverses its effect demonstrates chemical antagonism.
- A reversible competitive antagonist can be overcome by increasing the agonist concentration.
- Adrenaline bronchodilation physiologically antagonises histamine-mediated bronchoconstriction.
- Naloxone acts as a competitive antagonist of morphine at opioid receptors.
- An agonist has both receptor affinity and efficacy.
- Efficacy is the ability of a bound drug to produce a response.
- Therapeutic index is expressed as the ratio of a toxic or lethal dose to the effective dose.
- Therapeutic index is a measure of drug safety.
- A high therapeutic index generally indicates greater clinical safety.
- Potency can be represented by the dose that produces fifty percent of the maximal response.
- Chemical antagonism occurs when an antagonist interacts directly with the agonist rather than its receptor.
- A pharmacological antagonist occupies a receptor without activating it and blocks agonist action.
- A low therapeutic index indicates a greater risk of toxicity.
- Physiological antagonists act through independent receptors to produce opposite effects in the same tissue or organ.
- Antagonism occurs when one drug abolishes or reduces the effect of another.
- Competitive antagonism occurs when antagonist and agonist bind the same receptor site.
- Chemical antagonism can occur when an acidic and a basic drug interact and precipitate each other.
- Noncompetitive antagonism can occur when an antagonist binds a different receptor site and prevents agonist action.

### Clinical significance
Affinity, efficacy, potency, and therapeutic index help interpret dose-response relationships and drug safety.

### Common misconceptions
Do not confuse affinity with efficacy, potency with maximal response, or competitive antagonism with irreversible reduction of Emax.
