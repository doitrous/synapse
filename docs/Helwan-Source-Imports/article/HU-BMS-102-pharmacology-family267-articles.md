## summary
Drug pKa and environmental pH influence ionisation, membrane passage, absorption, tissue distribution, renal ion trapping, protein binding, and systemic bioavailability.
## sections
### Definition
Ionisation describes the charged fraction of a drug at a given pH, while distribution describes reversible drug movement between plasma and tissues.

### Mechanism
The relation between pKa and pH affects lipid-membrane passage and ion trapping. Protein binding, apparent distribution volume, first-pass metabolism, and route determine systemic exposure.

### Key determinants
- The source keys treatment of acidic-drug toxicity to administration of a basic substance.
- Among the listed compounds, a high-pKa weak base is least absorbed from the acidic stomach.
- The source keys sodium bicarbonate as the gastric-lavage fluid for an acidic-drug overdose.
- Ionised or lipid-insoluble drugs have poor passage across cell membranes.
- Drug pKa influences ionisation and therefore affects absorption.
- A weak acid is less ionised in an acidic medium.
- A weak base is less ionised in a basic medium.
- Passage across the blood-brain barrier generally requires sufficient lipid solubility.
- Greater lipid solubility generally increases passive drug absorption.
- Aspirin is predominantly non-ionised in the acidic stomach.
- Lower urinary pH increases ion trapping and urinary excretion of a weak base.
- A hydrophilic drug has a low ability to penetrate cell-membrane lipids.
- Urine acidification most strongly accelerates excretion of a weak base with a relatively high pKa.
- Weak acids are excreted faster in alkaline urine, whereas weak bases are excreted faster in acidic urine.
- Increasing pH increases ionisation of a weak acid and can decrease its absorption.
- The weak base amphetamine can become ionised and trapped in relatively acidic breast milk.
- Sodium bicarbonate alkalinises urine and increases urinary excretion of weak acidic drugs such as aspirin and barbiturates.
- Intravenous administration provides one-hundred-percent bioavailability.
- The liver is the principal organ responsible for the hepatic first-pass effect.
- Oral administration is the listed route most likely to subject a drug to first-pass metabolism.
- Calculating apparent volume of distribution requires the drug concentration in plasma.
- A loading dose is used to achieve the target or steady-state concentration rapidly.
- Loading dose is governed principally by apparent volume of distribution for a specified target concentration and bioavailability.
- For a 70 kg patient with distribution through total body water, a 5 mg/L initial level requires a source-keyed dose of 210 mg.
- Apparent volume of distribution is calculated as drug amount in the body divided by plasma concentration.
- Severe hypoalbuminaemia can increase the free fraction and toxic response to an extensively protein-bound drug.
- Highly albumin-bound drugs can compete with other drugs for albumin-binding sites.
- Aspirin can displace highly protein-bound warfarin and increase the risk of an excessive or toxic response.
- Local anaesthetics block voltage-dependent sodium channels in nerve fibres.
- Apparent volume of distribution is the hypothetical volume into which a drug appears to distribute at equilibrium.
- Albumin is the most important plasma protein for binding many drugs.
- Bioavailability is the fraction of an administered dose that reaches the systemic circulation.
- Bioavailability by a given route is assessed relative to intravenous administration.
- Liver damage may alter a drug’s bioavailability, particularly when hepatic first-pass metabolism is important.

### Clinical significance
Urine pH, plasma albumin, loading dose, and route can materially change drug concentration, toxicity, and bioavailability.

### Common misconceptions
Do not invent a missing fourth choice, detach a graph-dependent operation from its graph, or infer official authority from an instructor-attributed bank.
