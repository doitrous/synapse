## summary
Posology covers dose terminology, therapeutic concentration ranges, patient and route adjustments, paediatric rules, and loading and maintenance doses.
## sections
### Definition
Posology is the study of appropriate drug doses and dosage regimens.

### Mechanism
Dose selection integrates drug exposure, therapeutic and toxic concentration limits, route, elimination, patient characteristics, and the intended speed and duration of exposure.

### Key determinants
- Posology deals with determining the appropriate drug dose.
- The Greek word “posos” means “how much.”
- A dose is the specific amount of a drug taken at one time.
- A dosage regimen is the schedule of doses.
- The minimal effective dose is the lowest dose required to produce a therapeutic effect.
- The maximal tolerated dose is the greatest dose that can be given without inducing toxic effects.
- ED50 is the dose producing a specified therapeutic effect in 50% of a tested population.
- TD50 is the dose producing a specified toxic effect in 50% of a tested population.
- LD50 is the dose causing death in 50% of a tested population.
- The source calculates therapeutic index as TD50 divided by ED50.
- MEC stands for minimum effective concentration.
- MTC stands for minimum toxic concentration.
- The therapeutic window is the concentration range in which a drug is effective without being toxic.
- A drug concentration below the therapeutic window is ineffective.
- A drug concentration above the therapeutic window causes toxic harm.
- The source bases its standard adult dose on a 70 kg person aged 18–65 years.
- Neonatal hepatic CYP450 enzyme systems are underdeveloped.
- The source recommends a “start low, go slow” dosing approach in older adults.
- The source classifies female sex hormones as hepatic microsomal enzyme inhibitors.
- The source advises avoiding aspirin during menstruation.
- The source advises avoiding teratogenic drugs and lists aspirin and cortisone as examples during pregnancy.
- The source identifies body-surface-area dosing as the most accurate listed method for children.
- Pharmacogenomics helps explain ultra-rapid and poor drug-metabolizer phenotypes.
- Dose reduction for primarily renally excreted drugs can be based on creatinine clearance.
- Gentamicin dosing requires adjustment in renal impairment.
- The source warns that unadjusted propranolol dosing may cause toxic levels in hepatic disease.
- The source links nighttime statin dosing to greater cholesterol synthesis at night.
- The source generalizes that most orally administered drugs are better given before meals.
- The source recommends administering NSAIDs after meals.
- The source attributes a usually higher oral than parenteral dose to compensation for first-pass effect.
- The source attributes a higher subcutaneous than intramuscular dose to lower vascularity of subcutaneous tissue.
- The source describes oral magnesium sulphate as a cholagogue at small dose or purgative at large dose.
- The source describes intravenous magnesium sulphate as an anticonvulsant and skeletal-muscle relaxant.
- The source applies Young’s rule to children aged 1–12 years.
- Young’s rule calculates child dose as age divided by age plus 12, multiplied by adult dose.
- The source applies Dilling’s rule to ages 4–20 years.
- Dilling’s rule calculates child dose as age in years divided by 20, multiplied by adult dose.
- Clark’s rule uses body weight to estimate a child dose.
- The source’s percentage method assigns an approximately 25% adult dose at age 1 year.
- The source’s percentage method assigns an approximately 50% adult dose at age 7 years.
- The source’s percentage method assigns an approximately 75% adult dose at age 12 years.
- The Mosteller formula calculates body surface area.
- The source assigns two-thirds of the adult dose to adults aged 60–70 years.
- The source assigns half the adult dose to adults older than 70 years.
- The source attributes reduced older-adult dosing to weaker hepatic microsomal activity and reduced renal excretion.
- A loading dose is given to achieve a target concentration rapidly.
- The source expresses loading dose as (Vd × Cp) / F.
- A maintenance dose replaces the amount of drug eliminated.
- The source expresses maintenance dose as (CL × Cp × T) / F.

### Clinical significance
These relationships reproduce an instructor-attributed auxiliary bank. They require independent medical review, especially broad age-, meal-, menstruation-, pregnancy-, and route-based dosing generalisations; they are not patient-specific prescribing instructions.

### Common misconceptions
Do not interchange dose with dosage regimen, MEC with MTC, or loading-dose and maintenance-dose equations.
