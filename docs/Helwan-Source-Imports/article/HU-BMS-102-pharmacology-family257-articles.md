## summary
General pharmacology connects drug movement through the body with receptor action, dose-response relationships, repeated exposure, interactions, and safety.
## sections
### Definition
Pharmacokinetics describes what the body does to a drug, while pharmacodynamics describes what the drug does to the body.

### Mechanism
Absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion determine exposure. Receptors, ion channels, carrier proteins, second messengers, and dose-response relationships determine drug effects.

### Key determinants
- Pharmacokinetics studies drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion.
- Drug biotransformation is part of pharmacokinetics.
- Drug distribution in the body is part of pharmacokinetics.
- Drug excretion is part of pharmacokinetics.
- Passive lipid diffusion is the main mechanism of gastrointestinal absorption for most drugs.
- Hydrophilic substances do not readily cross lipid membranes by passive diffusion.
- Hydrophilic drugs have low ability to penetrate cell-membrane lipids.
- Active transport can move a drug against its concentration gradient.
- Bioavailability is the fraction of an administered dose that reaches the systemic circulation.
- The extent of absorption and the hepatic first-pass effect determine bioavailability.
- Rectal administration can reduce hepatic first-pass exposure compared with oral administration.
- Oral administration is the listed route most likely to produce a first-pass effect.
- Oral drug absorption depends on gastrointestinal secretion and motor function.
- Sublingual administration can produce relatively rapid absorption.
- Inhalation delivers a drug without gastrointestinal absorption.
- Parenteral administration usually produces a more rapid response than oral administration.
- Oily drug solutions may be administered by the intramuscular route.
- Oily solutions are not suitable for intravenous injection.
- Inhalation can provide rapid rather than slow access to the general circulation.
- Most drugs are not distributed homogeneously throughout the body.
- Cell membranes, capillary walls, and the placenta are biological barriers to drug distribution.
- The absence of pores in brain capillary endothelium restricts drug penetration across the blood-brain barrier.
- Apparent volume of distribution relates the amount of drug in the body to its plasma concentration.
- Plasma drug concentration is required to calculate apparent volume of distribution.
- Lipid-soluble drugs that penetrate barriers and distribute widely tend to have a large, not small, apparent volume of distribution.
- Drug biotransformation is the physicochemical and biochemical alteration of a drug in the body.
- Biotransformation generally makes drugs less lipid soluble.
- Microsomal oxidation is especially prominent for lipid-soluble drugs.
- Microsomal oxidation commonly increases drug ionisation and water solubility.
- Induction of hepatic microsomal enzymes may require an increased dose of some affected drugs.
- Phase I metabolic transformation includes oxidation, reduction, and hydrolysis.
- Biotransformation commonly facilitates faster urinary drug excretion.
- Conjugation couples a drug or metabolite with an endogenous substrate.
- Acetylation is a phase II biotransformation reaction.
- Hydrolysis is a phase I reaction rather than a conjugation reaction.
- Metabolic transformation and conjugation usually reduce rather than increase biological activity.
- Reduced microsomal enzyme activity in liver disease can prolong the action of some drugs.
- Elimination half-life is the time required for the plasma amount or concentration of a drug to fall by half.
- Elimination half-life does not depend on the time required for drug absorption.
- The elimination rate constant is mathematically related to elimination half-life.
- Pharmacodynamics studies drug effects and mechanisms, not absorption and distribution.
- Mechanisms of drug action are part of pharmacodynamics.
- Unwanted drug effects are studied within pharmacodynamics.
- A receptor is a cellular macromolecule to which a drug binds to elicit a specific effect.
- Affinity measures how tightly a drug binds to a receptor.
- Drug targets can include receptors, ion channels, and carrier proteins.
- An agonist binds a receptor and initiates a change in cell function.
- A full agonist can produce the maximal response and has high efficacy.
- A partial agonist produces a submaximal response and has lower efficacy than a full agonist.
- An antagonist binds a receptor without directly activating its function.
- A competitive antagonist binds the agonist receptor site and progressively inhibits the agonist response.
- An agonist-antagonist acts as an agonist at one receptor subtype and an antagonist at another.
- Irreversible receptor antagonism commonly results from covalent bonding.
- Gene replacement is not a mechanism of receptor-mediated transmembrane signalling.
- Cyclic AMP is an intracellular second messenger generated downstream of some G-protein-coupled receptors.
- A G protein changes effector activity but is not itself a second messenger.
- Increased second-messenger concentrations can activate protein kinases and increase protein phosphorylation.
- Sodium-channel blockers, calcium-channel blockers, and potassium-channel activators all act through ion-channel interactions.
- ED50 is a measure of potency rather than maximal efficacy.
- A therapeutic dose produces the required effect in most patients.
- A toxic dose produces effects that are hazardous to the organism.
- Drug accumulation during repeated administration can lead to toxic reactions.
- Tolerance is a gradual decrease in responsiveness that develops over days or weeks.
- Drug hypersensitivity is an unwanted reaction that is not predictably related to dose or ordinary pharmacodynamic action.
- An idiosyncratic drug reaction is an unpredictable, inherent, qualitatively abnormal response.
- Drug resistance describes loss of effectiveness of antimicrobial or antitumour drugs.
- Increased metabolic degradation can contribute to tolerance and drug resistance.
- Receptor change or loss and mediator exhaustion can contribute to tolerance and drug resistance.
- Tolerance is not adequately explained by diminished absorption or rapid excretion alone in this comparison.
- Drug dependence may include tolerance, physical withdrawal, and psychological craving.
- An abstinence syndrome occurs when stopping a drug produces serious psychological and somatic disturbances.
- A pharmacokinetic drug interaction alters absorption, distribution, metabolism, or excretion.
- A pharmacodynamic drug interaction occurs at receptor, cell, enzyme, or organ level.
- Combining drugs can produce synergism.
- An additive drug effect equals the sum of the effects produced by the drugs separately.
- Potentiation is an intensified drug effect produced by a drug combination.
- Competitive antagonism is a type of drug antagonism.
- Chemical antagonism occurs when two substances combine to form an inactive compound.
- A teratogenic effect harms the fetus and can cause fetal malformation.
- An amount of 2.0 g at a plasma concentration of 25 micrograms per millilitre corresponds to an apparent volume of distribution of 80 litres.
- High plasma-protein binding tends to reduce apparent volume of distribution.
- In first-order elimination, clearance remains constant and a constant fraction is eliminated per unit time.
- Repeated dosing at half-life intervals generally reaches steady state after about four to five half-lives.
- Muscarinic cholinergic receptors are G-protein-coupled receptors.
- Continuous agonist exposure can cause receptor down-regulation.
- Adrenaline and histamine show physiological antagonism because they produce opposite physiological effects.
- A noncompetitive antagonist reduces the slope of the log dose-response curve and suppresses the maximal response.
- Urinary alkalinisation reduces renal tubular reabsorption of the weak acid phenobarbital.
- A drug with a ten-hour half-life reaches near steady state during continuous infusion after about fifty hours.

### Clinical significance
Route, biological barriers, metabolism, elimination, receptor behaviour, repeated exposure, and drug interactions all influence therapeutic and toxic responses.

### Common misconceptions
Do not confuse pharmacokinetics with pharmacodynamics, potency with efficacy, tolerance with dependence, or first-order with zero-order elimination.
