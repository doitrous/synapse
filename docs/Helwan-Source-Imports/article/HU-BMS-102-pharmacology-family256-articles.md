## summary
Beta blockers inhibit beta-adrenergic receptors; their receptor selectivity and central penetration help explain selected uses and safety risks.
## sections
### Definition
Beta blockers inhibit beta-adrenergic receptor signalling.

### Mechanism
Cardiac beta blockade decreases heart rate and contractility, reducing myocardial oxygen demand in angina. Atenolol is relatively beta-1-selective, whereas propranolol is non-selective and is used when central effects are relevant.

### Key determinants
- Beta blockers inhibit beta-adrenergic receptors.
- Beta blockers help treat angina by decreasing heart rate and myocardial contractility.
- Non-selective beta blockers can harm a patient with asthma.
- Atenolol is a beta-1-selective beta blocker.
- Beta blockers can cause hypoglycaemia unawareness.
- Propranolol is the beta blocker selected for CNS effects in performance anxiety.
- Abrupt withdrawal after long-term beta-blocker use may cause myocardial infarction.

### Clinical significance
Non-selective beta blockade can worsen asthma by blocking pulmonary beta-2 receptors. Beta blockers can mask adrenergic warning signs of hypoglycaemia, and abrupt withdrawal after long-term use can create rebound ischaemic risk.

### Common misconceptions
Do not treat beta blockers as coronary vasodilators, describe beta-1 selectivity as absolute, or confuse ongoing blockade effects with rebound withdrawal risk.
