## summary
Tumour size alone does not describe growth kinetics. Growth fraction measures the percentage of proliferating tumour cells and helps explain relative chemotherapy susceptibility, while diffusion limits avascular growth to approximately 1–2 mm before angiogenesis becomes necessary for further enlargement.
## sections
### Definition
Growth fraction is the percentage of proliferating cells within a tumour. It is a proportion of the tumour-cell population, not total size, cell-cycle duration, angiogenesis rate or apoptotic fraction.

### Mechanism
Chemotherapeutic agents act most effectively on dividing cells. A tumour with a high growth fraction therefore presents more cycling targets and is relatively more susceptible to chemotherapy; a low-growth-fraction tumour is relatively resistant within this module-level model.

### Key determinants
Further growth also requires supply. The current lecture states that a tumour cannot enlarge beyond approximately 1–2 mm without inducing host blood-vessel growth. The Family-4 question asks for the maximum listed value and prints 2 mm.

### Clinical significance
Growth fraction connects cell kinetics to treatment sensitivity, but it does not by itself choose a patient-specific therapy. The avascular-size teaching point explains why angiogenesis is necessary for progressive enlargement rather than proving that every tumour switches at an identical exact diameter.

### Common misconceptions
Do not substitute tumour size for growth fraction. Do not call high growth fraction synonymous with every form of rapid growth. Do not turn the source's approximate 1–2 mm range into a universal exact constant; 2 mm is the assessment's maximum-value key.
