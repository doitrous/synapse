### Definition
A focused review of five adrenergic drugs and their receptor or transmitter mechanisms.

### Mechanism
Receptor selectivity, effects on adrenergic transmitter synthesis, and cholinesterase inhibition distinguish these drugs. Therapeutic indications follow from those pharmacological actions.

### Core relations
- Adrenaline stimulates alpha and beta adrenergic receptors.
- Alpha-methyldopa reduces adrenergic transmitter synthesis through its central sympatholytic pathway.
- Isoprenaline stimulates beta adrenergic receptors rather than alpha receptors.
- Clonidine is an alpha-2 adrenergic receptor agonist.
- Labetalol blocks both alpha and beta adrenergic receptors.

### Key determinants
Distinguish broad adrenergic agonism from beta-only agonism, alpha-2 agonism from mixed alpha/beta blockade, and direct muscarinic agonists from anticholinesterases.

### Clinical significance
These relations support foundational autonomic pharmacology. They do not provide prescribing, dosing, or patient-management instructions.

### Common misconceptions
Do not confuse isoprenaline with an alpha agonist, clonidine with an adrenergic blocker, or physostigmine with a peripheral-only anticholinesterase.

Receptor selectivity, effects on adrenergic transmitter synthesis, and cholinesterase inhibition distinguish these drugs. Therapeutic indications follow from those pharmacological actions.

### Core relations
- Pilocarpine is a muscarinic agonist used as a miotic and for glaucoma or xerostomia.
- Bethanechol is used to stimulate gastrointestinal smooth muscle in megacolon.
- Neostigmine reverses curare-type neuromuscular blockade and is used in myasthenia gravis.
- Physostigmine can reverse central and peripheral manifestations of atropine toxicity.
