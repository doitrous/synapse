## summary
Bacteria resist antimicrobials by inactivating the drug, modifying its target, bypassing an inhibited metabolic step, reducing entry or actively exporting the drug. The five mechanisms are distinct even though each lowers antimicrobial effectiveness.
## sections
### Definition
Beta-lactamase inactivates penicillin by enzymatic drug destruction. Altered penicillin-binding proteins cause modified-target resistance to penicillin. Sulphonamide-resistant bacteria can bypass inhibition by using preformed folic acid without extracellular PABA. Porin changes cause resistance by reducing antimicrobial permeability below an effective intracellular concentration. A multidrug-resistance pump causes resistance by actively exporting antibiotics and decreasing intracellular concentration.

### Mechanism
### Drug inactivation and target modification
Beta-lactamase destroys a susceptible beta-lactam drug. Altered PBPs instead change the drug target so effective binding is lost.

### Metabolic bypass
Sulphonamide-resistant bacteria may use preformed folate and avoid the PABA-dependent step that sulphonamides block.

### Entry and exit
Porin changes reduce antimicrobial influx across the outer membrane. MDR pumps actively export drug after it reaches the membrane or cytoplasm. Both lower intracellular concentration but act in opposite directions.

### Key determinants
Identify beta-lactamase production as enzymatic drug inactivation and distinguish it from other resistance mechanisms. Recognize altered PBPs as a modified-target resistance mechanism. Identify use of preformed folate as the metabolic-bypass mechanism of sulphonamide resistance. Explain how altered porins cause resistance by lowering antimicrobial permeability and intracellular concentration. Identify active drug export and reduced intracellular concentration as the MDR-pump resistance mechanism.

### Clinical significance
These distinctions support mechanism-based resistance interpretation and responsible antimicrobial use. They are educational summaries, not patient-specific treatment advice, and every linked record remains Draft pending independent review.

## summary
Resistance may be intrinsic because the organism lacks a drug target, or acquired through mobile genetic elements and chromosomal mutation. Mycoplasma lacks a cell wall; R factors are resistance plasmids; transposons move by transposition; chromosomal resistance commonly arises through spontaneous mutation.
## sections
### Definition
Mycoplasma is intrinsically resistant to penicillin because it lacks a cell wall. A resistance factor is a plasmid carrying resistance to one or more antimicrobial agents. Transposon movement between plasmids and chromosomes is called transposition. Chromosomal drug resistance develops through spontaneous mutation in a gene controlling antimicrobial susceptibility.

### Mechanism
### Intrinsic absent-target resistance
Mycoplasma naturally lacks peptidoglycan and therefore has no target for penicillin. This is intrinsic resistance rather than acquisition of a beta-lactamase.

### Mobile resistance genes
R factors are plasmids mediating resistance to one or more agents. Transposons can carry resistance genes between plasmids and chromosomes, and their movement is transposition.

### Chromosomal origin
A spontaneous mutation in a gene controlling drug susceptibility can establish chromosomal resistance. Drug exposure may select the mutant, but it is not substituted for the genetic event in this question.

### Key determinants
Explain Mycoplasma penicillin failure as intrinsic resistance due to absence of a cell wall. Identify a plasmid carrying multiple antimicrobial-resistance genes as a resistance factor. Name movement of a transposon between plasmid and chromosome as transposition. Identify spontaneous mutation in a susceptibility gene as the usual origin of chromosomal drug resistance.

### Clinical significance
These distinctions support mechanism-based resistance interpretation and responsible antimicrobial use. They are educational summaries, not patient-specific treatment advice, and every linked record remains Draft pending independent review.

## summary
Antimicrobial therapy can cause ecological and direct toxic complications. Suppression of normal oral flora may permit Candida superinfection, while streptomycin can injure the eighth cranial nerve and cause deafness.
## sections
### Definition
Antibiotic suppression of normal flora can cause Candida oral superinfection. Streptomycin can cause eighth-cranial-nerve injury and deafness.

### Mechanism
### Superinfection
Prolonged or intensive therapy can remove susceptible normal flora and allow resistant organisms to overgrow. Oral Candida thrush is the source example.

### Drug toxicity
Streptomycin is classically associated with eighth-cranial-nerve injury and deafness. Other listed toxicities belong to different drugs, so the adverse effect must be matched to the named agent.

### Distinguishing complications
Superinfection is replacement overgrowth after ecological disruption. Toxicity is direct drug injury, while hypersensitivity is an exaggerated immune response to the drug.

### Key determinants
Recognize oral Candida overgrowth after prolonged antibiotics as superinfection caused by suppression of normal flora. Associate the classic toxic adverse effect of deafness with streptomycin.

### Clinical significance
These distinctions support mechanism-based resistance interpretation and responsible antimicrobial use. They are educational summaries, not patient-specific treatment advice, and every linked record remains Draft pending independent review.

## summary
Synergism is a combined effect greater than the sum of the individual drug effects. Antibiotic stewardship also requires diagnosis-led, specimen-informed treatment whenever possible rather than routine use for every fever, operation or prophylactic situation.
## sections
### Definition
Synergism is a combined drug effect significantly greater than the sum of separate effects. Antibiotic treatment should be based on a clear clinical and bacteriological diagnosis whenever possible.

### Mechanism
### Combination effects
Indifference is no better than the more effective drug alone, addition equals the sum, synergism exceeds the sum, and antagonism reduces the action.

### Diagnosis before treatment
Suitable specimens should be collected and treatment should be based on a clear clinical and bacteriological diagnosis whenever possible. Empirical treatment can begin after sampling and should be revised using susceptibility results.

### Avoiding overuse
Antibiotics are not indicated for every febrile illness. Combination therapy and prophylaxis are reserved for defined situations rather than applied routinely.

### Key determinants
Distinguish synergism from addition, indifference and antagonism using the magnitude of the combined effect. Identify diagnosis-led, specimen-informed treatment as a stewardship principle that discourages resistance.

### Clinical significance
These distinctions support mechanism-based resistance interpretation and responsible antimicrobial use. They are educational summaries, not patient-specific treatment advice, and every linked record remains Draft pending independent review.
