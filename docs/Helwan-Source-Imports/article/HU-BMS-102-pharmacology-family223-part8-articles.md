## sections
### Definition
Placental transfer can expose the developing embryo or fetus to drugs, and teratogenicity describes exposure-related developmental malformation or harm.

### Mechanism
Transfer depends on drug and placental properties; developmental outcome also depends on agent, dose, timing, and susceptibility.

### Key determinants
Lipid solubility, ionisation, protein binding, molecular size, gestational timing, and exposure affect the comparison.

### Clinical significance
These items support foundational recognition only and do not recommend or prohibit any medicine in pregnancy.

### Common misconceptions
Not every placentally transferred drug is teratogenic, and placental transfer is not an all-or-none barrier.

### Source-tested concept lines
Drugs crossing the placenta can be teratogenic
A teratogenic effect harms the fetus and can cause fetal malformation.

### Definition
Absorption moves drug from an administration site into blood; first pass is presystemic gut-wall and hepatic metabolism; apparent volume of distribution relates amount in the body to plasma concentration.

### Mechanism
Intestinal surface area supports absorption, presystemic metabolism lowers unchanged systemic entry, and limited tissue distribution produces a low apparent Vd.

### Key determinants
Ionisation, surface area, perfusion, metabolism, plasma binding, and tissue affinity govern these distinct processes.

### Clinical significance
The source comparisons organise foundational pharmacokinetic interpretation rather than dosing decisions.

### Common misconceptions
Do not use pH alone to predict absorption, restrict first pass to the gut lumen, or treat Vd as an anatomical volume.

### Source-tested concept lines

First-pass effect includes gut-wall and liver metabolism before systemic circulation

### Definition
The source expresses therapeutic index as LD50 divided by ED50, identifies propranolol as a competitive antagonist, and describes a placebo as an inert preparation whose observed response can depend on psychological context.

### Mechanism
Dose separation estimates experimental safety margin, reversible receptor competition shifts agonist response, and expectation/context can influence placebo response.

### Key determinants
Population, endpoint, receptor interaction, comparator, expectation, and study design affect interpretation.

### Clinical significance
These are foundational classifications, not a full clinical safety assessment or treatment recommendation.

### Common misconceptions
Do not reverse the therapeutic-index ratio, classify agonists as antagonists, or call placebo response an active-drug effect.

### Source-tested concept lines

## sections
### Definition
Active transport uses energy and a carrier to move substrate, including movement against a concentration gradient.

### Mechanism
A finite number of carriers makes the process saturable, and substrates sharing a carrier can compete.

### Key determinants
Carrier abundance, energy availability, substrate concentration, affinity, and competitors affect transport.

### Clinical significance
Carrier properties help distinguish active transport from simple diffusion.

### Common misconceptions
Active transport is not energy-independent, non-saturable, or immune to competitors.

### Source-tested concept lines
Active transport is saturable and can be inhibited by competitors
