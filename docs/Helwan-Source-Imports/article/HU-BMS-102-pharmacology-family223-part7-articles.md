## sections
### Definition
Pharmacokinetics describes absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion rather than a drug’s therapeutic uses. Route and formulation influence the absorption component.

### Mechanism
A compatible aqueous irritant given intravenously is diluted in circulating blood rather than deposited in tissue, which is the comparison intended by Q98.

### Key determinants
Actual administration depends on product labelling, formulation, compatibility, venous access, dilution, monitoring, and extravasation risk.

### Clinical significance
ADME organises disposition reasoning. Q98 remains source-bounded recognition teaching and does not recommend administering any drug.

### Common misconceptions
Do not omit distribution from pharmacokinetics or turn the carrier’s intravenous answer into a universal clinical rule.

### Source-tested concept lines

## sections
### Definition
Human insulin can be manufactured using recombinant DNA technology. Simple drug diffusion across lipid membranes proceeds down a concentration gradient without energy or a carrier.

### Mechanism
Recombinant expression systems produce insulin-related polypeptides from introduced human genetic sequences. Passive membrane diffusion favours the non-ionised lipid-soluble fraction.

### Key determinants
Production method depends on the medicinal molecule; diffusion depends on size, lipid solubility, ionisation, and the concentration gradient.

### Clinical significance
These source items test distinct foundational principles and do not provide manufacturing or administration instructions.

### Common misconceptions
Not every medicine is recombinant, and ionised drug is not the fraction favoured for simple lipid-membrane diffusion.

### Source-tested concept lines
Human insulin is produced using recombinant DNA technology
Most drugs cross cell membranes by passive diffusion, which favours the small, lipid-soluble, non-ionised molecule

## sections
### Definition
Phase I commonly uses oxidation, reduction, or hydrolysis; Phase II combines a drug or metabolite with an endogenous moiety. Drug metabolism usually increases polarity and reduces lipid solubility.

### Mechanism
Phase I can inactivate a drug, retain activity in a changed metabolite, or activate a precursor. Phase II conjugation is a separate reaction class.

### Key determinants
Reaction class, substrate, enzyme system, and metabolite activity determine outcome. Hydrolysis remains Phase I in this source framework.

### Clinical significance
Greater polarity often facilitates excretion, but active and toxic metabolites remain possible.

### Common misconceptions
Combination with an endogenous substance is not a Phase I outcome, and metabolism does not invariably inactivate a drug.

### Source-tested concept lines
Phase I metabolic transformation includes oxidation, reduction, and hydrolysis
The liver is the main site of drug metabolism, but the gut wall, plasma, lung and kidney all metabolise drugs too
Hydrolysis is a phase I reaction rather than a conjugation reaction.

## sections
### Definition
Reversible plasma-protein binding provides a reservoir from which free drug can dissociate. Bioavailability is the fraction reaching systemic circulation unchanged, with intravenous dosing defined as 100 percent.

### Mechanism
Protein-bound drug is not immediately diffusible or filterable. Oral drug reaches portal blood and can undergo first-pass hepatic extraction before systemic entry.

### Key determinants
Binding affinity, protein concentration, route, absorption, gut-wall metabolism, and hepatic extraction affect these processes.

### Clinical significance
Extensive first-pass metabolism can make oral delivery inefficient, while intravenous delivery establishes complete initial systemic availability.

### Common misconceptions
Bound drug is not immediately pharmacologically active, and complete bioavailability does not mean a drug remains confined to plasma.

### Source-tested concept lines
High plasma protein binding can prolong drug action by providing a circulating reservoir
The extent of absorption and the hepatic first-pass effect determine bioavailability.
Bioavailability is the fraction of an oral dose that reaches the systemic circulation, and first-pass metabolism is what removes the rest
