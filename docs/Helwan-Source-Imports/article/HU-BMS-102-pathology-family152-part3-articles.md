## summary
Hyperaemia is active arteriolar inflow, whereas congestion is passive venous outflow failure. Chronic hepatic congestion produces nutmeg liver, and chronic pulmonary congestion produces haemosiderin-laden heart-failure cells.
## sections
### Definition
Hyperaemia is active whereas congestion is passive. Hyperaemia increases oxygenated inflow after arteriolar dilatation; congestion accumulates deoxygenated blood after venous outflow is impaired.

### Mechanism
Persistent congestion raises venous and capillary pressure, producing hypoxia, erythrocyte leakage, parenchymal injury and eventually fibrosis. The resulting morphology differs by organ but follows the same impaired-outflow principle.

### Key determinants
Chronic passive congestion of the liver produces a nutmeg appearance. Centrilobular congestion and necrosis alternate with midzonal fatty change to create a dark-red and yellow mottled cut surface. Chronic pulmonary congestion produces haemosiderin-laden alveolar macrophages called heart-failure cells. Alveolar macrophages acquire haemosiderin after ingesting extravasated erythrocytes.

### Clinical significance
Mitral stenosis causes chronic pulmonary venous congestion, so the lung finding is a siderophage rather than an acute inflammatory infiltrate. Chronic systemic venous congestion affects the liver and produces the nutmeg pattern before late congestive fibrosis develops.

## summary
A thrombus is read from more than its colour. Formation during life produces attachment to the wall, a rough firm friable mass and, when flow is sufficient, alternating pale and red laminae. Those features separate a true thrombus from a post-mortem clot and allow further classification as red, mural or occlusive.
## sections
### Definition
A thrombus is a solid intravascular mass formed during life from circulating blood constituents. Its gross identity rests on attachment and texture: it is firmly adherent to the vessel wall, firm but friable, and rough rather than smooth.

A post-mortem clot is different. It forms in stagnant blood after death, is soft and moist, has a smooth surface and does not adhere to the wall. Its darker dependent and yellow upper layers result from settling; they are not Lines of Zahn.

### Mechanism
Thrombus formation begins where platelets adhere to damaged endothelium. Activated platelets aggregate, and fibrin then traps cellular blood elements. Repeated deposition in moving blood produces lamination: pale platelet-fibrin layers alternate with darker erythrocyte-rich layers. These are Lines of Zahn and they support an antemortem origin.

Slow venous flow favours erythrocyte-rich red thrombi. The mass may mould to the shape of the vein, commonly occludes it and may propagate towards the heart. Faster arterial flow favours paler platelet-fibrin-rich thrombi with more conspicuous lamination.

### Key determinants
Use a fixed sequence when reading a specimen. First ask whether it is attached. Next assess surface and consistency: rough, firm and friable favours thrombus; smooth, soft and moist favours post-mortem clot. Then look for organised lamination. Only after those steps should colour be used.

An occlusive thrombus completely blocks the lumen of a small artery. A mural thrombus is attached to the wall of a vessel or heart but does not completely close the lumen. Attachment alone therefore does not make a thrombus occlusive.

### Clinical significance
The morphology predicts behaviour. A venous red thrombus may extend towards the heart and embolise into the pulmonary circulation. An occlusive arterial thrombus can abruptly stop tissue perfusion. A post-mortem clot has neither consequence because it formed after circulation ceased.

### Common misconceptions
Colour is not the deciding feature. A thrombus may be pale, red or mixed, while a post-mortem clot can also appear dark red. The reliable discriminators are formation during life, attachment, roughness, firmness and organised lamination.

Lines of Zahn are not the red and yellow separation of settled blood. They are alternating platelet-fibrin and erythrocyte-rich laminae laid down in moving blood.

## summary
Fat embolism begins when fat globules enter disrupted veins and lodge in pulmonary or systemic vessels. Bone fracture is a major setting because marrow fat is released into damaged venous channels. The clinically important syndrome is recognised by progressive respiratory distress with tachycardia, petechiae and neurological deterioration after the injury.
## sections
### Definition
Fat embolism is the circulation and vascular impaction of fat globules. Fat can enter ruptured veins after injury to fat-containing tissue and then reach pulmonary or, less often, systemic vessels.

Fat embolism syndrome is the symptomatic clinical state rather than the mere microscopic presence of fat globules. It can be fatal and combines respiratory, cutaneous and neurological manifestations.

### Mechanism
Bone fracture disrupts marrow fat and nearby venous channels. Fat globules enter the blood and obstruct small vessels. The resulting injury is not purely mechanical: fatty-acid release can add chemical endothelial and tissue injury.

The same mechanism can follow other injuries to fat-containing tissue, including burns and acute pancreatitis, but fracture is the recurrent BMS-102 assessment setting.

### Key determinants
Think of fat embolism syndrome when respiratory distress develops progressively in the days after a major fracture, especially with tachycardia, a petechial rash or neurological deterioration. A single fat globule seen microscopically does not by itself establish the syndrome.

### Clinical significance
The immediate task is recognition of the mechanism and pattern, not invention of a treatment algorithm beyond the module's source boundary. Progressive hypoxaemia after fracture has several differentials; timing, petechiae and neurological change support fat embolism syndrome.

### Common misconceptions
Fat embolism and fat embolism syndrome are not synonyms. The first describes the vascular material and route; the second describes symptomatic organ injury.

Do not make a source-free timing threshold. The assigned lecture describes deterioration over the next few days and a fatal example at three days, so the safe module-level wording is progressive deterioration in the days after fracture.
