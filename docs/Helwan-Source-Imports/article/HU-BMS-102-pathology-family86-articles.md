## summary
Inflammation mechanisms in case-based pathology, restricted to the exact Family-86 case scopes.
## sections
### Definition
Acute inflammation is a rapid, short-lived response in which neutrophils and exudate predominate. Chronic and granulomatous patterns are organised around mononuclear cells, especially activated macrophages.

### Mechanism
Prostaglandins and bradykinin contribute to inflammatory pain. Macrophage products can also sustain fibrosis after silica exposure or cause widespread vasodilatation and hypotension when nitric oxide production is excessive. Immediate anaphylaxis reflects mast-cell mediator release, particularly histamine.

### Key determinants
Purulent material with abundant neutrophils indicates suppurative inflammation. Persistent Helicobacter pylori infection produces chronic gastritis with a mononuclear infiltrate, while mycobacteria drive macrophage-rich granulomatous inflammation.

### Clinical significance
Duration, dominant cell, tissue pattern and mediator are separate clues. Reading them together prevents a broad label such as “inflammation” from replacing the precise tested mechanism.

## summary
Cell death, reperfusion injury and infarction, restricted to the exact Family-86 case scopes.
## sections
### Definition
Apoptosis removes individual cells through regulated fragmentation without producing the gross tissue architecture of an infarct. Necrosis is a tissue-level response whose morphology depends strongly on organ context.

### Mechanism
Cytotoxic treatment may trigger apoptosis in tumour cells. Restoration of blood flow can increase reactive oxygen species and worsen injury, whereas infarction in most solid organs produces coagulative necrosis. Brain infarction is the important exception and undergoes liquefactive necrosis, often resolving as a cystic space.

### Key determinants
Single-cell fragmentation favours apoptosis. A wedge-shaped splenic lesion after embolisation favours coagulative necrosis; a remote cystic cerebral infarct favours liquefactive necrosis.

### Clinical significance
The same word “infarct” does not imply the same morphology in every organ. The tissue, mechanism and time course determine the answer.

## summary
Intracellular accumulations in clinical pathology cases, restricted to the exact Family-86 case scopes.
## sections
### Definition
Intracellular and tissue accumulations include lipofuscin, haemosiderin, cholesterol and triglyceride. Each points to a different mechanism and clinical setting.

### Mechanism
Lipofuscin accumulates with ageing and atrophy, producing the brown appearance of an ageing heart. Repeated transfusion delivers iron that is stored as haemosiderin, especially in the liver. Chronic inflammation and membrane breakdown can leave cholesterol crystals, while chronic alcohol exposure commonly produces triglyceride accumulation in hepatocytes.

### Key determinants
Match pigment or material to its setting: brown atrophy to lipofuscin, transfusional overload to hepatic haemosiderin, chronic middle-ear inflammation to cholesterol crystals, and early alcohol-related liver injury to fatty change.

### Clinical significance
Colour alone is not enough. The organ, exposure and time course separate substances that may all be described as deposits.

## summary
Clinical patterns of necrosis and gangrene, restricted to the exact Family-86 case scopes.
## sections
### Definition
Gangrene is a clinical pattern of extensive tissue necrosis, while fat necrosis is a morphologic process caused by lipase-mediated injury or trauma.

### Mechanism
Diabetes promotes peripheral atherosclerotic disease and distal ischaemia, which can culminate in dry gangrene. In acute pancreatitis, released lipase splits triglycerides; fatty acids bind calcium and form insoluble soaps in enzymatic fat necrosis.

### Key determinants
Cold insensate black toes with absent pulses indicate ischaemic gangrene. Acute pancreatitis with peripancreatic injury indicates enzymatic fat necrosis and saponification.

### Clinical significance
The gross setting and causal mechanism distinguish a named clinical pattern from a microscopic form of necrosis.
