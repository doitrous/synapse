## title
Site of action of Omeprazole is:

## question
Site of action of Omeprazole is:

## subject
gi

## status
Draft

## owner
Helwan Year-3 authoring lane

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Active H and Cl cotransport

## explanation_a
Active H and Cl cotransport is not the target of omeprazole; gastric acid secretion is driven by the H+/K+-ATPase, not by a cotransport mechanism of this kind.

## answer_b
Cl/HCO3 exchange

## explanation_b
Cl/HCO3 exchange describes a different transport process, not the parietal cell proton pump that omeprazole targets.

## answer_c
H/K ATPase

## explanation_c
The site of action of omeprazole is the H+/K+-ATPase (the "proton pump") of the gastric parietal cell, the final common enzymatic step of acid secretion into the gastric lumen. Omeprazole irreversibly inhibits this pump, which is why proton pump inhibitors achieve more complete and longer-lasting acid suppression than drugs acting earlier in the secretory pathway, such as H2-receptor blockers. Recognising the H+/K+-ATPase as omeprazole’s specific molecular target, rather than the Na/K pump or a cotransport or exchange mechanism, is the fact this question tests.

## answer_d
Na/K pump

## explanation_d
The Na/K pump is a different ion transporter, present in essentially all cells, and is not the specific parietal cell target that omeprazole inhibits.

## question
The following anti-emetic drug is useful in treatment of motion sickness:

## subject
gi

## status
Draft

## owner
Helwan Year-3 authoring lane

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Dimenhydrinate

## explanation_a
Dimenhydrinate, an H1-antihistamine with central anticholinergic activity, is the classic drug of choice for preventing and treating the nausea and vomiting of motion sickness, since it blocks the histaminergic and cholinergic transmission along the vestibular pathway that drives motion-triggered vomiting. This vestibular mechanism is different from the chemoreceptor-trigger-zone dopamine blockade used by antiemetics aimed at chemotherapy- or drug-induced vomiting, which is why dimenhydrinate specifically, rather than a dopamine antagonist, is favoured for motion sickness. Recognising this vestibular, antihistamine-based mechanism is the fact tested here.

## answer_b
Chlorpromazine

## explanation_b
Chlorpromazine, a dopamine D2 antagonist, targets the chemoreceptor trigger zone rather than the vestibular pathway that drives motion sickness, so it is not the preferred agent here.

## answer_c
Haloperidol

## explanation_c
Haloperidol, another dopamine D2 antagonist, similarly targets the chemoreceptor trigger zone rather than the vestibular mechanism of motion sickness.

## answer_d
Metoclopramide

## explanation_d
Metoclopramide’s D2-antagonist, prokinetic mechanism targets the chemoreceptor trigger zone and gut motility, not the vestibular pathway that drives motion sickness.

## explanation_a

## explanation_b

## title

## question
The following purgative interferes with absorption of fat-soluble vitamins:

## subject
gi

## status
Draft

## owner
Helwan Year-3 authoring lane

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Liquid paraffin

## explanation_a
Liquid paraffin, a lubricant/physical purgative, is not absorbed itself but coats the stool and, with chronic use, interferes with the absorption of the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K, which dissolve into and are lost with it rather than being absorbed across the gut mucosa. This risk is a well-recognised limitation of long-term liquid paraffin use, distinguishing it from the other purgative types listed, whose primary mechanisms (bulk formation, osmotic pull, or mucosal irritation) do not directly interfere with fat-soluble vitamin absorption in this way. Recognising liquid paraffin as the purgative with this specific malabsorption risk is the fact tested here.

## answer_b
Methyl cellulose

## explanation_b
Methyl cellulose, a bulk-forming agent, works by increasing stool bulk and does not carry the specific fat-soluble vitamin malabsorption risk of a lubricant purgative.

## answer_c
Magnesium sulphate

## explanation_c
Magnesium sulphate, an osmotic purgative, draws water into the lumen and does not interfere with fat-soluble vitamin absorption the way a lubricant purgative does.

## answer_d
Lactulose

## explanation_d
Lactulose, an osmotic purgative, works by drawing water into the colon and does not carry the fat-soluble vitamin malabsorption risk specific to liquid paraffin.

## question
All of the following drugs can be used in treatment of peptic ulcer, EXCEPT:

## subject

## status

## vignette

## correct_answer

## answer_a
Sucralfate

## explanation_a
Sucralfate, a mucosal protectant, genuinely is used in the treatment of peptic ulcer, so it is not the exception.

## answer_b
Pirenzepine

## explanation_b
Pirenzepine, a selective M1-anticholinergic that reduces acid secretion, genuinely is used in the treatment of peptic ulcer, so it is not the exception.

## answer_c
Bismuth salts

## explanation_c
Bismuth salts, mucosal protectants, genuinely are used in the treatment of peptic ulcer, so they are not the exception.

## answer_d
Prednisolone

## explanation_d
Prednisolone, a corticosteroid, is not used in the treatment of peptic ulcer; by suppressing prostaglandin synthesis and impairing mucosal defence, corticosteroids are instead a recognised risk factor for causing ulcers, the opposite role from sucralfate, pirenzepine or bismuth salts. This is exactly why prednisolone is the exception among these options, and the answer to this question. Recognising corticosteroids as an ulcer-causing, rather than ulcer-treating, drug class is the point being tested.

## question
Which one of the following drugs is NOT a laxative agent?

## subject

## status

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Methyl cellulose

## explanation_a
Methyl cellulose genuinely is a laxative agent (bulk-forming), so it is not the answer to this "NOT a laxative" question.

## answer_b
Diphenoxylate

## explanation_b
Diphenoxylate is an antidiarrhoeal opioid-derivative drug, not a laxative agent at all; it slows gut motility to treat diarrhoea, the opposite pharmacological direction from a laxative, which is exactly why it is the drug that is NOT a laxative among these options. Recognising diphenoxylate’s antidiarrhoeal, rather than laxative, classification is the fact this question tests. Confusing the two opposite-acting drug categories is exactly the error this question is designed to catch.

## answer_c
Liquid paraffin

## explanation_c
Liquid paraffin genuinely is a laxative agent (lubricant/physical), so it is not the answer to this "NOT a laxative" question.

## answer_d
Lactulose

## explanation_d
Lactulose genuinely is a laxative agent (osmotic), so it is not the answer to this "NOT a laxative" question.
