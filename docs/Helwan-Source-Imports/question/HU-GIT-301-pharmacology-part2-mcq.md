## title
Diarrhoea in children is best managed by:

## question
Diarrhoea in children is best managed by:

## subject
gi

## status
Draft

## owner
Helwan Year-3 authoring lane

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Anticholinergic drug

## explanation_a
An anticholinergic drug is not the best management for diarrhoea in children; it does not address the primary danger of dehydration and carries its own risks in young children.

## answer_b
Loperamide

## explanation_b
Loperamide is not the best management for diarrhoea in children; antimotility agents carry particular risks in young children and do not address the underlying fluid loss.

## answer_c
Pectin and kaolin

## explanation_c
Pectin and kaolin, adsorbent agents, are not the best management for diarrhoea in children, since they do not correct the fluid and electrolyte loss that poses the real danger.

## answer_d
Correction of fluid and electrolyte disturbance

## explanation_d
Diarrhoea in children is best managed by correction of fluid and electrolyte disturbance, typically through oral rehydration therapy, because the primary life-threatening risk in paediatric diarrhoea is dehydration itself rather than the diarrhoea as a symptom. This fluid-and-electrolyte-first approach is safer and more directly effective in children than reaching for an antimotility or adsorbent drug, which do not correct the underlying fluid loss and, in the case of antimotility agents, can carry particular risks in young children. Recognising fluid and electrolyte correction as the mainstay of paediatric diarrhoea management is the fact this question tests.

## question
Which of the following drugs is the best choice to prevent vomiting of motion sickness?

## subject
gi

## status

## owner
Helwan Year-3 authoring lane

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Domperidone

## explanation_a
Domperidone, a peripheral dopamine D2 antagonist, targets the chemoreceptor trigger zone and gut motility rather than the vestibular pathway that drives motion sickness.

## answer_b
Metoclopramide

## explanation_b
Metoclopramide’s D2-antagonist, prokinetic mechanism targets the chemoreceptor trigger zone and gut motility, not the vestibular pathway of motion sickness.

## answer_c
Dimenhydrinate

## explanation_c
Dimenhydrinate, an H1-antihistamine with central anticholinergic activity, is the best choice to prevent vomiting of motion sickness, since it blocks the histaminergic and cholinergic transmission along the vestibular pathway responsible for motion-triggered nausea and vomiting. This vestibular-blocking mechanism is what distinguishes it from the dopamine- or serotonin-receptor-targeting antiemetics among the other options, which act on different vomiting pathways more relevant to chemotherapy- or drug-induced emesis. Recognising dimenhydrinate as the preferred agent for this specific, vestibular trigger is the fact this question tests.

## answer_d
Ondansetron

## explanation_d
Ondansetron, a 5-HT3 receptor antagonist, is not the preferred choice for motion sickness; its established use is chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting, a different triggering pathway.

## title
A patient has severe GERD. In addition to a combination antacid product for symptomatic relief, which drug is most likely prescribed to fully suppress gastric acid secretion?

## question
A patient has severe GERD. In addition to a combination antacid product for symptomatic relief, which drug is most likely prescribed to fully suppress gastric acid secretion?

## subject
gi

## status
Draft

## owner
Helwan Year-3 authoring lane

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Atropine

## explanation_a
Atropine, an anticholinergic, is not used to fully suppress gastric acid secretion in GERD; its anticholinergic side effects and incomplete acid suppression make it unsuitable for this purpose.

## answer_b
Calcium carbonate

## explanation_b
Calcium carbonate is an antacid, providing symptomatic relief by direct neutralisation, but it does not fully suppress gastric acid secretion the way a proton pump inhibitor does.

## answer_c
Cimetidine

## explanation_c
Cimetidine, an H2-receptor blocker, reduces but does not fully suppress gastric acid secretion, since histamine is only one of several pathways stimulating acid production.

## answer_d
Omeprazole

## explanation_d
Omeprazole, a proton pump inhibitor, is most likely prescribed to fully suppress gastric acid secretion in a patient with severe GERD, since it irreversibly blocks the H+/K+-ATPase, the parietal cell’s final common secretory step, giving more complete acid suppression than an H2 blocker, an anticholinergic, or an antacid alone. This is why, alongside a combination antacid for immediate symptomatic relief, a proton pump inhibitor is added specifically to achieve full acid suppression in severe disease. Recognising omeprazole as the drug for this purpose is the fact this question tests.

A patient on theophylline, warfarin, quinidine and phenytoin starts a drug for GI distress and develops toxicity from all other medications with raised serum concentrations. Which drug did he most likely take?

## question
A patient on theophylline, warfarin, quinidine and phenytoin starts a drug for GI distress and develops toxicity from all other medications with raised serum concentrations. Which drug did he most likely take?

## subject
gi

## status
Draft

## owner
Helwan Year-3 authoring lane

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Cimetidine

## explanation_a
Cimetidine is a potent inhibitor of hepatic cytochrome P450 enzymes, which slows the metabolism of many drugs cleared through this pathway, including theophylline, warfarin, quinidine and phenytoin, raising their serum concentrations and precipitating toxicity from all of them at once when cimetidine is started. This broad, multi-drug interaction pattern is one of cimetidine’s most clinically important features, and it is far less prominent with the newer H2 blockers, which do not share cimetidine’s potent cytochrome P450-inhibiting effect. Recognising cimetidine as the drug behind this pattern of interactions is the fact this question tests.

## answer_b
Sucralfate

## explanation_b
Sucralfate, a mucosal protectant with minimal systemic absorption, does not produce this pattern of cytochrome P450-mediated drug interactions.

## answer_c
Famotidine

## explanation_c
Famotidine, unlike cimetidine, has little to no clinically significant cytochrome P450-inhibiting effect and does not produce this pattern of multi-drug interactions.

## answer_d
Nizatidine

## explanation_d
Nizatidine, unlike cimetidine, has little to no clinically significant cytochrome P450-inhibiting effect and does not produce this pattern of multi-drug interactions.

One patient needs suppression of emesis from an anticancer drug; another has diabetic gastroparesis and GERD needing relief. Which drug suits both indications?

## question
One patient needs suppression of emesis from an anticancer drug; another has diabetic gastroparesis and GERD needing relief. Which drug suits both indications?

## subject
gi

## status
Draft

## owner
Helwan Year-3 authoring lane

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Diphenoxylate

## explanation_a
Diphenoxylate is an antidiarrhoeal opioid derivative, with neither an antiemetic-for-chemotherapy nor a prokinetic-for-gastroparesis/GERD profile suited to both indications described.

## answer_b
Aprepitant

## explanation_b
Aprepitant, a neurokinin-1 (NK1) receptor antagonist, is used for chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting but has no prokinetic effect useful for diabetic gastroparesis or GERD relief.

## answer_c
Pyridoxine

## explanation_c
Pyridoxine (vitamin B6) is used in some nausea contexts, such as pregnancy-related nausea, but it is not the drug that combines antiemetic and prokinetic action for these two indications.

## explanation_d

This dual mechanism, uniting central antiemetic action with peripheral prokinetic action, is uncommon among GI drugs and is exactly why metoclopramide, rather than a pure antiemetic or a pure prokinetic-only agent, fits both clinical needs described here. No other option on this list combines both a genuine antiemetic and a genuine prokinetic effect the way metoclopramide does.

## question
A patient with hepatitis C presents with hepatic portosystemic encephalopathy. Which drug, in relatively high doses, is most suitable for relief of signs and symptoms?

## answer_b
Lansoprazole

## explanation_b

Lactulose

Recognising lactulose’s ammonia-trapping mechanism, and its high-dose use for this specific indication, is the fact this question tests.

## answer_d
Loperamide

## explanation_d
Loperamide, an antimotility antidiarrhoeal, has no role in relieving hepatic encephalopathy and could risk worsening constipation-related ammonia retention.

## title
A patient is prescribed an OTC antacid combining a magnesium salt and an aluminium salt. Why do most such products contain these two particular salts?

## question
A patient is prescribed an OTC antacid combining a magnesium salt and an aluminium salt. Why do most such products contain these two particular salts?

## subject
gi

## status
Draft

## owner
Helwan Year-3 authoring lane

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Al salts counteract the gastric mucosal-irritating effects of Mg salts

## explanation_a
Aluminium salts do not counteract a mucosal-irritating effect of magnesium salts; magnesium salts are not known for mucosal irritation, so this is not the reason for the combination.

## answer_b
Al salts require activation by an Mg-dependent enzyme to inhibit the parietal cell proton pump

## explanation_b
Aluminium salts do not require activation by a magnesium-dependent enzyme to inhibit the parietal cell proton pump; antacids act by direct chemical neutralisation of acid in the lumen, not by inhibiting the proton pump enzymatically.

## answer_c
Mg salts cause a diuresis that helps reduce systemic accumulation of the Al salt

## explanation_c
Magnesium salts do not cause a diuresis that reduces systemic aluminium accumulation; this is not the pharmacological basis for combining the two salts.

## answer_d
Mg salts tend to cause a laxative effect that counteracts the constipating tendency of the Al salt

## explanation_d
Magnesium salts tend to cause a laxative (diarrhoea-producing) effect through their osmotic action, while aluminium salts tend to cause constipation; most combination OTC antacids pair a magnesium salt with an aluminium salt specifically so that these two opposite bowel effects counteract one another, giving a more tolerable overall bowel effect than either salt alone. This balancing strategy is a deliberate formulation choice built on the well-known, opposite GI side-effect profiles of the two cation types. Recognising this bowel-effect-balancing rationale is the fact this question tests.

A patient with severe abdominal pain and a burning sensation in the upper abdomen has several benign gastric ulcers on endoscopy. Which drug gives the fastest, albeit briefest, relief with a single dose?

## question
A patient with severe abdominal pain and a burning sensation in the upper abdomen has several benign gastric ulcers on endoscopy. Which drug gives the fastest, albeit briefest, relief with a single dose?

## subject
gi

## status
Draft

## owner
Helwan Year-3 authoring lane

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Antacids

## explanation_a
Antacids give the fastest, though briefest, symptomatic relief of any anti-ulcer drug class, because they act by immediate direct chemical neutralisation of acid already present in the stomach, unlike the H2 blockers, proton pump inhibitors or prostaglandin analogues, which must first alter ongoing acid secretion and take longer to produce an effect. This immediate-but-short-lived pattern is why antacids are best suited to rapid symptomatic relief of a single episode of burning pain, as described here, rather than to sustained, longer-term acid suppression. Recognising this fast-onset, brief-duration profile is the fact this question tests.

Belladonna alkaloids

## explanation_b
Belladonna alkaloids (anticholinergics) do not provide the rapid, direct-neutralisation relief of an antacid, and their effect is neither the fastest nor specific to acid neutralisation.

## answer_c
Cimetidine

## explanation_c
Cimetidine, an H2 blocker, acts by reducing ongoing acid secretion rather than immediately neutralising acid already present, so it does not give the fastest single-dose relief.

## answer_d
Misoprostol

## explanation_d
Misoprostol, a prostaglandin analogue, acts by replacing protective prostaglandins over a sustained dosing course, not by providing the fastest, briefest single-dose relief.

## question

gi

## answer_a
Antibiotic-associated pseudomembranous colitis

## explanation_a
Antibiotic-associated pseudomembranous colitis is treated with metronidazole or vancomycin alone, not with the proton-pump-inhibitor-plus-two-antibiotic combination described here.

## answer_b
Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)

## explanation_b
Irritable bowel syndrome is not treated with a proton pump inhibitor plus metronidazole and clarithromycin; its management targets motility and symptom control, not acid suppression combined with H. pylori-active antibiotics.

## answer_c
Refractory or recurrent, severe gastric or duodenal ulcers secondary to H. pylori

## explanation_c
A proton pump inhibitor combined with metronidazole and clarithromycin is a recognised H. pylori eradication regimen, most likely being used here for refractory or recurrent, severe gastric or duodenal ulcers secondary to H. pylori infection. Combining acid suppression with two antibiotics active against H. pylori reflects the standard eradication strategy used when ulcer disease is severe or has not responded to simpler treatment, aiming to clear the underlying infection driving the ulcer. Recognising this combination as an H. pylori eradication regimen for refractory ulcer disease is the fact this question tests.
