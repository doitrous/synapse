## title
Case 1: 30-year-old man, upper abdominal and right hypochondrial pain, itching, weight loss, fever, hepatomegaly, jaundice, anaemia and eosinophilia, stool examination free of ova. What is the most probable parasitological cause?

## question
Case 1: 30-year-old man, upper abdominal and right hypochondrial pain, itching, weight loss, fever, hepatomegaly, jaundice, anaemia and eosinophilia, stool examination free of ova. What is the most probable parasitological cause?

## subject
gi

## status
Draft

## owner
Helwan Year-3 authoring lane

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Fasciola spp

## explanation_a
Fasciola spp. (Fasciola hepatica or Fasciola gigantica) is the correct answer: the combination of right-hypochondrial pain, pruritus, weight loss, fever, tender hepatomegaly, jaundice, anaemia, marked eosinophilia and an egg-negative stool is the classic acute hepatic (migratory) phase presentation of fascioliasis. The stool is negative because the immature flukes are still migrating through the liver parenchyma and have not yet reached the bile ducts to mature and lay eggs, and the eosinophilia reflects the intense tissue-migratory immune response typical of this stage. Recognising this acute-phase picture, including the egg-negative stool, as still consistent with fascioliasis is the point of this vignette.

## answer_b
Clonorchis

## explanation_b
Clonorchis sinensis produces a broadly similar hepatobiliary picture but is a Far Eastern liver fluke acquired from freshwater fish, not the organism this bank's vignette (set in an Egyptian clinical context, with the classic acute-phase eosinophilic hepatitis) is describing.

## answer_c
Hydatid cyst

## explanation_c
Hydatid cyst typically has a more indolent, often asymptomatic course without this degree of fever, jaundice and marked eosinophilia; its imaging shows a well-defined cystic lesion rather than the acute inflammatory picture described here.

## answer_d
Ascaris

## explanation_d
Ascaris lumbricoides causes intestinal or, in heavy infection, biliary obstruction, but does not typically produce this specific acute hepatic eosinophilic-hepatitis picture with jaundice and tender hepatomegaly.

## question
Case 1 (continued): as above. How to confirm your diagnosis in this case?

## subject
gi

## status
Draft

## owner
Helwan Year-3 authoring lane

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Direct stool examination

## explanation_a
Direct stool examination depends on the fluke already being a mature, egg-laying adult in the bile ducts, which is not yet true during the acute hepatic migratory phase this vignette describes, so it would be unreliable here.

## answer_b
Serological diagnosis to detect specific antibodies

## explanation_b
Serological diagnosis to detect specific anti-Fasciola antibodies is the correct confirmatory test in this scenario, because serology becomes positive during the pre-patent (migratory) phase, before the fluke matures and begins shedding eggs, unlike stool-based methods which require a patent, egg-laying infection. This is why serology specifically, not a stool-based technique, is the confirmatory step when a compatible clinical and eosinophilic picture is accompanied by a negative stool examination. Recognising serology's specific value in the pre-patent phase is the fact tested here.

## answer_c
Duodenal aspiration

## explanation_c
Duodenal aspiration, like direct stool examination, recovers eggs from a mature, bile-duct-resident adult fluke and is therefore similarly unreliable during the pre-patent acute hepatic phase.

## answer_d
All of the above

## explanation_d
Since direct stool examination and duodenal aspiration are both unreliable during the pre-patent phase, "all of the above" is incorrect; serology alone is the confirmatory test appropriate to this stage.

Case 2: 18-year-old student in Cairo, recurrent abdominal colic, vomiting, diarrhoea, passage of white/creamy rectangular fleshy pieces (2x1cm) in stool. What is the most probable parasitological cause?

## question
Case 2: 18-year-old student in Cairo, recurrent abdominal colic, vomiting, diarrhoea, passage of white/creamy rectangular fleshy pieces (2x1cm) in stool. What is the most probable parasitological cause?

## subject
gi

## status
Draft

## owner
Helwan Year-3 authoring lane

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Taenia saginata

## explanation_a
Taenia saginata is the correct answer: recurrent abdominal colic, vomiting, diarrhoea and the passage of white or creamy, rectangular, fleshy segments (about 2x1cm) describe the actively motile gravid proglottids characteristic of the beef tapeworm, which detach and crawl out singly. This active, singly-detached motility is a specific clinical clue that helps distinguish Taenia saginata from Taenia solium, whose proglottids are typically passed more passively and often in short chains. Recognising this description as Taenia saginata's proglottid is the point of the vignette.

## answer_b
Taenia solium

## explanation_b
Taenia solium proglottids are typically passed more passively, often in short chains, rather than crawling out singly and actively as described in this vignette.

## answer_c
Hymenolepis nana

## explanation_c
Hymenolepis nana produces microscopic proglottids that are not passed as macroscopically visible fleshy segments, so it does not match this description.

## answer_d
Hymenolepis diminuta

## explanation_d
Hymenolepis diminuta, like Hymenolepis nana, does not produce macroscopically visible, rectangular fleshy segments of the size described here.

## correct_answer

## answer_a
Egg

## explanation_a
The egg is a genuine diagnostic finding in Taenia saginata infection, shed in stool when a gravid proglottid ruptures, so this true statement is one component of the correct combined answer, not the exception.

## answer_b
Scolex in successful treatment

## explanation_b
Recovery of the scolex intact in stool, specifically after successful treatment, is a genuine diagnostic/confirmatory finding — it confirms the whole worm, not just a chain of segments, has been expelled, since a retained scolex would regenerate the worm. This is a true statement and one component of the correct combined answer, not the exception.

## answer_c
All of the above

## explanation_c
All of the above is the correct answer: both the egg and the post-treatment scolex are genuine diagnostic stool findings for Taenia saginata, so "all of the above" correctly combines them rather than naming either alone. The egg is shed whenever a gravid proglottid ruptures in the gut, giving a routine stool-diagnostic finding independent of treatment status, while the scolex is specifically a post-treatment confirmatory finding. Recognising that this question asks for the full set of diagnostic stages, not a single one, is the point of the "all of the above" answer here.

## answer_d
Gravid proglottid (segment) in stool

## explanation_d
Gravid proglottid (segment) recovery in stool is also a genuine diagnostic finding for Taenia saginata, alongside the egg and the post-treatment scolex — a real diagnostic stage this bank's printed 3-option item did not separately list, but consistent with "all of the above" remaining the correct combined answer once this option is added.

## question
Case 3: 20-year-old male student, fever, abdominal pain, frequent motions with bulky offensive stool with mucus and blood, tender colon, sigmoidoscopy shows ulcers with narrow opening and healthy mucosa between. What parasitic stage can be seen microscopically in the aspirate material obtained by sigmoidoscopy?

## subject
gi

## status

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Cyst of E. histolytica

## explanation_a
The cyst of Entamoeba histolytica is the resistant, transmissible stage found in formed stool, not the stage seen in aspirate material taken directly from an active colonic ulcer, where the invasive trophozoite is instead found.

## answer_b
Trophozoite of E. histolytica

## explanation_b
The trophozoite of Entamoeba histolytica is the correct answer: it is the actively invasive, tissue-destroying form of the parasite, and material aspirated directly from the edge of a flask-shaped colonic ulcer at sigmoidoscopy shows this motile stage. This matches the vignette's fever, bloody mucoid dysentery and ulcerated colon, all signs of active tissue invasion rather than a quiescent, non-invasive state. Recognising the trophozoite as the stage found specifically at an active tissue lesion, in contrast to the environmentally resistant cyst found in formed stool, is the point of this question.

## answer_c
Egg of Trichuris

## explanation_c
An egg of Trichuris trichiura would not be found in sigmoidoscopic aspirate from a colonic ulcer; Trichuris does not produce this ulcerative sigmoidoscopic picture, and its egg is a stool, not tissue-aspirate, finding.

## answer_d
Cyst of Balantidium coli

## explanation_d
A cyst of Balantidium coli is a stool finding of a different ciliate protozoan and is not the material recovered from an active amoebic colonic ulcer's aspirate.

## question

## answer_a
Cyst of E. histolytica

The cyst of Entamoeba histolytica is the correct answer: its resistant wall allows it to survive gastric acidity and environmental exposure during transmission between hosts, making it the infective stage ingested with contaminated food or water. Trophozoite of E. histolytica

## explanation_b
The trophozoite of Entamoeba histolytica is acid-labile and destroyed by gastric acidity, so it cannot survive transmission between hosts and is not the infective stage, despite being the invasive tissue-destroying form.

## answer_c
Egg of Trichuris

## explanation_c
An egg of Trichuris trichiura is a completely different organism's transmissible stage and is not relevant to Entamoeba histolytica's own infective stage.

## answer_d
Cyst of Balantidium coli

## explanation_d
A cyst of Balantidium coli is the infective stage of a different ciliate protozoan, not of Entamoeba histolytica.

Capillaria

## explanation_d
Capillaria philippinensis does have its own severe-diarrhoea-causing autoinfective cycle, but it is not the organism classically paired with Cryptosporidium in AIDS-associated opportunistic diarrhoea teaching.

## question

## answer_a
Amoebic liver abscess

## explanation_a
Amoebic liver abscess is the correct diagnosis: fever with rigors, right-hypochondrial pain radiating to the shoulder and worsened by coughing, tender hepatomegaly, a raised right hemidiaphragm with a dense hepatic shadow on X-ray, and polymorph (neutrophilic) leucocytosis together form its classic presentation, reflecting invasive Entamoeba histolytica reaching the liver via the portal circulation. The acute febrile course with rigors and neutrophilic leucocytosis specifically argues for this pyogenic-type inflammatory process rather than a more indolent hepatic mass. Recognising this triad as amoebic liver abscess is the point of the vignette.

## answer_b
Hydatid cyst

## explanation_b
Hydatid cyst typically has a more indolent course, often without fever, rigors or this degree of neutrophilic leucocytosis, and its imaging more often shows a well-defined cystic lesion rather than a raised hemidiaphragm with a dense abscess shadow.

## answer_c
Pyogenic liver abscess

## explanation_c
A pyogenic (bacterial) liver abscess can present similarly with fever and tender hepatomegaly, but the vignette's overall clinical context (no antecedent biliary or intra-abdominal bacterial source described) and this bank's emphasis on Entamoeba histolytica as the covered organism point to the amoebic, not pyogenic, cause.

## answer_d
Fascioliasis

## explanation_d
Fascioliasis causes a more gradually progressive hepatic picture with marked eosinophilia rather than the acute, rigors-and-neutrophilia-dominant presentation described in this vignette.
