## title
Paralysis causing loss of ring-finger MCP adduction

## question
A 16-year-old patient is unable to adduct the metacarpophalangeal joint of the ring finger. Which of the following muscles is most likely to be paralyzed?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Flexor digitorum profundus

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Flexor digitorum profundus flexes the interphalangeal joints of the fingers; it plays no role in metacarpophalangeal abduction or adduction.

## answer_b
Lumbrical

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The lumbricals flex the metacarpophalangeal joints and extend the interphalangeal joints via the extensor expansion, but they do not abduct or adduct the fingers.

## answer_c
Palmar interossei

## explanation_c
Correct. The three palmar interossei adduct the index, ring and little fingers toward the axis of the middle finger. Losing ring-finger adduction specifically points to the palmar interosseous supplying that finger, all of which — like every interosseous muscle — are supplied by the deep branch of the ulnar nerve. Dorsal interossei, by contrast, abduct the fingers away from the same axis, the opposite action.

## answer_d
Dorsal interossei

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The dorsal interossei abduct the fingers away from the axis of the middle finger — the opposite action to the adduction described, which the palmar interossei alone perform.

## title
The axis of the hand

## question
The axis of the hand is a line that passes through which of the following fingers?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Index

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The index finger abducts away from and adducts toward the reference axis; it does not define the axis itself.

## answer_b
Middle

## explanation_b
Correct. Finger abduction and adduction are described relative to a reference line through the middle finger. Fingers moving away from this line are abducting, and fingers moving toward it are adducting, so the middle finger is conventionally treated as having no abduction/adduction of its own. This is the same axis the palmar and dorsal interossei use to define their actions, and it is distinct from the foot's own reference digit, the second toe.

## answer_c
Ring

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The ring finger abducts away from and adducts toward the reference axis through the middle finger; it does not define the axis itself.

## answer_d
Little

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The little finger abducts away from and adducts toward the reference axis through the middle finger; it does not define the axis itself.

## title
Fifth extensor compartment mass

## question
A localized mass compresses the fifth compartment of the extensor retinaculum. Which of the following actions may result?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Weakness of flexion of the wrist joint

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The extensor retinaculum's compartments hold extensor, not flexor, tendons; a mass here has no effect on wrist flexion.

## answer_b
Loss of extension of the wrist joint

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Wrist extension is produced by extensor carpi radialis longus/brevis (compartment 2) and extensor carpi ulnaris (compartment 6), both outside the fifth compartment, so wrist extension itself is preserved.

## answer_c
Loss of extension of the joints of the little finger

## explanation_c
Correct. The fifth compartment of the extensor retinaculum holds extensor digiti minimi alone. Compressing it selectively entraps this one tendon, so the deficit is confined to loss of extension at the little finger's metacarpophalangeal and interphalangeal joints, with the other fingers (extended by extensor digitorum in compartment 4) unaffected.

## answer_d
Loss of extension of the joints of the index finger

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The index finger is extended by extensor digitorum and extensor indicis, both in the fourth compartment, not the fifth — a fifth-compartment mass spares the index finger.

## title
Muscle spared by posterior interosseous nerve injury

## question
A 38-year-old male sustains a deep penetrating stab wound to the back of the forearm that causes severe damage to the posterior interosseous nerve. Which of the following muscles is normally functioning?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Extensor pollicis brevis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Extensor pollicis brevis is part of the deep extensor group, supplied by the posterior interosseous nerve after it pierces supinator, so it is paralysed by this injury.

## answer_b
Extensor indicis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Extensor indicis is part of the deep extensor group, supplied by the posterior interosseous nerve, so it is paralysed by this injury.

## answer_c
Extensor carpi radialis longus

## explanation_c
Correct. At the lateral epicondyle the radial nerve divides into a superficial (sensory) branch and a deep branch that becomes the posterior interosseous nerve after piercing supinator. Extensor carpi radialis longus, along with brachioradialis and anconeus, is supplied by the radial nerve proper above this division, so it is spared by an isolated posterior interosseous nerve injury — which is also why a posterior interosseous lesion causes finger drop without wrist drop.

## answer_d
Extensor carpi radialis brevis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Extensor carpi radialis brevis, unlike extensor carpi radialis longus, is part of the deep extensor group supplied by the posterior interosseous nerve, so it is paralysed by this injury.

## title
Wrist deficit after medial epicondyle fracture

## question
A 17-year-old girl comes to the local hospital after a fracture of her medial epicondyle. Examination reveals severe injury to the nerve lying behind the fractured bone. Which of the following conditions at the wrist occurs secondary to this injured nerve?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Loss of extension

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The nerve behind the medial epicondyle is the ulnar nerve, which is not a wrist extensor; extension is entirely a radial-nerve function and is unaffected here.

## answer_b
Weakness of extension

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Wrist extension is unaffected by an ulnar nerve injury, since extension is produced by the radial-nerve-supplied extensor group.

## answer_c
Loss of flexion

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Flexion is not lost, because flexor carpi radialis (median nerve) continues to flex the wrist even when the ulnar-supplied flexors are paralysed.

## answer_d
Weakness of flexion

## explanation_d
Correct. The ulnar nerve grooves the back of the medial epicondyle, making it the nerve at risk in this fracture. Wrist flexion is produced jointly by flexor carpi radialis (median) and flexor carpi ulnaris (ulnar); an ulnar nerve injury removes flexor carpi ulnaris and the ulnar half of flexor digitorum profundus from the flexor group, but flexor carpi radialis and the radial half of flexor digitorum profundus are spared. The result is weakness, not loss, of wrist flexion.

## title
Forearm muscle with double nerve supply

## question
Which of the following muscles in the forearm has a double nerve supply?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Flexor carpi radialis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Flexor carpi radialis is supplied entirely by the median nerve, with no ulnar contribution.

## answer_b
Flexor carpi ulnaris

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Flexor carpi ulnaris is supplied entirely by the ulnar nerve, with no median contribution — it is one of only two forearm flexors the median nerve does not reach.

## answer_c
Flexor digitorum superficialis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Flexor digitorum superficialis is supplied entirely by the median nerve.

## answer_d
Flexor digitorum profundus

## explanation_d
Correct. The median nerve supplies every muscle of the forearm's anterior compartment except flexor carpi ulnaris and the medial (ulnar) half of flexor digitorum profundus, which the ulnar nerve takes instead. This split gives flexor digitorum profundus a genuine dual median/ulnar supply, the lateral half to the index and middle fingers from the median nerve (via the anterior interosseous branch) and the medial half to the ring and little fingers from the ulnar nerve.

## title
Artery injured with a scaphoid fracture

## question
A 20-year-old boy falls on his outstretched hand and subsequently fractures his scaphoid bone. The fracture was accompanied by injury of which of the following arteries?

## subject
msk

## status

## owner

## vignette

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The ulnar artery runs on the medial side of the wrist and does not cross the scaphoid or the anatomical snuff box.

## answer_b
Radial

## explanation_b
Correct. The anatomical snuff box is floored by the scaphoid and crossed by the radial artery as it winds around the lateral wrist toward the deep palmar arch. A scaphoid fracture from a fall on the outstretched hand places the radial artery, lying directly against the fractured bone at the snuff box floor, at risk of injury.

Princeps pollicis

Incorrect. Princeps pollicis is a distal branch of the radial artery within the hand, given off after the artery has already crossed the snuff box, not the vessel directly overlying the fracture site.

## answer_d
Deep palmar arch

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The deep palmar arch is the radial artery's own termination in the palm, distal to the snuff box, not the vessel directly at risk at the fracture site itself.

## title
Bony attachment of the mid-prone-position muscle

## question
A 51-year-old man presents with inability to put his forearm in the mid-prone position. Which of the following bony attachments is related to the affected muscle?

## subject
msk

## status

## vignette

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The styloid process of the ulna is a distal landmark unrelated to brachioradialis, the muscle that holds the forearm in mid-prone position.

## answer_b

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The ulnar tuberosity is the insertion of brachialis, an elbow flexor unrelated to establishing the mid-prone resting position of the forearm.

Radial tuberosity

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The radial tuberosity is the insertion of biceps brachii, a supinator, not the origin of brachioradialis.

## title
Structure spared in a severe wrist joint injury

## question
A 19-year-old boy falls from a motorcycle onto his wrist joint. On examination the physician reveals severe damage of his wrist joint. Which of the following structures is normal?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Distal end of radius

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The distal radius is the principal weight-bearing structure of the forearm at the wrist and is classically damaged in a severe fall-related wrist injury.

## answer_b
Distal end of ulna

## explanation_b
Correct. A fall onto the wrist loads the radial side of the joint first, since the distal radius carries the great majority of the axial load. A severe fall-related wrist injury pattern therefore classically damages the distal radius, the scaphoid, and the triangular fibrocartilage complex, while the distal end of the ulna — which does not directly articulate with the carpal row — is the structure most likely to remain structurally normal.

Scaphoid carpal bone

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The scaphoid, floored by the snuff box and loaded directly in a fall on the outstretched hand, is one of the structures classically damaged in this injury pattern.

## answer_d
Fibrocartilaginous disc

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The fibrocartilaginous disc (triangular fibrocartilage complex) separates the ulnar head from the carpus and is among the structures classically injured in a severe fall-related wrist injury.

Which of these characteristics distinguishes smooth muscle contractility?

## subject
msk

## status

## vignette

C

## answer_a
It is easily fatigued

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Smooth muscle is comparatively resistant to fatigue, able to sustain tension for prolonged periods at low energy cost, the opposite of being easily fatigued.

## answer_b
Has rapid onset of contraction and relaxation

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Smooth muscle has a slow, not rapid, onset of contraction and relaxation compared with skeletal muscle.

## answer_c
Its contraction is maintained longer than skeletal muscle

## explanation_c
Correct. Smooth muscle's defining contractile advantage is the ability to sustain tension for far longer than skeletal muscle can — the so-called latch state. This sustained contraction is achieved at low energy cost. It does not show the rapid fatigue that skeletal muscle shows under sustained tetanic contraction.

## answer_d
Its tension is maintained and continuously increases during stretching

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Smooth muscle actually shows stress relaxation on sustained stretch — its tension falls back toward baseline over time rather than continuously increasing.

Skeletal muscle changes with nerve denervation

## question
During nerve denervation, which of the following happens in the skeletal muscle?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Atrophy of muscle within 2-4 months

## explanation_a
Correct. Denervated skeletal muscle progressively wastes, passing through atrophy over the following weeks to months, in the range of about 2-4 months for substantial loss. This is followed by visible fasciculations from the dying nerve fibres. Later still, fine fibrillations appear, from the muscle's own denervation hypersensitivity to acetylcholine.

## answer_b
Loss of movement and increase in muscle tone

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Denervation causes loss of movement together with decreased, not increased, muscle tone (flaccidity), since the muscle no longer receives its normal nervous drive.

Appearance of muscle fibrillations visible to the naked eye

Incorrect. Fibrillations following denervation are fine, individual muscle-fiber contractions detectable electromyographically, not visible to the naked eye — visible naked-eye twitching corresponds to fasciculations of remaining motor units, a distinct phenomenon.

## answer_d
Compensatory increase in blood supply to the muscle

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Denervated muscle shows reduced, not increased, blood supply and metabolic activity as it atrophies.

## title
Cause of the staircase (treppe) phenomenon

## question
What is the cause of the stair-case (treppe) phenomenon?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Increasing intracellular K+

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Rising intracellular potassium is not the mechanism of treppe; it is more associated with fatigue and altered membrane excitability than with progressively increasing twitch tension.

## answer_b
Decreasing intracellular Na+

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Falling intracellular sodium is not the mechanism responsible for the progressive rise in twitch tension seen in treppe.

## answer_c
Decreased temperature of the muscle

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Warming, not cooling, a muscle favours the staircase phenomenon; decreased temperature slows the contractile and calcium-handling machinery rather than enhancing it.

## answer_d
Increased Ca2+ release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum

## explanation_d
Correct. With repeated identical low-frequency twitches, each stimulus releases calcium from the sarcoplasmic reticulum faster than the muscle's calcium pumps can fully re-sequester it. Residual cytosolic calcium therefore accumulates over the first several twitches. This progressively increases cross-bridge activation and tension until a new steady state is reached, producing the treppe (staircase) phenomenon.

## title
Differences between isotonic and isometric contraction

## question
Concerning the differences between isotonic and isometric contractions, which of the following best describes these differences?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Length of muscle is constant in isotonic contraction

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Muscle length is held fixed in isometric, not isotonic, contraction — isotonic contraction is defined by the muscle shortening at constant tension once its load is overcome.

## answer_b
Muscle tension is increased in isometric contraction

## explanation_b
Correct. In isometric contraction the muscle's length is held fixed while its tension rises, because the load is too great for the muscle to shorten. In isotonic contraction, by contrast, tension is held fixed (once developed tension exceeds the load) while the muscle shortens and length changes — the two contraction types are distinguished by which variable, length or tension, stays constant.

## answer_c
Duration of contraction is longer in isometric contraction

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The duration of contraction is not the defining difference between the two contraction types; both are defined by which mechanical variable (length or tension) is held constant.

## answer_d
Waste heat is greater in isotonic contraction

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Waste heat production is not the property this comparison is built around; the defining distinction is the length-versus-tension relationship, not heat output.
