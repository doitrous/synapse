## title
Direction of proton movement through ATP synthase

## question
What is the direction of the movement of protons through ATP synthase?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Matrix to the intermembrane space

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Protons move matrix to intermembrane space through Complexes I, III and IV, which pump them out — the opposite direction to the one this option names, and not through ATP synthase itself.

## answer_b
Matrix to the cytoplasm

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The cytoplasm is not a compartment of the mitochondrion; protons never leave the organelle to reach it, and this option confuses the intermembrane space with the cell's own cytoplasm.

## answer_c
Intermembrane space to the matrix

## explanation_c
Correct. The electron transport chain pumps protons out of the matrix into the intermembrane space, building an electrochemical gradient — the proton motive force. Protons can only return to the matrix through the F0 channel of ATP synthase, and it is this controlled return flow, from the intermembrane space back into the matrix, that drives the F1 head to synthesise ATP. Blocking this channel (oligomycin) stops ATP synthesis without stopping electron transport, which is how the two processes are shown to be chemiosmotically coupled rather than directly linked.

## answer_d
Intermembrane space to the cytoplasm

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The cytoplasm plays no part in this exchange; protons pumped into the intermembrane space return specifically to the matrix, not onward across the outer mitochondrial membrane into the cell.

## title
Site of cyanide inhibition in the electron transport chain

## question
Cyanide inhibits electron transport chain at which of the following?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Complex I

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase) is the target of rotenone, a different classic ETC poison, not cyanide.

## answer_b
Complex II

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Complex II (succinate dehydrogenase) is inhibited by malonate; it is also the one complex that does not pump protons, and is not cyanide's site of action.

## answer_c
Complex III

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Complex III (cytochrome bc1) is the site antimycin A blocks, a separate inhibitor from cyanide acting one step earlier in the chain.

## answer_d
Complex IV

## explanation_d
Correct. Cyanide binds the ferric iron of cytochrome a3 within cytochrome c oxidase, Complex IV, and blocks electron transfer to molecular oxygen at the chain's final step. With Complex IV inhibited, the whole chain backs up and oxidative phosphorylation stops even though oxygen is still delivered to the tissues in normal amounts — this histotoxic hypoxia is why cyanide poisoning kills rapidly, hitting the most oxidative-phosphorylation-dependent organs (brain, heart) first.

## title
ATP yield from complete oxidation of acetyl-CoA in the citric acid cycle

## question
How many ATP molecules are produced on complete oxidation of acetyl CoA in the citric acid cycle?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Three

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Three is far below the true yield; it ignores almost all of the electron-carrier output the cycle generates per turn.

## answer_b
Twelve

## explanation_b
Correct. One turn of the citric acid cycle, starting from acetyl-CoA, yields 3 NADH, 1 FADH2 and 1 GTP (equivalent to ATP) directly. Using the standard P:O ratios of 2.5 ATP per NADH and 1.5 ATP per FADH2 through oxidative phosphorylation, this comes to (3 × 2.5) + (1 × 1.5) + 1 = 7.5 + 1.5 + 1 = 10, which is conventionally rounded up to the commonly taught figure of 12 ATP using the older P:O ratios of 3 and 2 — the number examined here and in most undergraduate biochemistry teaching.

## answer_c
Six

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Six accounts for roughly half the true yield, undercounting the NADH and FADH2 generated across the cycle's four oxidative steps.

## answer_d
Fifteen

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Fifteen overstates the yield; no combination of the cycle's actual electron carriers and their P:O ratios reaches this figure for one acetyl-CoA.

## title
Heme-synthesis enzyme requiring pyridoxal phosphate as coenzyme

## question
Pyridoxal phosphate is necessary for the pathway of heme biosynthesis, which of the following enzymes requires pyridoxal phosphate as a coenzyme?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Amino levulinic acid synthase

## explanation_a
Correct. ALA synthase (delta-aminolevulinate synthase) catalyses the first, rate-limiting step of heme synthesis, condensing glycine and succinyl-CoA to form delta-aminolevulinic acid (ALA). This condensation requires pyridoxal phosphate — the active form of vitamin B6 — as an essential coenzyme, which is why vitamin B6 deficiency or isoniazid therapy (which depletes pyridoxal phosphate) can produce a sideroblastic-type anaemia by starving this first committed step of the pathway.

## answer_b
ALA dehydratase

## explanation_b
Incorrect. ALA dehydratase (porphobilinogen synthase) condenses two molecules of ALA into porphobilinogen using a zinc-dependent mechanism, not pyridoxal phosphate, and is the enzyme classically inhibited by lead.

## answer_c
Coproporphyrinogen oxidase

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Coproporphyrinogen oxidase, a mitochondrial enzyme later in the pathway, oxidatively decarboxylates coproporphyrinogen III without a pyridoxal phosphate requirement.

## answer_d
Ferrochelatase

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Ferrochelatase, the terminal enzyme of the pathway, inserts ferrous iron into protoporphyrin IX to form heme, again without pyridoxal phosphate as a cofactor.

## title
Subcellular localisation of urea cycle enzymes

## question
Where are enzymes of the urea cycle localized?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Only in the mitochondria of the liver

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Two of the five urea cycle enzymes (carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I and ornithine transcarbamylase) are mitochondrial, but the remaining three are cytoplasmic, so the cycle is not confined to the mitochondria alone.

## answer_b
Only in the cytoplasm of the liver

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The first two steps of the cycle — carbamoyl phosphate synthesis and citrulline formation — happen inside the mitochondrial matrix, so the pathway is not purely cytoplasmic either.

## answer_c
In the kidney mitochondria and cytoplasm

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The urea cycle is a liver-specific pathway; the kidney lacks a complete set of urea cycle enzymes (notably full carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I activity) and cannot synthesise urea de novo.

## answer_d
In the liver mitochondria and cytoplasm

## explanation_d
Correct. The urea cycle spans two compartments within the hepatocyte. Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I and ornithine transcarbamylase operate in the mitochondrial matrix, producing citrulline, which is then transported into the cytoplasm where argininosuccinate synthetase, argininosuccinase and arginase complete the cycle, regenerating ornithine and releasing urea. This mitochondria-to-cytoplasm shuttle is why the cycle is described as split across both compartments of the liver cell specifically, not any other organ.

Cause of acquired porphyria
