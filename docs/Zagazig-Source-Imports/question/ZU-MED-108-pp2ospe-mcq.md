# Item

QST-ZUMED108-PP2OSPE-PP2OSPE-Q01

## title
Key phrase for CPR chest compression quality

## question
The "key phrase" for chest compression in CPR is:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Push at a rate of 30 compressions per minute

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The compression rate taught is at least 100 per minute, not 30 per minute; 30 is instead the number of compressions given per cycle of 30 compressions to 2 rescue breaths in single-rescuer CPR, a different number for a different purpose.

## answer_b
Check the pulse after each compression.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The pulse is not checked between individual compressions during CPR; doing so would interrupt the compressions that generate circulation and delay restarting them.

## answer_c
Push hard and fast.

## explanation_c
Correct. "Push hard and fast" is the key phrase summarising the two targets that matter most for compression quality: adequate depth, roughly two inches (5cm) in an adult, and adequate rate, at least 100 per minute, with full chest recoil between compressions. Compressions that are too shallow or too slow fail to generate enough forward blood flow to perfuse the brain and heart during cardiac arrest. This mnemonic is taught precisely because it is easy to remember and apply correctly under the pressure of a real resuscitation.

## answer_d
Push for at least 5 inches

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Five inches is far deeper than the roughly two-inch (5cm) depth taught for adult chest compressions; a compression this deep would risk rib or sternal fracture without added benefit.

Incorrect. "The heart" is not a peripheral pulse-check site at all; a pulse is felt at an artery, not by attempting to feel the heart directly through the chest wall.

## title
Signs of severe upper airway obstruction in an adult

## question
Adult patient with severe upper airway obstruction may present by all of the following EXCEPT:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Unable to breath or speak/vocalize.

## explanation_a
Incorrect (this statement is true, so it is not the answer to an EXCEPT question). Inability to breathe or speak is the classic presentation of severe, near-complete or complete upper airway obstruction, since air can no longer move past the obstruction to produce speech or adequate ventilation.

## answer_b
Cyanosis and diminishing conscious level.

## explanation_b
Incorrect (this statement is true). If severe obstruction is not relieved, hypoxia develops, producing cyanosis and a falling conscious level as recognised, worsening signs of ongoing obstruction.

## answer_c
Hypotension and vomiting

## explanation_c
Correct. Hypotension and vomiting are not recognised signs of severe upper airway obstruction; they belong instead to other conditions such as shock or a gastrointestinal problem. The recognised signs of choking are difficulty breathing or speaking, coughing, the universal choking sign of clutching the throat, cyanosis and falling consciousness if the obstruction persists, none of which is hypotension or vomiting, which is what makes this statement false and therefore the answer to this EXCEPT question.

## answer_d
Wheezy breath sounds

## explanation_d
Incorrect (this statement is true). Wheezy breath sounds can occur with partial upper airway obstruction as air is forced past a narrowed passage, so this is a recognised finding, not the false statement being asked for.

## title
First step in adult choking management

## question
In management of severe upper airway obstruction (choking) in adult patient begin with:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Abdominal thrusts are the second step, attempted only if five back blows fail to clear the obstruction, not the first step.

## answer_b
Five sharp back thrusts

## explanation_b
Correct. Management of severe choking in an adult begins with five sharp back blows between the shoulder blades; only if these fail does the rescuer move on to five abdominal thrusts, alternating the two in cycles of five if the obstruction persists. This back-blows-first sequence is what this paper's own course teaches, and reflects the same principle followed by several international resuscitation councils.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. CPR is started only if the choking patient becomes unresponsive, not as the first step while the patient is still conscious and obstructed.

## answer_d
AED

## explanation_d
Incorrect. An AED is used for cardiac arrest, not as an initial step in managing a conscious choking patient.

Correct hand position for a choking abdominal thrust

## question
When performing a choking abdominal thrust, which is the correct area to place your hand fist?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Placing the fist over the sternum describes chest-compression hand position for CPR, not the abdominal-thrust technique for choking.

## answer_b
On the back of the chest

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The back of the chest is the site for back blows, a different choking technique, not for an abdominal thrust.

## explanation_c
