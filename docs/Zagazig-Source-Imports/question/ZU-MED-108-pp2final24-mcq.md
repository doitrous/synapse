## title
Manoeuvre contraindicated in cervical spine injury

## question
A rescuer opens an unconscious patient's airway using the manoeuvre shown, lifting the angle of the jaw so it moves upward and forward. As regard to this manoeuvre, all the following statements are true EXCEPT:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
It is a jaw thrust manoeuvre.

## explanation_a
Incorrect (this statement is true, so it is not the answer to an EXCEPT question). The manoeuvre pictured, lifting the mandible without extending the neck, is exactly the jaw thrust.

## answer_b
It is considered a basic airway manoeuvre.

## explanation_b
Incorrect (this statement is true). Jaw thrust, alongside head tilt-chin lift, is one of the two basic, non-instrumental airway-opening manoeuvres taught for relieving obstruction caused by the tongue.

## answer_c
It is contraindicated in suspected cervical spine injury.

## explanation_c
Correct. Jaw thrust is in fact the preferred, not the contraindicated, basic airway manoeuvre when cervical spine injury is suspected, precisely because it opens the airway by lifting the jaw rather than by extending or rotating the neck. Head tilt-chin lift, which does require neck extension, is the manoeuvre avoided in this situation instead. This makes the statement in option C false, which is what an EXCEPT question is asking for.

## answer_d
During the manoeuvre, the jaw is moved upward and forward.

## explanation_d
Incorrect (this statement is true). The jaw thrust does move the mandible, and with it the tongue base attached to it, upward and forward, which is the mechanism by which it clears the airway.

## title
Definition of fainting (syncope)

## question
About fainting, which statement is true?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Loss of consciousness when blood supply to the brain is momentarily interrupted.

## explanation_a
Correct. Fainting (syncope) is a transient loss of consciousness caused by a momentary, global interruption of cerebral blood flow, with rapid onset, short duration and complete spontaneous recovery once perfusion is restored. This distinguishes it from a seizure or hypoglycaemic episode, which interrupt consciousness by a different mechanism, and from cardiac arrest, from which recovery is not spontaneous. Because the underlying problem is a brief circulatory interruption, not cardiac or respiratory arrest, management (lying the patient flat, raising the legs) is aimed at restoring cerebral perfusion.

## answer_b
The victim can respond to shake and shout.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. By definition, a fainting patient is unconscious during the episode and so cannot respond to shake and shout; responsiveness to shake and shout would mean the patient had already recovered or was never truly unconscious.

## answer_c
You can put the victim in the sitting position.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The correct first-aid position for a fainting (or about-to-faint) patient is lying flat with the legs raised, to restore blood flow to the brain, not sitting, which does not improve cerebral perfusion in the same way.

## answer_d
There is loss of consciousness, no pulse and no breathing.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. No pulse and no breathing describes cardiac arrest, not fainting; a fainting patient retains a pulse and breathing throughout the brief episode, which is why recovery is spontaneous.

## title
Signs of airway obstruction

## question
Signs of airway obstruction include:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Gurgling.

## explanation_a
Incorrect alone. Gurgling is a recognised sign of airway obstruction, but it is only one of several such signs, so it is not the single best answer when a more complete option is available.

## answer_b
Stridor.

## explanation_b
Incorrect alone. Stridor, a harsh high-pitched inspiratory sound suggesting laryngeal or upper-tracheal narrowing, is a recognised sign of obstruction, but again only one among several.

## answer_c
Chest wheezes.

## explanation_c
Incorrect alone. Chest wheezes, an expiratory sound suggesting lower-airway narrowing, are a recognised sign of obstruction, but not the only one listed.

## answer_d
Snoring.

## explanation_d
Incorrect alone. Snoring, suggesting partial obstruction by the tongue or soft palate, is a recognised sign of obstruction, but not the only one listed.

## answer_e
All of the above.

## explanation_e
Correct. Gurgling, stridor, chest wheezes and snoring are all recognised signs of airway obstruction, each pointing to a different level or cause (fluid, laryngeal/tracheal narrowing, lower-airway narrowing, and tongue/soft-palate obstruction respectively). Recognising the full range of obstruction sounds, not just one, is what lets a rescuer identify obstruction regardless of its cause.

## title
Checking responsiveness in the BLS sequence

## question
To check for a patient's response during the BLS (Basic Life Support) sequence, you can do:

## subject
fnd

## status

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Head tilt-chin lift is an airway-opening manoeuvre performed after unresponsiveness has already been confirmed, not the step used to check responsiveness itself.

## answer_b

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Look, listen and feel is the method used to assess breathing once the airway has been opened, a later step in the sequence than checking responsiveness.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Jaw thrust is an airway-opening manoeuvre, used after unresponsiveness is confirmed and especially when cervical spine injury is suspected, not the responsiveness check itself.

## title
Contraindication of the airway device shown (nasopharyngeal airway)

## question
The airway device shown in the figure above is contraindicated in:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Semiconscious patient.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A semiconscious patient with an intact gag reflex is in fact a typical indication for the nasopharyngeal airway, since it is better tolerated than an oral device in this situation, not a contraindication.

## answer_b
Fracture base of the skull.

## explanation_b
Correct. The nasopharyngeal airway is contraindicated when a fracture of the base of the skull is suspected — suggested clinically by CSF rhinorrhoea/otorrhoea, panda-eye bruising or Battle's sign — because the tube, passed through the nose, can travel through a fracture defect in the skull base and enter the cranial cavity. This is a serious, specific risk unique to the nasal route, which is why it does not apply to the oropharyngeal airway.

## answer_c
Trismus.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Trismus (inability to open the mouth) is actually a reason to prefer the nasopharyngeal airway over the oropharyngeal airway, which requires mouth opening for insertion, not a contraindication to the nasal device.

## answer_d
Clenched jaw.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Similarly, a clenched jaw prevents oral airway insertion but does not prevent the nasal route, so it is not a contraindication to the nasopharyngeal airway.

## answer_e
Deeply comatose patient.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A deeply comatose patient without a gag reflex is generally managed with an oropharyngeal airway, but this does not make the nasopharyngeal airway contraindicated by itself; the specific, named contraindication tested here is skull base fracture.

Sizing the airway device shown (nasopharyngeal airway)

## question
How is the suitable size of the airway device in the figure above chosen?

## subject
fnd

## status

A

## answer_a
Diameter of the patient's little finger.

## explanation_a
Correct. The nasopharyngeal airway's diameter is estimated at the bedside by matching it to the diameter of the patient's own little finger, which approximates the internal calibre of the nasal passage. A tube too wide for this estimate risks nasal trauma or failure to pass through the nostril, while one too narrow will not adequately bypass the obstruction it is meant to relieve. Size of the patient's anterior nares.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The size of the anterior nares is not the standard sizing landmark taught for the nasopharyngeal airway, which uses the little finger for diameter instead.

## answer_d
Distance between the patient's incisor and the angle of the eye.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This distractor landmark is not the recognised sizing method for either standard airway adjunct.

## title
Sizing an oropharyngeal airway

## question
How is the suitable size of an oropharyngeal airway chosen for any patient?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Diameter of the patient's middle finger.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Finger diameter is the sizing landmark used for the nasopharyngeal airway (little finger), not for the oropharyngeal airway, which is sized by a facial length measurement instead.

## answer_b
Distance between the patient's incisor and the angle of the eye.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Incisor-to-eye distance is not the recognised sizing landmark for the oropharyngeal airway.

## answer_c
Size of the patient's anterior nares.

An alternative landmark sometimes taught uses the incisors to the angle of the jaw, but the mouth-angle-to-ear-lobule measurement is the one this paper's marked key selects.

## title
Indication for abdominal thrusts

## question
The abdominal-thrust (Heimlich) manoeuvre is indicated in:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Fully conscious, effectively coughing choking patients.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A patient who is still coughing effectively has only a partial obstruction and should be encouraged to keep coughing, since effective coughing generates higher airway pressures than any manual manoeuvre can; abdominal thrusts are not indicated here.

## answer_b
Fully conscious choking patients who cannot cough or speak.

## explanation_b
Correct. Once a conscious choking patient can no longer cough, speak or breathe effectively, the obstruction has become complete and must be cleared mechanically rather than left to resolve on its own. Abdominal thrusts (the Heimlich manoeuvre) are delivered from behind, with an inward-and-upward thrust below the xiphoid, and are indicated specifically in this situation. They are alternated with back blows in current first-aid teaching until the obstruction clears or the patient becomes unresponsive, at which point CPR is started instead.

## answer_c
Collapsed cyanosed choking patients.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A collapsed, cyanosed patient has progressed beyond simple choking management; in an unresponsive patient the correct response is to start CPR, not to continue with abdominal thrusts.

## answer_d
All of the above.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Since abdominal thrusts are not indicated for an effectively coughing patient or for a collapsed unresponsive patient, "all of the above" is false.

## answer_e
None of the above.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Abdominal thrusts genuinely are indicated in the conscious, cannot-cough-or-speak patient described in option B, so "none of the above" is false.

## title
First step for laboured, noisy breathing in an unconscious patient

## question
When managing an unconscious patient who is making laboured and noisy respirations, the physician must first:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Commence positive pressure ventilation with a bag-mask device immediately.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Starting bag-mask ventilation before opening the airway is ineffective, since laboured, noisy breathing is most often caused by a simple, reversible mechanical obstruction (the tongue) that a basic manoeuvre resolves first.

## answer_b
Immediately perform endotracheal intubation.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Immediate endotracheal intubation is a more invasive step than is needed as a first response, and skips the simple manoeuvre that resolves most tongue-related obstruction.

## answer_c
Perform a "finger sweep" to clear the oropharynx of foreign material.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A blind finger sweep risks pushing an unseen foreign body further into the airway and is reserved for a directly visualised object, not used as the first step for laboured breathing in general.

## answer_d
Perform a simple manoeuvre such as a head tilt-chin lift to open the airway.

## explanation_d
Correct. The correct first action is a simple airway-opening manoeuvre — head tilt-chin lift, or jaw thrust if cervical spine injury is suspected. Laboured, noisy respiration in an unconscious patient is most often caused by the tongue partially obstructing the pharynx, a problem this simple step usually resolves. Only if breathing remains inadequate after this simple step is more invasive intervention, such as ventilation or intubation, considered.

## title
Complications of intramuscular injection

## question
Complications of intramuscular injection include:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Muscle fibrosis and contracture.

## explanation_a
Incorrect alone. Muscle fibrosis and contracture, from repeated injection into the same site, is a genuine complication of intramuscular injection, but not the only one listed.

## answer_b
Abscess at the injection site.

## explanation_b
Incorrect alone. Abscess at the injection site, from local infection or an irritant drug, is a genuine complication, but not the only one listed.

## answer_c
Nerve injury.

## explanation_c
Incorrect alone. Nerve injury (classically the sciatic nerve in a misplaced gluteal injection, or the femoral nerve in a misplaced vastus lateralis injection) is a genuine complication, but not the only one listed.

## answer_d
All of the above.

## explanation_d
Correct. Intramuscular injection carries all three of these recognised local complications: muscle fibrosis and contracture from repeated injection into the same site, abscess formation from local infection or an irritant drug, and nerve injury. The nerve at risk is classically the sciatic nerve if a gluteal injection is placed too medially or inferiorly, or the femoral nerve with a misplaced vastus lateralis injection. This is why correct site selection, such as the ventrogluteal or vastus lateralis site, and rotating injection sites are taught alongside the technique itself.

## title
Location of the brachial pulse

## question
Which pulsation is felt at the medial side of the antecubital fossa, just medial to the tendinous insertion of the biceps?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Radial pulse.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The radial pulse is felt at the lateral wrist, not at the antecubital fossa.

## answer_b
Carotid pulse.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The carotid pulse is felt in the neck, lateral to the larynx, not at the antecubital fossa.

## answer_c
Brachial pulse.

## explanation_c
Correct. The brachial artery runs down the medial arm and crosses the antecubital fossa before dividing into the radial and ulnar arteries. At the fossa it lies just medial to the biceps brachii tendon, which is why the tendon is used as the landmark to find the pulse. This same location is why the brachial artery, not the radial, is auscultated with a stethoscope during blood pressure measurement.

## answer_d
Femoral pulse.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The femoral pulse is felt in the groin, at the midpoint of the inguinal ligament, not at the antecubital fossa.

## title
Fastest injection route

## question
The fastest way to inject a medication is:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Intravenous.

## explanation_a
Correct. Intravenous injection delivers medication directly into the bloodstream, bypassing tissue absorption entirely. This gives it complete bioavailability and the fastest possible onset of action among the standard injection routes. It is chosen whenever the fastest possible drug effect is required, such as in emergencies, precisely because no absorption step delays the drug reaching the circulation.

## answer_b
Intramuscular.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Intramuscular injection is faster than subcutaneous or intradermal injection but still requires absorption from muscle tissue into the bloodstream, which is inherently slower than direct intravenous delivery.

## answer_c
Subcutaneous.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Subcutaneous injection is absorbed even more slowly than intramuscular injection, since fat is less vascular than muscle, making it slower still than the intravenous route.

## answer_d
Intradermal.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Intradermal injection is absorbed the most slowly of the standard routes, since the dermis is the least vascular of the tissue layers used for injection, so it is the furthest from the fastest, intravenous, route.
