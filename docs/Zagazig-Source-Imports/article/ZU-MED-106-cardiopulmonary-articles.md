## summary
This chapter works through six surgical-anatomy facts of the upper airway and neck tested by name in the Cardiopulmonary final: which vessel to ligate for inferior thyroid bleeding, which sinus the sphenoethmoidal recess drains, why an inhaled foreign body favours the right main bronchus, what thoracic duct injury is called, which named recess belongs to the laryngopharynx, and which artery dominates bleeding from Little's area of the nasal septum. Each fact turns on naming the right structure among several anatomically related distractors, not on a broader mechanism.

## sections
### Neck vasculature and thyroid surgery
The inferior thyroid artery, the vessel most commonly torn during thyroidectomy or radical neck dissection, arises from the thyrocervical trunk, a short branch of the first part of the subclavian artery. Because the inferior thyroid artery is short and retracts into the deep neck once torn, proximal control of profuse thyroid-region bleeding is achieved by ligating the thyrocervical trunk itself, not by chasing the torn vessel. The thyrocervical trunk also gives the transverse cervical and suprascapular arteries, and must not be confused with the costocervical trunk — a separate, more posteriorly-directed subclavian branch supplying the superior intercostal and deep cervical territory, with no role in thyroid bleeding.

### Nose and paranasal sinuses
The nasal cavity roof carries the sphenoethmoidal recess, a narrow space above the superior concha into which the sphenoid sinus alone drains — distinct from the middle meatus (frontal sinus, anterior and middle ethmoidal cells, maxillary sinus) and the superior meatus (posterior ethmoidal cells only). Mucosal swelling confined to the sphenoethmoidal recess therefore obstructs the sphenoid sinus specifically. Anteriorly, the nasal septum carries Little's area (Kiesselbach's plexus), an anastomotic network of the sphenopalatine, anterior ethmoidal, greater palatine and superior labial arteries; the sphenopalatine artery, a terminal branch of the maxillary artery, is the dominant contributor and the leading source of anterior epistaxis from this site.

### Pharynx, larynx and lower airway
The pharynx's three parts each carry a distinctive named feature: the nasopharynx has the adenoid (pharyngeal tonsil) and tubal tonsil, the oropharynx has the palatine tonsils, and the laryngopharynx (hypopharynx) has the piriform recess — a mucosal gutter beside the laryngeal inlet and the most common lodging site for a swallowed foreign body. Below the larynx, the trachea's bifurcation is asymmetric: the right main bronchus is wider, shorter and more vertical (a straighter continuation of the tracheal axis) than the left, which is longer and more oblique because it passes beneath the aortic arch — the anatomical reason an inhaled foreign body, and a misplaced endotracheal tube, both favour the right side. In the thorax, the thoracic duct runs alongside the oesophagus and aorta before draining into the left venous angle; traumatic injury to it lets chyle (fat-rich lymph) collect in the pleural cavity, a condition named chylothorax — distinct from pleurisy (pleural inflammation, not a fluid type), lymphedema (tissue, not cavity, accumulation) and pyothorax/empyema (pus, from infection).

## summary
This chapter covers six cardiovascular-physiology facts from the Cardiopulmonary final: what carotid buffer nerve stimulation does to pressure and heart rate, the mechanism that defines neurogenic shock, which heart sound marks isometric relaxation, how plasma volume is restored in the first day after haemorrhage, what angiotensin II does to thirst, and where the venous hydrostatic indifferent point normally sits. Each question turns on naming the correct mechanism among plausible, closely related physiological distractors.

## sections
### Baroreceptor reflex and shock mechanisms
The carotid buffer (baroreceptor) nerve carries afferent signals from carotid sinus stretch receptors to the medulla; rising pressure increases its firing, and the medulla responds by inhibiting sympathetic vasomotor and cardio-stimulatory outflow while raising vagal tone — producing reflex hypotension and bradycardia, never excitatory signalling to those centres. Neurogenic shock follows sudden loss of this same sympathetic vasoconstrictor tone — typically from high spinal cord injury or deep anaesthesia — leaving skeletal-muscle and other peripheral vascular beds unopposed and widely dilated, pooling blood peripherally and dropping venous return; this loss-of-tone mechanism is the opposite of the increased sympathetic activity and vasoconstriction the body mounts in hypovolaemic shock, and distinct from septic shock's mechanism (vasomotor centre stimulated by bacterial endotoxins).

### Cardiac cycle
Isometric (isovolumetric) relaxation is the phase immediately after ventricular ejection, when the semilunar valves close while the AV valves remain shut and ventricular pressure falls at constant volume; the second heart sound (S2), generated by semilunar valve closure, marks the very start of this phase — distinct from the first heart sound (S1, AV valve closure at the start of isovolumetric contraction), the third (rapid filling) and fourth (atrial contraction).

### Response to haemorrhage and blood pressure regulation
In the first 24 to 48 hours after acute haemorrhage, falling capillary hydrostatic pressure shifts the Starling balance so that interstitial fluid moves back into the vascular space — the capillary shift mechanism, and the dominant, fastest contributor to plasma volume restoration, well ahead of the slower renal/endocrine contributions of angiotensin II and ADH. Angiotensin II itself, generated by the renin-angiotensin system as pressure falls, raises pressure through several coordinated, uniformly pressure-raising actions: direct arteriolar vasoconstriction (never a vasodilator effect), facilitation of noradrenaline release from postganglionic sympathetic neurons, stimulation (never inhibition) of ADH secretion, and stimulation of the hypothalamic thirst centre, increasing water intake. Separately, the hydrostatic indifferent point — the level in the venous system where pressure does not change with posture — sits normally about 5 to 7 cm below the diaphragm; it is not a zero-pressure point, and its level can shift, moving downward when leg-vein capacity falls or during swimming.

## summary
This chapter covers six respiratory-physiology facts from the Cardiopulmonary final: what the conducting zone is called, which muscle drives quiet inspiration, which muscles drive forced expiration, what keeps pulmonary edema from developing, how hypoxia is defined, and which mediator relaxes bronchial smooth muscle. Together they trace one breath from the airway that carries no gas exchange, through the muscles that move it, to the alveolar surface where oxygen actually crosses into blood.

## sections
### Airway zones and dead space
The airway divides into a conducting zone (trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, terminal bronchioles) that carries no alveoli and does no gas exchange, and a respiratory zone where gas exchange occurs. Air occupying the conducting zone at end-inspiration takes no part in gas exchange; this volume, normally about 150 ml, is what the conducting zone is called — the anatomical dead space — distinct from the roughly 500 ml of a typical tidal breath, of which dead space is only a fraction, and distinct from the respiratory zone's alveoli, which alone carry the type II pneumocytes that secrete surfactant.

### Muscles of ventilation
Quiet (resting) inspiration is driven chiefly by the diaphragm, which contracts and descends to increase thoracic volume mainly in its vertical dimension; the external intercostals make a smaller accessory contribution, while the sternomastoid, scalene and abdominal muscles are recruited only during forced breathing. Quiet expiration is passive, needing no muscular contraction at all — elastic recoil alone does the work. Forced (active) expiration, by contrast, recruits the internal intercostal muscles (pulling the ribs down and in) together with the abdominal muscles (pushing the diaphragm upward), not the external intercostals or the diaphragm, which remain inspiratory muscles throughout.

### Surfactant, edema and hypoxia
The fluid film lining each alveolus generates surface tension that, left unchecked, would collapse the alveolus and draw fluid in from the capillaries; surfactant from type II pneumocytes lowers this surface tension, and it is specifically that reduction — not mucous secretion, macrophage activity or ciliary clearance, which serve other functions — that keeps pulmonary edema from developing under normal conditions. Bronchial smooth muscle tone is likewise a balance of opposing signals: vasoactive intestinal peptide (V.I.P.) relaxes it and lowers airway resistance, the opposite effect to histamine, leukotrienes and irritant stimuli, all of which constrict it. Finally, hypoxia itself is defined as diminished oxygen supply at the tissue level, regardless of mechanism — distinct from dyspnea (the sensation of laboured breathing), awareness of breathing, and cyanosis, which is only a possible accompanying sign, not the definition (it can be absent even in significant hypoxia, as in severe anaemia).

## hold_these
The conducting zone is the anatomical dead space (~150 ml), distinct from tidal volume (~500 ml) and from the alveoli-bearing respiratory zone.
The diaphragm is the chief muscle of quiet inspiration; accessory muscles act only in forced breathing.

Grouping V.I.P. with the bronchoconstrictor mediators.

## summary
This chapter covers six biochemistry facts from the Cardiopulmonary final's lipid block: what the LDL receptor recognises, the committed step of cholesterol biosynthesis, what apolipoprotein CII activates, where the NADPH for fatty acid synthesis mainly comes from, a further true statement about de novo fatty acid synthesis, and which enzyme deficiency causes Tay-Sachs disease. Each question turns on naming one specific molecule or step correctly among adjacent, easily-confused distractors from the same pathway.

## sections
### Lipoprotein receptors and activators
The LDL receptor recognises lipoproteins carrying apolipoprotein B100 or apolipoprotein E on their surface — LDL particles (apo B100) and remnant/IDL particles (apo E) — mediating their receptor-mediated endocytosis; it does not bind apo A (the major HDL apolipoprotein), apo D, or apo B48 alone (the intestinal chylomicron form of apo B, cleared instead via apo E on chylomicron remnants). Apolipoprotein CII, carried on chylomicrons and VLDL, is the obligatory activator of lipoprotein lipase, the endothelial enzyme that hydrolyses these particles' triglyceride core to release free fatty acids — distinct from apo A-I's role activating LCAT (cholesterol esterification in HDL), and from phospholipase C and ACAT, neither of which apo CII activates.

### Cholesterol and fatty acid biosynthesis
Cholesterol biosynthesis proceeds from acetyl CoA through acetoacetyl CoA and HMG CoA, and the pathway's committed, irreversible step — catalysed by the rate-limiting, statin-targeted enzyme HMG CoA reductase — is the conversion of HMG CoA to mevalonate; the earlier steps are reversible and shared with ketone body metabolism, and squalene formation comes later still. Fatty acid synthesis, a separate cytoplasmic pathway using malonyl CoA as the two-carbon chain-elongation donor and fatty acid synthase as the key enzyme, needs a large, continuous supply of NADPH+H+ for its reduction steps; the hexose monophosphate (pentose phosphate) pathway supplies the majority of this NADPH in lipogenic tissues, not glycolysis, which produces NADH rather than NADPH.

### Sphingolipidoses
Tay-Sachs disease is a lysosomal storage disorder caused by deficiency of hexosaminidase A, the enzyme that normally degrades GM2 ganglioside, which then accumulates progressively in neurons — distinct from the other classic sphingolipidoses, each defined by its own missing enzyme: Niemann-Pick (sphingomyelinase), Krabbe (galactocerebrosidase) and Gaucher (glucocerebrosidase) disease.

## hold_these
The LDL receptor is specific for apo B100 and apo E, not apo A, apo D or apo B48 alone.

Tay-Sachs disease is caused by hexosaminidase A deficiency — Niemann-Pick, Krabbe and Gaucher each have their own distinct enzyme.

Mismatching a sphingolipidosis to the wrong enzyme deficiency.

## summary
This short chapter covers two vascular-histology facts from the Cardiopulmonary final: the histological signature of the basilar artery, and how blood capillaries within nervous tissue are structured to form the blood-brain barrier.

## sections
### Cerebral artery histology
The basilar artery, formed by the union of the two vertebral arteries and supplying the brainstem and posterior brain, is histologically characterised — like other large cerebral arteries — by a wide lumen and a comparatively thin wall. It possesses an internal elastic lamina, like other muscular arteries (it does not lack one), has a thin rather than thick adventitia, and carries no unusual excess of valves.

### Capillaries of the nervous system
Capillaries within nervous tissue are continuous capillaries: an unfenestrated endothelium joined by tight junctions, with a complete (not incomplete) basal lamina — together the structural basis of the blood-brain barrier. Because the endothelium is tight and continuous rather than fenestrated, transport across it relies on pinocytic (transcytotic) vesicles rather than large pores; and like all capillary endothelium everywhere in the body, it is simple squamous, never simple cuboidal.

## hold_these
The basilar artery has a wide lumen and thin wall, an internal elastic lamina, and a thin adventitia.
Nervous-tissue capillaries are continuous, tight-junctioned, with a complete basal lamina, transporting material via pinocytic vesicles, not pores.

## lose_the_mark

Describing nervous-tissue capillaries as fenestrated with large pores, or lined by cuboidal (rather than squamous) epithelium.
