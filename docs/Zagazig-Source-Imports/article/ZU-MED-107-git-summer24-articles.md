## summary
This chapter works through six carbohydrate and energy metabolism facts tested by name in the GIT and Nutrition Summer resit: the ATP yield from one turn of the citric acid cycle, why the mature erythrocyte cannot run the cycle at all, why propionyl-CoA (unlike acetyl-CoA) is glucogenic, the net ATP yield of anaerobic glycolysis, which enzyme phosphorolyses glycogen's alpha-1,4 bonds, and the biopsy picture of McArdle disease. Each fact turns on precise numeric or mechanistic recall among closely related distractors.

## sections
### Definition
One turn of the citric acid cycle from acetyl-CoA yields 3 NADH, 1 FADH2 and 1 GTP, taught as a total of 12 ATP equivalents. The cycle cannot run at all in the mature erythrocyte, which has no mitochondria. Odd-chain fatty acid beta-oxidation ends in propionyl-CoA, converted via methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA — a citric acid cycle intermediate that can be drawn into gluconeogenesis, unlike acetyl-CoA. Anaerobic glycolysis yields a net 2 ATP per glucose. Glycogen phosphorylase phosphorolyses the alpha-1,4 glycosidic bonds of glycogen, releasing glucose-1-phosphate. McArdle disease is myophosphorylase deficiency: glycogen accumulates in structurally normal form because it cannot be broken down during exercise.

### Mechanism
The citric acid cycle's ATP accounting follows directly from its three NADH (2.5 ATP each), one FADH2 (1.5 ATP) and one GTP, taught in aggregate as 12 ATP using conventional P:O ratios — a figure specific to one acetyl-CoA, not to one whole glucose (which yields two acetyl-CoA and so far more). The erythrocyte's total absence of mitochondria, lost during maturation, removes both the matrix that would house citric acid cycle enzymes and the electron transport chain that would oxidise its products, leaving anaerobic glycolysis as the cell's only ATP source. Propionyl-CoA's three-carbon structure — the residue left after beta-oxidation removes two-carbon units repeatedly from an odd-numbered chain — is what lets it enter the cycle at succinyl-CoA, before the irreversible pyruvate dehydrogenase step that permanently commits acetyl-CoA's carbons to being lost as CO2 within one turn. Anaerobic glycolysis's net 2 ATP reflects 2 ATP invested early and 4 generated late, with NADH reoxidised via lactate formation rather than oxidative phosphorylation, since no oxygen is available to accept electrons at Complex IV. Glycogen phosphorylase's phosphorolytic (not hydrolytic) cleavage of alpha-1,4 bonds releases an already-phosphorylated sugar, sparing the hexokinase ATP cost that glucose entering from elsewhere would need; McArdle disease removes exactly this enzyme, leaving glycogen synthesis untouched but breakdown blocked.

### Key determinants
Each question turns on precise recall: 12 ATP as the acetyl-CoA-specific citric acid cycle yield (not the whole-glucose yield); the erythrocyte, not the hepatocyte, osteocyte or neuron, as the one cell type unable to run the cycle; propionyl-CoA, not acetyl-CoA or ketone bodies, as the beta-oxidation product that reaches gluconeogenesis; 2 ATP, not the aerobic or citric-acid-cycle figures, as anaerobic glycolysis's net yield; glycogen phosphorylase, not glycogen synthase, phosphatase or debranching enzyme, for alpha-1,4 phosphorolysis; and myophosphorylase deficiency with increased normal glycogen, not a glycogen synthase or hexokinase defect, for McArdle disease.

### Clinical significance
The erythrocyte's total dependence on anaerobic glycolysis explains why conditions impairing glycolytic enzymes (such as pyruvate kinase deficiency) cause haemolytic anaemia specifically, with no citric-acid-cycle backup available. Odd-chain fatty acid metabolism's route through propionyl-CoA and methylmalonyl-CoA is the basis of propionic and methylmalonic acidaemias when the pathway's enzymes are inherited deficient. McArdle disease's exercise-induced cramps and myalgia, from an inability to mobilise muscle glycogen, is a classic presentation distinguishing glycogen storage diseases affecting muscle from those affecting liver.

## summary
This chapter works through six vitamin, heme-synthesis and nitrogen-metabolism facts tested by name in the GIT and Nutrition Summer resit: which vitamin is the membrane's major lipid-soluble antioxidant, which heme-synthesis enzyme needs pyridoxal phosphate, where the urea cycle's enzymes are localised, what causes acquired porphyria, which amino acid is excluded from transamination, and which Phase 2 conjugation reaction is the most common. Each fact turns on precise recall among closely related distractors.

## sections
### Definition
Vitamin E is the major lipid-soluble antioxidant protecting cell membrane phospholipids from peroxidation. ALA synthase, the rate-limiting first enzyme of heme synthesis, requires pyridoxal phosphate (vitamin B6) as coenzyme. The urea cycle's enzymes are split between the liver's mitochondria (carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I, ornithine transcarbamylase) and cytoplasm (the remaining three enzymes) — a pathway confined to the liver. Lead poisoning is the classic cause of acquired porphyria, inhibiting ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase. Proline, an imino acid, is excluded from standard transamination because it lacks a free alpha-amino group. Glucuronidation, conjugating a compound with glucuronic acid, is the most commonly occurring Phase 2 xenobiotic conjugation reaction.

### Mechanism
Vitamin E's chromanol ring sits within the membrane bilayer and donates a hydrogen atom to peroxyl radicals, breaking the chain reaction of lipid peroxidation before it can propagate through adjacent unsaturated fatty acids. ALA synthase's condensation of glycine and succinyl-CoA to form delta-aminolevulinic acid specifically requires pyridoxal phosphate, which is why vitamin B6 deficiency or isoniazid (which depletes pyridoxal phosphate) can produce sideroblastic-type anaemia by starving this first committed step. The urea cycle's mitochondria-to-cytoplasm shuttle — citrulline crossing out of the mitochondrion after the first two steps — is what makes the pathway two-compartment rather than confined to one, and its restriction to the liver (the kidney lacks complete carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I activity) is what makes "liver mitochondria and cytoplasm" the only fully correct answer among organ/compartment combinations. Lead's sulfhydryl-binding action inhibits two separate heme-synthesis enzymes at once, producing a more severe block than a single-enzyme hereditary porphyria typically shows. Proline's side chain cyclises back onto its own nitrogen, forming a ring that leaves no free alpha-amino group for a standard aminotransferase to act on, the same structural reason lysine, threonine and hydroxyproline are also excluded. Glucuronidation's dominance among Phase 2 routes (ahead of sulfation via PAPS, acetylation via acetyl-CoA, and methylation via SAM) reflects UDP-glucuronosyltransferase's high capacity and broad substrate range, handling far more compounds than the other three conjugation donors combined.

### Key determinants
Each question turns on one specific fact: vitamin E, not A, D or K, as the membrane's lipid-soluble antioxidant; ALA synthase, not ALA dehydratase, coproporphyrinogen oxidase or ferrochelatase, as the pyridoxal-phosphate-dependent enzyme; "liver mitochondria and cytoplasm", not any single-compartment or single-organ option, for urea cycle localisation; lead poisoning, not a vitamin deficiency or cyanide, as the acquired porphyria cause; proline, not glutamic acid, serine or alanine, as the transamination exception; and glucuronic acid, not PAPS, acetyl-CoA or SAM, as the dominant Phase 2 conjugation partner.

### Clinical significance
Vitamin E's membrane-antioxidant role underlies its use (and the consequences of its deficiency, such as haemolysis and neurological signs) in conditions of fat malabsorption. Pyridoxal-phosphate depletion by isoniazid is a recognised, preventable cause of sideroblastic anaemia and peripheral neuropathy in tuberculosis treatment, managed by co-prescribing vitamin B6. Recognising the urea cycle as liver-specific explains why hepatic failure, not renal failure alone, produces hyperammonaemia. Lead poisoning's acquired porphyria, with its microcytic anaemia and basophilic stippling, remains a classic toxicology diagnosis. Proline's exclusion from transamination is part of why its catabolism follows a distinct route relevant to understanding hydroxyproline-based markers of collagen turnover. Glucuronidation's dominant capacity underlies drug metabolism and drug-drug interactions, and its role in bilirubin conjugation is the basis of neonatal jaundice from immature UDP-glucuronosyltransferase activity (and of Gilbert/Crigler-Najjar syndromes when the enzyme is inherited deficient).

## summary
This chapter works through eight gastric- and intestinal-regulation facts tested by name in the GIT and Nutrition Summer resit: the submucosal plexus's secretory role, lingual lipase's digestive contribution, the active-transport mechanism of gastric acid secretion, VIP's relaxant effects, what accelerates gastric emptying, the respiratory events of vomiting, protein's specific dynamic action, and prostaglandins' role in mucosal defence. Each fact turns on correctly assigning a mechanism or feature to the right structure or substance among closely related distractors.

## sections
### Definition
The submucosal (Meissner's) plexus predominantly controls local gastrointestinal secretion, distinct from the myenteric plexus's motility role. Lingual lipase, swallowed with saliva, begins triglyceride digestion, particularly in neonates. Gastric H+ secretion is active transport by the parietal cell's H+/K+-ATPase, pumping H+ into the lumen against a steep gradient in exchange for potassium. VIP relaxes the lower esophageal sphincter and other GI smooth muscle as part of its broadly inhibitory, vasodilatory profile. A high-carbohydrate meal accelerates gastric emptying relative to a high-fat meal. Respiration is briefly inhibited, with glottic closure, during the vomiting reflex. Protein has the largest specific dynamic action of the three macronutrients, raising basal metabolic rate by roughly 30%. Prostaglandins protect gastric mucosa by stimulating mucus and bicarbonate secretion, maintaining blood flow, and promoting epithelial restitution.

### Mechanism
The enteric nervous system's two plexuses divide labour spatially and functionally: the myenteric plexus, between the circular and longitudinal muscle layers, chiefly drives motility, while the submucosal plexus, within the submucosa itself, sits closer to the epithelium and chiefly drives local secretion and blood flow. Lingual lipase's action in the acidic stomach, ahead of pancreatic lipase in the alkaline duodenum, gives infants (whose pancreatic output is still maturing) a meaningful head start on fat digestion. The parietal cell's proton pump moves H+ against roughly a million-fold concentration gradient, an energetically expensive process that only active transport (never passive diffusion) can sustain, paired with a postprandial alkaline tide as bicarbonate enters the blood. VIP's broadly inhibitory GI profile — relaxing smooth muscle, dilating vessels, stimulating secretion, and inhibiting gastric acid — is the coordinated basis of VIPoma's severe secretory diarrhoea when this signalling runs unchecked. Gastric emptying rate tracks how much duodenal inhibitory feedback (CCK, secretin, the enterogastric reflex) a meal generates: carbohydrate generates the least, so it empties fastest, while fat and duodenal distension generate the most and slow emptying. The vomiting reflex coordinates diaphragmatic and abdominal contraction (raising intra-abdominal pressure), cardiac sphincter relaxation (allowing upward passage) and glottic closure with respiratory inhibition (protecting the airway) as one synchronised sequence. Protein's high specific dynamic action reflects the metabolic cost of amino acid deamination and urea synthesis, well above the cost of processing carbohydrate or fat. Prostaglandins coordinate several separate protective inputs — mucus, bicarbonate, blood flow, cell turnover — which is why NSAIDs, by blocking prostaglandin synthesis, remove several defences at once and predispose to ulceration.

### Key determinants
Each question turns on one specific assignment: the submucosal plexus, not the myenteric plexus, for local secretion; lingual lipase, not dipeptidase or trypsinogen, for salivary fat digestion; active transport against a gradient (exchanged for potassium, not sodium), not passive diffusion, for gastric H+ secretion; lower esophageal sphincter relaxation, not gastric acid stimulation or vasoconstriction, among VIP's effects; high-carbohydrate diet, not high-fat diet or duodenal distension, for accelerated gastric emptying; inhibition of respiration, not diaphragm relaxation or cardiac sphincter contraction, during vomiting; protein's ~30% figure, not fat's smaller effect or an incorrect timing claim, for specific dynamic action; and prostaglandin secretion, not an "acidic" mucus barrier, gap junctions or intrinsic factor, for gastric mucosal defence.

### Clinical significance
The submucosal plexus's secretory role is why enteric neuropathies can selectively disrupt secretion or motility depending on which plexus is affected. Lingual lipase's proportionally larger role in neonates matters in preterm infants with immature pancreatic function. The parietal cell's H+/K+-ATPase is the direct target of proton pump inhibitors, the mainstay of acid-suppressive therapy. VIP-secreting tumours (VIPomas) cause a distinct secretory diarrhoea syndrome directly explained by this hormone's normal GI actions. Recognising carbohydrate's faster gastric emptying informs dietary advice in conditions like gastroparesis. The airway-protective mechanics of vomiting explain why impaired consciousness raises aspiration risk during emesis. Protein's high specific dynamic action is factored into nutritional and metabolic calculations in critical care. NSAID-induced peptic ulceration is a direct clinical consequence of prostaglandin-dependent mucosal defence being pharmacologically removed.

## summary
This chapter works through four surgical- and splanchnic-anatomy facts tested by name in the GIT and Nutrition Summer resit: normal portal vein pressure, which structure's blood supply survives splenic artery ligation, where a gallstone lodges to cause biliary reflux pancreatitis, and the histological composition of the internal anal sphincter. Each fact turns on naming one correct value or structure among closely related distractors.

## sections
### Definition
Normal portal vein pressure is approximately 5-10 mmHg. The duodenum's blood supply, from the gastroduodenal and superior mesenteric arteries, is unaffected by splenic artery ligation, unlike the pancreatic body/tail, stomach fundus and part of the greater omentum, which the splenic artery itself supplies. The hepatopancreatic ampulla (ampulla of Vater) is the shared bile-pancreatic duct outlet whose gallstone obstruction causes bile to reflux into the pancreatic duct, producing pancreatitis. The internal anal sphincter is formed from smooth circular muscle continuous with the rectal wall, distinct from the skeletal, voluntary external sphincter.

### Mechanism
The portal system's low, roughly 5-10 mmHg pressure reflects its role as a low-resistance venous circuit draining the splanchnic capillary beds into the liver's sinusoids, well below systemic arterial pressure and only somewhat above ordinary systemic venous pressure given the added resistance of the hepatic sinusoidal bed. The splenic artery's specific branch territory — spleen, pancreatic body/tail via pancreatic branches, part of the greater omentum, and the stomach's fundus/greater curvature via short gastric and left gastroepiploic vessels — is what makes the duodenum, supplied entirely separately by the gastroduodenal/superior mesenteric system, the one structure genuinely unaffected by a proximal splenic artery ligation. The hepatopancreatic ampulla's status as the single shared channel for both the common bile duct and the main pancreatic duct is what makes a stone lodged there, and only there, capable of forcing bile backward into the pancreatic duct — a stone anywhere upstream of this confluence cannot produce that specific reflux. The internal anal sphincter's identity as a thickening of the rectal wall's own circular smooth muscle layer, rather than a discrete skeletal muscle like the external sphincter, is what makes it autonomically (not voluntarily) controlled.

### Key determinants
Each question turns on naming one correct value or structure: approximately 5-10 mmHg (not systemic-arterial or grossly under/overstated values) for portal vein pressure; the duodenum (not the pancreatic body, greater omentum, or stomach) as the structure unaffected by splenic artery ligation; the hepatopancreatic ampulla (not the common bile duct, cystic duct, or common hepatic duct) as the gallstone site causing biliary reflux pancreatitis; and smooth circular muscle (not a tripartite skeletal arrangement or a named somatic nerve) for the internal anal sphincter.

### Clinical significance
Portal pressure measurement underlies the diagnosis and monitoring of portal hypertension in cirrhosis, where clinically significant elevation predicts variceal bleeding risk. Knowing the splenic artery's exact branch territory guides safe splenic artery ligation or embolisation for trauma or hypersplenism without compromising the pancreas, stomach or duodenum unnecessarily. The hepatopancreatic ampulla's anatomy is the basis for understanding gallstone pancreatitis and for endoscopic sphincterotomy as its treatment. The internal anal sphincter's smooth-muscle, involuntary nature — distinct from the external sphincter's voluntary control — underlies the different continence mechanisms disrupted by obstetric tears reaching each structure.

## summary
This chapter works through two histology-identification facts tested by name in the GIT and Nutrition Summer resit: which gland counts among the major salivary glands, and what epithelium lines the esophagus. Each fact turns on matching one structure or feature to the correct classification among closely related distractors.

## sections
### Definition
The sublingual gland is one of the three paired major salivary glands, alongside the parotid and submandibular glands; the palatine, buccal and lingual glands are minor salivary glands scattered through the oral mucosa. The esophagus is lined along the great majority of its length by stratified squamous non-keratinised epithelium, with a short transition to simple columnar epithelium at its junction with the stomach (the gastroesophageal junction/cardia).

### Mechanism
The three major salivary glands (parotid, submandibular, sublingual) are anatomically discrete, encapsulated glands draining via defined ducts and together account for the great majority of both resting and stimulated salivary volume, distinguishing them from the numerous small minor glands embedded directly in the oral mucosa (palatine, buccal, lingual, and others) that contribute only modest additional volume. The esophagus's stratified squamous lining suits the mechanical wear of swallowed food along its length, while its short terminal segment transitions to simple columnar epithelium matching the adjacent gastric mucosa — a genuine but limited exception to the epithelium's otherwise uniform character, distinct from the separate fact that the esophagus's upper third also contains striated muscle, transitioning to smooth muscle lower down.

### Key determinants
Each question turns on one classification: the sublingual gland, not the palatine, buccal or lingual glands, as a major salivary gland; and the esophagus's predominant stratified squamous lining, with its short gastroesophageal-junction transition to simple (not stratified) columnar epithelium, as the epithelial fact this paper's key selects — distinct from the separate, also-true fact that its upper third contains striated muscle.

### Clinical significance
Major salivary gland anatomy is directly relevant to sialolithiasis, sialadenitis and salivary gland tumours, which present differently depending on which of the three major glands (or, less commonly, a minor gland) is involved. The esophagus's predominant stratified squamous epithelium, and its columnar transition at the gastroesophageal junction, underlie the histological basis of Barrett's esophagus, in which chronic acid reflux causes pathological columnar (intestinal) metaplasia extending well beyond this normal transition zone.
