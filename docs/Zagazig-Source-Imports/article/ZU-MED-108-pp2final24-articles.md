## summary
This chapter covers the basic (non-instrumental and adjunct) manoeuvres used to open an obstructed airway: recognising obstruction by its sounds, the jaw thrust and its cervical-spine-injury indication, the nasopharyngeal and oropharyngeal airway adjuncts and how each is sized and contraindicated, and what to do first for an unconscious patient with noisy breathing.

## sections
### Definition
Airway obstruction is recognised by abnormal breath sounds — gurgling (liquid), snoring (tongue/soft palate), stridor (laryngeal/upper-tracheal narrowing) or wheeze (lower-airway narrowing) — before any device is reached for. The basic manoeuvres are head tilt-chin lift and jaw thrust, both of which lift the tongue off the posterior pharyngeal wall; the basic adjuncts are the nasopharyngeal airway (NPA, a soft nasal tube) and the oropharyngeal airway (OPA, a rigid oral device), each with its own sizing method and contraindication.

### Mechanism
Jaw thrust lifts the mandible — and with it the tongue, which attaches to it — upward and forward without extending the neck, which is why it (not head tilt-chin lift) is used whenever cervical spine injury is suspected. The NPA is sized by the diameter of the patient's little finger and is contraindicated in suspected base-of-skull fracture, since it is passed through the nose and can enter the cranial cavity through a fracture defect. The OPA is sized by the distance from the angle of the mouth to the ear lobule and is reserved for a patient without a gag reflex. For an unconscious patient with laboured, noisy breathing, the first action is always a simple manoeuvre (head tilt-chin lift, or jaw thrust if the spine is at risk) rather than escalating immediately to bag-mask ventilation, intubation or a blind finger sweep.

### Key determinants
Each fact in this chapter turns on matching the right technique or landmark to the right device or situation: jaw thrust is the safe choice, not the contraindicated one, in cervical spine injury; the NPA's diameter (little finger) and the OPA's length (ear lobule to mouth angle) landmarks are not interchangeable; the NPA's contraindication is skull base fracture specifically, not simply "semiconscious" or "deeply comatose" (options the paper offers as distractors); and simple manoeuvres are always tried before invasive ones.

### Clinical significance
Choosing jaw thrust correctly in a trauma patient avoids worsening an unstable cervical spine injury. Correct NPA/OPA sizing prevents either failure to relieve obstruction (too short/narrow) or new trauma/worsened obstruction (too long, pushing the epiglottis down, or passed through an undetected skull-base fracture). Trying the simplest airway manoeuvre first, before ventilation or intubation, is the same "airway before breathing" principle that underlies the entire BLS sequence.

## summary
This chapter covers the recognition of choking (its signs and symptoms, including the universal choking sign) and when abdominal thrusts are indicated versus when a still-coughing patient needs no manual intervention.

## sections
### Definition
Choking presents with sudden difficulty breathing and/or speaking, coughing, the universal choking sign (clutching or pointing to the throat), and, if unrelieved, cyanosis. Management escalates with severity: a patient coughing effectively needs only encouragement to keep coughing; one who can no longer cough, speak or breathe needs mechanical clearance.

### Mechanism
Effective coughing generates higher airway pressures than any manual manoeuvre can, so it remains the first-line response for partial obstruction. Once obstruction becomes complete, abdominal thrusts (the Heimlich manoeuvre) — delivered from behind, inward and upward below the xiphoid — mechanically raise intrathoracic/intra-abdominal pressure to expel the foreign body, alternated with back blows in current first-aid teaching.

### Key determinants
The paper's own distractor structure turns on severity: abdominal thrusts are indicated specifically for the "cannot cough or speak" patient, not the "coughing effectively" one, and cyanosis is a late sign of ongoing obstruction, not a prerequisite for recognising choking in the first place.

### Clinical significance
Recognising the universal choking sign and the transition from partial to complete obstruction is what tells a rescuer when to stop encouraging coughing and start abdominal thrusts — a delay at this transition, or premature intervention on an effectively coughing patient, both worsen outcomes.

## published_summary


## published_sections


## hold_these
A choking patient who is coughing effectively should be encouraged to keep coughing, not given abdominal thrusts.
Abdominal thrusts are indicated once a conscious choking patient can no longer cough or speak.
Signs and symptoms of choking include difficulty breathing/speaking, coughing, the throat-clutching sign, and cyanosis if breathing is not restored.

## lose_the_mark
Performing abdominal thrusts on a patient who is still coughing effectively.
Waiting for cyanosis before recognising choking, instead of acting on the earlier signs.

## summary
This chapter covers the basic life support sequence from checking responsiveness through the BLS flow chart to recognising cardiac arrest, delivering correctly performed chest compressions, and applying an AED.

## sections
### Definition
BLS begins with checking responsiveness (shake the shoulders and shout), then follows a fixed flow chart: R (response), D (danger/hazards), S (send for help), then airway, breathing and circulation/CPR. Cardiac arrest is recognised clinically by the triad of unconsciousness, no pulse and no breathing, and is managed with chest compressions and, once available, an AED.

### Mechanism
Correct compression technique requires the rescuer to kneel beside the patient's chest (not the legs), keep the arms straight, and drive each compression from the hip joint rather than the shoulder or elbow, sustaining depth and rate without rapid fatigue. AED pads are applied to bare, dry skin because clothing blocks adhesion and moisture can conduct current across the skin surface rather than through the heart, posing a shock hazard.

### Key determinants
The BLS flow chart's "D" stands for danger/hazards, not disability (which belongs to the separate trauma ABCDE primary survey) — a frequently tested distractor. Chest-compression technique questions turn on body mechanics (hip-driven, straight arms, kneeling at the chest) rather than compression depth or rate. AED questions turn on the bare-dry-chest requirement, distinguishing it from wet skin, clothing or the wrong pad site (abdomen).

### Clinical significance
Following the BLS sequence in order, without skipping the danger check or misapplying trauma-survey terminology, keeps both rescuer and patient safe. Correct hip-driven compression mechanics is what allows a single rescuer to sustain effective compressions until help or an AED arrives, and correct AED pad placement is what allows the shock, when advised, to actually reach the heart.

## published_summary


## published_sections


## hold_these
Responsiveness is checked by shaking the shoulders and shouting.
The BLS flow chart is R (response), D (danger), S (send for help), then airway/breathing/CPR — "disability" belongs to the trauma ABCDE survey, not BLS.
Cardiac arrest is recognised by loss of consciousness with no pulse and no breathing.
Chest compressions are driven from the hip joint, with straight arms, kneeling beside the patient's chest.
AED pads go on bare, dry skin.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing "D for danger" (BLS) with "D for disability" (trauma ABCDE primary survey).
Powering compressions from the shoulder or elbow, or kneeling beside the legs rather than the chest.
Applying AED pads over clothing, wet skin, or on the abdomen.

## summary
This chapter covers the purpose of the recovery position for an unconscious, breathing patient and the use of an ambulance for safe patient transfer.

## sections
### Definition
The recovery position is a stable, semi-prone lateral position used for an unconscious patient who is breathing normally. An ambulance is the equipped, staffed vehicle used to transfer a sick or injured patient safely between locations.

### Mechanism
The recovery position keeps the airway open by letting the tongue fall forward instead of back into the pharynx, and lets vomit or secretions drain out of the mouth rather than pool in the airway, reducing aspiration risk while help arrives. An ambulance is purpose-built and staffed to monitor the patient, maintain the airway, continue treatment and manage deterioration en route — none of which ordinary transport (truck, train, commercial aircraft) provides.

### Key determinants
The recovery position is for the unconscious-but-breathing patient specifically — not for a patient with no pulse/no breathing (who needs CPR) or a suspected spine/limb fracture (where it is unsafe). Safe transfer questions turn on the equipped-and-staffed nature of an ambulance, not merely "a vehicle that moves."

### Clinical significance
Placing the wrong patient in the recovery position (one needing CPR, or with a spinal injury) can cause harm or delay resuscitation; using ordinary transport instead of an ambulance for a sick patient removes monitoring and treatment capability exactly when it may be needed.

## published_summary


## published_sections


## hold_these
The recovery position maintains a patent airway in an unconscious, normally breathing victim.
An ambulance, not ordinary transport, is the appropriate vehicle for safe patient transfer.

## lose_the_mark
Using the recovery position for a patient who needs CPR or has a suspected spinal injury.
Treating any vehicle as adequate for patient transfer.

## summary
This chapter covers where basic identifying data sits within the structured medical history (personal history) and the graded ladder of consciousness from alert down to comatose.

## sections
### Definition
The general medical history is structured into named sections; personal history records identifying/demographic data (name, age, sex, occupation, marital status, residence), distinct from the presenting complaint, history of present illness, and past/family/social history. Level of consciousness is described along a ladder: alert, lethargic/somnolent, obtunded, stupor/semi-coma, and coma.

### Mechanism
Each rung of the consciousness ladder is defined by the stimulus needed to elicit a response and the quality of that response: alert needs no stimulus; lethargic is aroused by voice; obtunded needs loud/repeated stimulation; stupor/semi-coma responds only to vigorous, persistent pain, with an appropriate withdrawal; coma has no response to pain or any stimulus at all.

### Key determinants
"Name, age, sex" belongs to personal history, not to past history or the presenting complaint — a structural, not clinical-reasoning, distinction. "No response to pain or any stimulus" is specifically comatose, distinguished from stupor/semi-coma (which still withdraws from pain) by the complete absence of any response.

### Clinical significance
Correctly structuring a history (personal history separate from the presenting complaint and past history) is a basic communication and documentation skill assessed throughout clinical training. Correctly grading consciousness level guides both the urgency of intervention and the vocabulary used to hand over a patient's status to colleagues.

## published_summary


## published_sections


## hold_these
Name, age and sex are recorded under personal history in a structured medical history.
No response to pain or any stimulus at all defines comatose, distinct from lethargic, obtunded and stupor/semi-coma.

## lose_the_mark
Filing basic identifying data under past history or the presenting complaint.
Calling a patient who still withdraws from pain "comatose" — that response places them at stupor/semi-coma instead.

## summary
This chapter covers the components of vital signs, the pulse deficit and brachial-pulse landmark, and the diagnostic threshold for postural hypotension.

## sections
### Definition
Vital signs are pulse, blood pressure, temperature and respiratory rate — basic physiological measurements distinct from a physical-examination step such as abdominal examination. Pulse deficit is the difference between the apical (auscultated) and peripheral (palpated) pulse rates, counted simultaneously; the brachial pulse is palpated in the antecubital fossa, medial to the biceps tendon. Postural hypotension is diagnosed when systolic blood pressure falls by 20 mmHg or more within 2 minutes of standing.

### Mechanism
In arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation, some ventricular contractions are too weak to generate a palpable peripheral pulse wave even though they are audible at the apex, producing a pulse deficit when apical and radial rates are compared. Standing normally triggers reflex vasoconstriction and a modest heart-rate rise that keep blood pressure stable within 1-2 minutes; postural hypotension is diagnosed when this compensation fails and systolic pressure drops 20 mmHg or more.

### Key determinants
Vital-signs questions turn on the fixed four-item list (pulse, BP, temperature, respiratory rate) versus an examination step like abdominal exam. Pulse-deficit questions turn on distinguishing a rate difference between two sites from pulse volume or named rhythm patterns (bigeminy, alternans). The brachial pulse's landmark (medial to the biceps tendon, antecubital fossa) is distinct from the radial, carotid and femoral sites. Postural hypotension's threshold (≥20 mmHg systolic within 2 minutes) is a fixed diagnostic criterion, not any measurable drop.

### Clinical significance
A pulse deficit points toward an arrhythmia (classically atrial fibrillation) that a peripheral pulse count alone would miss. The brachial-pulse landmark is exactly where blood pressure is auscultated with a stethoscope during cuff deflation. Recognising true postural hypotension by its threshold, rather than any BP change on standing, avoids both over- and under-diagnosis in patients being assessed for falls or dizziness.

## summary
This chapter compares the four standard injection routes — intradermal, subcutaneous, intramuscular and intravenous — by tissue depth, needle angle, typical use and complications, including the Z-track technique and the fastest route for onset of action.

## sections
### Definition
The four routes are layered by depth: intradermal (into the dermis itself, ~5-15° angle) for TB screening, allergy testing and local anaesthesia; subcutaneous (the fatty layer beneath the dermis, ~45° angle) for agents like insulin needing steady systemic absorption; intramuscular (~70-90°, into muscle) for larger volumes and irritant drugs, sometimes using the Z-track technique; and intravenous (directly into the bloodstream) for fluids and medications needing complete, rapid bioavailability, as in dehydration, malnutrition, cancer and autoimmune therapy.

### Mechanism
Because intravenous injection bypasses tissue absorption entirely, it has the fastest onset of any route and 100% bioavailability, which is why it is chosen both for the fastest possible drug effect and for conditions needing large-volume or precisely dosed therapy. The Z-track technique displaces skin and subcutaneous tissue sideways before intramuscular needle insertion, sealing the drug inside the muscle once the tissue layers slide back, to prevent an irritant drug leaking back along the needle track. Recognised intramuscular complications — muscle fibrosis/contracture, abscess, nerve injury (sciatic in gluteal, femoral in vastus lateralis injections) — follow from incorrect site selection or repeated use of one site.

### Key determinants
Each question in this chapter turns on matching route to depth/angle/use correctly: intradermal's shallow 5-15° angle versus subcutaneous's 45° or intramuscular's 70-90°; insulin as the exception among intradermal uses (it needs subcutaneous, not intradermal, delivery); Z-track as specifically an intramuscular (not IV/SC/ID) technique; and intravenous, not intramuscular, as the fastest-onset route despite IM's common emergency use.

### Clinical significance
Choosing the wrong angle or depth for an injection either fails to deposit the drug in the intended tissue plane or causes unnecessary trauma. Recognising insulin's subcutaneous requirement, using Z-track correctly for irritant intramuscular drugs, and knowing intramuscular injection's complication profile are all basic safe-prescribing and safe-administration skills. Reaching first for the intravenous route in a genuine emergency reflects its unmatched onset speed.
