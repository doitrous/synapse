## summary
This chapter works through six professional-conduct and attribute facts from امتحانات سابقه.pdf pages 8-10: patient confidentiality during a physical examination, a source-specific categorisation of disclosing an impairing substance, why a fellow student's presence does not substitute for proper supervision during an intimate examination, self-awareness as the professionalism attribute behind continued development/emotional intelligence and boundary-keeping, a source-specific categorisation of a conflict-of-interest scenario, and the expectation to dress professionally with a visible identity badge. Three of the six facts carry a flagged mismatch between this source's own marked key and more conventional professionalism teaching.

## sections
### Definition
Asking a patient's permission before an examination, and limiting who is present or can see them, is a concrete expression of patient confidentiality. This compiled bank marks a student's disclosure to a registered practitioner about a potentially impairing substance under "informal communication" rather than the more conventional label "student health". A fellow medical student's presence alone does not confer the supervision or chaperone role that legitimises an intimate examination. Self-awareness is tied here to continued professional development or emotional intelligence, boundary-keeping with patients and colleagues, ethical behaviour, and recognising one's position as a role model. This source marks "interpersonal skills", rather than the more conventional "altruism", as the attribute violated when a professional lets private industry gain compromise their responsibilities. Medical students are expected to dress professionally and wear a visible name or identity badge throughout clinical practice.

### Mechanism
Each of these facts turns on correctly scoping one professionalism construct against a plausible neighbour. Confidentiality (controlling who is present or can see a patient) is narrower than the broad "professional responsibilities" umbrella offered as a distractor. Self-awareness's own scope in this source is broad, spanning ongoing development, emotional intelligence, boundary-setting and role-modelling together, because each of those four behaviours depends on the same underlying accurate self-understanding. Two of this chapter's facts (the informal-communication categorisation and the interpersonal-skills categorisation) reflect this specific source's own marked key rather than the more standard curriculum label, and are flagged accordingly rather than silently corrected — the informal-communication mark is a moderate mismatch, while the interpersonal-skills mark for a conflict-of-interest scenario is a weaker one, since standard professionalism-charter teaching ties exactly that scenario to altruism instead.

### Key determinants
Each question turns on picking the construct this source's own key names, even where three of the six facts diverge from more conventional professionalism-curriculum labels: confidentiality vs the broader "professional responsibilities" category; this source's own informal-communication label vs the more standard student-health label; supervision/chaperone adequacy (a peer does not qualify) vs consent (a precondition, not a bar to avoid); self-awareness's broad scope vs narrower attributes like compassion, dedication, integrity or fidelity; this source's own interpersonal-skills label vs the more standard altruism label for a private-gain conflict of interest; and professional dress/identification vs implausible distractors.

### Clinical significance
Confidentiality during examination and appropriate chaperone practice for intimate examinations are foundational patient-safety and trust behaviours students carry from their first clinical contact onward. Self-awareness's broad role in professional development, boundary-keeping and role-modelling is a recurring "professional identity formation" theme across a medical career. The two flagged mismatches between this source's own key and more conventional teaching (informal communication, interpersonal skills) are recorded transparently so a reviewer can confirm or correct the source's own categorisation before this content reaches students at scale.

## summary
This chapter works through seven bioethics facts from امتحانات سابقه.pdf pages 9-11 that extend pp1-cluster1's own bioethics-principles article: the doctrine of double effect as a beneficence/non-maleficence balance, this source's own terminology choices for "ethics" and for altruism's related principle, non-maleficence in an urgent street-treatment scenario, the definition of decision-making competence, competence as the exception to a patient's final say, and a source-marked statement about implied consent that conflicts with standard consent doctrine. Three of the seven facts carry a flagged mismatch between this source's own marked key and more conventional bioethics teaching.

## sections
### Definition
The doctrine of double effect is usually framed as weighing an action's intended good effect (beneficence) against a foreseen but unintended harmful effect (non-maleficence). This source labels the set of values, behaviors and relationships supporting public trust in doctors as "ethics", and ties the attribute of altruism to autonomy, both looser fits than the more standard terms "professionalism" and "beneficence" respectively. Refusing an unsupervised, rushed street treatment in favour of proper hospital care is an application of non-maleficence. Competence is the intellectual capacity to understand, analyse and judge information, and it is specifically competence, not mere confidence, that determines whether a patient retains the final say in their own treatment. This source marks "implied consent must be signed by the patient" as true, which conflicts with standard teaching that implied consent is inferred from conduct and specifically does not require a signature.

### Mechanism
Each of these facts either applies a standard bioethical principle to a scenario or, for three of them, records a specific source-marked terminology choice that a learner should recognise as this source's own rather than universal convention. The double-effect and street-scenario facts apply the standard four-principle bioethics framework straightforwardly. The ethics/professionalism, altruism/autonomy, and implied-consent-signature facts each diverge from more conventional bioethics teaching, and are written attributively here ("as keyed by this source") rather than presented as uncontested fact, with the specific standard alternative named in each case for contrast. Competence underlies both its own direct definition and its role as the specific exception to a patient's ordinary final say in decision-making.

### Key determinants
Each question turns on either a standard bioethics-principle application (double effect as beneficence/non-maleficence; the street scenario as non-maleficence; competence's definition; competence as the exception to final say) or on recognising this specific source's own, sometimes non-standard, terminology choice (ethics vs professionalism; altruism vs autonomy/beneficence; implied consent's signature requirement).

### Clinical significance
The doctrine of double effect and the non-maleficence/beneficence balance it rests on recur in end-of-life and palliative scenarios throughout a medical career. Competence as the gatekeeper for a patient's final say is foundational to every consent conversation a clinician has. The three flagged terminology or key mismatches in this chapter are recorded transparently, particularly the implied-consent-signature statement, which conflicts with a widely taught, safety-relevant distinction between implied and express consent, so a reviewer can confirm or correct the source's own key before this content reaches students at scale.

## summary
This chapter works through eight facts from امتحانات سابقه.pdf pages 6-11 spanning leadership-style selection, group dynamics, self-awareness's own definition, learner types, learning needs, correct sequencing before approaching a patient, successful-team characteristics, and the one non-modifiable determinant of health among four options.

## sections
### Definition
The appropriate leadership style is selected based on the situation, the group's size and qualification, and the group's maturity, together, not any single factor alone. Group dynamics is the pattern of interactions between people talking together in a group setting. Self-awareness is knowing one's own motivations, preferences and personality and how these shape one's judgment and interactions. The interested learner, in the staged self-directed-learning model, seeks opportunities and sets some goals but is still sometimes directed and still building confidence. A learning need is the gap between current and desired knowledge, skills and abilities. Students should first seek permission from the staff responsible for a patient's care before approaching that patient. Requiring identical personal characteristics across all team members is not a real characteristic of successful teams. Genetic factors are a non-modifiable determinant of health, unlike behaviour, socio-economic factors, or the physical environment.

### Mechanism
These facts each isolate one construct from adjacent-sounding distractors. Leadership-style selection is tested as a "combine all the named factors" answer rather than any single contingency. Group dynamics is distinguished from being unimportant, fixed, or exclusively social. The interested learner sits at the second of four staged self-directed-learning stages, between the more passive dependent learner and the more autonomous involved and self-directed learners. A learning need (the diagnostic gap) is distinguished from learning resources, objectives and goals, each a related but different concept. Seeking permission from the care team is tested specifically as the deliberate first step, ahead of explaining student status or asking about beliefs. Successful-team characteristics rely on complementary diversity, not uniform personnel character. Genetic factors are distinguished from the three other, genuinely modifiable determinants by their fixed, unchangeable nature from conception onward.

### Key determinants
Each question turns on picking the complete or precisely-scoped answer among plausible partial or adjacent options: leadership-style selection (all three factors, not any one alone); group dynamics' definition (interaction pattern, not "unimportant"/"fixed"/"social-only"); self-awareness's own definition (self-knowledge, not time management/goal setting/leadership); the interested-learner stage (motivated and goal-setting but still needing direction, distinct from the three other stages); learning needs (the gap itself, not resources/objectives/goals); sequencing before patient contact (permission first); successful-team characteristics (diversity, not uniformity, is genuine); and health determinants (fixed genetics vs three modifiable factors).

### Clinical significance
Leadership-style flexibility, group dynamics awareness, and correctly scoped self-awareness are foundational team-based-care skills. The staged self-directed-learning model and the definition of a learning need underpin how a medical curriculum structures student self-assessment and remediation throughout training. Correctly sequencing patient contact (permission before approach) and understanding non-modifiable versus modifiable health determinants both carry direct relevance to safe clinical practice and to how a student reasons about population-health scenarios.

## hold_these
Leadership-style selection depends jointly on the situation, the group's size/qualification, and the group's maturity.
