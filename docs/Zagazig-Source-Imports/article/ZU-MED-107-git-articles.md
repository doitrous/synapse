## summary
This chapter works through six enzyme-identity facts tested by name in the GIT and Nutrition final: which complex antimycin blocks in the electron transport chain, which TCA step performs substrate-level phosphorylation, what makes cytochrome P450 a monooxygenase, which pancreatic protease is specific for aromatic residues, why acetyl-CoA cannot feed gluconeogenesis, and how epinephrine/glucagon signalling moves glycogen phosphorylase and glycogen synthase in opposite phosphorylation directions at once. Each fact turns on naming or reasoning through one mechanism correctly among closely related distractors.

## sections
### Definition
Six enzyme facts anchor this chapter. Antimycin A binds cytochrome b in Complex III of the electron transport chain and blocks electron transfer to cytochrome c. Substrate-level phosphorylation — direct phosphorylation from a reaction's own energy, not from the electron transport chain — happens once in the TCA cycle, at the succinyl-CoA synthetase step. Cytochrome P450 is a haem monooxygenase that inserts one oxygen atom into its substrate using NADPH, active on both endogenous and exogenous compounds. Chymotrypsin is a pancreatic endopeptidase specific for peptide bonds on the carboxyl side of aromatic residues (phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan). Acetyl-CoA cannot serve as a gluconeogenic substrate because the pyruvate dehydrogenase reaction that forms it is irreversible and its two carbons are exactly balanced by two lost as CO2 in one TCA turn. Epinephrine and glucagon, via cAMP/protein kinase A, phosphorylate glycogen phosphorylase into its active form and glycogen synthase into its inactive form simultaneously.

### Mechanism
Complex III sits between Complexes I/II and Complex IV in the chain; blocking it (antimycin) stops all downstream electron flow just as blocking Complex I (rotenone) or Complex IV (cyanide, azide) does, but at a different point — exam questions test the specific complex, not just "an ETC blocker". Substrate-level phosphorylation at succinyl-CoA synthetase generates GTP directly from the succinyl-CoA-to-succinate conversion, unlike the NAD+/FAD-linked dehydrogenase steps (isocitrate dehydrogenase, malate dehydrogenase, the aconitase step) that only hand off electrons for later oxidative phosphorylation. Cytochrome P450's monooxygenase mechanism reduces one oxygen atom to water while hydroxylating the substrate with the other — it is not a di-oxygenase, and "P450" names its 450 nm reduced-CO-complex absorbance peak, not an amino-acid count. Chymotrypsin's aromatic-residue specificity contrasts with trypsin (basic residues) and with the exopeptidases that trim single residues from chain ends rather than cutting mid-chain. Acetyl-CoA's exclusion from gluconeogenesis follows directly from carbon balance across one TCA turn, not from any transport restriction. Reciprocal phosphorylation of glycogen phosphorylase (activating) and glycogen synthase (inactivating) under the same hormonal signal is what lets one signal switch net glycogen flux cleanly in one direction.

### Key determinants
Each fact turns on distinguishing closely related options: Complex III vs I, II or IV; succinyl-CoA synthetase vs the NAD+/FAD-linked TCA steps; monooxygenase vs di-oxygenase, and endogenous-and-exogenous vs exogenous-only substrate scope for cytochrome P450; chymotrypsin vs trypsin vs the exopeptidases; acetyl-CoA vs lactate/alanine/glycerol as gluconeogenic substrates; and reciprocal (opposite-direction) vs uniform phosphorylation of glycogen phosphorylase and synthase.

### Clinical significance
Knowing exactly which ETC complex a poison or drug blocks (antimycin, rotenone, cyanide) is the basis for recognising and treating electron-transport-chain toxicity. Cytochrome P450's broad substrate range explains both drug metabolism and drug-drug interactions via enzyme induction/inhibition. Chymotrypsin and trypsin deficiency states (as in chronic pancreatitis or cystic fibrosis) present with different patterns of protein malabsorption depending on which protease activity is lost. The reciprocal phosphorylation of glycogen phosphorylase and synthase is the molecular basis for how glucagon (fasting) and epinephrine (stress) both mobilise glucose from liver glycogen without wasting energy synthesising and breaking down glycogen at once.

## summary
This chapter works through seven GI-physiology facts tested by name in the GIT and Nutrition final: what inhibits gastrin secretion, what relaxes (and what closes) the ileocecal sphincter, what triggers reflex vomiting, the biochemical signature of post-hepatic jaundice, what stimulates gastric secretion through the vagus, and two core gastric functions (storage of food, bactericidal HCl). Each fact turns on correctly assigning a stimulus or feature to the right structure among closely related distractors.

## sections
### Definition
Somatostatin, from antral D cells, inhibits gastrin secretion in a negative-feedback loop that limits acid output once the stomach is already acidic. The ileocecal sphincter relaxes with ileal distension, gastric distension and gastrin (the gastroileal reflex) but closes with caecal overdistension. Reflex vomiting is triggered directly by irritation of the gastric mucosa, distinct from the hormonal, metabolic and toxin-mediated routes of pregnancy, diabetic ketoacidosis and renal failure. Post-hepatic (obstructive) jaundice gives a direct van den Bergh reaction, falling urobilinogen, pale stool and dark urine. Hypoglycaemia stimulates gastric acid secretion through a vagal reflex, independent of food in the stomach. The stomach stores a meal and releases it gradually (its reservoir function), and its HCl kills most ingested bacteria before they reach the small intestine.

### Mechanism
Somatostatin's inhibitory role sits opposite to the stimulatory effects of protein digestion products and gastric distension on gastrin, forming the antral acid-brake loop. The ileocecal sphincter's directional logic — proximal distension (stomach, ileum) relaxes it to allow forward flow, distal distension (caecum) closes it to prevent reflux — is a spatial-reasoning fact, not a simple "distension relaxes sphincters" rule. Post-hepatic jaundice's biochemical picture follows directly from conjugated bilirubin being blocked from reaching the gut: no bilirubin means no urobilinogen and pale stool, while the water-soluble conjugated bilirubin backs up into blood and spills into urine, darkening it. The vagal (cephalic-type) reflex triggered by hypoglycaemia is the same reflex arc tested clinically by the insulin (hypoglycaemia) test of vagal completeness after vagotomy, and is distinct from the enterogastric reflex (duodenal hyperosmolarity, enterogastric hormones), which inhibits rather than stimulates gastric emptying/secretion.

### Key determinants
Each question turns on one specific assignment: somatostatin as the inhibitory outlier among gastrin-secretion factors; caecal overdistension as the one sphincter-closing factor among otherwise-relaxing distension stimuli; gastric mucosal irritation as a direct reflex trigger distinct from the hormonal/metabolic/toxin-mediated vomiting causes; the direct van den Bergh reaction plus pale stool and dark urine as the post-hepatic (not pre-hepatic or hepatic) jaundice pattern; the vagal reflex (not the inhibitory enterogastric reflex) as hypoglycaemia's route to increased gastric secretion; and storage/bactericidal action (not water/iron absorption or complete protein digestion) as genuine gastric functions.

### Clinical significance
The somatostatin-gastrin feedback loop is the physiological basis for somatostatin analogues (octreotide) used to suppress gastrin-driven acid hypersecretion in Zollinger-Ellison syndrome. Ileocecal valve competence matters clinically in bacterial overgrowth and in surgical planning around ileocaecal resection. Distinguishing post-hepatic jaundice's stool/urine pattern from pre-hepatic (unconjugated, normal stool/urine) and hepatic jaundice is a core bedside skill. The vagal hypoglycaemia (insulin) test is used clinically to confirm complete vagotomy in peptic ulcer surgery follow-up. Recognising the stomach's storage and bactericidal roles explains post-gastrectomy dumping syndrome and the increased enteric infection risk seen with achlorhydria or acid-suppressing drugs.

## summary
This chapter works through three surgical-anatomy facts tested by name in the GIT and Nutrition final: which vessel a pancreatic neck cyst compresses, which structure a quadrate-lobe liver injury involves, and where the gallbladder fundus surfaces on the anterior abdominal wall. Each fact turns on naming one correct structure among anatomically adjacent distractors.

## sections
### Definition
The pancreatic neck lies directly anterior to where the splenic vein joins the superior mesenteric vein to form the portal vein, so a cyst there compresses the portal vein directly. The quadrate lobe sits on the liver's visceral surface, bounded by the gallbladder fossa, the fissure for the ligamentum teres and the porta hepatis, and relates to the transverse colon. The gallbladder fundus projects at the tip of the right 9th costal cartilage, where the linea semilunaris crosses the costal margin — the anatomical basis of Murphy's point.

### Mechanism
Because the portal vein forms immediately posterior to the pancreatic neck, any mass effect there — cyst, pseudocyst, tumour — has a specific, predictable venous target, distinct from the left gastric vein, inferior mesenteric artery or superior pancreaticoduodenal vein. The quadrate lobe's relation to the transverse colon (rather than the right kidney, suprarenal gland or right colic flexure, which relate to the right lobe proper) reflects its position as a separate subdivision of the liver's visceral surface. The 9th-costal-cartilage/linea-semilunaris landmark for the gallbladder fundus is a fixed surface projection independent of the liver's own variable size and position.

### Key determinants
Each question turns on naming one correct structure among close anatomical neighbours: the portal vein (not the left gastric vein, inferior mesenteric artery, or superior pancreaticoduodenal vein) for the pancreatic-neck relation; the transverse colon (not the right kidney, suprarenal gland, or right colic flexure) for the quadrate-lobe relation; and the 9th costal cartilage (not the 7th, 8th or 10th) for the gallbladder fundus's surface marking.

### Clinical significance
Recognising the pancreatic neck's relation to the portal vein is essential in staging pancreatic masses (venous involvement changes resectability) and in understanding pancreatitis-related venous thrombosis. Awareness of the quadrate lobe's relations guards against iatrogenic bowel injury during hepatobiliary surgery in that region. The gallbladder fundus's 9th-costal-cartilage landmark underlies Murphy's point, used at the bedside to examine for acute cholecystitis.

## summary
This chapter works through three histology-identification facts tested by name in the GIT and Nutrition final: which liver cell stores vitamin A, which gastric cell is marked by apical zymogen granules, and where fungiform papillae are distributed on the tongue. Each fact turns on matching one structure or feature to the correct cell type among closely related distractors.

## sections
### Definition
Ito cells (hepatic stellate cells, fat-storing cells) sit in the space of Disse and store most of the body's vitamin A as retinyl ester droplets. Chief (zymogenic) cells, mainly at the base of the gastric glands, are identified by apical zymogen granules containing pepsinogen and gastric lipase. Fungiform papillae are mushroom-shaped and scattered especially at the tip and lateral margins of the tongue.

### Mechanism
Ito cells' vitamin A storage distinguishes them from the liver's other non-parenchymal cells: endothelial cells (line the sinusoids), Kupffer cells (resident macrophages, phagocytic), and cholangiocytes (line the bile ducts) — none of which store vitamin A. Chief cells' apical zymogen granules distinguish their secretory ultrastructure from parietal cells (extensive intracellular canaliculi, abundant mitochondria), mucous neck cells (apical mucinogen droplets) and enteroendocrine cells (small basal granules). Fungiform papillae's tip-of-tongue distribution, non-keratinised covering and relatively few taste buds contrast with circumvallate papillae (V-shaped row, most numerous taste buds) and filiform papillae (keratinised, no taste buds, most numerous overall).

### Key determinants
Each question turns on matching one feature to the correct cell type among close distractors: Ito cells (not endothelial, Kupffer or cholangiocyte cells) for vitamin A storage; chief cells (not parietal, mucous or enteroendocrine cells) for apical zymogen granules; and fungiform papillae's tip-of-tongue scattering, non-keratinised covering and modest taste-bud density (not circumvallate's numerous-taste-bud, most-numerous status, nor filiform's keratinised covering).

### Clinical significance
Ito cell activation and transformation into a myofibroblast-like, collagen-producing phenotype is the central cellular event in liver fibrosis, making this cell type clinically important well beyond its normal vitamin A storage role. Chief cell recognition underlies understanding of pepsinogen-driven peptic disease and of chief cell hyperplasia in some gastric pathology. Papillary distribution and structure underlie clinical assessment of taste and tongue surface changes (for example, atrophic glossitis flattening the papillae) seen in nutritional deficiency states.
