## summary
This chapter works through three closely related identity facts tested by the Final exam: which substage of meiosis I prophase hosts crossing over (pachytene), which base the tRNA TψC arm characteristically contains (thymine, an unusual base for RNA), and what reversible non-competitive enzyme inhibition does to Vmax (lowers it, without requiring structural resemblance to the substrate). Each turns on distinguishing the correct mechanism from a closely related but wrong option.

## sections
### Definition
Crossing over — reciprocal exchange of genetic material between paired homologous chromosomes — happens at pachytene, a named substage within meiosis I prophase, after full synapsis and before the chromosomes begin separating. The tRNA TψC arm is named for the three bases found there: thymine (unusual for RNA), pseudouridine and cytosine. Reversible non-competitive enzyme inhibition binds a site distinct from the substrate-binding site and lowers the maximum velocity (Vmax) attainable with a given amount of enzyme, without requiring any structural resemblance to the substrate.

### Mechanism
Meiosis I prophase has five substages (leptotene, zygotene, pachytene, diplotene, diakinesis); crossing over specifically needs the full synapsis achieved by zygotene, so it happens at pachytene, not at metaphase, anaphase or telophase, when chromosomes are already condensed or moving apart. The TψC arm's thymine is a genuine structural exception in an otherwise ribonucleotide molecule, distinguishing tRNA's tertiary fold from ordinary RNA; the same tRNA has other structural facts often paired against it as distractors — amino-acid specificity lives in the anticodon (not the acceptor arm), the acceptor arm's terminal sequence is 3'-CCA-OH (not "5' COOH"), and an average tRNA is roughly 75–90 nucleotides (not 275). Non-competitive inhibition binding elsewhere on the enzyme (or enzyme-substrate complex) removes usable enzyme from the pool regardless of substrate concentration, so no amount of added substrate can restore Vmax — unlike competitive inhibition, which raises the apparent Km but still reaches the same Vmax at high substrate.

### Key determinants
Pachytene vs the other four prophase-I substages (and vs the later stages of meiosis I); the TψC arm's thymine vs the acceptor arm's CCA end, the anticodon's amino-acid specificity, and tRNA's true average length; non-competitive inhibition's Vmax-lowering, Km-unchanged signature vs competitive inhibition's Km-raising, Vmax-reachable signature.

### Clinical significance
Errors during meiotic crossing over and chromosome segregation (of which pachytene is the recombination step) underlie several clinically important chromosomal disorders. Understanding tRNA's structural landmarks (acceptor arm, anticodon, TψC arm) underpins how antibiotics and other agents that target bacterial translation machinery are designed to be selective. Recognising the Vmax/Km signature of non-competitive vs competitive inhibition is the basis for classifying how a drug or toxin interacts with its target enzyme, which in turn predicts whether raising substrate/drug concentration can overcome the inhibition.

## summary
This chapter pairs two "match the tissue to the structure" identity facts: which intermediate filament protein builds skin epithelium (keratin, distinct from tubulin, vimentin and desmin) and which fibre arrangement gives the organ capsule its strength (dense irregular connective tissue, distinct from the tendon/ligament's dense regular arrangement).

## sections
### Definition
Intermediate filaments are tissue-specific: keratin in epithelium (including skin), vimentin in mesenchymal-derived cells, desmin in muscle — tubulin is a different cytoskeletal system (microtubules) entirely, not an intermediate filament at all. Dense irregular connective tissue has thick collagen bundles running in multiple, interwoven directions (the organ capsule, the dermis); dense regular connective tissue has parallel fibres along one axis (the tendon, the ligament).

### Mechanism
Keratin filaments anchor epidermal cells to each other (desmosomes) and to the basement membrane (hemidesmosomes), giving skin its mechanical resilience against shearing forces from multiple directions — consistent with skin's own multidirectional stress environment, much as the organ capsule's interwoven collagen resists stress from every direction around an organ's variably-shaped surface, while a tendon's parallel fibres are built to resist force along the single axis of muscle pull.

### Key determinants
Keratin (epithelium) vs vimentin (mesenchyme) vs desmin (muscle) vs tubulin (not an intermediate filament at all); dense irregular (multidirectional fibres — capsule, dermis) vs dense regular (parallel fibres — tendon, ligament) vs loose connective tissue (the lamina propria distractor, neither dense pattern).

### Clinical significance
Loss of keratin's structural integrity (as in epidermolysis bullosa simplex, a keratin gene mutation) causes skin fragility and blistering, illustrating why the correct filament-to-tissue assignment matters beyond the exam. Surgical planning around organs relies on knowing that a capsule (dense irregular) behaves differently under tension and suturing than a tendon or ligament repair (dense regular).

## summary
This chapter covers three hematology facts from the Final exam: the band cell as the neutrophil's immature form, kidney failure's mechanism for depressing bone-marrow erythropoiesis (via reduced erythropoietin), and the rise in plasma potassium that occurs during blood storage.

## sections
### Definition
The band cell is the neutrophil's immature form, with an unsegmented, curved (band-shaped) nucleus rather than the mature neutrophil's multi-lobed nucleus; a rising band-cell count ("left shift") signals an acute inflammatory or infectious response. The kidney is the principal erythropoietin source, so kidney failure lowers erythropoietin and, with it, bone-marrow red-cell production. Stored blood undergoes a "storage lesion": red cells slowly leak intracellular potassium into the plasma, so plasma K+ rises the longer blood is stored.

### Mechanism
Neutrophils mature through band forms before reaching the fully segmented (2–5 lobe) adult form; the mature neutrophil's true features (multi-lobed nucleus, neutrophilic — not acidophilic — granules, and the presence of azurophilic granules alongside specific granules) are frequently misstated as distractors alongside the band-cell fact. Erythropoietin drives marrow erythropoiesis; its loss in renal failure is the mechanistic basis of the anemia of chronic kidney disease, distinct from a primary bone-marrow lesion (which causes aplastic-type failure, not specifically megaloblastic anemia) or hypoxia (which stimulates, not inhibits, erythropoietin). Stored red cells also lose 2,3-DPG (impairing oxygen offloading after transfusion) and consume glucose, producing lactate — both falling, not rising, alongside potassium's rise, a detail this exam's distractors deliberately reverse.

### Key determinants
Band cell (immature) vs the mature neutrophil's true nuclear/granule features; kidney failure/low erythropoietin vs a primary bone-marrow lesion or hypoxia as causes of impaired erythropoiesis; potassium rising vs 2,3-DPG and glucose falling during blood storage.

### Clinical significance
A rising band-cell (left-shift) count on a complete blood count is a bedside marker of acute bacterial infection or inflammation. Renal anemia (from erythropoietin deficiency) is treated with recombinant erythropoietin in chronic kidney disease. The storage-lesion potassium rise is clinically relevant in massive or rapid transfusion, where a large potassium load can precipitate arrhythmia, especially in neonates or patients with renal impairment.

## summary
This chapter works through nine physiology facts spanning membrane transport, the autonomic nervous system and renal water regulation: symport vs antiport, Fick's law and membrane thickness, amino acids and secondary active transport, sympathetic inhibition of intestinal secretion, vagal bronchoconstriction, hypovolemia and juxtaglomerular cells, angiotensin II and ADH, adrenaline in bronchial asthma, and pancreatic lipase as an extracellular enzyme.

## sections
### Definition
Symport (co-transport) moves two solutes in the same direction using one's downhill gradient to drive the other uphill (Na+/amino acid); antiport moves them in opposite directions (Na+/H+, Ca2+/Na+); the Na+/K+ pump is primary active transport, using ATP directly. Fick's law makes diffusion rate directly proportional to concentration gradient, surface area and temperature, but inversely proportional to membrane thickness. Secondary active transport (Na+-coupled carriers) is what amino acid absorption depends on, unlike urea, alcohol and O2, which cross by simple diffusion. Sympathetic stimulation inhibits intestinal secretion and motility and insulin release while promoting glycogenolysis; vagal (cranial parasympathetic) stimulation constricts bronchioles, distinct from the sacral parasympathetic outflow that controls micturition. Hypovolemia stimulates juxtaglomerular-cell renin release; downstream angiotensin II increases ADH secretion (alongside thirst and aldosterone). Adrenaline treats bronchial asthma via beta-2 bronchodilation. Pancreatic lipase, secreted into the gut lumen, is a genuine extracellular enzyme, unlike lactate dehydrogenase, cytochrome oxidase or hexokinase.

### Mechanism
Each of these facts turns on distinguishing the correct mechanism from a closely related but different one: symport from antiport and from primary active transport; the one inversely-proportional Fick's-law variable (thickness) from the three directly-proportional ones; secondary active transport's Na+-gradient dependence from simple diffusion's independence from any transporter; sympathetic effects from the "activates everything" assumption (it specifically inhibits GI secretion/motility/insulin while promoting glycogenolysis); vagal bronchoconstriction from sympathetic bronchodilation and from the separate sacral parasympathetic outflow governing micturition; the JG-cell pressure/volume sensor from the hypothalamic osmoreceptor sensor; angiotensin II's water-retention-promoting direction on all four of its targets (thirst, aldosterone, ADH, ANP) from a reversed distractor; adrenaline's beta-2 bronchodilation from beta-3's metabolic role and beta-1's chronotropic role; and pancreatic lipase's genuinely extracellular site of action from intracellular enzymes that merely leak into blood after cell damage.

### Key determinants
Symport vs antiport vs primary active transport; membrane thickness (inverse) vs gradient/area/temperature (direct) in Fick's law; amino acids (secondary active transport-dependent) vs urea/alcohol/O2 (simple diffusion); sympathetic inhibition vs a naive "sympathetic activates everything" assumption; vagal cranial vs sacral parasympathetic outflow; JG-cell pressure sensing vs hypothalamic osmolarity sensing; angiotensin II's coordinated water-retention direction across four targets; beta-2 (asthma) vs beta-1 (heart rate) vs beta-3 (metabolism) adrenergic effects; and extracellular (pancreatic lipase) vs intracellular (LDH, cytochrome oxidase, hexokinase) enzyme localisation.

### Clinical significance
Symporter and antiporter defects underlie several inherited transport disorders (e.g. cystinuria, a defective amino acid symporter). Sympathetic and vagal balance is manipulated pharmacologically across cardiology, pulmonology and gastroenterology (beta-blockers, bronchodilators, prokinetics). The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system is a major drug target (ACE inhibitors, ARBs) in hypertension and heart failure. Recognising beta-receptor subtype selectivity (beta-2 for asthma, beta-1 for heart rate, beta-3 for metabolism) underlies rational bronchodilator and cardiac drug selection. Serum enzyme levels (LDH, and others normally intracellular) are used diagnostically precisely because their normal site of action is intracellular, and their appearance in blood signals cell damage.

## summary
This chapter works through nine biochemistry facts spanning lipids, amino acids/peptides and vitamins: linoleic acid as a true essential fatty acid, cerebroside composition, glutathione's hydrolytic products, threonine as a hydroxyl-bearing essential amino acid, and five vitamin deficiency syndromes (A, K, B1, B12, folic acid).

## sections
### Definition
Linoleic acid is a true essential fatty acid (the body cannot synthesise it); arachidonic acid is only conditionally essential, since it can be made from linoleic acid. Cerebrosides are ceramide (sphingosine + fatty acid) plus a single sugar (galactose), with no glycerol and no phosphate — distinct from sphingomyelin, which has phosphate but no sugar. Glutathione hydrolyses to exactly glutamic acid, cysteine and glycine. Threonine is the essential amino acid bearing a hydroxyl group (serine and tyrosine also have one but are non-essential). Vitamin A deficiency causes night blindness, xerophthalmia and xeroderma; vitamin K deficiency causes hemorrhagic disease of the newborn; vitamin B1 (thiamine) deficiency causes beriberi; vitamin B12 deficiency on a vegetarian diet takes years to manifest (large hepatic stores); folic acid's core function is one-carbon metabolism.

### Mechanism
Each fact turns on a specific distinction: linoleic acid's true dietary essentiality vs arachidonic acid's conditional essentiality (synthesisable from linoleic acid); the cerebroside's sphingosine/fatty-acid/galactose composition vs sphingomyelin's phosphate-containing, sugar-free one; glutathione's exact three-amino-acid hydrolysis products vs near-miss distractor amino acids (serine, glutamine, taurine); threonine's dual property (essential and hydroxyl-bearing) vs serine/tyrosine (hydroxyl-bearing but not essential in the same sense); vitamin A's visual-cycle/epithelial role vs C/D/E's distinct deficiency pictures; vitamin K's role in gamma-carboxylating clotting factors, low at birth, vs B6/B2/C deficiency; thiamine pyrophosphate's role as a coenzyme (pyruvate dehydrogenase, alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, transketolase) vs the other numbered B vitamins; B12's large hepatic stores giving years-long latency vs folate's smaller stores and months-long latency; and folate's specific one-carbon-transfer role (purine and thymidylate synthesis, homocysteine remethylation) vs unrelated metabolic pathways offered as distractors.

### Key determinants
Linoleic (essential) vs arachidonic (conditionally essential) vs oleic/palmitic (non-essential) fatty acids; cerebroside vs sphingomyelin composition; glutathione's exact three components vs near-miss substitutions; threonine vs serine/tyrosine/valine/tryptophan/phenylalanine among hydroxyl-bearing and essential amino acids; and the distinct clinical pictures of vitamin A, K, B1, B12 and folate deficiency.

### Clinical significance
Essential fatty acid deficiency (rare, but seen in severe malabsorption or fat-free parenteral nutrition) causes dermatitis and impaired wound healing. Cerebroside and sphingomyelin metabolism defects underlie the sphingolipidoses (e.g. Gaucher disease, Niemann-Pick disease). Glutathione's cysteine thiol is central to cellular antioxidant defence and drug/toxin detoxification (e.g. acetaminophen overdose). Vitamin A deficiency remains a leading cause of preventable blindness worldwide; vitamin K is given prophylactically to newborns specifically to prevent hemorrhagic disease of the newborn; thiamine is given before glucose in suspected Wernicke's encephalopathy to avoid precipitating it; B12 deficiency causes both megaloblastic anemia and (unlike folate deficiency) neurological damage if uncorrected; folate supplementation in pregnancy prevents neural tube defects, a direct consequence of impaired one-carbon metabolism during embryogenesis.
