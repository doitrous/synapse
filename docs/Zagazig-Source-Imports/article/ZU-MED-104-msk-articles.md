## summary
This chapter works through four hand/wrist/forearm facts tested by name in the MSK final: which finger defines the hand's own axis of abduction/adduction, which extensor compartment a mass must compress to selectively lose little-finger extension, why a medial epicondyle fracture only weakens (not abolishes) wrist flexion, and which wrist structure is spared in a severe fall-related wrist injury. Each turns on naming the right structure or degree of deficit among closely related distractors.

## sections
### Definition
The axis of the hand runs through the middle finger, the reference line against which the other digits abduct (away from it) and adduct (toward it). The extensor retinaculum divides the back of the wrist into six compartments, of which the fifth holds extensor digiti minimi alone. The ulnar nerve grooves the back of the medial epicondyle. A severe fall onto the wrist typically loads the radial side (distal radius, scaphoid, triangular fibrocartilage complex), leaving the distal ulna comparatively spared.

### Mechanism
A mass compressing the fifth extensor compartment entraps only extensor digiti minimi, so the deficit is confined to the little finger, sparing the ring/middle/index fingers (extensor digitorum, in compartment 4) and wrist extension itself (extensor carpi radialis longus/brevis and extensor carpi ulnaris, compartments 2 and 6). A medial epicondyle fracture injures the ulnar nerve, removing flexor carpi ulnaris and the ulnar half of flexor digitorum profundus from the wrist flexor group — but flexor carpi radialis (median) and the radial half of flexor digitorum profundus are untouched, so the deficit is weakness, not loss, of flexion. Because the distal radius bears most of the axial load transmitted through the wrist, a severe fall-related wrist injury classically damages the radius, scaphoid and TFCC while the ulnar head, not directly articulating with the carpus, tends to remain structurally normal.

### Key determinants
Each question turns on one precise discrimination: middle finger (not the wrist's own long axis) as the hand's reference line; the fifth compartment specifically (not any compartment) for isolated little-finger loss; weakness (not loss) of wrist flexion after an isolated ulnar nerve lesion, because a median-supplied flexor remains; and the distal ulna (not the radius, scaphoid, or TFCC) as the structure spared in a radial-sided wrist injury.

### Clinical significance
Recognising the fifth-compartment pattern lets a clinician localise a ganglion or tenosynovitis to extensor digiti minimi specifically. Grading an ulnar nerve wrist deficit as weakness rather than loss avoids over-stating a partial nerve injury's severity. Correctly predicting which wrist structures are damaged versus spared in a fall-related injury guides which structures to image first (distal radius and scaphoid) when a patient presents after a FOOSH-type mechanism.

## summary
This chapter works through five lower-limb facts tested by name in the MSK final: why gluteal intramuscular injections target the upper lateral quadrant, which muscle protects the knee's synovial membrane during extension, which muscle produces the tailor's sitting position, why tibialis anterior survives a fibula fracture that disables its neighbours, and why a tibial nerve injury weakens rather than abolishes inversion.

## sections
### Definition
The gluteal region's upper lateral quadrant overlies gluteus medius, away from the sciatic nerve's course through the lower quadrants. Articularis genu, a detached slip of vastus intermedius, inserts into the suprapatellar synovial membrane and bursa. Sartorius crosses both hip and knee and, acting alone among the thigh flexors listed, can flex, abduct and laterally rotate the hip while flexing the knee — the combination needed for the tailor position. Tibialis anterior arises mainly from the tibia, unlike its three anterior-compartment neighbours, which arise mainly from the fibula. The tibial nerve supplies the whole posterior leg compartment, including tibialis posterior, the chief invertor.

### Mechanism
An intramuscular injection in the upper lateral gluteal quadrant avoids the sciatic nerve, the inferior gluteal neurovascular bundle and the pudendal neurovascular bundle, all of which run through the lower and medial quadrants. Articularis genu contracts with the quadriceps during extension to pull the redundant suprapatellar synovial membrane and bursa proximally, preventing it from being pinched between femur and patella. Sartorius's combined hip flexion/abduction/lateral-rotation and knee flexion is what the other listed thigh flexors (pectineus, adductor longus/brevis, all adductors rather than abductors) cannot reproduce. Because extensor hallucis longus, extensor digitorum longus and peroneus tertius attach mainly to the fibula, a fibula fracture damaging them leaves tibialis anterior — tibia-attached — functioning, even though all four share the deep peroneal nerve. Tibial nerve injury denervates tibialis posterior, the strongest invertor, but tibialis anterior (deep peroneal nerve) still contributes a smaller inversion component, so the deficit is weakness rather than loss.

### Key determinants
Quadrant (upper lateral, not any quadrant) for safe gluteal injection; articularis genu (not vastus medialis/lateralis) for synovial membrane retraction; sartorius (not the adductor-group distractors) for the tailor position; bony origin (tibial vs fibular), not shared nerve supply, for which anterior-compartment muscle survives a fibula fracture; and weakness (not loss) of inversion after an isolated tibial nerve lesion, because tibialis anterior's contribution persists.

### Clinical significance
The upper-lateral-quadrant rule is the anatomical basis of safe intramuscular injection technique. Recognising articularis genu's role explains an otherwise puzzling synovial-membrane injury pattern during forced knee extension. The tibialis-anterior/fibula-fracture pattern and the tibial-nerve/inversion-weakness pattern both illustrate that predicting a post-traumatic deficit requires tracking both the bony attachment and the nerve supply, not either alone.

## summary
This chapter works through three histology facts tested by name in the MSK final: which sarcomere band contains thick (myosin) filaments exclusively, which hair follicle region holds the stem cells for hair growth, and which dermal feature distinguishes thin skin from thick skin.

## sections
### Definition
The A band of the striated-muscle sarcomere is the zone defined by, and containing, thick (myosin) filaments; thin (actin) filaments run from the Z line into the I band and partway into the A band. The hair matrix, a cap of proliferative cells at the base of the hair bulb surrounding the dermal papilla, is the hair follicle's stem-cell region. Thin skin, covering most of the body, has a dermis with few dermal papillae, unlike the numerous tall papillae of thick skin (palms and soles).

### Mechanism
Thick filaments occupy the A band alone; where thin filaments overlap them (the outer A band) the two coexist, but only the A band as a whole is defined by thick-filament presence — the I band (thin filaments only) and the H zone at rest (thick filaments only, no overlap) are distinguished by that same principle. Hair matrix cells divide continually, driven by dermal papilla signalling, to produce the hair shaft and internal root sheath — the papilla itself is connective tissue, not the proliferative epithelial source. Thin skin's flatter dermo-epidermal junction (few, shallow papillae) contrasts with thick skin's numerous tall papillae, which interdigitate with a thick, ridged epidermis to resist mechanical stress and produce fingerprints; thin skin nonetheless retains hair follicles, sensory corpuscles and melanophores.

### Key determinants
A band (not I band or "crossed by the Z line") for thick-filament location; hair matrix (not dermal papilla or either root sheath) for the follicle's stem-cell region; and "numerous dermal papillae" as the one listed feature that is not a character of thin skin's dermis, since hair follicles, melanophores and sensory corpuscles all are.

### Clinical significance
Correct sarcomere-band anatomy underlies interpreting muscle biopsy electron micrographs. The hair matrix's identity as the follicle's proliferative compartment explains why chemotherapy (which targets rapidly dividing cells) characteristically causes hair loss. Recognising thin skin's sparse dermal papillae versus thick skin's dense papillae explains the regional difference in tactile discrimination and fingerprint formation.

## summary
This chapter works through five physiology facts tested by name in the MSK final: what distinguishes smooth muscle's contractility from skeletal muscle's, what causes the treppe (staircase) phenomenon, why myasthenia gravis is potentially fatal, which factors decrease nerve excitability, and which ion current sustains the cardiac action potential's plateau phase.

## sections
### Definition
Smooth muscle's defining contractile property is a sustained ("latch") contraction maintained longer than skeletal muscle can sustain, without corresponding early fatigue. Treppe (staircase) is a small, progressive rise in twitch tension over the first several stimuli of a low-frequency train. Myasthenia gravis is autoimmune neuromuscular-junction disease (anti-acetylcholine-receptor antibodies) that can become fatal if it involves the respiratory muscles. Nerve excitability is raised by warming, alkalosis and decreased extracellular calcium, and lowered by mechanical pressure, cooling and acidosis. The cardiac ventricular action potential's plateau (phase 2) is sustained mainly by calcium influx through L-type calcium channels.

### Mechanism
Smooth muscle sustains tension at low energy cost and resists fatigue, unlike skeletal muscle's rapid-onset, fatigue-prone contraction; it also shows stress relaxation (falling tension on sustained stretch) rather than continuously rising tension. Treppe arises because each stimulus releases sarcoplasmic-reticulum calcium faster than the muscle's pumps can re-sequester it, so residual cytosolic calcium accumulates over the first few twitches, increasing cross-bridge activation until a steady state is reached — a calcium-handling mechanism, distinct from the temporal summation that produces tetanus. Myasthenic weakness, when it reaches the diaphragm and intercostals, can progress to a myasthenic crisis requiring ventilatory support. Mechanical pressure on a nerve trunk distorts the axon and impairs ion channel function, raising the excitation threshold — the opposite direction from warming, alkalosis or low extracellular calcium, which lower the threshold. The cardiac plateau's calcium influx both sustains membrane depolarisation (balanced against a small K+ efflux) and triggers calcium-induced calcium release, coupling excitation to contraction, and it is what gives cardiac muscle its long refractory period, preventing tetany of the heart.

### Key determinants
Sustained/latch contraction (not rapid onset or continuously rising tension) for smooth muscle; increased SR calcium release (not K+/Na+/temperature changes) for treppe; respiratory muscle involvement (not exercise response or sex distribution) as myasthenia's fatal mechanism; mechanical pressure grouped with cooling/acidosis as excitability-lowering (not with warming/alkalosis/low calcium, which raise it); and calcium (not sodium or potassium) as the plateau-phase current.

### Clinical significance
Smooth muscle's latch state underlies sustained tone in vascular and visceral smooth muscle without energy exhaustion. Treppe is the physiological basis for an athlete's warm-up improving initial contractile performance. Recognising myasthenic crisis as a respiratory emergency changes management priority from symptomatic treatment to airway/ventilatory support. The mechanical-pressure-lowers-excitability fact explains pressure-induced (tourniquet, positional) nerve palsies. The calcium-dependent cardiac plateau is the pharmacological target of calcium channel blockers used in some arrhythmias.
