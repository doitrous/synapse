## summary
This chapter works through seven bioethics-and-law facts tested by name across this module's Final and past-exam papers: the justice principle applied to both resource distribution and organ-transplant allocation, vertical equity as need-based care, the emergency exception to informed consent, who can consent on behalf of a mentally incapacitated patient, what is (and is not) a justified breach of confidentiality, and what proper conduct is required of a student on first contact with a patient. Each fact turns on naming the right principle or rule among adjacent-sounding distractors, not on a broader mechanism.

## sections
### Definition
Justice is the bioethical principle governing fair distribution — of scarce healthcare resources broadly, and of specific goods like organ transplants, without discrimination by race, gender, socioeconomic status, ethnicity or religion. Vertical equity is the related but distinct concept of allocating care according to differing need, rather than identical treatment for everyone (horizontal equity) or one-size-fits-all "equality". Informed consent is presumed necessary before any procedure, with a narrow emergency exception for lifesaving operations when the patient cannot consent. Mental incapacity raises the question of who may consent on a patient's behalf. Confidentiality may be justifiably breached only for patient consent, public-good infectious-disease reporting, or forensic court testimony — not to showcase a physician's own skill. Proper student-patient contact requires staff permission, disclosure of student status, and patient consent, together.

### Mechanism
Justice as a principle sits alongside autonomy (respecting patient choice), beneficence (acting for patient benefit) and non-maleficence (avoiding harm) as one of the four core bioethical principles — it is invoked specifically when a scenario is about who gets access to something scarce, not about respecting an individual's wishes or weighing benefit against harm for one patient. Vertical equity operationalises justice by prescribing that need, not uniform treatment, should drive resource allocation. The emergency exception to consent exists because the presumption is that a reasonable patient facing imminent death would consent to lifesaving treatment if able — it does not extend to non-emergency or cosmetic procedures, where the ordinary consent requirement still applies regardless of time pressure. Confidentiality's justified-breach list (consent, public good, legal duty) is a closed, specific set — a physician's own professional interest in showcasing skill is not on it, however tempting a rationalisation.

### Key determinants
Each question in this chapter turns on picking the one correct principle or rule among adjacent options: justice vs autonomy/beneficence/non-maleficence (distribution/allocation vs individual choice/benefit/harm); vertical vs horizontal equity vs plain equality (need-based vs equal-need-based vs identical); the emergency lifesaving exception vs "any procedure"/non-life-threatening/cosmetic (narrowly scoped to life-or-death urgency); a mentally incapacitated patient's consent proxy (this source names the treating physician — flagged for reviewer cross-check, see the concept's own uncertainty note); confidentiality's justified-breach list (consent/public-good/forensic testimony) vs showcasing skill (not justified); and student-patient conduct requiring all three of permission, disclosure and consent together, not any one alone.

### Clinical significance
Distinguishing justice from the other three bioethics principles is what lets a learner correctly reason through resource-allocation dilemmas (triage, transplant lists, rationed care) without conflating them with individual-patient-benefit questions. Knowing the emergency consent exception's true scope prevents both under-treating a dying patient who cannot consent and over-extending the exception to routine or elective care. The confidentiality justified-breach list is directly tested in professional-conduct and licensing contexts, where "I wanted to demonstrate my skill" is never an acceptable defence for disclosure. Proper student-patient contact conduct (permission, disclosure, consent) is a foundational clinical-skills-course expectation students are held to from their very first patient encounter.

## summary
This chapter works through ten core-professionalism facts tested across this module's Final papers: when the physician-patient relationship formally begins, what it is built on, the base precondition in an accountability model, the checklist tool's assessment use, the self-assessment/feedback and reflective-practice components of professionalism, compassion, the purpose of early professionalism education, why professionalism supports public trust, and a physician's key duty to colleagues. Each fact names one specific professionalism concept among plausible-sounding neighbours.

## sections
### Definition
The physician-patient relationship formally begins when the patient selects the physician (not at the first care visit, billing, or diagnosis) and is built on trust and communication (not financial or transactional elements). An accountability-preconditions model places ability at its base. A checklist tool is used to assess student satisfaction. Professionalism's self-assessment-and-feedback component covers using peer feedback to identify improvement areas; the best self-assessment method is reflective practice. Compassion is showing empathy and care towards patients. Early professionalism education exists chiefly to prevent later disciplinary action. Maintaining professionalism supports public trust in doctors as a group. A key physician duty to colleagues is education and skills transfer.

### Mechanism
These facts each isolate one professionalism construct from a set of plausible neighbours: the relationship's formal *onset* (selection) is a distinct milestone from its first clinical *encounter* (going for care); its foundation (trust/communication) is distinct from its administrative consequences (billing). An accountability model's precondition chain treats ability as foundational because responsibility (being assigned a task) presupposes the capacity to carry it out. Self-assessment specifically means the learner judging their own performance — peer and external evaluation are valuable but categorically different feedback sources, and reflective practice is the named "best method" among them. Professionalism education's preventive purpose (heading off disciplinary action before it occurs) is why curricula introduce it early rather than only after a lapse. Public trust is professionalism's outward-facing rationale — it is not served by inter-colleague competitiveness, challenging seniority, or self-interest, which professionalism norms actively discourage.

### Key determinants
Each question turns on distinguishing one specific professionalism construct from its near neighbours: relationship onset (selection) vs first encounter (care-seeking); relationship foundation (trust/communication) vs administrative elements (billing, social ties); accountability's base precondition (ability) vs later-stage elements (authority, responsibility); checklist tool's specific use (satisfaction) vs administrative metrics it does not measure (attendance, graduation); self-assessment (learner judging themselves, best via reflective practice) vs peer/external evaluation (someone else judging the learner); professionalism education's purpose (prevention) vs implausible distractors; and duties to colleagues (peer education) vs duties to the wider community (public health education, resource stewardship, disease reporting).

### Clinical significance
Recognising exactly when and on what a physician-patient relationship is founded matters for questions of duty, abandonment, and liability that hinge on whether a relationship legally exists yet. The accountability-model and self-assessment facts underpin how professionalism curricula structure student evaluation and remediation. Understanding professionalism's public-trust rationale — rather than treating professionalism rules as arbitrary — helps a learner reason through novel professionalism scenarios by asking "does this action support or undermine public trust in the profession", the same test the source questions themselves apply.

## summary
This chapter works through seven self-awareness, emotional-intelligence and goal-setting facts drawn from a compiled past-exam bank: what remedies a pattern of wrong decisions rooted in low self-awareness, what a good level of self-awareness leads to, what affects self-awareness and where it comes from, the definitions of emotional intelligence and goal setting, and what lacking self-awareness can cause. Each fact names one specific construct among adjacent soft-skills distractors (time management, leadership, management).

## sections
### Definition
Self-awareness is understanding one's own motivations, preferences and personality, and how these shape one's judgment and interactions. A person who habitually makes wrong decisions because they lack this understanding needs to increase their self-awareness — studying more or withdrawing socially does not address the actual deficit. A good level of self-awareness leads to setting appropriate life and career goals; a lack of it can result in wrong decisions with negative consequences. Self-awareness is affected by, and can be gained from, one's own behaviour and perception, and specifically from self-analysis. Emotional intelligence is the ability to understand and manage one's own emotions and those of people around oneself. Goal setting is identifying life/work priorities and developing strategies to attain personal and professional objectives.

### Mechanism
These questions each isolate one construct from soft-skills neighbours that share vocabulary but differ in scope: self-awareness (understanding one's own motivations/personality) is a narrower, foundational component of the broader construct of emotional intelligence, which additionally requires managing emotions and reading/responding to others' emotions — so a stem describing awareness of one's own emotions plus managing others' points to EQ, not self-awareness alone. Goal setting is specifically about identifying priorities and objectives, a narrower and earlier step than the broader coordination that "management" implies. Self-awareness's own two-way relationship with behaviour and perception (each shapes and is informed by the other) and its buildable, not-fixed nature (self-analysis is a real, actionable source) are the two facts this compiled bank repeats with only minor wording changes across near-duplicate items.

### Key determinants
Each question turns on distinguishing self-awareness, emotional intelligence and goal setting from one another and from time-management/leadership distractors: self-awareness-as-remedy (increase self-understanding) vs implausible study/avoidance distractors; self-awareness's positive/negative consequences (appropriate goals vs wrong decisions) framed as opposite-polarity item pairs; self-awareness's affectors/sources (behaviour, perception, self-analysis) vs false-absolute distractors (it cannot be improved, it is unaffected by X); emotional intelligence's fuller definition (managing one's own AND others' emotions) vs self-awareness's narrower one (understanding oneself only); and goal setting's specific scope (priorities/strategies) vs management's broader one.

### Clinical significance
Self-awareness and emotional intelligence are foundational, curriculum-wide "professional identity formation" constructs that recur across a medical career — in giving and receiving feedback, managing patient interactions under stress, and recognising one's own biases or limits. Goal setting underpins the structured personal-development planning most medical curricula require of students from Year 1 onward. Distinguishing these constructs precisely (rather than treating "soft skills" as one undifferentiated category) is itself the skill this module's own exam questions are testing.

## summary
This chapter works through six team-dynamics and leadership facts tested across all three sources: the storming stage of team development, the definition of a clinical "core team", negotiation as a leadership skill, compromising as a conflict-resolution strategy, democratic leadership style's defining behaviour, and the general definition of leadership itself. Each fact names one specific construct among adjacent team/leadership vocabulary.

## sections
### Definition
In the standard forming-storming-norming-performing-adjourning model, storming is the stage marked by conflict between team members and resistance to assigned tasks. A clinical "core team" is the group of members directly involved in caring for patients, distinct from operational-management staff, event-specific groups, or support staff. Negotiation is the leadership skill of understanding all parties' interests and working toward a mutually satisfying solution. Compromising is the conflict-resolution strategy that resolves a problem short-term while working toward a longer-term solution. Democratic leadership style is defined by encouraging others to volunteer for responsibilities and confirming their commitments. Leadership itself is the ability to mobilize and/or influence a group of individuals to achieve results.

### Mechanism
Storming is distinguished from performing (the team functions smoothly) and forming (initial orientation, no real friction yet) by the presence of active conflict over roles and tasks — a stage every functioning team model expects a team to pass through, not a sign of team failure. Core team membership is defined by the criterion of direct patient-care involvement, not by administrative rank or event-specificity, which is why cleaners/domestic staff and day-to-day operational managers, despite being part of the wider care organisation, fall outside the "core team" as tested here. Negotiation and compromising are related but distinct: negotiation is a leadership skill/process (understanding interests, working toward a solution), while compromising is a specific conflict-resolution outcome/strategy (a short-term fix pursued alongside a longer-term one) — they can co-occur but are not synonyms. Democratic leadership is defined by active solicitation of buy-in (asking for votes, confirming commitments) as opposed to autocratic styles that withhold votes or hoard responsibilities. Leadership's own general definition (mobilize/influence a group toward results) is distinguished from management (resource/process coordination), membership (belonging without necessarily directing) and administration (organisational oversight).

### Key determinants
Each question turns on picking the one correct construct among team/leadership neighbours: storming vs the other four Tuckman stages (conflict/resistance is storming's defining feature); core team vs administrative/event-specific/support groupings (direct patient-care involvement is the deciding criterion); negotiation vs conflict-resolution generally or plain communication (the goal-directed, multi-party-interest-satisfying process specifically); compromising vs competing/accommodating/avoiding (a mutual, partial concession pursued alongside a longer-term fix); democratic vs autocratic leadership behaviours (soliciting votes/commitments vs withholding them); and leadership vs management/membership/administration (mobilizing and influencing people toward results, specifically).

### Clinical significance
Recognising storming as an expected, not pathological, stage helps a learner respond constructively to early team friction rather than treating it as a sign of team dysfunction. Correctly scoping "core team" membership matters for handoff, confidentiality, and consent-sharing decisions about who is entitled to full clinical information. Distinguishing negotiation, compromising and democratic leadership by name is directly tested in professionalism curricula that expect students to name, not just practise, these behaviours.

## summary
This chapter works through four time-management and stress facts drawn from the past-exam bank: what time management means, the prioritization principle behind it, the definition of stress, and the to-do list as a daily prioritization tool. Each fact rejects a common misconception (working constantly, refusing delegation, uniform stress response, stress as fixed and single-source) in favour of the correct, more nuanced statement.

## sections
### Definition
Time management means spending the time available to you much more effectively (or, worded differently in a second copy of the same bank, "productively") — not eliminating leisure, refusing to delegate, or simply rushing tasks. It specifically requires deciding what is really important and what can wait. Stress is the gap between our expectations and reality, and is a normal part of life. A to-do list is the tool that helps decide what is really important to get done today versus what can wait.

### Mechanism
Each of these facts corrects a specific misconception: "spend more time working" is not the same as "manage time effectively" — effective time management can include delegating tasks and preserving rest, which the distractors explicitly (and incorrectly) rule out. Time management's underlying mechanism is prioritization — sorting tasks by real importance rather than urgency or habit — which the to-do list operationalises as a concrete daily tool. Stress's definition as an expectation-reality gap explains why it is universal (everyone has expectations) yet individually variable (everyone's reality and expectations differ) — directly contradicting the distractor claim that "all people respond exactly the same way".

### Key determinants
Each question turns on rejecting an over-generalised or false-absolute distractor in favour of the correctly-hedged statement: time management as effective/productive time use (not maximised hours, no delegation, or simple speed); prioritization as deciding what's important vs what can wait (not an inborn, fixed trait); stress as a normal, expectation-driven gap (not a uniform response, not independent of time management, not single-sourced); and the to-do list as the specific tool named for this daily-prioritization purpose (not a goal, which operates at a longer time horizon, or delegation, a different skill entirely).

### Clinical significance
Time-management and stress literacy are foundational self-management skills a medical curriculum expects students to apply to their own study and clinical workload from Year 1, and the distinction between managing time (a learnable, delegatable skill) and simply working harder (not the same thing) is directly relevant to preventing burnout later in training.
