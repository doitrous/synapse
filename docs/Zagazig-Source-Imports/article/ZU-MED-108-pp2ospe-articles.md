## summary
This chapter covers what makes a chest compression effective during CPR: the "push hard and fast" mnemonic for depth and rate, and the correct hand position over the lower half of the sternum, distinguished from the different hand position used for abdominal thrusts in choking.

## sections
### Definition
"Push hard and fast" is the key phrase for compression quality: push the chest down fully (roughly two inches/5cm in an adult) at a rate of at least 100 per minute, allowing full recoil between compressions. Correct hand position is the heel of one hand on the centre of the chest, over the lower half of the sternum, with the second hand on top.

### Mechanism
Compressing the lower sternum, the strongest part of the bony thorax, squeezes the heart between the sternum and the spine, generating forward blood flow. Compressing too shallow, too slow, or over the wrong landmark (such as the upper abdomen) fails to generate this flow and, in the abdomen's case, risks injuring organs such as the liver instead.

### Key determinants
Compression-quality questions turn on the "push hard and fast" mnemonic (depth and rate together), not on checking the pulse between compressions, which is never done during CPR. Hand-position questions turn on the lower sternum, not the upper abdomen, which is instead the landmark for an entirely different technique: the abdominal thrust used in choking, not chest compressions.

### Clinical significance
Getting both compression quality and hand position right is what allows chest compressions to actually generate the circulation a patient in cardiac arrest needs; either error, too shallow/slow or wrongly placed, can render compressions ineffective or harmful.

## published_sections

## hold_these

## summary
This chapter covers the order and technique of choking management: the adult sequence of back blows before abdominal thrusts, correct abdominal-thrust hand placement, and the infant-specific technique of back blows and chest thrusts (never abdominal thrusts), including the prone, head-down positioning used for infant back blows.

## sections
### Definition
In an adult with severe choking, management begins with five back blows between the shoulder blades before abdominal thrusts (the Heimlich manoeuvre) are attempted, alternating in cycles of five if the obstruction persists. In an infant under one year, back blows and chest thrusts are used instead, delivered with the infant prone along the rescuer's forearm and the head lower than the trunk; abdominal thrusts are never used in an infant.

### Mechanism
Abdominal thrusts are delivered with a fist on the upper abdomen, between the umbilicus and the tip of the sternum, generating a sudden rise in intra-abdominal and intrathoracic pressure that can expel the obstructing object. In an infant, the same rise in pressure from an abdominal thrust risks injuring the relatively large, poorly protected liver, which is why back blows and chest thrusts are substituted instead, and why the head-down, prone position is used to let gravity help dislodge the object.

### Key determinants
Adult choking questions turn on sequence (back blows before abdominal thrusts) and on abdominal-thrust hand placement (upper abdomen, not the sternum, back, or lower abdomen). Infant choking questions turn on technique substitution (back blows and chest thrusts, never abdominal thrusts, CPR as first step, or holding the infant upside down) and on positioning (prone, head-down) during the back blows themselves.

### Clinical significance
Using the wrong technique, abdominal thrusts in an infant, or the wrong sequence, thrusts before back blows in an adult, either fails to relieve the obstruction efficiently or risks unnecessary injury; getting the age-appropriate technique and sequence right is what makes choking rescue both effective and safe.

## summary
This chapter covers three bedside BLS assessment steps taught by this paper: the pulse-check landmark for an unconscious child, the look/listen/feel breathing check, and the safety precautions around AED shock delivery.

## sections
### Definition
Breathing is assessed in an unconscious patient by looking for chest movement, listening for breath sounds, and feeling for exhaled air, all within about ten seconds; no imaging is involved. This paper's own key teaches checking an unconscious child's pulse at the brachial artery with two fingers. When an AED advises a shock, everyone must stand clear of the patient before the shock is delivered, and rescue breathing is never given during the shock itself.

### Mechanism
The look/listen/feel technique works because it uses only the rescuer's own senses at the bedside, fast enough to complete before deciding whether to start CPR. AED shock safety exists because the current delivered can pass through anyone touching the patient at the moment of the shock, and CPR (including rescue breaths) is deliberately paused for that moment and resumed immediately afterwards, rather than continued through it.

### Key determinants
Breathing-check questions turn on which options describe a bedside sensory check (look/listen/feel) versus an investigation such as an X-ray, which has no place in the initial assessment. AED questions turn on the moment of shock delivery specifically: CPR continues while the AED is attached and analysing, but stops, and nobody touches the patient, for the shock itself.

### Clinical significance
An incomplete or too-slow breathing check delays recognising the need for CPR; a rescuer who does not stand clear during an AED shock risks their own injury, and one who is not clear of the airway is in no position to give rescue breaths at that moment even if they mistakenly tried to.

## published_summary

## published_sections

## hold_these

## lose_the_mark
Treating an X-ray as part of the immediate bedside breathing check.
Believing rescue breathing is given during, rather than paused for, AED shock delivery.

## summary
This chapter covers the structured approach to a polytraumatized patient: the ABCDE primary survey, the wider primary-survey/secondary-survey/definitive-treatment sequence, cervical spine immobilisation, the Glasgow Coma Scale as the tool for grading consciousness, the definition of a definitive airway, and the requirements for safely transferring a polytraumatized patient.

## sections
### Definition
A trauma patient's primary survey follows the ABCDE sequence, airway with cervical spine control, breathing, circulation, disability, exposure, and runs alongside simultaneous resuscitation; a secondary survey with investigations and definitive treatment follow once the patient is stabilised. Suspected cervical spine injury is managed with a cervical collar rather than sitting, neck extension, or analgesic injection alone. The Glasgow Coma Scale measures level of consciousness. A definitive airway is a cuffed tube in the trachea, most often placed by orotracheal intubation. Safe transfer requires a secured airway, IV access, and cardiac/pulse-oximetry monitoring together.

### Mechanism
The ABCDE order exists because each letter's problem kills faster than the next: an obstructed airway kills before a breathing problem, which kills before a circulation problem, and so on, so each is fixed before moving to the next. Cervical collar fixation prevents movement of an unstable spine that could convert a stable fracture into a cord injury. A definitive airway seals the trachea itself, unlike adjuncts or non-invasive ventilation, which only support the existing airway.

### Key determinants
Primary-survey questions turn on ABCDE itself, not on investigations (X-ray, blood tests) or treatments (antibiotics) that belong to later stages. Polytrauma-sequence questions turn on primary survey with resuscitation, then secondary survey, then definitive treatment — early oral feeding is not part of this sequence. Cervical spine questions turn on mechanical immobilisation (a collar), not analgesia or positioning. GCS questions turn on its purpose (consciousness level), not shock, airway, or fracture grading. Definitive-airway questions turn on a cuffed tracheal tube (orotracheal intubation), not adjuncts or non-invasive methods. Transfer questions turn on all three of airway, IV access and monitoring together, not any one alone.

### Clinical significance
Following ABCDE in order, immobilising a potentially unstable cervical spine, grading consciousness correctly with GCS, securing a true definitive airway when one is needed, and transferring the patient with airway, access and monitoring all in place together are what keep a polytraumatized patient from deteriorating unnoticed between the scene and definitive care.

## published_summary

## published_sections

## hold_these
A trauma patient's primary survey follows the ABCDE guidelines.

Suspected cervical spine injury is managed by transporting the patient with the neck immobilised in a cervical collar.

The definitive airway is orotracheal (orolaryngeal) intubation.

## lose_the_mark

Treating analgesia, sitting position, or neck extension as substitutes for cervical collar immobilisation.
Confusing GCS's purpose (consciousness level) with shock, airway-obstruction, or fracture grading.
Calling an airway adjunct or non-invasive ventilation method a "definitive airway".
