## summary
A narrowing that is invisible at rest and limiting on the stairs. What makes it stable is that the threshold does not move — and what makes it dangerous is the day it does.
## sections
### Definition
Stable angina is chest discomfort caused by reversible myocardial ischaemia, provoked by a reproducible amount of exertion or emotion and relieved within minutes by rest or a nitrate.

Stability describes the pattern over time, not the severity. Severe angina at a low workload can be stable; mild angina of recent onset is not.
### Epidemiology
Angina is a common presentation of coronary artery disease in middle and later life, and its prevalence rises with age and with the burden of vascular risk factors. It is more often the first manifestation of coronary disease in women, whereas men more often present with infarction.

No prevalence figure is asserted here. Local Egyptian estimates require a verified national source and are recorded as an evidence gap rather than imported from a Western series.
### Aetiology and risk factors
The substrate is atherosclerosis. Modifiable risk factors are smoking, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, obesity and physical inactivity; non-modifiable are age, male sex and family history of premature coronary disease.

Angina can also occur without flow-limiting epicardial disease — in severe aortic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and in coronary microvascular dysfunction — because the mechanism is a supply-demand mismatch rather than any particular lesion.
### Pathophysiology
An atherosclerotic plaque narrows the lumen. Distal arterioles dilate to maintain resting flow, so nothing is felt until that reserve is exhausted. When demand rises with exertion, supply cannot follow, and the subendocardium — furthest from the epicardial vessels and most compressed during systole — becomes ischaemic first.

Ischaemic metabolites stimulate cardiac visceral afferents entering the cord at T1 to T4, which is why the pain is diffuse and referred rather than point-tender. Because the stenosis is fixed, the workload that provokes it is reproducible.
### Clinical picture
Discomfort is central, described as heaviness, tightness or pressure rather than as sharp pain, and often demonstrated with a closed fist rather than a fingertip. It radiates to the jaw, the neck, the inner left arm or the epigastrium.

It comes on with exertion — classically worse walking uphill, after a meal, or in cold weather — and settles within a few minutes of stopping. Breathlessness may accompany or replace the pain, particularly in older patients, in women and in diabetes.

Examination is often entirely normal between episodes, and a normal examination carries no reassurance.
### Investigation
A resting ECG is recorded in everyone, chiefly to look for evidence of previous infarction, hypertrophy or conduction disease. It is frequently normal in stable angina, and a normal trace does not exclude the diagnosis.

Bloods assess the risk factors and the differential: full blood count for anaemia, glucose and HbA1c, lipids, renal and thyroid function.

The diagnostic test is anatomical or functional imaging rather than the resting trace. CT coronary angiography demonstrates the anatomy and excludes obstructive disease with high reliability; functional testing — stress echocardiography, perfusion imaging or stress MRI — demonstrates whether ischaemia occurs and in which territory. Which is chosen depends on the pre-test probability and on what is available, and that choice follows the chronic coronary syndrome guideline in force locally.

Invasive coronary angiography is reserved for patients in whom revascularisation is being considered or in whom non-invasive testing is inconclusive.
### Management
Two aims run in parallel, and they are not the same aim.

**Preventing infarction and death** is the priority: stopping smoking, controlling blood pressure, glucose and lipids, and antiplatelet and lipid-lowering therapy. These modify prognosis.

**Relieving symptoms** uses antianginal drug classes that either reduce myocardial oxygen demand or improve supply, with a second agent added when one is insufficient.

**Revascularisation** — percutaneous coronary intervention or bypass surgery — is offered for symptoms not controlled medically, and for specific anatomical patterns where it improves prognosis. In stable disease, revascularisation is a treatment for symptoms and for defined high-risk anatomy rather than a routine alternative to medical therapy, which is a frequent misconception.

Agents, doses, targets and the anatomical criteria for revascularisation follow the chronic coronary syndrome guideline in force where the patient is treated. None is reproduced here, because these are the recommendations that change between editions.
### Complications and prognosis
The principal risk is progression to an acute coronary syndrome through plaque disruption, which is not predicted by the severity of the stenosis causing the symptoms. Heart failure follows repeated or extensive ischaemic damage.

Prognosis depends on ventricular function, the extent of disease and the control of risk factors, and is substantially better with treatment than the symptom burden alone suggests.
### Prevention
Because angina is one expression of systemic atherosclerosis, prevention is the same programme as for vascular disease generally: tobacco cessation, blood pressure and lipid control, glycaemic control, weight and activity.
## hold_these
Stability is about a reproducible threshold, not about severity.
A normal resting ECG does not exclude stable angina.
Revascularisation in stable disease treats symptoms and defined high-risk anatomy; it is not a routine alternative to medical therapy.
## lose_the_mark
Excluding coronary disease because the resting ECG and the examination were normal.
Assuming the tightest stenosis is the one that will cause the next infarct.
Treating the symptom and neglecting the prognosis — the antianginal helps the patient feel better and the risk-factor work keeps them alive.

## summary
The same disease with the alarm disconnected. Everything that makes angina dangerous is present; only the warning is missing.
## sections
### Definition
Silent ischaemia is objectively demonstrable myocardial ischaemia — on the ECG, on ambulatory monitoring, or on functional imaging — occurring without angina or any anginal equivalent.

It occurs in patients who never experience pain, and in patients with typical angina who also have unrecognised episodes between the symptomatic ones.
### Epidemiology
Silent episodes are common among patients with established coronary disease, and silent infarction accounts for a meaningful proportion of all infarcts, often discovered later on an ECG recorded for another reason.

Diabetes, advancing age and female sex are the groups in which pain is most often absent. No proportion is quoted here; the figures vary by detection method and population, and a verified source is recorded as an evidence gap.
### Aetiology and risk factors
The substrate is the same coronary atherosclerosis that causes angina. What differs is the reporting pathway.

Diabetes is the strongest association, through autonomic neuropathy damaging the visceral afferents that carry cardiac pain. Older age, previous infarction with denervation of the scarred territory, and possibly a higher individual pain threshold contribute.
### Pathophysiology
Ischaemia proceeds in its usual order whether or not it is felt: perfusion falls, then diastolic relaxation is impaired, then systolic function, then the ECG changes, and only last does pain appear. Pain is the final and least sensitive event in that sequence, so losing it removes the last step and leaves everything before it intact.

Where the afferent fibres are damaged, as in diabetic autonomic neuropathy, the signal is never generated. The myocardium is injured on the same timetable as anyone else's.
### Clinical picture
By definition there is no chest pain. What appears instead is either nothing at all, or an anginal equivalent: breathlessness on exertion, unusual fatigue, a fall in exercise tolerance, or in older patients confusion or a fall.

Silent infarction may present later as unexplained heart failure, an arrhythmia, or Q waves on an ECG taken for another purpose.
### Investigation
Because there is no symptom to correlate with, detection depends on looking. A resting ECG may show previous infarction. Ambulatory ECG monitoring detects transient ST-segment shifts during ordinary activity. Exercise or pharmacological stress testing with imaging demonstrates inducible ischaemia and its territory.

Whether to screen asymptomatic patients — including asymptomatic patients with diabetes — is contested, because detecting ischaemia has not consistently been shown to improve outcomes over treating risk factors well. The position taken should follow the guidance in force locally rather than a general assumption that finding disease must be beneficial.
### Management
Treatment is that of coronary artery disease, not of the symptom, since there is no symptom to relieve. Risk-factor modification and secondary prevention therapy carry the whole benefit.

Revascularisation is considered on the same grounds as in stable angina — for prognostically significant anatomy or a large ischaemic burden — rather than because ischaemia has been demonstrated. Specific thresholds follow the guideline in force.
### Complications and prognosis
Silent ischaemia carries a prognosis at least as adverse as symptomatic ischaemia of similar extent, and arguably worse, because it is recognised later. Silent infarction leads to the same remodelling, heart failure and arrhythmia risk as a recognised one.
### Egyptian context
Diabetes is a major and rising contributor to cardiovascular disease in Egypt, which raises the clinical importance of atypical and silent presentation locally. A prevalence figure requires a verified national source and is not asserted here.
## hold_these
Ischaemia is not defined by pain; pain is the last and least sensitive step in the ischaemic cascade.
In diabetes, breathlessness or a fall in exercise tolerance may be the only presentation of coronary disease.
Silent ischaemia carries at least the same prognostic weight as symptomatic ischaemia.
## lose_the_mark
Using absence of chest pain to exclude coronary disease, particularly in a patient with long-standing diabetes.
Assuming that detecting silent ischaemia by screening must improve outcome. That is contested and follows local guidance.

## summary
The plaque has ruptured and the thrombus has not finished the job. Same mechanism as an infarct, stopped just short of it — which is why it is an emergency and not a worse kind of angina.
## sections
### Definition
Unstable angina is myocardial ischaemia at rest or on minimal exertion, of new onset, or worsening in frequency, duration or threshold, without a rise in cardiac troponin.

The absence of a troponin rise is what separates it from non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction. Clinically the two are indistinguishable at the bedside.
### Epidemiology
Unstable angina and NSTEMI together account for the majority of acute coronary syndrome presentations. The proportion diagnosed as unstable angina rather than NSTEMI has fallen as troponin assays have become more sensitive, since events formerly classified as unstable angina now show a detectable rise.
### Aetiology and risk factors
The risk factors are those of atherosclerosis. The immediate precipitant is disruption of a vulnerable plaque — a thin fibrous cap over a large lipid core — which is not necessarily the most stenotic lesion.

Secondary unstable angina occurs where a fixed stenosis meets a sudden rise in demand or fall in supply: anaemia, tachyarrhythmia, thyrotoxicosis, hypoxia.
### Pathophysiology
A plaque ruptures or erodes and exposes thrombogenic material. Platelets adhere and aggregate, thrombin is generated, and a thrombus forms. In unstable angina the thrombus is subtotal or transient — it restricts flow enough to cause ischaemia at rest but not long or completely enough to kill myocytes.

Distal embolisation of platelet aggregates and vasoconstriction from released mediators contribute. The clinical instability reflects an active, evolving lesion rather than a fixed one, which is why the risk of progression to infarction is high in the following hours and days.
### Clinical picture
Chest discomfort of anginal character occurring at rest, typically lasting longer than the patient's usual episodes and responding less well or not at all to rest and nitrate.

Alternatively, angina of new onset that is limiting, or established angina that has become more frequent, more prolonged, or provoked by less exertion than before.

Examination is often normal. Signs of haemodynamic compromise, a new murmur, or pulmonary oedema indicate a large ischaemic burden and a much higher risk.
### Investigation
A 12-lead ECG is recorded immediately and repeated, because the trace changes with the ischaemia. ST depression and T-wave inversion support the diagnosis; a normal ECG does not exclude it, and serial recording is more informative than any single one.

High-sensitivity troponin is measured on arrival and repeated after a defined interval. It is normal or unchanged in unstable angina by definition. The serial pattern, not the single value, distinguishes it from NSTEMI.

Bloods, echocardiography where function or a complication is in question, and subsequent coronary angiography follow risk assessment.
### Management
Unstable angina is treated as an acute coronary syndrome, on the same pathway as NSTEMI, because the two cannot be separated at presentation and the mechanism is identical.

The elements are: immediate ECG and continuous monitoring; antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy to arrest thrombus propagation; anti-ischaemic therapy to reduce demand; and risk stratification to decide the timing of invasive assessment. Higher-risk patients undergo coronary angiography earlier.

Agents, combinations, doses and the timing thresholds for invasive management follow the acute coronary syndrome guideline in force where the patient is treated. None is reproduced here; these are among the most frequently revised recommendations in cardiology.
### Complications and prognosis
The principal complication is progression to myocardial infarction, and the risk is concentrated in the first hours to days. Arrhythmia, heart failure and recurrent ischaemia follow the extent of jeopardised myocardium.

With appropriate antithrombotic therapy and risk-directed intervention, outcomes are substantially better than the presentation suggests. Prognosis is driven by the extent of coronary disease and ventricular function.
### Classification
Unstable angina, NSTEMI and STEMI are one spectrum. The ECG separates ST-elevation from non-ST-elevation presentations, and troponin then separates unstable angina from NSTEMI within the latter group. That two-step separation is the organising logic of the whole topic.
## hold_these
Unstable angina and NSTEMI are indistinguishable clinically; troponin is what separates them.
Instability means change — at rest, new, or worsening — not severity.
The risk of progression to infarction is concentrated in the first hours to days, which is why this is an emergency.
## lose_the_mark
Sending a patient home because a single early troponin was normal.
Treating unstable angina as a milder outpatient problem because no infarct has occurred. The mechanism is an active thrombus.
Assuming the culprit plaque is the tightest stenosis on the angiogram.

## summary
Myocardium has died, and the ECG does not say so. The diagnosis rests on a changing troponin read alongside the clinical picture — and on remembering that troponin reports injury, not its cause.
## sections
### Definition
NSTEMI is myocardial infarction — a rise and fall in cardiac troponin with evidence of ischaemia — occurring without persistent ST-segment elevation on the ECG.

Infarction requires both halves: the biochemical change and the ischaemic context. A troponin rise alone is myocardial injury, which has many causes, and is not by itself an infarct.
### Epidemiology
NSTEMI now accounts for a larger share of acute myocardial infarction than STEMI in most health systems, and its incidence rises with age. Patients are on average older, with more comorbidity and more extensive coronary disease than those presenting with STEMI, which is why long-term outcomes are not better despite the less dramatic presentation.
### Aetiology and risk factors
Most cases are type 1: plaque disruption with a subtotal or intermittently occlusive thrombus, on a background of the usual atherosclerotic risk factors.

A substantial minority are type 2: myocardial oxygen supply and demand diverge without plaque disruption, in severe anaemia, sustained tachyarrhythmia, hypotension, sepsis, hypoxaemia or severe hypertension. The distinction changes treatment completely, since antithrombotic therapy and catheterisation address a thrombus that a type 2 event does not have.
### Pathophysiology
Thrombus restricts but does not permanently abolish flow. Ischaemia is most severe in the subendocardium, which is furthest from the epicardial vessels and most compressed in systole, so necrosis is typically subendocardial rather than transmural.

Because the wall is not ischaemic through its full thickness, no injury current lifts the ST segment; the ECG shows ST depression, T-wave inversion, or nothing at all. Necrosis releases cardiac troponin, which rises over hours and falls over days — and it is that trajectory, not a single value, that identifies the event.
### Clinical picture
Chest discomfort of anginal character, usually at rest and prolonged, often with sweating, nausea or breathlessness.

Presentation is atypical more often than in STEMI, particularly in older patients, in women and in diabetes, where breathlessness, fatigue, syncope or confusion may replace pain entirely.

Examination looks for the consequences rather than the diagnosis: heart failure, a new murmur, arrhythmia, and haemodynamic compromise, each of which raises risk substantially.
### Investigation
A 12-lead ECG is recorded within minutes and repeated, both to exclude ST elevation and because dynamic change during pain is highly informative. ST depression and deep symmetrical T-wave inversion support ischaemia; a normal ECG does not exclude NSTEMI.

High-sensitivity cardiac troponin is measured on arrival and repeated after a defined interval, using the assay's own protocol. The rise or fall is the diagnostic feature. Interpret it against the clinical context: renal impairment, sepsis, arrhythmia, pulmonary embolism, myocarditis and heart failure all raise troponin without a coronary occlusion.

Echocardiography assesses regional wall motion, ventricular function and complications. Coronary angiography defines the anatomy, with its timing decided by risk.
### Management
The immediate priorities are monitoring for arrhythmia, arresting thrombus propagation with antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy, and reducing ischaemia.

Risk stratification then determines how soon coronary angiography is performed, with higher-risk patients assessed earlier and the highest-risk group treated as urgently as a STEMI.

Type 2 infarction is treated by correcting the cause — the anaemia, the arrhythmia, the sepsis — rather than by antithrombotic therapy and catheterisation, which add bleeding risk without addressing the mechanism.

Secondary prevention begins before discharge and is the intervention that determines long-term outcome.

Every agent, dose, combination, risk score and timing threshold follows the acute coronary syndrome guideline in force where the patient is treated. None is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Arrhythmia, heart failure, recurrent ischaemia and reinfarction. Mechanical complications are less common than after STEMI because necrosis is usually subendocardial.

Short-term mortality is lower than in STEMI; longer-term mortality is comparable or higher, because these patients are older with more extensive disease. Prognosis is determined by ventricular function, the extent of coronary disease, renal function and the quality of secondary prevention.
### Classification
NSTEMI sits between unstable angina and STEMI. The ECG separates it from STEMI; troponin separates it from unstable angina. The type 1 and type 2 distinction cuts across that classification and is decided by mechanism rather than by ECG or troponin.
## hold_these
Infarction requires a troponin rise and fall together with evidence of ischaemia; the biochemical change alone is myocardial injury.
Type 2 infarction is treated by correcting its cause, not by antithrombotic therapy and catheterisation.
Short-term mortality in NSTEMI is lower than in STEMI, but longer-term mortality is not.
## lose_the_mark
Diagnosing infarction from one raised troponin without an ischaemic context.
Ruling out NSTEMI on a normal first ECG and a normal first troponin.
Anticoagulating and catheterising a septic or severely anaemic patient whose troponin rose for that reason.

## summary
An artery is shut and muscle is dying from the inside outwards. Everything about the management follows from one fact: the amount saved depends on how fast flow is restored.
## sections
### Definition
STEMI is myocardial infarction with persistent ST-segment elevation on the ECG, indicating complete occlusion of a coronary artery and transmural ischaemia.

The diagnosis is made on the ECG and the clinical picture. It does not wait for troponin, which rises only after necrosis has begun.
### Epidemiology
STEMI accounts for a minority of acute myocardial infarction in most contemporary series but a disproportionate share of early mortality, much of which occurs before the patient reaches hospital, from ventricular fibrillation.

Patients are on average younger than those with NSTEMI. Premature presentation is associated with smoking and with familial dyslipidaemia.
### Aetiology and risk factors
Almost always plaque disruption with complete occlusive thrombus, on the usual atherosclerotic background: smoking, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, family history.

Less commonly: coronary embolism, spontaneous coronary artery dissection — which is disproportionately a cause in younger women and in the peripartum period — coronary spasm, and thrombosis in a previously placed stent.
### Pathophysiology
Complete occlusion abolishes flow to the territory. Necrosis begins in the subendocardium within about half an hour and advances as a wavefront towards the epicardium over the following hours. The extent of final infarction depends on the duration of occlusion, the size of the territory, collateral supply and myocardial oxygen demand.

Because ischaemia is transmural, the injured segment generates a current that elevates the ST segment in the leads facing it, with reciprocal depression in the leads opposite. Over hours to days the ST segment settles, T waves invert, and pathological Q waves develop where full-thickness necrosis has occurred.

Salvageable myocardium is what has not yet been reached by the wavefront, which is the entire basis for treating this as a time-critical emergency.
### Clinical picture
Severe, prolonged chest pain of anginal character, typically with sweating, nausea, vomiting and a sense of impending doom, unrelieved by rest or nitrate.

Presentation may be atypical or silent, particularly in diabetes, in older patients and in women, where breathlessness, syncope, epigastric pain or collapse can be the only feature.

Examination may show pallor, sweating and distress. Look for the complications that change management immediately: hypotension, pulmonary oedema, a new murmur, a raised jugular venous pressure with clear lungs suggesting right ventricular involvement, and arrhythmia.
### Investigation
A 12-lead ECG within minutes of first medical contact is the single most important investigation, and it establishes the diagnosis. The pattern localises the territory: anterior leads for the anterior interventricular artery, inferior leads for the right coronary in most hearts, lateral leads for the circumflex. In inferior infarction, right-sided leads are recorded because right ventricular involvement changes management. Posterior infarction may show only reciprocal changes anteriorly and requires posterior leads to be seen.

Troponin is measured but does not delay treatment. Its value is in confirmation and in estimating infarct size afterwards.

Echocardiography assesses ventricular function and complications, and helps where the ECG is equivocal or a mechanical complication is suspected. Coronary angiography is both diagnostic and the route to treatment.
### Management
Reperfusion is the treatment, and time is the variable that determines its benefit.

The sequence is: immediate ECG, continuous cardiac monitoring with defibrillation available, and immediate activation of the reperfusion pathway. Primary percutaneous coronary intervention is the preferred strategy where it can be delivered within the time window defined by the guideline in force; fibrinolysis is used where it cannot, followed by transfer.

Antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy accompany reperfusion. Oxygen is given only for hypoxia. Analgesia is given for pain.

The early risk is ventricular fibrillation, which is why monitoring and immediate availability of defibrillation matter more in the first hours than any drug.

Secondary prevention starts before discharge and determines long-term outcome.

Every drug, dose, combination, and every time target — first medical contact to reperfusion, and the window within which fibrinolysis is preferred — follows the STEMI guideline in force where the patient is treated. None is reproduced here, and using a remembered figure instead of the current one is unsafe.
### Complications and prognosis
Early: ventricular fibrillation and other arrhythmias, cardiogenic shock, acute heart failure, and right ventricular infarction complicating inferior events.

Later: mechanical complications in the days after infarction, pericarditis, mural thrombus and embolism, ventricular aneurysm, and adverse remodelling leading to chronic heart failure.

Prognosis depends on infarct size, the time to reperfusion, ventricular function afterwards and the completeness of secondary prevention. Much of the total mortality occurs before hospital arrival.
### Classification
STEMI sits at the occlusive end of the acute coronary syndrome spectrum. The ECG, not the troponin, places it there — which is why it is the one acute coronary syndrome diagnosed and treated before any blood result returns.
## hold_these
ST elevation means an occluded artery; the diagnosis does not wait for troponin.
Necrosis advances as a wavefront from subendocardium outwards, so the muscle saved depends on how early flow is restored.
The early risk is ventricular fibrillation, which is why monitoring and defibrillator availability come before any drug.
## lose_the_mark
Waiting for a troponin result before activating reperfusion in a patient with ST elevation.
Missing right ventricular involvement in inferior infarction by not recording right-sided leads.
Missing posterior infarction, which may show only reciprocal anterior changes.
Reciting a remembered time target instead of the one in the guideline currently in force.

## summary
The infarct is an event; its complications are a timetable. What can go wrong on day one is not what goes wrong on day five, and knowing the order is what makes the deterioration interpretable.
## sections
### Definition
The complications of myocardial infarction are the electrical, mechanical, inflammatory and structural consequences that follow necrosis of a region of myocardium. They are grouped most usefully by when they occur, because the timing follows the histological stage of healing.
### Epidemiology
Complication rates have fallen substantially with reperfusion therapy, and mechanical complications in particular have become uncommon where reperfusion is delivered promptly. They remain more frequent after late presentation, which makes them disproportionately relevant wherever patients arrive late.

No rate is quoted; figures vary by era, by reperfusion access and by registry.
### Aetiology and risk factors
Larger infarcts, anterior territory, late or absent reperfusion, older age, diabetes, and previous infarction all increase the risk. Mechanical complications are associated particularly with a first, large, transmural, late-reperfused infarct.
### Pathophysiology
Healing runs to a schedule, and the complications follow it.

In the **first hours**, ischaemic myocardium is electrically unstable, generating ventricular arrhythmia. If enough muscle is lost, the pump fails.

Over the **next days**, neutrophils and then macrophages clear necrotic tissue. The wall is at its weakest during this clearance, before fibrous replacement has produced any tensile strength — which is why free wall rupture, septal rupture and papillary muscle rupture cluster in this window rather than at the onset.

Over **weeks to months**, collagen replaces the necrotic tissue and the scar matures. A thin non-contractile scar may bulge to form an aneurysm; the surviving myocardium, now more heavily loaded, dilates and becomes more spherical, which is adverse remodelling and the route from infarction to chronic heart failure.
### Clinical picture
**Arrhythmia.** Ventricular fibrillation is the commonest early cause of death and often occurs before hospital arrival. Ventricular tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, and bradyarrhythmia or heart block — particularly after inferior infarction, where the nodal arteries usually arise from the right coronary artery — all occur.

**Pump failure.** Acute heart failure and pulmonary oedema, or cardiogenic shock where a large mass of myocardium is lost. Right ventricular infarction, complicating inferior events, presents with hypotension and a raised venous pressure with clear lung fields, and is managed quite differently from left-sided failure.

**Mechanical.** Papillary muscle rupture causes sudden severe mitral regurgitation with pulmonary oedema and a new murmur. Ventricular septal rupture causes a new harsh murmur with a left-to-right shunt and rapid deterioration. Free wall rupture causes tamponade and is usually immediately fatal. All are heralded by sudden deterioration days after an infarct that had been settling.

**Inflammatory.** Early post-infarction pericarditis causes pleuritic pain relieved by sitting forward, with a rub. Dressler syndrome is a later immune-mediated pericarditis occurring weeks afterwards.

**Thromboembolic.** Mural thrombus may form over akinetic myocardium, particularly a large anterior infarct or an aneurysm, and embolise.
### Investigation
Continuous ECG monitoring detects arrhythmia in the early phase, which is why it is the first intervention rather than an optional one.

Echocardiography is the key investigation for any post-infarction deterioration. It identifies mechanical complications, quantifies ventricular function, detects pericardial effusion, mural thrombus and aneurysm, and distinguishes right ventricular infarction.

A new murmur after infarction is an indication for urgent echocardiography, not for observation.
### Management
The early priority is monitoring and immediate availability of defibrillation, because the early deaths are electrical.

Mechanical complications are surgical emergencies. Recognition — sudden deterioration with a new murmur days after infarction — and urgent echocardiography with immediate surgical referral are what determine survival.

Right ventricular infarction is preload-dependent and is made worse by treatments that reduce preload, which is a trap where the reflex response to hypotension is exactly wrong.

Pericarditis is managed with anti-inflammatory therapy chosen with awareness of its interaction with healing and with antithrombotic treatment.

Adverse remodelling is limited by the drug classes shown to do so, started early and continued long-term rather than given for symptoms.

Every agent, dose and surgical threshold follows the guideline in force where the patient is treated; none is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Prognosis after infarction is determined chiefly by residual ventricular function, by the extent of coronary disease and by whether adverse remodelling is limited. Mechanical complications carry a high mortality even with surgery, and their outcome depends heavily on how quickly they are recognised.
### Classification
Grouping by time — hours, days, weeks — is the classification that carries clinical information, because it tells you what to expect when a patient deteriorates and when.
## hold_these
Complications follow the timetable of infarct healing: electrical in hours, mechanical in days, remodelling over months.
A new murmur with sudden deterioration days after an infarct is a mechanical complication until echocardiography says otherwise.
Right ventricular infarction is preload-dependent, so treatments that reduce preload make it worse.
## lose_the_mark
Expecting rupture at the time of the infarct rather than during the days when necrotic tissue is being cleared.
Missing right ventricular infarction in an inferior event, and treating the hypotension in a way that worsens it.
Treating a post-infarction deterioration without an echocardiogram.
