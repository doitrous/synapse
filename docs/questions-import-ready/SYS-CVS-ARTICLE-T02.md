## summary
Most chest pain is not dangerous. The work is not deciding what it probably is — it is deciding, quickly, whether it is one of the handful of things that kills within hours.
## sections
### Definition
Chest pain is any discomfort felt between the diaphragm and the base of the neck. The word covers pressure, burning, tightness and sharpness, and patients often reject the word "pain" for what turns out to be the most dangerous kind.

Chest pain is owned by the cardiovascular system in this library, and the respiratory and gastrointestinal causes are cross-referenced rather than duplicated.
### Mechanisms
Three kinds of structure generate it, and they produce different pain.

**Visceral afferents** from the heart and great vessels enter the cord at roughly T1 to T4. They are sparse and poorly mapped, so the pain is diffuse, hard to localise, and referred to somatic territories sharing those segments — the inner arm, the jaw, the epigastrium. Ischaemic cardiac pain is generated when myocardial oxygen demand exceeds supply.

**Somatic structures** — chest wall, parietal pleura, pericardium — are richly innervated and produce sharp, well-localised pain that moves with respiration or position.

**Oesophageal afferents** run with the cardiac ones and enter the same segments, which is why oesophageal pain can be indistinguishable from cardiac pain at the bedside.
### Differential diagnosis
Order it by what kills, not by what is common.

**Immediately life-threatening:** acute coronary syndrome; aortic dissection; pulmonary embolism; tension pneumothorax; oesophageal rupture; cardiac tamponade.

**Serious but less immediate:** pericarditis, myocarditis, pneumonia, severe aortic stenosis.

**Common and not dangerous:** musculoskeletal chest wall pain, gastro-oesophageal reflux, anxiety, costochondritis. These account for most presentations and are diagnoses of exclusion in anyone at risk.
### Focused history
Character, radiation, onset, duration and what changes it carry most of the information.

Crushing or heavy pressure radiating to the jaw or left arm, coming on with exertion and easing with rest, is the anginal pattern. Sudden tearing pain radiating to the back, maximal at onset, suggests dissection. Sharp pain worse on inspiration and relieved by sitting forward suggests pericarditis. Burning after meals, worse lying flat, suggests reflux — but reflux and ischaemia overlap enough that the history alone does not exclude the heart.

Ask about risk: age, diabetes, hypertension, smoking, lipids, family history, previous vascular disease. Ask about the last episode and whether this one is different.
### Focused examination
Examination rarely makes the diagnosis and frequently changes the urgency.

Compare blood pressure in both arms — a large difference raises the possibility of dissection. Feel the peripheral pulses. Listen for a new murmur, a pericardial rub, a third heart sound. Look at the jugular venous pressure. Examine the legs for asymmetric swelling suggesting venous thrombosis.

Reproducible tenderness on palpation points away from a cardiac cause, but it does not exclude one, and it should never be the only reason a serious cause is dismissed.
### Initial investigation
A 12-lead ECG is the first test in anyone with chest pain that could be cardiac, and it should be recorded and read within minutes of arrival. A normal ECG does not exclude acute coronary syndrome.

High-sensitivity cardiac troponin, measured on arrival and repeated after an interval, is the biochemical test for myocardial injury. A single early sample is not sufficient to rule it out; the serial change is what carries the information.

A chest radiograph looks for pneumothorax, a widened mediastinum and pulmonary causes. Further imaging — CT aortography, CT pulmonary angiography, echocardiography — follows the suspicion the history raised rather than being ordered indiscriminately.
### Red flags
Pain that is sudden and maximal at onset. Pain with syncope, or with a difference in pulses or blood pressure between limbs. Pain with haemodynamic instability, hypoxia or a new murmur. Pain that persists at rest. Any of these moves the patient to immediate assessment rather than to a waiting list.
### Immediate approach
The sequence is airway, breathing and circulation first, then an ECG within minutes, then a decision about which of the dangerous causes is in play.

Give oxygen only if the patient is hypoxic. Obtain intravenous access and continuous monitoring in anyone with suspected acute coronary syndrome, because the early risk is ventricular arrhythmia. Where the ECG shows ST elevation, the priority is immediate reperfusion and the pathway is time-critical.

Specific drug and reperfusion decisions follow the current national or European guidance in force where the patient is treated; this article names the strategy and deliberately states no drug, dose or time target, which change between guideline editions.
## hold_these
A normal initial ECG does not exclude acute coronary syndrome.
Cardiac pain is diffuse and referred; a pain the patient localises with one finger is rarely cardiac.
The chest pain differential is ordered by lethality, not by prevalence.
## lose_the_mark
Excluding a cardiac cause because the chest wall is tender. Tenderness makes it less likely and does not rule it out.
Accepting a single early troponin as a negative result. The serial change is the test.
Forgetting that epigastric pain can be a myocardial infarction, which is why it is so often first treated as indigestion.

## summary
The symptom says the patient can feel their heart. It does not say the rhythm is abnormal, and the whole difficulty is that the evidence disappears before anyone can record it.
## sections
### Definition
A palpitation is an awareness of one's own heartbeat. Patients describe it as pounding, racing, fluttering, thumping, or a single missed beat followed by a heavy one.

The sensation may accompany a completely normal rhythm at a normal rate, so it localises the problem to the heart without establishing that a rhythm disturbance exists.
### Mechanisms
Three things make a heartbeat noticeable.

**A change in rate** — anything fast enough to be felt, whether sinus tachycardia from fever, anaemia or anxiety, or a genuine tachyarrhythmia.

**A change in rhythm** — an ectopic beat produces a pause, and the beat after the pause is felt as a thump because the extra filling time raises its stroke volume. Patients usually notice the compensatory beat rather than the ectopic one.

**A change in stroke volume** — a large stroke volume is felt even at a normal rate, which is why aortic regurgitation, thyrotoxicosis and pregnancy produce the sensation.
### Differential diagnosis
**Not an arrhythmia at all:** anxiety and panic, anaemia, thyrotoxicosis, fever, pregnancy, caffeine and stimulants, and the ordinary awareness of a fast rate after exertion.

**Benign arrhythmia:** atrial and ventricular ectopic beats, which are common in structurally normal hearts.

**Arrhythmia needing attention:** atrial fibrillation and flutter, supraventricular tachycardia, ventricular tachycardia, and the inherited channel disorders that predispose to it.

**Structural disease presenting through rhythm:** hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, valvular disease, previous infarction.
### Focused history
Ask the patient to tap out the rhythm. A regular fast tapping suggests a regular tachycardia; irregularly irregular tapping suggests atrial fibrillation; single skipped beats suggest ectopy.

Establish onset and offset. Abrupt start and abrupt stop suggests a re-entrant tachycardia; gradual acceleration and gradual settling suggests sinus tachycardia and a systemic cause.

Ask what accompanies it. Syncope, chest pain or breathlessness during the episode raises the concern substantially. Ask about triggers, caffeine, alcohol, stimulants and thyroid symptoms, and about family history of sudden death, which is the question most often omitted.
### Focused examination
Between episodes the examination is frequently normal, and a normal examination is not reassurance.

Look for a cause rather than for the arrhythmia: signs of thyrotoxicosis, pallor of anaemia, a murmur suggesting structural disease, and evidence of heart failure. During an episode, record the rate, the regularity and the blood pressure.
### Initial investigation
A 12-lead ECG is taken in every patient, both to catch an ongoing arrhythmia and to look for the substrate between episodes: pre-excitation, a long QT interval, evidence of previous infarction or of hypertrophy.

Blood tests look for the systemic causes — full blood count, thyroid function, electrolytes.

Because palpitations are intermittent, the diagnostic aim is symptom-rhythm correlation: capturing the rhythm while the patient feels the symptom. The choice of ambulatory monitoring follows how often episodes occur, which is why the frequency question in the history determines the investigation.
### Red flags
Palpitations with syncope or near-syncope. Palpitations during exertion rather than after it. A family history of sudden cardiac death under 40. Known structural heart disease or previous myocardial infarction. An ECG showing pre-excitation, a prolonged QT interval or ventricular ectopy of concerning morphology.
### Immediate approach
A haemodynamically unstable patient with a tachyarrhythmia is an emergency and is managed on the arrhythmia pathway in force locally.

A stable patient with palpitations is investigated rather than treated: record a 12-lead ECG, look for a systemic cause, and arrange monitoring matched to the episode frequency. Reassurance is appropriate only once the red flags above have been excluded, and it is safe to say that ectopic beats in a structurally normal heart do not shorten life.

Rhythm-specific drug and ablation decisions follow current arrhythmia guidance; no agent or dose is stated here.
## hold_these
A palpitation is a symptom, not a diagnosis; the rhythm may be entirely normal.
The diagnostic aim is symptom-rhythm correlation — capturing the rhythm while the symptom is happening.
Palpitations with syncope, or on exertion, or with a family history of sudden death, are a different problem from palpitations alone.
## lose_the_mark
Reassuring a patient because the resting ECG between episodes was normal. It is uninformative, not negative.
Forgetting to ask about family history of sudden death, which is the question that changes the workup most.

## summary
Almost everyone who faints is well. The task is to find the few whose faint was the first sign of something that can kill them, and the history does most of that work.
## sections
### Definition
Syncope is a transient loss of consciousness caused by global cerebral hypoperfusion, with rapid onset, short duration and complete spontaneous recovery.

Each clause excludes something. The mechanism excludes seizure and hypoglycaemia. The spontaneous, complete recovery excludes coma and cardiac arrest. A transient loss of consciousness that does not meet the definition is not syncope and is investigated differently.
### Mechanisms
Consciousness requires cerebral perfusion, and cerebral perfusion requires an adequate arterial pressure. Anything that drops it far enough for a few seconds produces syncope.

**Reflex (neurally mediated)** — inappropriate vagal activation and withdrawal of sympathetic tone cause bradycardia, vasodilatation or both. This is the vasovagal faint, and it is by far the commonest.

**Orthostatic** — standing shifts blood to the legs, and a failure of the normal baroreflex response leaves pressure unsupported. Common causes are volume depletion, drugs and autonomic failure.

**Cardiac** — an arrhythmia too fast or too slow to maintain output, or a structural obstruction to output such as severe aortic stenosis or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. This group is the one that matters most, because it carries a risk of sudden death.
### Differential diagnosis
The first branch is whether this was syncope at all. Seizure, hypoglycaemia, intoxication, head injury and functional episodes all interrupt consciousness by other means.

Within syncope, the branches are reflex, orthostatic and cardiac. Reflex syncope accounts for most cases in young people; cardiac causes rise sharply with age and with structural heart disease.
### Focused history
The account of the event, from the patient and from anyone who saw it, is the highest-yield investigation available.

**Before:** a trigger — prolonged standing, heat, pain, the sight of blood, coughing, micturition — and a prodrome of nausea, warmth, sweating and greying vision, both point to reflex syncope. No warning at all points towards a cardiac cause.

**During:** posture matters. Reflex syncope happens upright. Syncope while sitting or lying, or during exertion, is cardiac until proven otherwise. Brief myoclonic jerks occur in syncope of any cause and do not establish epilepsy; prolonged rhythmic convulsion, tongue-biting and prolonged confusion afterwards suggest seizure.

**After:** recovery from syncope is rapid and complete. A prolonged postictal state points away from it.

Ask about family history of sudden death under 40, known heart disease, and every drug the patient takes.
### Focused examination
Measure lying and standing blood pressure. Examine for a murmur, particularly the ejection systolic murmur of aortic stenosis and the dynamic murmur of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Check for signs of anaemia and of volume depletion. Examine the neurological system, chiefly to look for evidence that this was not syncope.
### Initial investigation
A 12-lead ECG is taken in every patient with syncope. It looks for bradyarrhythmia and conduction disease, pre-excitation, a prolonged QT interval, evidence of previous infarction, and the patterns of the inherited arrhythmia syndromes.

Blood glucose and haemoglobin are checked. Echocardiography follows when the history, examination or ECG suggest structural disease. Ambulatory monitoring follows when an arrhythmia is suspected and the ECG was unrevealing, matched to how often episodes occur.

Routine brain imaging and EEG have no place in the assessment of uncomplicated syncope, and ordering them for a typical faint is a common and expensive error.
### Red flags
Syncope during exertion. Syncope while sitting or lying. No prodrome at all. New chest pain or breathlessness. A family history of sudden death under 40. Known structural heart disease. An abnormal ECG. A significant injury sustained because there was no warning.
### Immediate approach
Check that the patient has recovered fully, and look for injury sustained in the fall.

Assess whether this was syncope, then triage by the red flags above. A patient with any of them is assessed urgently and usually needs monitoring, because the risk is a recurrence that is not survived. A patient with a clear reflex faint, a normal ECG and no red flags can be reassured and managed with explanation, trigger avoidance and counter-pressure manoeuvres.

Admission thresholds, risk scores and device decisions follow the current syncope guidance in force locally; none is reproduced here, because these are exactly the recommendations that change between editions.
## hold_these
Syncope is defined by global cerebral hypoperfusion with rapid, complete, spontaneous recovery.
Syncope on exertion, or while sitting or lying, or with no warning at all, is cardiac until proven otherwise.
A 12-lead ECG is taken in every patient with syncope; brain imaging and EEG are not part of the routine assessment.
## lose_the_mark
Calling a faint with brief myoclonic jerks a seizure. Short jerks are common in syncope of any cause.
Ordering a CT head for a typical vasovagal faint.
Omitting the family history of sudden death, which is the single question most likely to change management.

## summary
Swelling is one sign with four mechanisms behind it. Where it is, and what it does when you press it, narrows the cause faster than any blood test.
## sections
### Definition
Oedema is a palpable accumulation of fluid in the interstitial space. It becomes clinically detectable only after several litres have accumulated in an adult, which is why a patient can gain considerable weight before any swelling is visible.

Oedema is owned by the cardiovascular system in this library; the renal and hepatic causes are cross-referenced rather than duplicated.
### Mechanisms
Fluid movement across the capillary wall is governed by the balance of hydrostatic and oncotic pressures on either side of it. Oedema appears when that balance shifts towards filtration, or when the lymphatics that normally clear the excess cannot keep up. Four routes:

**Raised capillary hydrostatic pressure** — venous obstruction, heart failure, or salt and water retention. This is the commonest cardiovascular route.

**Reduced plasma oncotic pressure** — a low plasma albumin from nephrotic syndrome, liver disease or malnutrition.

**Increased capillary permeability** — inflammation, sepsis and burns, where protein escapes and takes water with it.

**Lymphatic obstruction** — surgery, malignancy, radiotherapy, or filariasis, which matters locally in a way it does not in Western textbooks.
### Differential diagnosis
**Bilateral and dependent:** heart failure, chronic venous insufficiency, drug causes, and the dependent oedema of prolonged immobility.

**Bilateral and generalised, including the face:** low plasma oncotic pressure — nephrotic syndrome, liver failure, protein-losing states, severe malnutrition.

**Unilateral:** deep vein thrombosis, cellulitis, lymphoedema, or local venous obstruction. A single swollen limb is a different problem from two.
### Focused history
Ask how it began and how it changes through the day. Cardiac and venous oedema is worse in the evening and better after a night lying flat. Ask about breathlessness, orthopnoea and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea, which point to the heart; about frothy urine, which points to the kidney; about jaundice and alcohol, which point to the liver.

Ask about drugs — calcium channel blockers and several others cause dependent oedema without any organ failure and are a frequent and easily reversed cause.

Ask about pain and speed of onset. Rapid, painful, unilateral swelling is venous thrombosis or infection until it is excluded.
### Focused examination
Press firmly over a bony prominence for several seconds and look for pitting. Pitting oedema indicates fluid that is still mobile; non-pitting swelling suggests lymphoedema or myxoedema.

Establish the upper level of the oedema, and check the sacrum in anyone confined to bed, where dependent fluid collects instead of the ankles. Assess the jugular venous pressure, which distinguishes a raised systemic venous pressure from a low albumin. Examine for ascites, hepatomegaly and pleural effusion.
### Initial investigation
Urinalysis for protein is the single most informative early test, because it separates the renal cause from the rest at almost no cost.

Serum albumin, renal and liver function follow. Where the history suggests the heart, natriuretic peptide and echocardiography assess it. Where the swelling is unilateral, the question is venous thrombosis and the investigation is compression ultrasonography, decided by the clinical probability rather than ordered reflexively.
### Red flags
Unilateral leg swelling, particularly if painful or of rapid onset. Oedema with breathlessness at rest or on lying flat. Facial or periorbital swelling with proteinuria. Oedema with fever and skin changes suggesting infection. Sudden generalised swelling with hypotension.
### Immediate approach
Establish first whether the swelling is unilateral or bilateral, because that single question separates a local problem from a systemic one and changes what happens next.

For unilateral swelling, assess the probability of deep vein thrombosis and investigate on that basis. For bilateral swelling, look for the organ system responsible — heart, kidney, liver — and review the drug list before attributing it to any of them.

Diuretic and specific therapy follow the cause and the guidance in force; no agent or dose is stated here, and treating oedema without establishing its mechanism is how a low-albumin state gets made worse.
## hold_these
Oedema becomes clinically detectable only after several litres have accumulated.
Bilateral dependent oedema is systemic; unilateral swelling is local and is thrombosis until excluded.
Urinalysis for protein is the highest-yield early test, because it separates the renal cause at once.
## lose_the_mark
Attributing all bilateral ankle oedema to the heart without checking the drug list or the albumin.
Missing sacral oedema in a bed-bound patient by looking only at the ankles.
Treating unilateral leg swelling as postural.

## summary
Angina of the leg. The pain is the least of it — the artery that hurts when walking is a marker for the arteries supplying the heart and brain.
## sections
### Definition
Claudication is cramping muscle pain brought on by walking a reproducible distance and relieved within minutes by standing still. The word comes from the Latin for limping.

It is the symptomatic expression of peripheral arterial disease: a fixed narrowing that delivers enough blood at rest and not enough during exercise.
### Mechanisms
The mechanism is identical to angina. A stenosis reduces maximal flow without reducing resting flow, so the muscle is adequately supplied until demand rises. During exercise, supply falls short, anaerobic metabolites accumulate, and pain follows. Rest restores the balance and the pain settles.

Because the limitation is fixed, the distance that provokes it is reproducible — which is what distinguishes it from most other causes of leg pain on walking.

The site of pain lies one level below the diseased segment: buttock and hip pain points to aorto-iliac disease, thigh pain to the common femoral, and calf pain — the commonest — to the superficial femoral artery.
### Differential diagnosis
**Spinal stenosis** is the important mimic. It also produces leg pain on walking, but it is relieved by sitting or bending forwards rather than by simply stopping, the distance is not reproducible, and walking uphill is often easier than downhill.

**Venous claudication** follows extensive deep venous obstruction: bursting rather than cramping pain, relieved by elevation.

**Musculoskeletal causes** — osteoarthritis of hip or knee — produce pain related to weight-bearing rather than to distance, and it does not settle promptly with rest.

**Chronic compartment syndrome** occurs in younger, athletic patients at high exercise intensity.
### Focused history
Establish the claudication distance and whether it is reproducible, what relieves it and how quickly, and whether the distance has been shortening.

Ask specifically about rest pain — pain in the forefoot at night, relieved by hanging the leg out of bed — and about ulceration or gangrene, because these mark a threatened limb rather than a limited one.

Take a full vascular risk history: smoking above all, then diabetes, hypertension, lipids and family history. Ask about angina, previous infarction and stroke, since the same disease is elsewhere.
### Focused examination
Palpate all peripheral pulses — femoral, popliteal, posterior tibial and dorsalis pedis — and compare sides. Listen for femoral bruits.

Look at the skin: hair loss, thin shiny skin, thickened nails, and ulceration at pressure points or between the toes. Check capillary refill, and elevate the leg and lower it to look for pallor on elevation and reactive redness on dependency.

Examine the heart and check for an abdominal aortic aneurysm, which coexists often enough to be worth the moment it takes.
### Initial investigation
The ankle–brachial pressure index is the first-line test: a Doppler measurement of ankle systolic pressure divided by brachial, with a reduced ratio confirming arterial disease. In diabetes and chronic kidney disease the arteries may be incompressible and the index falsely raised, in which case toe pressures or waveform analysis are used instead.

Duplex ultrasonography maps the disease when intervention is contemplated. CT or MR angiography defines the anatomy before revascularisation. None of this is needed to make the diagnosis, which is clinical.

Because the finding is a marker of generalised atherosclerosis, assessment includes glucose, lipids, renal function and blood pressure.
### Red flags
Pain at rest, particularly in the forefoot at night. Ulceration or gangrene. A sudden change from stable claudication to severe pain with a cold, pale, pulseless limb, which is acute limb ischaemia and an emergency. A rapidly shortening claudication distance.
### Immediate approach
For stable claudication the first priority is not the leg. Peripheral arterial disease is a marker of coronary and cerebrovascular disease, and the risk to the patient's life exceeds the risk to the limb. Cardiovascular risk reduction — smoking cessation above all — and a supervised exercise programme are the foundation of management, and exercise reliably improves walking distance.

For rest pain, tissue loss or acute limb ischaemia, the limb is threatened and the patient needs urgent vascular assessment.

## hold_these
Claudication is reproducible at a fixed distance and relieved within minutes of stopping.
Rest pain and tissue loss mark a threatened limb, not a limited one.
Peripheral arterial disease is a marker of coronary and cerebrovascular disease; the greater risk is to the patient, not to the leg.
## lose_the_mark
Missing spinal stenosis, which is relieved by sitting or bending forwards rather than by stopping.
Trusting a normal ankle–brachial index in diabetes or chronic kidney disease, where incompressible arteries falsely raise it.
Treating the leg and neglecting the smoking, lipids and glucose that will determine the outcome.
