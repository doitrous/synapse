## summary
A pump that cannot empty, and a body that responds by making the job harder. The treatment that prolongs life blocks the compensation rather than stimulating the heart — which is the single most counter-intuitive fact in cardiology.
## sections
### Definition
Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction is the syndrome of characteristic symptoms and signs together with a left ventricular ejection fraction below the threshold defined by the guideline in force.

Heart failure is a syndrome rather than a diagnosis. Naming it does not name a cause, and the diagnosis is incomplete until the underlying disease is identified.
### Epidemiology
Heart failure is common, its prevalence rises steeply with age, and it accounts for a substantial share of medical admissions and of readmission. Roughly half of all patients with heart failure have a reduced ejection fraction and half a preserved one.

No incidence or prevalence figure is asserted. Egyptian data require a verified national source and are recorded as an evidence gap.
### Aetiology and risk factors
Ischaemic heart disease is the commonest cause in most populations, followed by hypertension. Others: valvular disease, dilated cardiomyopathy of any cause, myocarditis, arrhythmia — particularly sustained tachycardia — toxins including alcohol and anthracyclines, thyroid disease and peripartum cardiomyopathy.

Locally, rheumatic valvular disease remains a more prominent contributor than in Western series, which changes the differential when a young patient presents with heart failure.
### Pathophysiology
The ventricle cannot eject adequately, so end-systolic volume rises. Filling adds to that residual volume and the chamber dilates; the muscle adds sarcomeres in series and the wall thins relative to the cavity. Dilatation raises wall stress, which raises oxygen demand and worsens the problem.

The fall in output activates the sympathetic nervous system and the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system. Both restore output initially — by raising rate, contractility, vascular tone and filling — and both are destructive when sustained. Raised afterload opposes ejection, retained fluid produces congestion, and angiotensin II and aldosterone drive fibrosis and adverse remodelling.

That is why the drugs that prolong life in this condition are those that block these systems, and why inotropes improve the numbers acutely while worsening survival.
### Clinical picture
Exertional breathlessness progressing to breathlessness at rest, orthopnoea, and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea. Fatigue and exercise intolerance reflect the low output. Ankle swelling and abdominal distension reflect systemic congestion once the right side is involved.

Signs: tachycardia, a displaced and diffuse apex beat, a third heart sound, raised jugular venous pressure, bibasal crackles, pleural effusion, hepatomegaly, ascites and peripheral oedema. Cool peripheries and a narrow pulse pressure indicate low output.
### Investigation
The purpose of investigation is to confirm the syndrome, measure the ejection fraction and find the cause.

Natriuretic peptide is measured where the diagnosis is uncertain. A low value in an untreated breathless patient makes heart failure very unlikely, which is the property that makes the test worth doing. A raised value has many other causes.

Echocardiography is the central investigation: it measures the ejection fraction, defines chamber size and wall thickness, assesses valves, estimates filling pressures and often reveals the cause.

ECG looks for previous infarction, arrhythmia, conduction disease and hypertrophy; a completely normal ECG makes heart failure with reduced ejection fraction unlikely. Chest radiograph, bloods including renal function, glucose, thyroid function, full blood count and iron studies complete the initial work. Ischaemia is investigated because it is the commonest cause and the most treatable.
### Management
Management has three strands and they are not equally weighted.

**Disease-modifying therapy** is the strand that prolongs life. It consists of drug classes that block the neurohormonal compensations, introduced together and up-titrated as tolerated. This is the counter-intuitive core of treatment: the failing heart is not stimulated but protected.

**Symptomatic therapy** relieves congestion. It improves how the patient feels without altering prognosis.

**Device and interventional therapy** — resynchronisation where conduction delay is causing dyssynchrony, defibrillator implantation where arrhythmic risk is high, revascularisation where ischaemia is the driver, and valve intervention where valve disease is — is considered against the criteria in the guideline in force.

Alongside these: treat the cause, correct precipitants, manage comorbidity, and enrol the patient in cardiac rehabilitation and structured follow-up, which reduce readmission.

Every agent, dose, target, ejection fraction threshold and device criterion follows the heart failure guideline in force where the patient is treated. None is reproduced here; these recommendations change substantially between editions.
### Complications and prognosis
Progressive pump failure, arrhythmia and sudden death, thromboembolism, renal dysfunction, hepatic congestion and cardiac cachexia.

Prognosis has improved considerably with modern therapy but remains serious, and is worse than that of many common cancers when treatment is not optimised. It depends on ejection fraction, functional class, renal function, natriuretic peptide level and whether disease-modifying therapy is actually delivered at effective doses.
### Classification
Functional classification by symptom severity, and staging by structural progression, are both used and answer different questions — one describes how the patient is now, the other how far the disease has advanced. Both follow the definitions in the guideline in force.
## hold_these
Heart failure is a syndrome; the diagnosis is incomplete until the cause is named.
The drugs that prolong life block the neurohormonal compensations rather than stimulating the heart.
A completely normal ECG makes heart failure with reduced ejection fraction unlikely.
## lose_the_mark
Treating congestion and stopping there. Diuretics make the patient feel better and do not change prognosis.
Reaching for an inotrope because the pump is weak; that improves haemodynamics and worsens survival.
Recording "heart failure" as the diagnosis without pursuing ischaemia, valve disease or arrhythmia.

## summary
The ejection fraction is normal and the patient is drowning. Half of all heart failure looks like this, and the number that reassures you is the one measuring the wrong thing.
## sections
### Definition
Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction is the syndrome of heart failure with a left ventricular ejection fraction at or above the threshold defined by the guideline in force, together with objective evidence of a structural or functional cardiac abnormality — raised filling pressures, left atrial enlargement or left ventricular hypertrophy.

The extra requirement exists because a normal fraction alone cannot support the diagnosis; something must demonstrate the abnormal physiology.
### Epidemiology
Approximately half of patients with heart failure have a preserved ejection fraction, and the proportion increases with age. Patients are older on average, more often women, and more often have hypertension, obesity, diabetes, atrial fibrillation and chronic kidney disease.

No proportion is asserted as a figure; estimates depend on the fraction threshold used and on the population studied.
### Aetiology and risk factors
Hypertension is the dominant contributor, through concentric hypertrophy and interstitial fibrosis. Ageing, obesity, diabetes, chronic kidney disease and atrial fibrillation are strongly associated. Specific causes producing a similar physiology include hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, infiltrative disease such as amyloidosis, and constrictive pericarditis.
### Pathophysiology
The ventricle is stiff and relaxes poorly. Filling therefore requires a raised left atrial pressure, and that pressure is transmitted to the pulmonary circulation, producing breathlessness and congestion.

Because the cavity is small and stiff, the ventricle ejects a normal fraction of a reduced end-diastolic volume — the ratio looks normal while the stroke volume is limited. Ejection fraction is a ratio, and a normal ratio of a small number is still a small number.

Two dependencies follow. Passive filling is impaired, so the atrial contribution matters more than usual; and filling takes time, so a shortened diastole matters more than usual. Atrial fibrillation removes the first and tachycardia removes the second, which is why either precipitates decompensation out of proportion to the rhythm change.

On exertion the limitation is exposed: filling pressure rises steeply, stroke volume cannot increase, and the patient stops.
### Clinical picture
Exertional breathlessness and fatigue, orthopnoea, and congestion — indistinguishable at the bedside from heart failure with a reduced fraction.

Signs point to the substrate rather than the syndrome: hypertension, a sustained non-displaced apex beat, a fourth heart sound, and often atrial fibrillation. The apex is characteristically not displaced, because the cavity is not dilated.
### Investigation
Echocardiography is central and must go beyond the ejection fraction: left ventricular hypertrophy, left atrial enlargement, diastolic function indices and estimated filling pressures are what support the diagnosis.

Natriuretic peptide is useful, with the caveat that values are lower in this group than in heart failure with a reduced fraction and lower again in obesity, so a normal value is less reassuring here.

Where the picture is uncertain, exercise testing with echocardiography or invasive measurement of filling pressure during exercise resolves it. Investigation should also look for the specific mimics — amyloidosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, constrictive pericarditis — because those have specific treatments.
### Management
Treatment of the syndrome has historically been far less effective than for a reduced ejection fraction, and the emphasis is different.

Congestion is relieved with diuretic therapy, titrated carefully: these patients sit on a steep filling-pressure curve and tolerate both over- and under-filling badly.

The substrate is treated — blood pressure control above all, then weight, glycaemic control, and rate and rhythm management for atrial fibrillation, where restoring or preserving atrial contraction may matter more than in other patients.

Specific mimics receive their own specific therapy, which is the main reason for pursuing them.

Disease-modifying drug therapy for this phenotype has developed considerably and is defined by the guideline in force; classes and thresholds are not reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Recurrent decompensation and hospitalisation, atrial fibrillation, pulmonary hypertension, right heart failure and renal dysfunction.

Mortality approaches that of heart failure with a reduced ejection fraction, and a greater proportion of deaths is non-cardiovascular, reflecting the comorbidity burden. The prognosis is not benign, which is the practical consequence of the diagnosis being missed when the fraction looks normal.
### Classification
The ejection fraction classifies heart failure because the two phenotypes respond differently to treatment. It is a classifier chosen for its therapeutic consequences, not the criterion that defines the syndrome.
## hold_these
Ejection fraction is a ratio; a normal ratio of a small end-diastolic volume still gives a small stroke volume.
A stiff ventricle depends on atrial contraction and on diastolic time, so atrial fibrillation and tachycardia decompensate it disproportionately.
Mortality approaches that of heart failure with a reduced ejection fraction.
## lose_the_mark
Excluding heart failure because the ejection fraction is normal.
Reading a non-displaced apex as evidence against heart failure; the cavity is not dilated in this phenotype.
Diuresing aggressively. These patients tolerate under-filling as badly as over-filling.

## summary
Two questions answered at the bedside — is the patient congested, and are they perfused — sort acute heart failure into four situations that need different things. The ejection fraction can wait.
## sections
### Definition
Acute heart failure is the rapid onset or worsening of the symptoms and signs of heart failure, requiring urgent assessment and treatment. It may be the first presentation, or decompensation of established disease.
### Epidemiology
Acute heart failure is among the commonest causes of emergency medical admission in adults over sixty-five, and readmission within months is frequent. Most presentations are decompensation of known heart failure rather than a first episode.
### Aetiology and risk factors
Most decompensations have a precipitant, and finding it is part of the treatment. Common precipitants: acute coronary syndrome, arrhythmia — particularly atrial fibrillation with a fast ventricular rate — uncontrolled hypertension, infection, non-adherence to medication or to fluid and salt restriction, anaemia, thyroid disease, renal deterioration, and drugs that cause fluid retention or depress contractility.

First presentations follow acute coronary syndrome, acute valvular failure, myocarditis, hypertensive crisis and arrhythmia.
### Pathophysiology
Two mechanisms produce two different clinical pictures.

**Fluid accumulation** develops over days to weeks: sodium and water retention progressively expand the intravascular and interstitial volume, producing weight gain, peripheral oedema and gradually worsening breathlessness.

**Fluid redistribution** develops over minutes to hours: a sudden rise in afterload or filling pressure shifts blood from the systemic venous reservoir into the pulmonary circulation. The lungs flood in a patient whose total body fluid may be entirely normal. Such patients are typically hypertensive and are not volume-overloaded, and they respond to reducing afterload and preload rather than to large doses of diuretic.

Where output falls far enough that tissue perfusion fails, the picture becomes cardiogenic shock — cold, underperfused and often oliguric — which is a different problem from congestion and carries a much worse prognosis.
### Clinical picture
Severe breathlessness, orthopnoea, distress, sweating, tachypnoea and tachycardia. Widespread crackles and sometimes wheeze. Frothy sputum, occasionally pink, in florid pulmonary oedema.

Congestion is assessed by jugular venous pressure, crackles, oedema, hepatomegaly and orthopnoea. Perfusion is assessed by peripheral temperature, capillary refill, pulse pressure, mental state and urine output.

Those two assessments place the patient on the two axes: **wet or dry**, and **warm or cold**. The four combinations need different treatment, and both axes are available immediately without any investigation.
### Investigation
Assessment and treatment run in parallel; investigation does not delay treatment in a patient who is severely compromised.

ECG immediately, to identify acute coronary syndrome or arrhythmia as the precipitant. Chest radiograph for pulmonary congestion and alternative diagnoses. Natriuretic peptide where the diagnosis is uncertain. Troponin, renal function, electrolytes, full blood count, glucose and thyroid function. Arterial or venous blood gas where the patient is severely unwell, and lactate as a marker of perfusion.

Echocardiography is performed urgently where a mechanical cause, valvular failure or tamponade is suspected, and early in all first presentations.
### Management
Treatment is directed by the congestion–perfusion profile rather than by the ejection fraction.

The **wet and warm** patient — congested with adequate perfusion, the commonest profile — is treated by relieving congestion, with vasodilatation where the blood pressure is high and the mechanism is redistribution.

The **wet and cold** patient is congested and underperfused, and needs perfusion addressed as well as congestion; this group requires senior and often critical care involvement.

The **dry and cold** patient is underfilled and underperfused, and the response to further diuresis would be harmful.

Alongside this: sit the patient up, give oxygen for hypoxia only, provide ventilatory support where indicated, treat the precipitant — reperfusion for an acute coronary syndrome, rate or rhythm control for arrhythmia — and monitor fluid balance, weight, renal function and electrolytes.

Every agent, dose, infusion, ventilation mode and threshold follows the acute heart failure guideline in force where the patient is treated. None is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Respiratory failure, cardiogenic shock, arrhythmia, acute kidney injury from congestion and from treatment, and death. In-hospital mortality is significant and post-discharge mortality and readmission rates are high, which is why the transition to optimised long-term therapy before discharge matters as much as the acute treatment.
### Classification
Classification by congestion and perfusion is used because it is clinical, immediate and directs treatment. Classification by ejection fraction matters for long-term therapy and is largely irrelevant in the first hour.
## hold_these
Assess congestion and perfusion separately; those two axes direct acute treatment, not the ejection fraction.
Flash pulmonary oedema in a hypertensive patient is often redistribution rather than fluid overload, and responds to reducing afterload.
Always look for the precipitant — finding it is part of the treatment.
## lose_the_mark
Giving a large diuretic dose to every breathless patient, including the hypertensive redistribution presentation who is not overloaded, and the cold underfilled patient in whom it is harmful.
Giving oxygen to a patient who is not hypoxic.
Treating the episode without identifying and correcting what precipitated it.

## summary
The chamber built for a low-pressure circuit, asked to work against a high-pressure one. Where the congestion appears tells you which side failed; what raised the load tells you why.
## sections
### Definition
Right heart failure is the inability of the right ventricle to deliver an adequate output without a raised filling pressure. The congestion appears in the systemic veins, because that is the circulation draining into the failing chamber.

Cor pulmonale is the subset caused by disease of the lungs, the airways, the pulmonary vasculature or the control of ventilation — right heart failure due to left heart disease is excluded from the term by definition.
### Epidemiology
Right heart failure most often accompanies left heart failure. Isolated right heart failure is less common and is dominated by chronic lung disease and pulmonary vascular disease.

Locally, schistosomiasis is a recognised cause of pulmonary hypertension and therefore of cor pulmonale, and is a differential that Western textbooks give little weight. Prevalence figures require a verified Egyptian source and are recorded as an evidence gap.
### Aetiology and risk factors
**Left heart disease** is the commonest cause, by transmitted pressure — this must be excluded before any other cause is pursued.

**Lung and pulmonary vascular disease**: chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, interstitial lung disease, obstructive sleep apnoea, pulmonary embolism acute or chronic, pulmonary arterial hypertension, and schistosomiasis.

**Right-sided cardiac disease**: right ventricular infarction, tricuspid and pulmonary valve disease, congenital heart disease including left-to-right shunts that eventually reverse, and arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy.
### Pathophysiology
The right ventricle is thin-walled and built for a low-resistance circuit. It tolerates volume load well and pressure load badly — an acute rise in pulmonary pressure, as in massive pulmonary embolism, can fail it within hours, whereas a gradual rise allows hypertrophy and years of compensation.

As it fails, the chamber dilates. Dilatation stretches the tricuspid annulus and produces functional tricuspid regurgitation, which worsens the volume load and accelerates the process. Pressure and volume are transmitted back into the systemic veins, producing the congestive signs.

Dilatation also pushes the interventricular septum leftwards, impairing left ventricular filling, so a failing right ventricle reduces left-sided output too. Perfusion of the right ventricle depends on the pressure gradient across it, which is why systemic hypotension can start a spiral that worsens right ventricular ischaemia.
### Clinical picture
Fatigue and exertional limitation from low output, and congestive symptoms: ankle and sacral oedema, abdominal distension from ascites, right upper quadrant discomfort from hepatic congestion, and early satiety and nausea from gut congestion.

Signs: raised jugular venous pressure, sometimes with a large regurgitant wave; a right ventricular heave; a pansystolic murmur at the lower left sternal edge increasing with inspiration where tricuspid regurgitation is present; tender pulsatile hepatomegaly, ascites and peripheral oedema.

Lung fields are typically clear in isolated right heart failure — a raised venous pressure with clear lungs is the pattern that should direct attention to the right side.
### Investigation
Echocardiography is the central investigation: right ventricular size and function, tricuspid regurgitation, estimated pulmonary artery pressure, and — crucially — assessment of the left heart, since left heart disease is the commonest cause.

ECG may show right axis deviation, right ventricular hypertrophy, right atrial enlargement or right bundle branch block. Chest radiograph, lung function tests, arterial blood gas, overnight oximetry and CT pulmonary angiography are directed by the suspected pulmonary cause. Bloods include liver and renal function, which are affected by congestion.

Right heart catheterisation measures pulmonary pressures directly where the diagnosis or the mechanism remains uncertain.
### Management
Treat the cause. That is the whole of the management strategy, and the specific treatment differs entirely between left heart disease, chronic lung disease, pulmonary embolism and pulmonary arterial hypertension.

Congestion is relieved with diuretic therapy, titrated with care because the right ventricle is preload-dependent and over-diuresis drops output.

Correct hypoxia, which is a pulmonary vasoconstrictor and therefore part of the load. Treat arrhythmia, since atrial fibrillation is poorly tolerated.

Pulmonary vasodilator therapy is appropriate for specific pulmonary vascular diagnoses and harmful where pulmonary hypertension is due to left heart disease — one of the clearest examples of why the cause must be established before treatment.

Agents, doses and thresholds follow the relevant guideline in force.
### Complications and prognosis
Progressive congestion, cardiac cirrhosis from chronic hepatic congestion, protein-losing enteropathy, arrhythmia, renal dysfunction from venous congestion, and death.

Prognosis is determined by the underlying cause rather than by the right ventricular failure itself.
### Egyptian context
Schistosomiasis is a recognised cause of pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale, arising from egg embolisation to the pulmonary vasculature with granulomatous obliteration and arteriopathy. In a patient with cor pulmonale and a history of exposure to fresh water in an endemic area, it belongs in the differential, and it will not be found by following a Western differential list.
## hold_these
Left heart failure is the commonest cause of right heart failure; exclude it before investigating anything else.
A raised jugular venous pressure with clear lung fields points to the right side.
The right ventricle is preload-dependent, so over-diuresis and any treatment reducing preload can drop output sharply.
## lose_the_mark
Investigating a primary pulmonary cause before excluding left heart disease.
Giving pulmonary vasodilator therapy for pulmonary hypertension that is secondary to left heart disease.
Omitting schistosomiasis from the differential in a patient with relevant exposure.

## summary
A big, weak ventricle with no loading condition and no coronary disease to blame. The definition is an exclusion, which means the work is in what you rule out.
## sections
### Definition
Dilated cardiomyopathy is left ventricular or biventricular dilatation with impaired systolic function, in the absence of abnormal loading conditions or coronary artery disease sufficient to explain it.

The exclusions are part of the definition. A dilated, poorly contracting ventricle caused by coronary disease is ischaemic cardiomyopathy, and by severe valve disease or hypertension it is that disease — not this one.
### Epidemiology
Dilated cardiomyopathy is the commonest cardiomyopathy and a leading indication for cardiac transplantation. It presents at any age, more often in men.
### Aetiology and risk factors
**Genetic** causes account for a substantial share, most commonly autosomal dominant with variable penetrance, which is why family history and family screening matter.

**Acquired** causes: previous myocarditis, alcohol, cocaine and amphetamines, anthracycline and trastuzumab chemotherapy, iron overload, thyroid disease, chronic sustained tachycardia — tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy, which is reversible if the rhythm is controlled — sarcoidosis, and severe prolonged nutritional deficiency including thiamine.

**Peripartum cardiomyopathy** arises in late pregnancy or the months after delivery and is a distinct entity with its own prognosis and recurrence risk.

A proportion remains idiopathic after full assessment.
### Pathophysiology
Whatever the insult, myocyte loss or dysfunction reduces contractility. The chamber dilates and adds sarcomeres in series; the wall thins relative to the enlarged cavity, so wall stress rises and demand with it.

Dilatation stretches the mitral annulus, producing functional mitral regurgitation, which adds volume load and accelerates the dilatation. Neurohormonal activation follows, and the same compensations that support output initially drive fibrosis and further remodelling.

Fibrosis and chamber dilatation create the substrate for both ventricular arrhythmia and intracardiac thrombus.
### Clinical picture
Most present with heart failure: exertional breathlessness, orthopnoea, fatigue, oedema.

Others present with arrhythmia — palpitation, syncope, or sudden death as the first event — or with thromboembolism from mural thrombus, or are found incidentally on imaging or family screening.

Signs are those of heart failure with a dilated ventricle: displaced diffuse apex beat, third heart sound, functional mitral regurgitation murmur, and congestion.
### Investigation
Echocardiography establishes the phenotype: dilated chamber, reduced ejection fraction, functional mitral regurgitation, and mural thrombus where present.

The rest of the investigation exists to find the cause, and to exclude the conditions the definition excludes. Coronary assessment is mandatory, since ischaemic cardiomyopathy is indistinguishable on echocardiography. Bloods: full blood count, renal and liver function, thyroid function, iron studies, calcium, and screening for infective and inflammatory causes as indicated.

Cardiac MRI characterises the myocardium, quantifies fibrosis and often distinguishes ischaemic from non-ischaemic patterns. Genetic testing and family screening are offered where an inherited cause is suspected. Endomyocardial biopsy is reserved for specific situations.
### Management
Treat as heart failure with reduced ejection fraction — the full disease-modifying regimen, congestion relief, and device therapy where criteria are met.

Treat or remove the cause: abstinence from alcohol, rate or rhythm control for tachycardia-induced disease, iron removal, immunosuppression for specific inflammatory causes. Several of these can produce substantial or complete recovery of function, which is why the aetiological work is worth doing thoroughly.

Anticoagulation is considered where mural thrombus is present or the risk of embolism is high.

Family screening is offered to first-degree relatives where a genetic cause is likely.

Agents, doses, device criteria and anticoagulation thresholds follow the guideline in force.
### Complications and prognosis
Progressive heart failure, ventricular arrhythmia and sudden death, atrial fibrillation, mural thrombus and systemic embolism.

Prognosis has improved substantially with modern heart failure therapy. It is better where a reversible cause is identified and removed, and worse with severe ventricular dysfunction, extensive fibrosis on MRI and a poor functional class.
### Classification
Cardiomyopathies are classified by phenotype — dilated, hypertrophic, restrictive, arrhythmogenic — because the phenotype determines presentation and management, and a single cause can produce more than one.
## hold_these
The exclusions are part of the definition, so coronary disease must be excluded before the diagnosis is made.
Several causes are reversible — alcohol, tachycardia, iron overload, thyroid disease — which is why the aetiological work matters.
Functional mitral regurgitation from annular stretch worsens the volume load and accelerates dilatation.
## lose_the_mark
Diagnosing dilated cardiomyopathy without assessing the coronary arteries.
Missing tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy and treating only the heart failure, when controlling the rhythm may restore function.
Omitting family screening where an inherited cause is likely.

## summary
A thick ventricle in a young person with nothing to explain it. The obstruction is dynamic, which is why the murmur behaves backwards — and why the first presentation can be a death on a football pitch.
## sections
### Definition
Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is left ventricular hypertrophy not explained by abnormal loading conditions, caused in most identified cases by mutations in genes encoding sarcomere proteins.

The hypertrophy is characteristically asymmetric and septal, though other distributions occur. Histology shows myocyte hypertrophy, myocyte disarray and interstitial fibrosis.
### Epidemiology
It is among the commonest inherited cardiac conditions and a leading cause of sudden cardiac death in young people and in athletes. Many affected individuals are asymptomatic and are identified only through family screening or incidental findings.
### Aetiology and risk factors
Most identified cases follow autosomal dominant inheritance of a sarcomere protein gene mutation, with variable penetrance and expression — which is why relatives may be affected to very different degrees, and why a normal echocardiogram in adolescence does not close the question.

Phenocopies produce a similar appearance by different mechanisms: Fabry disease, amyloidosis, Friedreich ataxia and some glycogen storage disorders. They matter because several have specific treatments.
### Pathophysiology
Three mechanisms produce the clinical picture.

**Diastolic dysfunction.** The hypertrophied, fibrotic ventricle is stiff and fills poorly, so filling pressures rise and breathlessness follows even when systolic function is vigorous.

**Dynamic outflow obstruction.** In many patients the hypertrophied basal septum narrows the outflow tract, and the accelerated flow draws the anterior mitral leaflet towards the septum during systole. This obstructs outflow and simultaneously causes mitral regurgitation. The obstruction is dynamic: it worsens when the ventricle is emptier, when contractility is higher, and when afterload is lower.

**Arrhythmia.** Myocyte disarray and patchy fibrosis provide the substrate for ventricular arrhythmia, which is the mechanism of sudden death.

Myocardial ischaemia occurs without coronary disease, because the hypertrophied muscle outgrows its capillary supply and the small intramural vessels are abnormal.
### Clinical picture
Many are asymptomatic. Symptoms when they occur are exertional breathlessness, chest pain, palpitation, presyncope and syncope. Exertional syncope is a warning symptom and must never be dismissed as benign in this population.

Sudden cardiac death may be the first manifestation, sometimes during or shortly after exertion.

Signs where obstruction is present: a jerky carotid pulse with a rapid upstroke, a double apical impulse, a fourth heart sound, and an ejection systolic murmur at the left sternal edge. The murmur increases with manoeuvres that reduce preload or afterload — standing from squatting, the strain phase of Valsalva — and decreases with squatting or handgrip. That direction is the opposite of aortic stenosis and is the bedside signature of dynamic obstruction.
### Investigation
Echocardiography is the primary investigation: the distribution and extent of hypertrophy, systolic anterior motion of the mitral valve, the outflow gradient at rest and with provocation, mitral regurgitation and diastolic function. Provocation matters because a resting gradient may be absent in a patient who obstructs on exertion.

ECG is almost always abnormal — voltage criteria for hypertrophy, ST and T-wave change, deep Q waves — and an entirely normal ECG makes the diagnosis less likely.

Cardiac MRI quantifies hypertrophy where echocardiographic windows are poor and demonstrates fibrosis, which contributes to risk assessment. Ambulatory monitoring and exercise testing contribute to arrhythmic risk stratification. Genetic testing and cascade screening of first-degree relatives are offered.
### Management
Three separate problems are managed, and they are not the same problem.

**Symptoms** from obstruction and diastolic dysfunction are treated with drug classes that reduce contractility and slow the heart, lengthening filling time and reducing the dynamic gradient. Agents that reduce preload or afterload, or that increase contractility, worsen obstruction and are avoided. Where symptoms persist despite medical therapy, septal reduction by surgical myectomy or alcohol septal ablation is considered.

**Sudden death risk** is assessed formally, and an implantable defibrillator is offered where the estimated risk crosses the threshold in the guideline in force. Risk assessment is repeated over time rather than performed once.

**The family** is screened, because the condition is inherited and relatives may be affected and asymptomatic.

Exercise advice is individualised against current guidance rather than a blanket prohibition.

Every agent, dose, gradient threshold, risk model and device criterion follows the hypertrophic cardiomyopathy guideline in force. None is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Sudden cardiac death, progressive heart failure, atrial fibrillation with a high embolic risk, infective endocarditis where there is significant regurgitation, and progression to a dilated hypokinetic phase in a minority.

With modern risk stratification and defibrillator therapy, most patients have a near-normal life expectancy — which makes identifying them the intervention that matters most.
### Classification
Patients are described as obstructive or non-obstructive by whether an outflow gradient is present at rest or on provocation, because that determines symptomatic management. The distinction does not by itself determine arrhythmic risk.
## hold_these
The murmur of dynamic obstruction increases with standing and Valsalva and decreases with squatting — the opposite of aortic stenosis.
Exertional syncope in a young person is a warning symptom, not a benign one.
Family screening is part of management, because relatives may be affected and asymptomatic.
## lose_the_mark
Treating the outflow gradient with a vasodilator or a diuretic; reducing preload or afterload worsens dynamic obstruction.
Excluding the diagnosis because there is no gradient at rest, without provocation testing.
Assessing sudden death risk once and never revisiting it.

## summary
Small stiff ventricles and enormous atria. It looks exactly like constrictive pericarditis — and telling them apart decides whether an operation can help.
## sections
### Definition
Restrictive cardiomyopathy is myocardial disease producing a stiff, non-compliant ventricle with impaired diastolic filling, normal or near-normal systolic function, and normal or reduced ventricular cavity size.

The atria dilate markedly, because they empty against a ventricle that will not accept blood. Biatrial enlargement with non-dilated ventricles is the shape that should suggest the diagnosis.
### Epidemiology
It is the least common of the major cardiomyopathy phenotypes in most populations. Its causes vary sharply by geography, which is what makes the local differential different from the textbook one.
### Aetiology and risk factors
**Infiltrative**: amyloidosis — the commonest identified cause in many series — and sarcoidosis.

**Storage**: haemochromatosis, Fabry disease and glycogen storage disorders.

**Endomyocardial**: endomyocardial fibrosis, hypereosinophilic syndrome with endomyocardial involvement, carcinoid heart disease, and radiation or anthracycline injury.

**Idiopathic and familial** forms occur.

Endomyocardial fibrosis is a leading cause of restrictive cardiomyopathy in tropical and subtropical regions, including parts of Africa, which raises its relevance to the local differential.
### Pathophysiology
Infiltration, storage or fibrosis stiffens the myocardium or obliterates the ventricular cavity. Ventricular pressure rises steeply for a small increase in volume, so filling is rapid in early diastole and then stops abruptly as the chamber reaches its limit.

Filling pressures rise in both ventricles and are transmitted to the atria, which dilate, and then to the pulmonary and systemic veins. Cardiac output is limited because the ventricles cannot fill, not because they cannot contract.

Atrial dilatation causes atrial fibrillation, which is poorly tolerated: a ventricle dependent on atrial contraction and on filling time loses both at once.
### Clinical picture
Progressive breathlessness and fatigue with prominent right-sided congestion — raised jugular venous pressure, hepatomegaly, ascites and peripheral oedema, often with ascites disproportionate to the leg swelling.

Signs: a raised venous pressure that rises rather than falls on inspiration where the ventricles cannot accommodate the increased return, a third or fourth heart sound, and frequently atrial fibrillation. The apex beat is not displaced.

Features of the underlying disease may dominate — the neuropathy, macroglossia or proteinuria of amyloidosis, the skin pigmentation and diabetes of haemochromatosis.
### Investigation
Echocardiography shows non-dilated ventricles with normal or mildly reduced systolic function, marked biatrial enlargement, thickened walls where infiltration is present, and a restrictive filling pattern.

The essential differential is constrictive pericarditis, which produces an almost identical picture. Ventricular interdependence — exaggerated respiratory variation in filling between the two ventricles — favours constriction, and its absence favours restriction. Pericardial thickening or calcification on CT or MRI supports constriction, and cardiac catheterisation with simultaneous pressure measurement resolves difficult cases.

Cardiac MRI characterises the myocardium and identifies infiltration. Investigation is then directed at the cause: serum and urine protein studies for amyloidosis, iron studies for haemochromatosis, and biopsy of an affected tissue or of the myocardium where required.
### Management
Treat the cause where it can be treated. Several causes now have specific and effective therapy — amyloidosis and haemochromatosis in particular — which is the strongest reason to pursue the aetiology rather than accepting the phenotype.

Congestion is managed with careful diuresis. These patients depend on a high filling pressure and tolerate over-diuresis badly, so the therapeutic window is narrow.

Maintaining sinus rhythm is valuable where achievable, and anticoagulation is considered in atrial fibrillation and in the presence of intracardiac thrombus.

Rate-slowing therapy that would be routine in other forms of heart failure may be poorly tolerated, since output in a fixed-stroke-volume state depends on rate.

Transplantation is considered in selected patients. Agents, doses and thresholds follow the guideline in force.
### Complications and prognosis
Progressive heart failure, atrial fibrillation, thromboembolism, conduction disease requiring pacing — particularly in infiltrative disease — and death.

Prognosis is determined chiefly by the underlying cause, and has improved markedly for those causes with specific therapy.
### Classification
Restriction is a disease of the myocardium; constriction is a disease of the pericardium. Both impair filling and present almost identically. The distinction is made because constriction can be relieved by pericardiectomy and restriction generally cannot.
## hold_these
Non-dilated ventricles with markedly dilated atria is the shape that suggests restriction.
Constrictive pericarditis is the essential differential, and it is surgically treatable.
These patients depend on a high filling pressure; over-diuresis drops output sharply.
## lose_the_mark
Accepting the phenotype without pursuing the cause, when several causes now have specific effective therapy.
Missing constrictive pericarditis and denying a patient an operation that could transform their condition.
Applying standard heart failure rate-slowing therapy without considering that output here may depend on rate.

## summary
Chest pain, a raised troponin and an abnormal ECG in a young patient — with normal coronary arteries. The commonest mistake is calling that reassuring.
## sections
### Definition
Myocarditis is inflammation of the myocardium, diagnosed on histological, immunological and immunohistochemical criteria. Where it is accompanied by cardiac dysfunction it is termed inflammatory cardiomyopathy.
### Epidemiology
The true incidence is unknown, because mild cases resolve unrecognised and confirmation requires investigation that is not always performed. It is an important cause of sudden cardiac death in young adults and a recognised cause of dilated cardiomyopathy.
### Aetiology and risk factors
**Infective** causes dominate, and viruses are the commonest — enteroviruses, adenovirus, parvovirus B19, human herpesvirus 6, influenza and SARS-CoV-2 among others. Bacterial, fungal and protozoal causes occur; **Chagas disease** is a major cause in Latin America and is a differential to remember in a patient from an endemic region.

**Immune-mediated** causes: systemic autoimmune disease, sarcoidosis, giant cell myocarditis, and hypersensitivity reactions to drugs. Immune checkpoint inhibitor myocarditis is an increasingly recognised and severe form.

**Toxic** causes: alcohol, cocaine and amphetamines, and some chemotherapeutic agents.
### Pathophysiology
The initial insult — usually viral — injures myocytes directly and triggers an immune response. In most people that response clears the agent and resolves.

In some, the immune response persists after the trigger has gone and continues to damage myocardium, which is how an acute infection becomes a chronic inflammatory cardiomyopathy. Ongoing inflammation and fibrosis impair contraction, dilate the chamber and create an arrhythmic substrate.

The clinical spectrum runs from subclinical inflammation, through a presentation that mimics an acute coronary syndrome, to fulminant myocarditis with cardiogenic shock.
### Clinical picture
Three patterns predominate.

**Chest pain**, often pleuritic and accompanied by pericarditis, with a raised troponin and ECG change. This is the presentation that mimics an acute coronary syndrome, typically in a younger patient with few vascular risk factors, sometimes with a preceding viral illness.

**Heart failure**, of new onset, in a young person without another explanation.

**Arrhythmia**, from palpitation to ventricular arrhythmia, complete heart block — characteristic of some causes including sarcoidosis and giant cell myocarditis — or sudden death.

A prodrome of fever, myalgia or gastrointestinal upset in the preceding weeks supports the diagnosis but is often absent.
### Investigation
ECG changes are common and non-specific: ST elevation that is often widespread and concave rather than territorial, T-wave change, conduction abnormality and arrhythmia.

Troponin is raised and reflects myocardial injury rather than the mechanism. Inflammatory markers are often raised.

Echocardiography assesses ventricular function, wall motion abnormalities that may not follow a coronary territory, and pericardial effusion.

Coronary assessment is usually necessary to exclude an acute coronary syndrome, since the presentations overlap. **Unobstructed coronary arteries in a patient with chest pain and a raised troponin is a finding that requires explanation, not reassurance.**

Cardiac MRI is the key non-invasive test, demonstrating oedema and a non-ischaemic pattern of late enhancement. Endomyocardial biopsy remains the diagnostic standard and is reserved for situations where the result will change treatment — particularly suspected giant cell myocarditis, sarcoidosis or a fulminant course.
### Management
Supportive treatment is the foundation: heart failure therapy as indicated, arrhythmia management, and haemodynamic and mechanical circulatory support in fulminant disease, which can be survived with full recovery if the patient is supported through it.

Exercise restriction during the acute illness is advised, since exertion during active myocarditis is associated with arrhythmia and sudden death. The duration follows current guidance.

Cause-specific treatment is given where a cause is identified: immunosuppression for giant cell myocarditis, sarcoidosis and checkpoint inhibitor myocarditis, and withdrawal of the offending agent in toxic and hypersensitivity forms. Routine immunosuppression for viral myocarditis is not established.

Follow-up assesses recovery of function, since a proportion progresses to dilated cardiomyopathy.

Agents, doses, restriction periods and support thresholds follow the guideline in force; none is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Dilated cardiomyopathy, chronic heart failure, ventricular arrhythmia and sudden death, and complete heart block.

Most patients with uncomplicated viral myocarditis recover fully. Prognosis is worse with impaired ventricular function at presentation, with extensive fibrosis on MRI, and in giant cell myocarditis. Fulminant myocarditis carries high acute mortality but good recovery in those who survive the acute phase.
### Classification
Classification by cause — infective, immune-mediated, toxic — is what determines whether specific treatment exists, which is why the aetiological question is pursued despite most cases being viral and self-limiting.
## hold_these
Myocarditis mimics acute coronary syndrome; a young patient with chest pain, raised troponin and unobstructed coronary arteries needs an explanation, not reassurance.
Exertion during active myocarditis is associated with arrhythmia and sudden death.
Fulminant myocarditis has high acute mortality and good recovery in survivors, so aggressive support is justified.
## lose_the_mark
Discharging a young patient with a raised troponin and normal coronary arteries as having had nothing wrong.
Assuming all myocarditis is viral and self-limiting, and missing giant cell myocarditis or sarcoidosis where immunosuppression changes the outcome.
Failing to follow up ventricular function, when a proportion progresses to dilated cardiomyopathy.
