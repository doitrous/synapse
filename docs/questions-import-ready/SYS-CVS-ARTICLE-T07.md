## summary
A disease with no symptoms, no single cause and no natural threshold — diagnosed by measuring people who feel well, and treated because of what it does over decades rather than how it feels today.
## sections
### Definition
Primary hypertension is persistently raised arterial blood pressure without an identifiable single cause. It accounts for the large majority of hypertension.

The diagnostic threshold is a convention rather than a biological boundary: cardiovascular risk rises continuously with pressure, and the cut-off is drawn where treatment does more good than harm. That is why thresholds differ between guidelines and move over time.
### Epidemiology
Hypertension is among the most prevalent chronic conditions worldwide and the leading modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular death. Prevalence rises with age and with obesity.

It is a major public health problem in Egypt, where detection, treatment and control rates are all incomplete — the well-described gap in which a common, treatable and asymptomatic condition goes unmanaged. No prevalence or control figure is asserted here; verified national data are recorded as an evidence gap.
### Aetiology and risk factors
Primary hypertension arises from genetic susceptibility interacting with environment. Contributing factors: high sodium and low potassium intake, obesity, excess alcohol, physical inactivity, psychosocial stress, and obstructive sleep apnoea.

Non-modifiable: age, family history, and — in many populations — ethnicity, with differences in sodium handling and in response to particular drug classes.
### Pathophysiology
Mean arterial pressure is the product of cardiac output and total peripheral resistance, so every mechanism must act through one of them. In established primary hypertension the abnormality is chiefly raised peripheral resistance.

Several systems contribute rather than one. **Renal sodium handling** is central: a rightward shift in the pressure–natriuresis relationship means a higher pressure is required to excrete a given sodium load. **Sympathetic activity** raises resistance and heart rate. **The renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system** contributes vasoconstriction and sodium retention. **Vascular remodelling** thickens resistance vessel walls and narrows their lumen, which sustains the raised resistance and makes the state self-perpetuating.

**Arterial stiffening** with age reduces the aorta's ability to buffer each stroke volume, raising systolic pressure and lowering diastolic — which is why isolated systolic hypertension predominates in older people.

Damage accumulates silently in the heart, brain, kidneys, retina and large arteries. Its presence is a record of cumulative exposure and raises risk independently of the current reading.
### Clinical picture
Uncomplicated hypertension produces no symptoms at any level. Headache, epistaxis and dizziness are unreliable, and their absence provides no reassurance.

The condition is therefore found by measurement in people who feel well, or through the presentation of a complication — stroke, infarction, heart failure or renal impairment.

Examination assesses target organ damage and screens for secondary causes: fundoscopy for retinopathy, cardiovascular examination for a displaced apex or added sounds, abdominal examination for bruits and masses, and comparison of the pulses.
### Investigation
Confirm the diagnosis before committing a patient to lifelong treatment. Clinic readings misclassify in both directions, so out-of-office measurement — ambulatory or home — is part of making the diagnosis. White coat hypertension avoids unnecessary treatment; masked hypertension, which is the more dangerous, avoids missed treatment.

Assess target organ damage: ECG for left ventricular hypertrophy, urinalysis and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, serum creatinine with estimated filtration rate, and fundoscopy. Echocardiography where hypertrophy or heart failure is suspected.

Assess overall cardiovascular risk: lipids, glucose or HbA1c, and a formal risk estimate, because treatment decisions depend on total risk rather than on the pressure alone.

Screen for secondary causes where the features described in the secondary hypertension article are present.
### Management
**Lifestyle measures** apply to everyone: sodium restriction, weight reduction, regular aerobic activity, moderation of alcohol, increased dietary potassium where renal function permits, and smoking cessation for overall risk. These produce clinically meaningful reductions and are not a preliminary to be skipped.

**Drug treatment** is offered according to the pressure and the total cardiovascular risk. The major classes act on the mechanisms above, combination therapy at moderate doses is generally more effective and better tolerated than a single agent at high dose, and choice is influenced by age, ethnicity, comorbidity and pregnancy.

**Adherence** is the dominant practical problem in an asymptomatic condition treated for decades. Single-pill combinations, simple regimens and explanation of why a symptomless condition is being treated do more for outcomes than fine adjustment of agent choice.

**Treat the whole risk profile**, not the pressure alone — lipids, glycaemia and smoking.

Every threshold, target, agent, dose and combination follows the hypertension guideline in force where the patient is treated. None is reproduced here; these are among the most frequently revised recommendations in medicine.
### Complications and prognosis
Stroke, both ischaemic and haemorrhagic — hypertension is the single most important modifiable risk factor for stroke; ischaemic heart disease; heart failure, both phenotypes; chronic kidney disease; atrial fibrillation; aortic aneurysm and dissection; peripheral arterial disease; and hypertensive retinopathy.

Prognosis is substantially improved by treatment, and the benefit is proportional to the reduction achieved and sustained. Because the disease is silent, most of that benefit depends on the patient continuing treatment they cannot feel working.
### Prevention
Population-level measures — reducing dietary sodium, improving diet quality, physical activity, and systematic detection through opportunistic measurement — have greater effect on the burden of hypertensive disease than any individual treatment decision.
### Egyptian context
Hypertension is a leading contributor to cardiovascular morbidity in Egypt, with substantial gaps in detection, treatment and control. Salt intake is high in the regional diet, which makes dietary sodium reduction a locally relevant intervention rather than a generic recommendation. Specific national figures require a verified source and are recorded as an evidence gap.
## hold_these
The diagnostic threshold is a treatment convention across a continuous risk relationship, not a biological boundary.
Uncomplicated hypertension is asymptomatic; symptoms neither confirm nor exclude it.
Out-of-office measurement is part of making the diagnosis, because clinic readings misclassify in both directions.
## lose_the_mark
Committing a patient to lifelong treatment on clinic readings alone.
Accepting a normal clinic reading in a high-risk patient without considering masked hypertension.
Treating the number and ignoring lipids, glycaemia and smoking, which carry much of the same risk.

## summary
A minority of hypertension has a findable cause, and some of those causes are curable. The skill is knowing which patients to investigate — screening everyone wastes resources, screening no one misses the operable ones.
### Definition
## sections
### Definition
Secondary hypertension is raised blood pressure with an identifiable underlying cause. It accounts for a minority of all hypertension but a much higher proportion among young patients, and among those with severe or resistant disease.

Resistant hypertension is pressure remaining above target despite an appropriate multi-drug regimen at adequate doses, with adherence confirmed — a definition that requires excluding poor adherence and inaccurate measurement before the label is applied.
### Epidemiology
Secondary causes are concentrated in identifiable groups. Prevalence estimates vary widely with the population studied and the intensity of screening, so no proportion is asserted here.

Primary aldosteronism is now recognised as the commonest endocrine cause and is substantially more frequent than the classical teaching implied, chiefly because it was historically sought only in hypokalaemic patients.
### Aetiology and risk factors
**Renal**: chronic kidney disease of any cause — the commonest secondary cause overall — and renovascular disease, from atherosclerosis in older patients and fibromuscular dysplasia in younger women.

**Endocrine**: primary aldosteronism; phaeochromocytoma and paraganglioma; Cushing syndrome; thyroid disease, both over- and underactivity; hyperparathyroidism; acromegaly.

**Vascular**: coarctation of the aorta, which classically presents with hypertension in the arms and reduced, delayed femoral pulses.

**Other**: obstructive sleep apnoea, which is common and frequently overlooked; drugs including oral contraceptives, corticosteroids, non-steroidal anti-inflammatories, sympathomimetics and some herbal preparations; and pregnancy-related hypertensive disorders.
### Pathophysiology
Each cause acts through the same final determinants — cardiac output and peripheral resistance — but by a different route, which is what makes each treatable in its own way.

**Renovascular disease**: a stenosis reduces perfusion pressure at the affected kidney, which releases renin. Angiotensin II then raises systemic pressure by vasoconstriction and sodium retention. This has a direct treatment consequence: in bilateral disease or a single functioning kidney, blocking the renin–angiotensin system removes the efferent arteriolar tone maintaining glomerular filtration, and renal function can deteriorate sharply.

**Primary aldosteronism**: autonomous aldosterone secretion causes sodium retention, volume expansion and potassium loss, with renin suppressed. Most patients are normokalaemic, which is precisely why relying on a low potassium as the trigger to test misses the majority.

**Phaeochromocytoma**: episodic catecholamine release produces paroxysmal hypertension with headache, palpitation and sweating, though sustained hypertension is also common.

**Coarctation**: mechanical obstruction raises pressure proximally and reduces it distally, producing the arm–leg gradient and radiofemoral delay.

**Obstructive sleep apnoea**: repeated nocturnal hypoxia and arousal raise sympathetic activity, producing hypertension that is characteristically resistant and non-dipping overnight.
### Clinical picture
The features that should prompt a search, rather than symptoms of the hypertension itself:

- onset at a young age, or after the age at which primary hypertension usually appears;
- severe hypertension, or hypertension resistant to an appropriate regimen;
- abrupt onset, or deterioration in a previously well-controlled patient;
- spontaneous or easily provoked hypokalaemia;
- clinical clues — paroxysmal symptoms, cushingoid appearance, radiofemoral delay, an abdominal bruit, snoring with daytime sleepiness, or a palpable renal mass.
### Investigation
Investigation is directed by the suspected cause rather than performed as a panel.

Baseline in all: electrolytes, creatinine, urinalysis and albumin-to-creatinine ratio.

Directed: aldosterone-to-renin ratio for primary aldosteronism, with attention to the drugs and potassium level that affect its interpretation; plasma or urinary metanephrines for phaeochromocytoma; overnight dexamethasone suppression or urinary free cortisol for Cushing syndrome; renal imaging and renal artery studies for renovascular disease; sleep studies for apnoea; echocardiography and cross-sectional imaging for coarctation.

Confirmatory testing, imaging and lateralisation studies follow specialist pathways, and interpretation is affected by concurrent drugs — so preparation for the test is part of the test.
### Management
Treat the cause where it can be treated, since several are curable or substantially improved: adrenalectomy for a unilateral aldosterone-producing adenoma, resection of a phaeochromocytoma after appropriate preparation, repair of coarctation, and treatment of sleep apnoea.

Where the cause is not correctable, treatment is directed by mechanism — a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist for aldosteronism managed medically, for instance — which is more effective than empirical escalation.

Confirm adherence and measurement accuracy before labelling hypertension resistant, since both are commoner explanations than a rare secondary cause.

Every test threshold, agent, dose and preoperative preparation follows the guideline in force; none is reproduced here. Preparation before surgery for phaeochromocytoma in particular is a specific sequence that must be taken from the current guideline rather than from memory.
### Complications and prognosis
The complications of hypertension itself, often more severe because the pressure is higher and less well controlled, together with the complications of the underlying disease.

Prognosis is good where a curable cause is identified and treated, and cure or substantial improvement is achievable in a meaningful proportion — which is the argument for investigating the right patients.
### Classification
Causes are grouped as renal, endocrine, vascular and other, because that grouping matches the investigations used to find them.
## hold_these
Most patients with primary aldosteronism have a normal potassium, so hypokalaemia is an insensitive trigger for testing.
Blocking the renin–angiotensin system in bilateral renal artery stenosis can cause a sharp fall in renal function.
Confirm adherence and measurement accuracy before calling hypertension resistant.
## lose_the_mark
Waiting for hypokalaemia before testing for primary aldosteronism.
Screening every hypertensive patient for secondary causes, or screening none.
Operating on a phaeochromocytoma without the preparation sequence specified in the current guideline.

## summary
Defined by the organ damage, not by the number — and the commonest error is treating the number. Lowering pressure too fast in a chronically hypertensive patient causes the ischaemia it was meant to prevent.
## sections
### Definition
A hypertensive emergency is severe hypertension accompanied by acute, ongoing target organ damage: hypertensive encephalopathy, intracranial haemorrhage, acute heart failure or pulmonary oedema, acute coronary syndrome, aortic dissection, acute kidney injury, retinal haemorrhage or papilloedema, or eclampsia.

Severe hypertension without acute organ damage is not an emergency. The distinction is the whole of the management decision.
### Epidemiology
Hypertensive emergencies are uncommon relative to the prevalence of hypertension, and occur most often in patients with known but poorly controlled or untreated disease, and in those who have stopped their medication.

They are relatively more frequent where detection and control rates are low, which makes them locally more relevant than their frequency in well-controlled populations would suggest.
### Aetiology and risk factors
Untreated or poorly controlled primary hypertension; abrupt cessation of antihypertensive therapy; secondary causes, particularly renovascular disease and phaeochromocytoma; acute kidney injury and glomerulonephritis; pre-eclampsia and eclampsia; sympathomimetic drugs including cocaine and amphetamines; and autoimmune disease with renal crisis.
### Pathophysiology
Beyond a critical pressure, the protective autoregulatory vasoconstriction of small arteries fails. Pressure is transmitted to the capillary bed, producing endothelial injury, fibrinoid necrosis of arterioles, increased permeability and, in the brain, cerebral oedema — which is hypertensive encephalopathy.

Endothelial damage activates the coagulation system and can produce a microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia. Renal injury activates the renin–angiotensin system, which raises pressure further: a self-sustaining cycle.

**Autoregulation has also reset.** In chronic hypertension the range over which cerebral and renal flow is held constant shifts upwards. A pressure that is normal for the population may lie below that patient's lower autoregulatory limit, so rapid normalisation causes cerebral, renal or myocardial ischaemia. This is why controlled partial reduction over hours is the principle, and why the specific exceptions requiring faster reduction — aortic dissection above all — must be known as exceptions.
### Clinical picture
Determined by which organ is affected.

**Neurological**: headache, visual disturbance, confusion, seizures, focal deficit, reduced consciousness.
**Cardiac**: chest pain, severe breathlessness, pulmonary oedema.
**Renal**: oliguria, haematuria, rapidly rising creatinine.
**Ophthalmic**: retinal haemorrhages, exudates and papilloedema — fundoscopy is a direct view of the microvasculature and is part of the assessment, not an optional refinement.
**Vascular**: tearing chest or back pain with pulse asymmetry, suggesting dissection.
**Obstetric**: headache, visual disturbance, epigastric pain and seizures in pregnancy or the puerperium.
### Investigation
Assessment and treatment run together; investigation does not delay treatment where an emergency is established.

In all: fundoscopy, ECG, urinalysis, renal function and electrolytes, full blood count with film for fragmentation, and troponin where cardiac involvement is suspected.

Directed: CT head for neurological presentation; chest radiograph and CT aortography where dissection is suspected; echocardiography for heart failure; and investigation for a secondary cause once the acute phase is controlled, since these patients have a higher yield.
### Management
The first decision is whether this is an emergency or severe asymptomatic hypertension, because the two are managed entirely differently.

**Severe hypertension without acute organ damage** does not require rapid reduction. Oral therapy is started or adjusted and the pressure is brought down over days with outpatient follow-up. Rapid reduction here causes harm without benefit.

**A hypertensive emergency** requires controlled reduction with intravenous therapy, with monitoring, in an appropriate care setting. The principle is partial, controlled reduction over the first hours rather than normalisation, because of the autoregulatory shift.

**The exceptions matter and are specific.** Aortic dissection requires rapid reduction of both pressure and the rate of rise of pressure. Eclampsia, acute pulmonary oedema and some other presentations have their own targets and preferred agents. In acute ischaemic stroke the thresholds are different again, and inappropriate lowering worsens the injury.

Agent choice is dictated by the affected organ, which is why there is no single correct drug for hypertensive emergency.

Every agent, dose, infusion, target and time course follows the guideline in force where the patient is treated. None is reproduced here, and this is a setting where using a remembered target rather than the current one causes harm.
### Complications and prognosis
Stroke, intracranial haemorrhage, seizure, permanent visual loss, acute kidney injury progressing to chronic kidney disease, myocardial infarction, heart failure, aortic dissection, and death.

Prognosis has improved greatly with treatment but remains serious, and depends on the organ affected and on whether reduction was controlled. Long-term outcome depends on the control achieved afterwards, so the discharge plan matters as much as the acute episode.
### Classification
Emergency against urgency is the classification that determines management. The distinction rests on acute organ damage, not on any threshold reading.
## hold_these
A hypertensive emergency is defined by acute target organ damage, not by the height of the reading.
Autoregulation resets upwards in chronic hypertension, so rapid normalisation causes ischaemia.
Aortic dissection is the exception requiring rapid reduction, and the target agent differs by the organ affected.
## lose_the_mark
Giving rapid intravenous reduction to a patient with a very high reading and no acute organ damage.
Aiming for a normal blood pressure in a hypertensive emergency.
Omitting fundoscopy, which is a direct view of the microvasculature.

## summary
A dilatation that widens itself, silently, until it leaks. The physics is the whole disease: wall stress rises with radius, so the bigger it gets the faster it grows.
## sections
### Definition
An aortic aneurysm is permanent localised dilatation of the aorta to more than one and a half times its expected diameter, involving all three layers of the wall.

A false aneurysm is a contained rupture rather than a true dilatation, and dissection is a different process entirely — a tear splitting the wall rather than a widening of it.
### Epidemiology
Abdominal aneurysms are considerably more common than thoracic and occur predominantly in older men who smoke or have smoked. Rupture carries very high mortality, much of it before the patient reaches hospital.

Screening programmes for abdominal aneurysm in older men exist in several countries and reduce rupture-related mortality. Whether such a programme operates locally determines how most aneurysms are detected.
### Aetiology and risk factors
**Degenerative** disease accounts for the majority: smoking is the strongest modifiable risk factor, with age, male sex, hypertension, family history and atherosclerosis.

**Connective tissue disorders** — Marfan syndrome, Loeys–Dietz syndrome, vascular Ehlers–Danlos — cause aneurysm at younger ages and characteristically involve the aortic root.

**Inflammatory and infective**: large-vessel vasculitis; infective or mycotic aneurysm, including tuberculous and syphilitic aortitis, which remain relevant where those infections are prevalent.

**Bicuspid aortic valve** is associated with dilatation of the ascending aorta independently of the valve lesion.
### Pathophysiology
Degradation of elastin and collagen in the media weakens the wall, and the wall dilates under arterial pressure.

The law of Laplace then governs the rest. Wall tension is proportional to pressure and to radius, so as the aorta dilates, the stress on its wall rises, which promotes further dilatation. The process accelerates, and rupture risk rises steeply rather than linearly with diameter. This is why surveillance intervals shorten as the aneurysm enlarges, and why diameter is the principal criterion for intervention.

Thrombus lines many aneurysms and may embolise distally. Rupture is usually into the retroperitoneum, which may temporarily contain the bleeding and produce a period of relative stability before catastrophic decompensation.
### Clinical picture
Most are asymptomatic and are found incidentally on imaging performed for another reason, or by screening.

Symptomatic but unruptured aneurysms cause abdominal, back or flank pain, and a thoracic aneurysm may cause chest or back pain, hoarseness from recurrent laryngeal nerve stretch, dysphagia or breathlessness.

A tender aneurysm, or new pain in a known aneurysm, suggests impending rupture and is an emergency.

**Rupture** presents with sudden severe abdominal or back pain, hypotension and a pulsatile abdominal mass. The full triad is frequently incomplete, and the diagnosis must be considered in any older patient with abdominal or back pain and hypotension — misdiagnosis as renal colic or musculoskeletal pain is a recognised and lethal error.
### Investigation
**Ultrasound** is the investigation of choice for detection and surveillance of abdominal aneurysm: accurate, quick and without radiation.

**CT angiography** defines anatomy precisely for planning intervention and is the investigation in suspected rupture — in a haemodynamically unstable patient with a known aneurysm and a compatible presentation, however, transfer to theatre must not be delayed for imaging.

**Echocardiography and cross-sectional imaging** assess the thoracic aorta and root, particularly where a connective tissue disorder or bicuspid valve is present.

Screening for aneurysm elsewhere, and assessment of cardiovascular risk, follow, since these patients have generalised vascular disease.
### Management
**Surveillance** for aneurysms below the intervention threshold, with imaging intervals determined by diameter, alongside aggressive cardiovascular risk reduction: smoking cessation above all, blood pressure control, and lipid-lowering and antiplatelet therapy for overall vascular risk.

Smoking cessation is the single intervention that slows expansion and it is worth stating plainly to the patient.

**Elective repair** — open or endovascular — is offered when diameter reaches the threshold in the guideline in force, when growth exceeds a defined rate, or when the aneurysm becomes symptomatic. The threshold differs for thoracic and abdominal aneurysms, and is lower in connective tissue disorders and in women.

**Rupture** is a surgical emergency.

Every diameter threshold, growth rate, surveillance interval and technique choice follows the guideline in force; none is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Rupture, which is the complication that matters; distal embolisation from mural thrombus; and compression of adjacent structures. Aortoenteric and aortocaval fistula are rare.

Prognosis after elective repair is good; mortality after rupture remains high despite emergency surgery, and a substantial proportion never reach hospital. That difference is the entire argument for surveillance and elective repair.
### Classification
Aneurysms are classified by site — thoracic, thoracoabdominal, abdominal — by shape, and by aetiology. Site and diameter together determine the intervention threshold.
## hold_these
Wall stress rises with radius, so expansion accelerates and rupture risk rises steeply with diameter.
Most aneurysms are silent until they rupture, which is why surveillance and screening exist.
The triad of pain, hypotension and pulsatile mass is often incomplete; consider rupture in any older patient with abdominal or back pain and hypotension.
## lose_the_mark
Misdiagnosing rupture as renal colic or musculoskeletal back pain.
Delaying theatre for imaging in an unstable patient with a known aneurysm.
Managing an aneurysm without addressing smoking, which is the strongest modifiable factor for both formation and expansion.

## summary
The great imitator of the chest. It can present as stroke, infarction, limb ischaemia or abdominal pain — and one anatomical question, whether the ascending aorta is involved, decides everything that happens next.
## sections
### Definition
Aortic dissection is separation of the layers of the aortic wall by blood entering the media through a tear in the intima, creating a false lumen alongside the true one.

**Stanford type A** involves the ascending aorta, whatever the site of the tear. **Stanford type B** does not. That single distinction determines the treatment pathway.
### Epidemiology
Dissection is uncommon but carries very high mortality, which rises with every hour untreated in type A disease. It affects men more than women and presents most often in the sixth and seventh decades, and much younger in connective tissue disease and in pregnancy.

It is frequently misdiagnosed at first presentation, because it imitates so many other conditions.
### Aetiology and risk factors
**Hypertension** is present in the great majority and is the dominant risk factor.

**Connective tissue disorders**: Marfan syndrome, Loeys–Dietz syndrome, vascular Ehlers–Danlos — these present decades earlier.

**Congenital**: bicuspid aortic valve, coarctation, Turner syndrome.

**Acquired**: pre-existing aneurysm, aortitis including syphilitic and large-vessel vasculitis, cocaine and amphetamine use, trauma, deceleration injury, and iatrogenic injury from catheterisation or cardiac surgery.

**Pregnancy**, particularly the third trimester and peripartum period, in women with underlying aortopathy.
### Pathophysiology
An intimal tear allows pulsatile blood into the media, which splits along the length of the aorta. The false lumen may propagate antegradely or retrogradely, and everything clinically important follows from where it goes.

**Branch vessel occlusion** produces the protean presentations: coronary ostia — infarction, characteristically inferior; brachiocephalic and carotid — stroke; spinal arteries — paraplegia; renal — acute kidney injury and refractory hypertension; mesenteric — abdominal pain and ischaemia; iliac — limb ischaemia and pulse deficit.

**Retrograde extension** in type A dissection produces the lethal complications: aortic regurgitation from disruption of the valve commissures, rupture into the pericardium causing tamponade, and coronary ostial involvement.

**External rupture** into the pericardium, pleura or mediastinum.

The false lumen may re-enter the true lumen distally, thrombose, or continue to expand.
### Clinical picture
**Pain** is present in almost all: sudden, severe, maximal at onset — a feature that distinguishes it from the crescendo of ischaemic pain — and often described as tearing or ripping. Anterior chest pain suggests ascending involvement; interscapular back pain suggests descending. Pain may migrate as the dissection propagates.

**Signs**: hypertension is common, and hypotension is ominous, suggesting tamponade, rupture or severe aortic regurgitation. A pulse deficit or a blood pressure difference between the arms is characteristic but present in a minority — so its absence does not exclude the diagnosis. A new early diastolic murmur indicates aortic regurgitation. Focal neurological deficit, abdominal tenderness or a cold limb reflect branch involvement.

The combination of chest pain with a neurological deficit, or chest pain with abdominal or limb ischaemia, should raise dissection immediately, because few other conditions produce it.
### Investigation
The diagnosis requires imaging, and the clinical index of suspicion determines whether it is sought.

**CT angiography** of the whole aorta is the usual investigation: rapid, available and definitive. **Transoesophageal echocardiography** is used where the patient cannot be moved and assesses the ascending aorta, the valve and the pericardium. **MRI** is accurate but generally too slow acutely.

**Transthoracic echocardiography** is performed early in a shocked patient to identify tamponade and aortic regurgitation.

ECG may be normal, may show hypertrophy, or may show inferior ST elevation where the right coronary ostium is involved — which is the trap. **Chest radiograph** may show a widened mediastinum, and a normal film does not exclude dissection.

**D-dimer** is typically raised, and a negative result in a low-probability patient argues against dissection, but it must never be used alone to exclude it in a high-suspicion presentation.
### Management
Immediate priorities: analgesia, and reduction of both blood pressure and the rate of rise of pressure, since the shearing force on the intimal flap depends on both. This is one of the specific exceptions to the general rule of gradual pressure reduction.

**Type A dissection is a surgical emergency.** Mortality rises hour by hour without operation, and transfer to a cardiac surgical centre takes priority over completing investigation.

**Type B dissection** is managed medically — pressure and rate control, and monitoring — unless complications develop: malperfusion of a branch territory, rupture, refractory pain or refractory hypertension, in which case endovascular repair is undertaken.

**Thrombolysis and antiplatelet therapy are dangerous** where dissection is causing an apparent acute coronary syndrome or stroke, which is why the diagnosis must be considered before treating either.

Every agent, target, sequence and surgical threshold follows the guideline in force; none is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Cardiac tamponade, acute severe aortic regurgitation, myocardial infarction, stroke, paraplegia, mesenteric and renal ischaemia, limb ischaemia, rupture and death.

Untreated type A dissection has extremely high early mortality. Outcomes after prompt surgery are substantially better, which is why speed of diagnosis is the principal determinant of survival. Type B dissection managed medically has better early survival, with late aneurysmal degeneration requiring long-term surveillance.
### Classification
The Stanford system — type A involving the ascending aorta, type B not — is used because it maps directly onto treatment. The DeBakey system describes the extent in more detail.

A pulse deficit is characteristic but present in a minority, so its absence does not exclude dissection.
Type A dissection is a surgical emergency; type B is managed medically unless complicated.
## lose_the_mark
Giving thrombolysis or antiplatelet therapy for an apparent infarct or stroke that is actually a dissection.
Excluding dissection on a normal chest radiograph or an absent pulse deficit.
Treating chest pain with a neurological deficit as two separate problems.

## summary
Angina of the leg, and a marker that the same disease is in the coronary and cerebral arteries. Most patients with claudication die of a heart attack, not of the leg — which is what makes it a systemic diagnosis.
## sections
### Definition
Peripheral arterial disease is atherosclerotic obstruction of the arteries supplying the limbs, most often the lower limbs.

It ranges from asymptomatic disease, through intermittent claudication, to chronic limb-threatening ischaemia with rest pain, ulceration or gangrene. Acute limb ischaemia is a separate emergency, usually embolic or thrombotic.
### Epidemiology
Peripheral arterial disease is common, increases steeply with age, and is substantially under-diagnosed because many patients are asymptomatic or attribute reduced walking to age.

Its presence indicates generalised atherosclerosis, and cardiovascular mortality in these patients is high — most die of coronary or cerebrovascular disease rather than of limb complications. That fact should shape how the diagnosis is treated.
### Aetiology and risk factors
Atherosclerosis, with the usual risk factors. **Smoking and diabetes are the two strongest** for peripheral disease specifically, more so than for coronary disease.

Also hypertension, dyslipidaemia, chronic kidney disease, hyperhomocysteinaemia, age and male sex.

Non-atherosclerotic causes are uncommon but matter in younger patients: thromboangiitis obliterans, strongly associated with smoking; popliteal entrapment; cystic adventitial disease; vasculitis; and radiation arteritis.
### Pathophysiology
A stenosis limits maximal rather than resting flow — the same mechanism as stable angina. Collateral vessels develop over time and partly compensate, which is why the relationship between the anatomical lesion and the symptom is loose.

Walking raises muscle oxygen demand; supply cannot follow; ischaemic metabolites accumulate and produce cramping pain that resolves within minutes of stopping, because demand falls at once. The distance is reproducible because the stenosis is fixed.

The **site of pain lies one level below the disease**: aortoiliac disease causes buttock and thigh claudication, femoropopliteal disease causes calf claudication, and distal disease causes foot symptoms.

In **chronic limb-threatening ischaemia**, resting flow is inadequate. Pain occurs at rest, characteristically in the forefoot at night when the leg is elevated and cardiac output falls — and is relieved by hanging the leg down, which adds gravitational pressure. Tissue breaks down, producing ulceration and gangrene.

In diabetes the pattern differs: disease is more distal, medial calcification makes vessels incompressible, and coexisting neuropathy removes the protective pain that would otherwise prompt presentation.
### Clinical picture
**Claudication**: cramping pain in a muscle group on walking, reproducible at a given distance, relieved within minutes by standing still.

**Chronic limb-threatening ischaemia**: rest pain in the forefoot, worse at night and on elevation, relieved by dependency; non-healing ulceration, characteristically at pressure points and the toes, with a punched-out appearance; and gangrene.

**Signs**: absent or reduced pulses; a bruit; cool pale skin; hair loss; thickened nails; muscle wasting; delayed capillary refill; pallor on elevation with dependent rubor; and ulcers at the toes and pressure points.

**Acute limb ischaemia** — pain, pallor, pulselessness, perishing cold, paraesthesia and paralysis — is an emergency, and the presence of sensory or motor deficit indicates a threatened limb requiring immediate action.
### Investigation
**Ankle–brachial pressure index** is the first-line test, comparing ankle and brachial systolic pressures.

Its critical limitation: in medial calcification, characteristically in diabetes and chronic kidney disease, the vessels are incompressible and the index is falsely normal or high. In that population a normal index does not exclude disease, and toe pressures, toe–brachial index or waveform analysis are used instead. This is exactly the group in whom the disease is most often severe and most often missed.

**Exercise testing** unmasks disease where the resting index is normal but symptoms are typical.

**Duplex ultrasound**, **CT angiography** and **MR angiography** define the anatomy where revascularisation is being considered.

Assess the whole vascular risk profile: lipids, glucose or HbA1c, renal function, and cardiovascular assessment — because the coronary and cerebral circulations are the ones most likely to kill the patient.
### Management
**Risk factor modification is the priority, and it treats the patient rather than the leg.** Smoking cessation is the single most important intervention. Antiplatelet and lipid-lowering therapy, blood pressure control and glycaemic control reduce cardiovascular events.

**Supervised exercise therapy** improves walking distance substantially in claudication and is first-line, working through collateral development and improved muscle efficiency. It is under-used because it seems counter-intuitive to ask a patient with pain on walking to walk more.

**Revascularisation** — endovascular or surgical — is offered for claudication that limits quality of life despite exercise and medical therapy, and is required in chronic limb-threatening ischaemia, where it is limb-saving rather than symptomatic.

**Foot care and protection** are essential in diabetes, where neuropathy removes the warning that would otherwise prompt presentation.

**Acute limb ischaemia** requires immediate vascular assessment.

### Complications and prognosis
Progression to limb-threatening ischaemia, ulceration, infection, gangrene and amputation.

The dominant prognostic issue is cardiovascular: myocardial infarction and stroke account for most deaths in this population. Prognosis for the limb in claudication is comparatively good with risk factor control; prognosis for the patient depends on how thoroughly the systemic disease is treated.
### Classification
Disease is classified by symptom severity and by anatomical level. Level matters because it determines the site of pain and the feasibility of revascularisation.

The ankle–brachial index is falsely normal or high in calcified incompressible vessels, characteristically in diabetes.
Most patients with peripheral arterial disease die of coronary or cerebrovascular disease, so the diagnosis is systemic.

Excluding peripheral arterial disease on a normal ankle–brachial index in a diabetic patient with symptoms or an ulcer.
Treating the leg and neglecting the cardiovascular risk that will actually kill the patient.

## summary
Three mechanisms generate every risk factor, and one test is only useful when you have already decided how likely the diagnosis is. Getting the order right is most of the skill.
## sections
### Definition
Deep vein thrombosis is thrombus formation within a deep vein, most commonly of the lower limb. With pulmonary embolism it forms the spectrum of venous thromboembolism.

Proximal thrombosis — popliteal vein and above — carries substantially greater embolic risk than isolated distal calf thrombosis, which is why the distinction is made.
### Epidemiology
Venous thromboembolism is common, and a leading cause of preventable death in hospital inpatients. Incidence rises with age.

A substantial proportion of hospital-acquired cases are preventable by risk assessment and appropriate prophylaxis, which makes this one of the clearest examples of a condition where systematic process outperforms individual clinical judgement.
### Aetiology and risk factors
Every risk factor acts through one or more elements of **Virchow triad** — stasis, endothelial injury and hypercoagulability — and learning it that way generates the list rather than requiring it to be memorised.

**Stasis**: immobility, long-haul travel, hospital admission, paralysis, obesity, pregnancy, heart failure, external compression by a mass.

**Endothelial injury**: surgery, particularly orthopaedic; trauma; central venous catheters; previous thrombosis; inflammation.

**Hypercoagulability**: malignancy; inherited thrombophilias including factor V Leiden and antithrombin, protein C and protein S deficiency; antiphospholipid syndrome; oestrogen-containing contraception and hormone replacement; pregnancy and the puerperium; nephrotic syndrome; and inflammatory disease.

An unprovoked thrombosis in an older patient raises the question of occult malignancy.
### Pathophysiology
Thrombus typically begins in the valve pockets of calf veins, where flow is slowest, and may propagate proximally.

Three outcomes follow. It may **resolve** by fibrinolysis and recanalisation. It may **embolise** to the pulmonary circulation. Or it may **organise**, damaging the valves and leaving chronic venous outflow obstruction and reflux — post-thrombotic syndrome.

The thrombus obstructs venous return, raising pressure distally and producing the limb swelling, and the accompanying inflammation produces pain and tenderness.
### Clinical picture
Unilateral limb swelling, pain, tenderness along the deep veins, warmth, erythema and distended superficial veins.

Clinical assessment alone is unreliable: many patients with these findings do not have thrombosis, and thrombosis frequently occurs with minimal signs. This is precisely why formal probability scoring exists.

**Phlegmasia** — massive proximal thrombosis with severe swelling and compromised arterial inflow — is a limb-threatening emergency.

Assess for pulmonary embolism in every patient: breathlessness, pleuritic pain, haemoptysis, tachycardia or hypoxia.

The main differentials are cellulitis, a ruptured Baker cyst, muscle injury, and lymphoedema.
### Investigation
**Assess pre-test probability first, using a validated score.** This is not a formality: it determines what the subsequent tests mean.

**D-dimer** is sensitive and non-specific. In a patient assessed as low probability, a negative result makes thrombosis very unlikely and safely ends the pathway. A positive result is uninformative on its own, since infection, malignancy, pregnancy, trauma and surgery all raise it. In a high-probability patient, imaging is required whatever the D-dimer shows — so sending the test first wastes it.

**Compression ultrasound** is the diagnostic investigation, with repeat scanning where the initial study is negative but suspicion remains high.

Investigate for underlying cause where the thrombosis is unprovoked, and consider thrombophilia testing selectively, since results rarely change immediate management and are affected by acute thrombosis and by anticoagulation.
### Management
**Anticoagulation** is the treatment. Choice of agent, duration and the approach in cancer-associated and pregnancy-associated thrombosis all follow the guideline in force, and none is reproduced here.

**Duration** is determined by whether the event was provoked by a transient risk factor, provoked by a persistent one, or unprovoked — the balance between recurrence risk and bleeding risk.

**Inferior vena cava filters** are reserved for patients in whom anticoagulation is contraindicated.

**Catheter-directed thrombolysis** is considered in extensive proximal thrombosis or phlegmasia.

**Compression stockings** relieve symptoms; their role in preventing post-thrombotic syndrome is less certain than was once believed.

**Prophylaxis** is where the greatest benefit lies: systematic risk assessment of every admitted patient, with mechanical or pharmacological prophylaxis as indicated. Most hospital-acquired venous thromboembolism is preventable.
### Complications and prognosis
Pulmonary embolism, which may be fatal; post-thrombotic syndrome, which causes long-term pain, swelling and ulceration in a significant minority; recurrence; and bleeding from treatment.

Prognosis with prompt anticoagulation is good. Recurrence risk is substantially higher after unprovoked thrombosis, which is what drives the duration decision.
### Prevention
Risk assessment on admission, appropriate prophylaxis, early mobilisation, and attention to hydration. This is the single highest-yield intervention in the whole topic.

Assess pre-test probability before sending a D-dimer; in a high-probability patient the test cannot exclude the diagnosis.
Most hospital-acquired venous thromboembolism is preventable by systematic risk assessment and prophylaxis.
## lose_the_mark

## summary
Valves that no longer point the flow upwards, so walking stops lowering venous pressure. Everything visible on the leg follows from that one sustained pressure — and compression is the treatment, unless the arteries cannot take it.
## sections
### Definition
Chronic venous insufficiency is persistent venous hypertension in the lower limb caused by valvular incompetence, venous outflow obstruction, or failure of the calf muscle pump.

It produces a spectrum from varicose veins and oedema through skin changes to ulceration.
### Epidemiology
Varicose veins and chronic venous disease are very common, increasing with age, and more frequent in women. Venous ulceration is the commonest cause of chronic leg ulceration and carries a substantial burden of morbidity and healthcare cost, with high recurrence rates.
### Aetiology and risk factors
**Primary valvular incompetence**, the commonest, with age, female sex, pregnancy, obesity, prolonged standing and family history.

**Secondary**, following deep vein thrombosis and the resulting valve damage and outflow obstruction — post-thrombotic syndrome.

**Calf muscle pump failure**, from immobility, neuromuscular disease or ankle joint fixity.

**Congenital** venous malformations are uncommon.
### Pathophysiology
Walking normally lowers venous pressure in the leg: the calf muscle pump empties the deep veins, and competent valves ensure flow proceeds upwards and does not return.

When valves fail, blood refluxes during and after each contraction, so pressure does not fall during walking. This sustained **ambulatory venous hypertension** is the single mechanism from which every clinical feature follows.

Raised capillary pressure drives fluid, protein and red cells into the interstitium. Haemosiderin from extravasated red cells produces the brown **pigmentation**. Chronic inflammation and fibrosis of the subcutaneous tissue produce **lipodermatosclerosis**, giving the tapered "inverted champagne bottle" leg. Impaired tissue oxygenation and chronic inflammation produce **ulceration**, characteristically in the gaiter area above the medial malleolus, where hydrostatic pressure is greatest and perforating veins are numerous.

The distribution of the changes is therefore a direct consequence of where venous pressure is highest.
### Clinical picture
Aching, heaviness, itching and swelling, worse at the end of the day and on prolonged standing, relieved by elevation — the opposite pattern to arterial disease, in which elevation worsens symptoms.

Signs progress through visible varicose veins; ankle oedema; skin changes — pigmentation, venous eczema, lipodermatosclerosis and atrophie blanche; and ulceration.

**Venous ulcers** are typically in the gaiter area, shallow, with sloping irregular edges, a granulating base and surrounding skin changes, and are relatively painless — in contrast to arterial ulcers, which occur at pressure points and the toes, are punched-out and deep, and are painful.

Examine the arterial supply in every patient with a leg ulcer, because that assessment determines whether compression is safe.
### Investigation
**Duplex ultrasound** is the principal investigation, demonstrating reflux, its site, and any deep venous obstruction — and distinguishing primary from post-thrombotic disease, which affects management.

**Ankle–brachial pressure index is mandatory before compression**, to exclude significant arterial disease. Applying compression to a limb with arterial insufficiency can cause tissue necrosis, and this is the most consequential error in the topic.

Investigate for other causes of ulceration where the appearance is atypical or healing fails: diabetes, vasculitis, infection and malignancy in a long-standing non-healing ulcer.
### Management
**Compression therapy is the mainstay** — for oedema, for skin changes and for ulcer healing — and it works by opposing the venous hypertension that causes the disease. It is only applied once arterial disease has been excluded.

**Elevation, exercise and weight reduction** support the calf muscle pump and reduce ambulatory venous pressure.

**Wound care** for ulceration, with attention to the surrounding skin.

**Endovenous intervention** — thermal ablation, mechanochemical or adhesive closure — has largely replaced open surgery for superficial reflux, and treating reflux improves ulcer healing and reduces recurrence.

**Prevention of recurrence** with continued compression after healing is essential, since recurrence rates are high without it.

Compression classes, dressing choices and intervention criteria follow the guideline in force; none is reproduced here.
### Complications and prognosis
Ulceration, recurrent cellulitis, bleeding from a varix, venous eczema, and rarely malignant change in a long-standing ulcer.

Ulcers heal with appropriate compression in the majority, though slowly. Recurrence is common without continued compression and correction of reflux, so this is a long-term condition to be managed rather than an episode to be treated.
### Classification
Chronic venous disorders are classified by clinical severity, aetiology, anatomy and pathophysiology. The clinical component, running from telangiectasia to active ulceration, is the part used in everyday practice.
## hold_these

Venous ulcers are in the gaiter area, shallow and relatively painless; arterial ulcers are at pressure points, punched-out and painful.
Assess the arterial supply before applying compression.

Stopping compression once an ulcer has healed, when recurrence is largely preventable by continuing it.
Attributing a leg ulcer to venous disease without considering diabetes, vasculitis or malignancy where healing fails.
