## summary
The mandibular nerve (V3), the third and largest division of the trigeminal nerve, is the sole carrier of motor innervation to the muscles of mastication — a fact frequently tested against the purely sensory V1/V2 divisions and against the facial nerve's separate, unrelated supply to facial expression.

## sections
### Definition
The muscles of mastication — masseter, temporalis, and the medial and lateral pterygoids — receive their motor innervation from the mandibular nerve (V3). V3 is a mixed nerve, unlike the purely sensory ophthalmic (V1) and maxillary (V2) divisions: it carries general sensation from the lower face, mandible and anterior two-thirds of the tongue, and it alone carries the trigeminal nerve's entire motor root.

### Mechanism
The trigeminal motor root travels together with the sensory fibres of V3 through the foramen ovale, then distributes to all four muscles of mastication plus a small accessory group (mylohyoid, anterior belly of digastric, tensor tympani, tensor veli palatini). Centrally, the trigeminal motor nucleus that gives rise to this root is classified as special visceral efferent (branchiomotor), because the muscles of mastication are derivatives of the first pharyngeal arch — the same functional column as the facial, glossopharyngeal, vagal and accessory nuclei, even though those nerves supply entirely different muscle groups peripherally.

### Key determinants
The deciding fact is which single named nerve carries the motor supply: mandibular nerve, not facial, maxillary or glossopharyngeal. The facial nerve is the most common distractor, because both are "lower face" nerves in casual description — but facial supplies the muscles of facial expression, an entirely separate muscle group from the muscles of mastication.

### Clinical significance
Damage isolated to the mandibular nerve's motor root, such as from a lesion at the foramen ovale, produces weakness of chewing (deviation of the jaw toward the weak side on opening, because the unopposed contralateral lateral pterygoid pushes the jaw over) without any facial-expression weakness — a clean way to localise a lesion to V3 rather than to the facial nerve.

## summary
Four discrete identification facts grouped here: a smooth muscle fibre's nucleus is single, central and oval; a macrophage's nucleus sits eccentrically; epithelial tissue is one of the four basic tissues (not a body system); and the walls of blood vessels are composed primarily of smooth muscle tissue.

## sections
### Definition
A smooth muscle fibre (leiomyocyte) has a single, central, oval nucleus, contrasting with skeletal muscle's multiple, peripheral, flattened nuclei. A macrophage, the activated tissue form of the monocyte, characteristically has an eccentrically positioned nucleus, reflecting its abundant peripheral phagocytic cytoplasm. Epithelial tissue is one of the body's four basic tissues (with connective, muscular and nervous tissue) — a fundamental tissue category, not a body system such as respiratory or immune tissue. The walls of blood vessels are composed primarily of smooth muscle tissue, concentrated in the tunica media.

### Mechanism
A cell's nuclear position and shape follow its functional architecture: smooth muscle's single central nucleus reflects its uninucleate, spindle-shaped structure, while a macrophage's eccentric nucleus reflects the volume its cytoplasm devotes to lysosomes and phagocytic vacuoles pushing the nucleus aside. Vascular smooth muscle in the tunica media allows involuntary regulation of vessel diameter (vasoconstriction/vasodilation) without conscious control, unlike the voluntary skeletal muscle of the tongue and limbs or the involuntary-but-structurally-distinct cardiac muscle of the heart.

### Key determinants
For nuclear position/shape questions, the deciding pair is single-central-oval (smooth muscle) versus multiple-peripheral-flattened (skeletal muscle), and central (lymphocyte-like) versus eccentric (macrophage-like). For the basic-tissue question, the deciding move is recognising that "respiratory," "endocrine" and "immune" name body systems built FROM the four basic tissues, not a fifth basic tissue category themselves. For blood vessel walls, the deciding fact is smooth (involuntary, tunica media), not skeletal (tongue, limb muscles) or cardiac (heart only).

### Clinical significance
Recognising smooth muscle's central-oval nucleus and lack of striations on a slide is a routine step in identifying vascular, gastrointestinal and airway wall tissue in histopathology. A macrophage's eccentric, often kidney-shaped nucleus and irregular outline are used alongside its phagocytic vacuoles to identify it in inflamed or infected tissue, distinguishing it from lymphocytes and other mononuclear cells on the same slide.

## summary
Three introductory identification/classification facts: a virus's basic structure is a protein coat around its nucleic acid genome; Gram-positive bacteria are identified by a cell wall with a thick peptidoglycan layer; and Sarcodina is the old taxonomic protozoan group defined by pseudopodial movement.

## sections
### Definition
A virus has no organelles, cytoplasm or cell wall of its own — its basic structure is a nucleic acid genome (DNA or RNA, never both) enclosed in a protein coat (capsid). A Gram-positive bacterium's cell wall, specifically, is the structure that contains a thick layer of peptidoglycan, unlike the nucleoid, capsule or cell membrane. Under the old locomotion-based protozoan classification, Sarcodina is the group defined by pseudopodia (amoeboid movement), alongside Mastigophora (flagella), Ciliophora (cilia) and Sporozoa (no locomotor organelle).

### Mechanism
The protein coat plus nucleic acid pairing is what every virus shares, regardless of whether an outer lipid envelope is also present; the virus depends entirely on a host cell's machinery to replicate, unlike a true cell. The thick peptidoglycan layer of the Gram-positive wall is also what retains the crystal violet-iodine complex during Gram staining, producing the characteristic violet colour, in contrast to the thin peptidoglycan and outer lipid membrane of a Gram-negative wall. Sarcodina's pseudopodia are temporary cytoplasmic extensions used for both locomotion and phagocytic feeding, as in Entamoeba.

### Key determinants
For virus structure, the deciding fact is protein coat plus nucleic acid — not a complete cell with a nucleus, organelles, or a peptidoglycan wall like bacteria have. For Gram-positive cell wall questions, the deciding structure is the cell wall itself, not the nucleoid, capsule or cell membrane. For old protozoan classification, the deciding movement type is pseudopodia for Sarcodina, distinguished from flagella (Mastigophora) and cilia (Ciliophora).

### Clinical significance
A virus's total dependence on host-cell machinery (having no organelles or independent metabolism of its own) is why antiviral drugs must target virus-specific enzymes or the host-virus interaction rather than general cell processes, unlike antibacterial drugs, which can exploit bacteria-specific structures such as the peptidoglycan cell wall. Sarcodina's pseudopodial movement and phagocytic feeding underlie the pathogenic behaviour of Entamoeba histolytica, which uses the same mechanism to invade host tissue.

## summary
Three unrelated but commonly co-tested general-pharmacology facts: aspirin is the common name for acetylsalicylic acid; cross tolerance is tolerance to one drug extending to other drugs in the same class; and morphine is derived primarily from a plant source (the opium poppy).

## sections
### Definition
Aspirin is the common (and originally brand) name for acetylsalicylic acid, an NSAID that acetylates and irreversibly inhibits cyclooxygenase — chemically distinct from ibuprofen, diclofenac sodium and acetaminophen. Cross tolerance is tolerance developed to one drug that also reduces the response to other drugs within the same pharmacological group, even without prior exposure to those other drugs. Morphine is an opioid alkaloid derived primarily from a plant source, the opium poppy (Papaver somniferum), placing it among plant-derived drugs rather than microbial, animal or synthetic ones.

### Mechanism
Aspirin's acetylsalicylic acid structure irreversibly acetylates cyclooxygenase, distinguishing its mechanism and duration of action from reversible NSAIDs such as ibuprofen. Cross tolerance arises because drugs in the same class typically share a receptor or downstream signalling mechanism, so adaptive changes (such as receptor downregulation) produced by chronic exposure to one drug blunt the response to any other drug acting on that same target. Morphine is extracted from the opium poppy's latex, alongside other naturally occurring opium alkaloids, rather than being synthesised from scratch or fermented by a microorganism.

### Key determinants
For aspirin, the deciding fact is its specific chemical identity (acetylsalicylic acid) among four distinct common analgesic compounds. For cross tolerance, the deciding distinction is from tachyphylaxis (a much faster loss of response to repeated doses of the SAME drug) and from idiosyncrasy (an unpredictable reaction unrelated to prior exposure). For morphine, the deciding source category is plant, distinguished from microbial (most antibiotics), animal (historical insulin) and fully synthetic sources.

### Clinical significance
Aspirin's irreversible cyclooxygenase inhibition is the basis of its long-lasting antiplatelet effect (the platelet cannot resynthesise cyclooxygenase, since it has no nucleus), which is why low-dose aspirin is used for cardiovascular prophylaxis despite its short plasma half-life. Cross tolerance is clinically relevant in dosing patients already tolerant to one opioid or one benzodiazepine, who will typically need a higher-than-average dose of a second drug in the same class to achieve the same effect. Morphine's plant origin as an opium alkaloid is the historical basis for the entire opioid drug class, including semi-synthetic (such as oxycodone) and fully synthetic (such as fentanyl) opioids developed later from or alongside it.
