## summary
The four muscles of mastication are learned by pairing each one with a single defining action and, for the elevators, a specific insertion site — not as an undifferentiated list. Lateral pterygoid alone protrudes; temporalis alone (via its posterior fibres) retracts; masseter and medial pterygoid together form an elevator sling on opposite surfaces of the mandibular ramus and angle. A muscle's classification by fibre architecture (as in the sartorius, a strap muscle) is a separate but related idea worth anchoring alongside these action-based facts.

## sections
### Definition
The four muscles of mastication — masseter, temporalis, medial pterygoid and lateral pterygoid — all act on the temporomandibular joint, but each has one defining action worth memorising first. Masseter, running from the zygomatic arch to the lateral surface of the mandibular ramus and angle, is a powerful elevator. Temporalis, converging from the temporal fossa onto the coronoid process, has anterior fibres that elevate and posterior fibres that retract — the only muscle of mastication credited with both actions. Medial pterygoid, running from the pterygoid fossa to the medial surface of the ramus and angle, elevates (and mildly assists protrusion). Lateral pterygoid, arising from the lateral pterygoid plate and inserting on the neck of the mandible, is the sole protrudor — pulling the condyle and articular disc forward onto the articular eminence when the jaw opens.

### Mechanism
Masseter and medial pterygoid physically form a muscular sling around the mandibular angle: masseter wraps the lateral (outer, palpable) surface of the ramus and angle, medial pterygoid wraps the medial (inner) surface of the same region, and together they suspend and elevate the mandible from both sides at once. This sling arrangement is exactly why the two are taught as a pair — an exam question naming one surface (lateral vs medial) of the ramus/angle is testing which of the two muscles occupies it. Lateral pterygoid's mechanism is different in kind: acting bilaterally it protrudes (opens) the jaw by dragging the condyle forward; acting unilaterally on one side only, it instead produces the side-to-side grinding component of chewing.

### Key determinants
The elevator-versus-protrudor split is the first branch point: masseter, temporalis and medial pterygoid elevate; lateral pterygoid alone protrudes. The second branch point, for the two elevators that insert near the ramus and angle, is which surface: masseter is lateral, medial pterygoid is medial. A third, separate idea entirely is muscle shape by fibre architecture, illustrated here by the sartorius: a strap muscle has long, parallel fibres running the muscle's full length (as in sartorius, the body's longest muscle), distinct from a fusiform muscle's tapering spindle shape (biceps brachii), a multipennate muscle's fan of converging heads (deltoid), or a segmented muscle interrupted by tendinous intersections (rectus abdominis).

### Clinical significance
Because masseter and medial pterygoid together sling the mandible, a fracture or nerve injury affecting one side of that sling (for instance, damage to the nerve to medial pterygoid, a branch of the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve) weakens elevation asymmetrically. Because lateral pterygoid is the sole protrudor, its paralysis (as in some trigeminal nerve lesions) causes the mandible to deviate toward the paralysed side on opening, since the unaffected side's lateral pterygoid still protrudes unopposed. Muscle architecture matters clinically too: a strap muscle like sartorius, with its long parallel-fibre course, is a common surgical landmark and graft donor precisely because of its predictable length and superficial course.

## summary
Skull surface landmarks are learned by position along the head's front-to-back and midline axes, not as an unordered list of Latin names. Glabella and nasion sit at the front, on the forehead and nasal root; the external occipital protuberance sits at the back, on the midline of the occipital bone. A separate but neighbouring fact is where structures attach or sit relative to the mandible's mylohyoid line, and frontalis's own action (eyebrow elevation) rounds out the region's muscles of facial expression.

## sections
### Definition
Glabella is the smooth midline elevation of the frontal bone between the two superciliary arches (brow ridges), sitting just above the nasion — the point where the two nasal bones meet the frontal bone at the root of the nose. At the opposite (posterior) end of the skull, the external occipital protuberance (inion) is the midline bony prominence of the occipital bone, positioned midway between lambda (where the parietal bones meet the occipital bone) and the foramen magnum. Frontalis, the frontal belly of occipitofrontalis, is a muscle of facial expression with no bony attachment inferiorly — it inserts into the eyebrow skin and epicranial aponeurosis, and its contraction elevates the eyebrows.

### Mechanism
Glabella and nasion are anterior landmarks, ordered top-to-bottom: glabella (between the brows) sits above nasion (at the nasal root), which itself sits above the tip of the nose. The external occipital protuberance is unrelated to either — it is a posterior landmark, and it marks the point on the skull's exterior that lies directly opposite the internal confluence of the venous sinuses, making it more than a surface curiosity. Separately, the mandible's mylohyoid line divides its medial surface into two territories: above the line is the sublingual fossa (housing the sublingual gland, inside the oral cavity proper), and below the line is the submandibular fossa (housing the submandibular gland's larger, superficial part, in the neck).

### Key determinants
An anterior-versus-posterior sort settles glabella/nasion against the external occipital protuberance immediately — they cannot be confused if the question specifies "between the superciliary arches" (anterior) versus "midway between lambda and the foramen magnum" (posterior). Within the anterior pair, glabella is the elevation itself; nasion is the suture point just below it — a common one-step trap. Frontalis's action (elevating the eyebrows) is easily confused with the unrelated skull-viewing-position term "norma frontalis" (the anterior view of the skull as a whole) — the same word describing two different things, one a muscle action and the other an orientation for describing the skull.

### Clinical significance
The external occipital protuberance is a key surgical landmark precisely because of what sits opposite it internally — the confluence of sinuses — making it relevant to posterior fossa approaches and to avoiding that venous confluence during procedures near the occiput. The mylohyoid line's above/below split matters clinically for submandibular space infections (a dental abscess spreading below the mylohyoid line tracks into the submandibular space of the neck, a different clinical picture from one spreading above the line into the sublingual space) and for identifying the plane surgeons use to access the submandibular gland.

## summary
The anterior scalp and cheek are supplied by named branches of the trigeminal nerve and the ophthalmic artery, and the exam traps here are almost all about matching the right branch to the right territory rather than testing new anatomy. The buccal nerve (V3, sensory) and the buccal branch of the facial nerve (VII, motor) share a name but nothing else; the supraorbital artery is the anterior-scalp exception to the rule that scalp vessels come from the external carotid.

## sections
### Definition
The anterior scalp — forehead back to roughly the vertex — is supplied by sensory branches of the trigeminal nerve's ophthalmic (V1) and maxillary (V2) divisions, chiefly the supraorbital and supratrochlear nerves from V1. The cheek is supplied by the buccal nerve (long buccal nerve), a sensory branch of the mandibular division (V3), which passes forward between the two heads of lateral pterygoid to reach the cheek skin and mucosa and the buccal gingiva of the mandibular molars. The supraorbital artery is a direct branch of the ophthalmic artery (itself from the internal carotid artery), given off inside the orbit and exiting through the supraorbital notch or foramen alongside the supraorbital nerve to supply the anterior scalp.

### Mechanism
The scalp's sensory supply splits front-to-back by nerve origin: the anterior scalp is trigeminal (a cranial nerve), while the posterior scalp is supplied instead by cervical spinal nerves (the greater and lesser occipital nerves) — an embryological and clinical boundary roughly at the vertex. The scalp's arterial supply mostly comes from the external carotid artery (occipital, superficial temporal and posterior auricular arteries), but the anterior scalp is the exception: the supraorbital and supratrochlear arteries reach it via the ophthalmic artery, a branch of the internal carotid — so the anterior scalp is doubly distinct from the posterior scalp, in both its nerve and its artery of origin.

### Key determinants
The buccal nerve (V3, mandibular division, sensory) and the buccal branch of the facial nerve (CN VII, motor, supplying buccinator) are the classic naming trap in this region: identical names, entirely different nerves, entirely different functions — one carries pain, the other moves muscle. For scalp arteries, the determining question is simply "internal or external carotid": occipital, superficial temporal and posterior auricular all trace to the external carotid; supraorbital (and supratrochlear) trace to the internal carotid via the ophthalmic artery.

### Clinical significance
Because the buccal nerve carries the cheek's pain sensation, it — not the identically-named motor branch of the facial nerve — is the target for local anaesthesia before draining a cheek abscess or performing buccal surgery; anaesthetising the wrong "buccal" nerve leaves the patient in pain despite a technically successful facial-nerve block. The anterior scalp's internal-carotid (ophthalmic artery) supply is relevant to surgical scalp flaps and to understanding why anterior scalp lacerations can bleed briskly from vessels with a different pressure source than the posterior scalp's external-carotid branches.
