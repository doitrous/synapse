## summary
Three foundational anatomical-terminology facts grouped here: ventral is the exact positional synonym of anterior; bilateral means relating to both the right and left sides of the body; and a synergist is the muscle that assists the prime mover in producing a specific movement.

## sections
### Definition
Ventral is synonymous with anterior (the front, or belly-side, surface or direction), the opposite of dorsal (posterior), and distinct from the separate superior/inferior (up/down) axis. Bilateral means relating to both the right and left sides of the body together, distinct from unilateral (one side only), ipsilateral (the same side as a reference point) and contralateral (the opposite side from a reference point). A synergist is a muscle that assists the prime mover (agonist) in producing a specific movement, one of four standard muscle-action roles alongside the agonist itself, the antagonist (opposes the movement) and the fixator (stabilises the origin).

### Mechanism
Ventral/dorsal and superior/inferior are two independent axes of anatomical description that combine to fully locate a point on the body — a structure can be, for example, both anterior (ventral) and superior at once. Laterality terms (bilateral/unilateral/ipsilateral/contralateral) all require a stated or implied reference point except bilateral, which by definition spans both sides without needing one. A synergist assists the prime mover either by adding force in the same direction or, in some muscle groups, by counteracting an unwanted secondary action the prime mover would otherwise also produce, letting the prime mover's main action proceed cleanly.

### Key determinants
For ventral, the deciding pair is ventral=anterior versus dorsal=posterior, kept separate from the superior/inferior axis. For bilateral, the deciding contrast is "both sides together" versus the three reference-point-dependent terms (unilateral, ipsilateral, contralateral). For synergist, the deciding role is "assists," distinguishing it from the antagonist (opposes) and the fixator (stabilises the origin), and from the agonist/prime mover itself (the muscle chiefly responsible).

### Clinical significance
Precise anatomical terminology (ventral/dorsal, bilateral/unilateral/ipsilateral/contralateral) is the shared vocabulary used throughout clinical description, imaging reports and surgical notes, so imprecise use of these terms is a common source of miscommunication for a beginning student. Recognising a muscle's synergist role, versus its antagonist or fixator role, underlies understanding of movements that require coordinated multi-muscle action, such as most functional daily movements, and is tested repeatedly across musculoskeletal anatomy and physiology.

## summary
Two foundational neuroanatomy facts: white matter in the nervous system is formed by nerve fibers (mostly myelinated axons), not cell bodies; and the medulla oblongata is a core part of the brainstem, alongside the pons and midbrain.

## sections
### Definition
White matter is formed by nerve fibers (axons), most myelinated — the myelin's lipid content gives white matter its pale colour — distinct from grey matter, formed by nerve cell bodies. The medulla oblongata is a core part of the brainstem, the stalk-like lower part of the brain connecting the spinal cord to the rest of the brain and housing the vital cardiorespiratory centres; the brainstem's other core parts are the pons and midbrain.

### Mechanism
The fibers-versus-cell-bodies distinction that separates white from grey matter holds throughout the CNS, though the two are arranged differently in different structures (for example, cortex has grey matter outermost with white matter deep to it, while the spinal cord has the reverse arrangement). The cerebellum, though attached to the brainstem by the cerebellar peduncles and functionally intertwined with it, is anatomically and developmentally a separate structure — not itself a brainstem component, unlike the medulla, pons and midbrain.

### Key determinants
For white vs grey matter, the deciding structural component is fibers (white) versus cell bodies (grey) — synaptic vesicles and dendritic trees are parts of individual neurons, not the tissue-level components that define the colour distinction. For brainstem composition, the deciding list is medulla oblongata, pons and midbrain — the cerebral cortex, cerebellar hemispheres and corpus callosum are each distinct structures outside that list.

### Clinical significance
The medulla oblongata's core brainstem location, housing the cardiorespiratory centres, is why medullary lesions (such as from a posterior circulation stroke) can be immediately life-threatening in a way that a cortical or cerebellar lesion of similar size typically is not. Recognising a lesion's white-matter (fiber-tract) versus grey-matter (cell-body/nucleus) location is a routine first step in localising neurological disease on imaging.

## summary
Per this FOMSCU source's own teaching timetable, the developing morula remains free (unattached) in the uterine cavity for about three days before it transforms into a blastocyst.

## sections
### Definition
The morula, a solid ball of blastomeres produced by cleavage of the fertilised zygote, has no internal cavity. Per this source's own teaching timetable, it remains free within the uterine cavity for about three days before it transforms into a blastocyst, the stage defined by the appearance of a fluid-filled cavity (the blastocele).

### Mechanism
Cleavage divisions progressively subdivide the zygote's cytoplasm into smaller blastomeres without an overall increase in size, producing the compact morula. Per this source, the free interval in the uterine cavity precedes the blastocyst transformation, after which implantation into the uterine wall can begin.

### Key determinants
The deciding fact is which interval the "three days" describes — the time the already-formed morula spends free in the uterine cavity before becoming a blastocyst, not the time from fertilisation to the morula's first appearance.

### Clinical significance
The timing of the morula-to-blastocyst transition and subsequent implantation underlies the window in which assisted reproduction protocols (such as blastocyst-stage embryo transfer) are planned, and is relevant to understanding early pregnancy dating.
