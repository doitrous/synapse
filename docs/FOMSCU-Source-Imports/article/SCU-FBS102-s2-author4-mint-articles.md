## summary
A smooth muscle fibre is a single spindle-shaped cell with one central nucleus; what that fibre and its nucleus look like on a slide depends on which way the section cuts across the spindle, and exam questions exploit exactly that dependence.

## sections
### Definition
Smooth muscle is the involuntary, non-striated muscle type found in the walls of hollow viscera — the gut, blood vessels, airways, bladder and uterus. Each fibre is a single, spindle-shaped cell (unlike a multi-cell cardiac fibre), thinner than a skeletal or cardiac fibre, with one central, cigar-shaped nucleus and no cross-striations because its thick and thin filaments are irregularly arranged rather than organised into sarcomeres.

### Mechanism
A fibre's and its nucleus's appearance on a slide is a direct consequence of the plane the knife cuts through the spindle shape. A longitudinal section runs along the fibre's long axis and shows the true spindle outline and the nucleus's true elongated, cigar-shaped form. A transverse (cross) section instead cuts straight across the narrow fibre, so both the fibre's outline and its nucleus are caught end-on: the fibre appears round and homogeneously acidophilic, and the nucleus — the same single central nucleus, not a different one — appears rounded rather than elongated.

### Key determinants
The feature that separates smooth muscle from skeletal and cardiac muscle in a comparison table is the same feature tested here in miniature: skeletal fibres are multi-nucleated and peripheral, cardiac fibres are single-nucleated and central but the fibre itself is multi-cellular, and smooth fibres are single-celled with one central nucleus — the plane-of-section question only refines "central" into "central and cigar-shaped, appearing rounded or elongated depending on the cut."

### Clinical significance
Recognising smooth muscle on histology — round, spindle-nucleated fibres with no striation — identifies the muscularis of a viscus on a biopsy or resected specimen and distinguishes it from the striated, multi-nucleated fibres of adjacent skeletal muscle or the branching, intercalated-disc-bearing fibres of cardiac muscle.

## summary
Three unrelated basic-science facts share one common exam shape: naming which member of a larger class is the biologically "special" or "required" one — which fatty acids are essential, which amino-acid stereoisomer the body actually uses, and which vitamin's deficiency causes scurvy.

## sections
### Definition
Essential fatty acids are the fatty acids the human body cannot synthesise and must obtain from the diet — linoleic acid (omega-6) and alpha-linolenic acid (omega-3), both polyunsaturated, so polyunsaturated fatty acids as a class are generally classed as the essential ones (saturated fatty acids are freely made from carbohydrate and are never essential). Amino acids other than glycine have a chiral alpha-carbon and exist as D- and L-stereoisomers; only the L-isomer is incorporated into human protein. Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a water-soluble vitamin whose chronic deficiency classically causes scurvy.

### Mechanism
Arachidonic acid, an omega-6 polyunsaturated fatty acid, is normally synthesised from dietary linoleic acid and becomes essential only when linoleic acid is deficient — a conditional case that does not weaken the general PUFA-essential association tested at the class level. L-selectivity comes from the stereospecificity of the ribosomal protein-synthesis machinery, not from any difference in chemical stability between the D- and L-forms; D-amino acids occur in nature (bacterial cell walls, some peptide antibiotics) but are excluded from human protein synthesis. Vitamin C acts as a required cofactor for lysyl and prolyl hydroxylase, the enzymes that hydroxylate collagen's lysine and proline residues; without hydroxylation, collagen cannot form a stable triple helix and cross-link properly, producing scurvy's connective-tissue signs.

### Key determinants
Glycine is the one amino acid with no chiral centre at all — it is neither D nor L — which is a separate fact from the other nineteen amino acids' L-selectivity and a common distractor pairing. Vitamin C also has a second, narrower biochemical role in dietary (non-haem) iron absorption; a gastrectomy or vitamin C deficiency can each independently impair that absorption, but that fact belongs to iron-deficiency anaemia, not to naming scurvy as vitamin C's classic deficiency disease.

### Clinical significance
Essential fatty acid deficiency, though rare, causes dermatitis, fatty liver and growth retardation, particularly in children fed a very low-fat diet. Scurvy presents with bleeding gums, poor wound healing and fragile blood vessels causing easy bruising and perifollicular haemorrhages, directly traceable to defective collagen cross-linking from unhydroxylated lysine and proline.
