## summary
The action potential's depolarization, repolarization, afterhyperpolarization and refractory period are each governed by a specific, sequential change in voltage-gated sodium and potassium channel state; the same excitable machinery underlies synaptic transmission and the reflex arc that depends on it.

## sections
### Definition
A neuronal action potential is a rapid, transient, all-or-none reversal of membrane potential produced by sequential opening and closing of voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels. Its phases — depolarization, repolarization, and afterhyperpolarization — each correspond to a specific, identifiable channel event, and the transient unavailability of sodium channels during and just after the spike defines the refractory periods that limit how quickly a neuron can fire again.
### Mechanism
Depolarization begins when a stimulus opens enough voltage-gated sodium channels for their inward Na+ current to become self-reinforcing, driving the membrane rapidly toward the sodium equilibrium potential. These sodium channels then inactivate within about a millisecond, ending depolarization, while voltage-gated potassium channels — opened by the same depolarization but with a characteristic delay — now dominate, and their outward K+ efflux repolarizes the membrane. Because these potassium channels close more slowly than they opened, K+ continues to leave briefly after the membrane reaches resting potential, producing the afterhyperpolarization (undershoot) before the resting potential is finally restored. While the majority of sodium channels sit inactivated (spanning depolarization through much of repolarization), no stimulus can trigger a second action potential — the absolute refractory period; only once channels have returned to the closed, resting state does excitability recover, first partially and then fully. At a chemical synapse, each arriving action potential triggers calcium-dependent release of neurotransmitter from a limited, readily releasable vesicle pool; sustained high-frequency firing can deplete this pool faster than it can be replenished, producing synaptic fatigue (exhaustion) — a protective ceiling on how long a synapse can keep transmitting at high rates.
### Key determinants
The specific channel responsible for each phase is what distinguishes these related, frequently confused facts on exam: sodium channel opening for depolarization, potassium channel opening for repolarization, delayed potassium channel closing for afterhyperpolarization, and sodium channel inactivation (not simple closing) for the absolute refractory period. A reflex arc — receptor, afferent pathway, integration center, efferent pathway, effector — depends on this same excitable machinery at every relay: the withdrawal reflex from a hot stove fires action potentials along sensory and motor axons that repolarize, refire and transmit by exactly the mechanisms above, ending at the effector muscle that carries out the visible response.
### Clinical significance
Local anaesthetics block voltage-gated sodium channels, preventing depolarization and thus action potential propagation along the axon — the pharmacological basis of nerve block. Drugs and channelopathies that alter potassium channel kinetics change the duration of repolarization and the refractory period, altering how rapidly a neuron or cardiac cell can be re-excited, which is directly relevant to arrhythmia risk in the cardiovascular system and to the mechanism of some anticonvulsants and local anaesthetics in the nervous system.

## summary
Which molecules cross the plasma membrane unaided, which need the Na+/K+-ATPase's continuous activity, how a large molecule leaves the cell in bulk by exocytosis, and how much of the body is water are among the most heavily recurring foundation-level physiology facts, and each has one specific, testable mechanism behind it.

## sections
### Definition
The plasma membrane's lipid bilayer is freely permeable only to small, nonpolar, lipid-soluble molecules such as O2 and CO2, which cross by simple diffusion with no protein assistance and no energy cost. Everything else — ions, glucose, amino acids, and especially large molecules — needs a specific membrane transport mechanism, and if that transport moves cargo against its concentration gradient, or in bulk rather than molecule by molecule, it needs an energy source too. Total body water and its distribution between compartments is a separate, related foundational topic: how much of body weight is water, and at what solute concentration (osmolarity), depends on body composition and is tightly regulated.
### Mechanism
Simple diffusion of CO2 requires no transporter because the molecule is small, uncharged and lipid soluble enough to dissolve directly in the bilayer's hydrophobic core; polar solutes such as glucose instead require facilitated diffusion through a specific carrier, and ions that must move against their gradient require active transport. The single most important active transporter in essentially every human cell is the Na+/K+-ATPase, which hydrolyses ATP to pump 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in per cycle; because leak channels continuously let some of each ion back across, the pump must run continuously — not intermittently — to hold the resting gradients steady, making it a major baseline consumer of cellular ATP. A molecule too large for any channel or carrier instead leaves the cell packaged inside a membrane-bound vesicle that migrates to the plasma membrane and fuses with it, releasing its contents outside — exocytosis, the export-direction counterpart of endocytosis, and, like active transport, an energy-requiring process. Total body water averages about 60% of body weight in adult males and about 50% in adult females, a difference driven by adipose tissue's low water content and adult females' proportionally higher average body fat; extracellular fluid osmolarity, meanwhile, sits at a tightly regulated ~300 mOsm/L, matched by intracellular fluid because water equilibrates freely across most cell membranes.
### Key determinants
The exam-relevant discriminators here are: which molecules qualify for simple diffusion (small, nonpolar, lipid soluble — not glucose or amino acids); the unequal, continuous stoichiometry of the Na+/K+ pump (3 Na+ out : 2 K+ in, running constantly, not only when needed); exocytosis versus endocytosis as opposite-direction vesicular transport, not variants of diffusion; and the correct units and typical value for osmolarity (milliosmoles, not milligrams or whole osmoles, per liter, at ~300 mOsm/L).
### Clinical significance
Because the Na+/K+-ATPase's continuous activity underlies the resting membrane potential of every excitable cell, drugs that inhibit it (such as cardiac glycosides acting on the closely related pump) have profound effects on cardiac and neuromuscular function. Regulated exocytosis is the release mechanism for insulin, neurotransmitters and many other signalling molecules, making it a direct pharmacological target; disorders of body water and osmolarity regulation — dehydration, overhydration, hyponatremia, hypernatremia — are common clinical problems that build directly on these foundation-level facts about compartment size and normal osmolarity.

## summary
Homeostatic control is built from a receptor, a control center and an effector, operating mostly by negative feedback that corrects a deviation after it occurs — with labor contractions as the rare, deliberately self-amplifying positive-feedback exception, and feedforward as a third, anticipatory pattern.

## sections
### Definition
The body's structural hierarchy — chemicals, cells, tissues, organs, systems, whole body — is the physical substrate on which homeostatic control operates. A homeostatic control loop itself has three functional components: a receptor that senses a variable, a control center that compares the sensed value against a set point and decides what response is needed, and an effector that carries out that response.
### Mechanism
Most physiological control is negative feedback: a deviation from the set point is sensed, the control center commands a corrective response, and the effector's action drives the variable back toward the set point, shrinking the original deviation. Positive feedback instead amplifies the initial change: the classic physiological example is labor, where cervical stretch triggers oxytocin release, oxytocin strengthens uterine contractions, and stronger contractions stretch the cervix further, triggering yet more oxytocin — the Ferguson reflex — driving the process to completion (delivery) rather than back to a stable point. Feedforward control is a third, distinct pattern: it acts in anticipation of an expected disturbance, before the controlled variable has actually changed, based on a predictive cue — such as salivation and early insulin release triggered by the sight or smell of food, ahead of any actual rise in blood glucose.
### Key determinants
Distinguishing the three patterns is the recurring exam task: negative feedback corrects a deviation that has already happened; positive feedback amplifies a change instead of correcting it, and is deliberately rare because it is self-reinforcing rather than self-limiting; feedforward acts before any deviation has occurred at all, based on a predictive signal. Within the loop's own components, the control center is specifically the comparison-and-decision step — distinct from the receptor that senses and the effector that acts.
### Clinical significance
Recognising positive feedback in labor is clinically important because interventions that mimic or block the Ferguson reflex (synthetic oxytocin to induce or augment labor; oxytocin receptor antagonists to arrest preterm labor) work by manipulating this specific feedback loop. Feedforward anticipatory responses are relevant to understanding cephalic-phase digestive and endocrine responses, which prepare the gut and pancreas for a meal before nutrients are actually absorbed.

## summary
Fever is not a breakdown of thermoregulation but a pyrogen-driven upward reset of the hypothalamic set point, and the same set-point logic explains both the shivering/vasoconstriction of fever onset and the sweating/flushing of fever's resolution — alongside the everyday four routes of physical heat loss, of which conduction is the one direct-contact wet clothing exploits.

## sections
### Definition
Core body temperature is normally held close to a hypothalamic set point of about 37 degrees Celsius through a classic negative-feedback loop. The body loses heat to its environment through four physical routes: radiation (infrared emission, no contact needed), convection (heat carried away by a moving current of air or water), evaporation (heat consumed as sweat or insensible water changes phase to vapour), and conduction (direct transfer to a cooler object or medium in physical contact with the skin).
### Mechanism
Fever begins when pyrogens — exogenous microbial products or endogenous cytokines such as IL-1, IL-6 and TNF-alpha released during infection — act on the hypothalamus to reset its set point upward. Because actual core temperature is now below this new, higher target, the hypothalamus drives heat-gain and heat-conservation responses (shivering, cutaneous vasoconstriction) exactly as it would for a normal set point that felt too cold, and temperature climbs until it matches the new set point. When the pyrogenic stimulus subsides, the set point resets back down; core temperature is now briefly above this restored, lower target, so the hypothalamus switches on heat-loss responses — sweating and cutaneous vasodilation, producing the classic flushed, sweating appearance of a fever 'breaking.' Conduction, one of the four everyday heat-loss routes, is markedly accelerated when the contacting medium is water rather than air, which is why wet clothing in cold weather causes rapid heat loss even without any wind (which would instead be convection).
### Key determinants
The recurring discriminator is direction: a rising fever reflects the set point moving up while temperature lags below it (shivering, vasoconstriction); a breaking fever reflects the set point moving back down while temperature lags above it (sweating, vasodilation). Among the four heat-loss routes, conduction specifically requires direct physical contact with a cooler medium, distinguishing it from convection (a moving current) and evaporation (phase change of water).
### Clinical significance
Antipyretics such as paracetamol and NSAIDs work by blocking the prostaglandin-mediated elevation of the hypothalamic set point, allowing it to return toward normal and triggering the same sweating/vasodilation heat-loss response seen when a fever breaks naturally. Recognising conduction as the dominant heat-loss route in wet or immersed patients is directly relevant to preventing hypothermia in cold-water near-drowning and in patients with wet clothing or bedding in a cold environment.

## summary
Strenuous exercise raises systolic blood pressure through increased cardiac output while diastolic pressure holds steady as exercising muscle vasodilates, and the same hydrostatic-versus-oncotic pressure logic that shapes blood pressure also governs how fluid moves in bulk across the capillary wall.

## sections
### Definition
Systolic and diastolic blood pressure reflect different phases of the cardiac cycle and respond differently to exercise, while capillary exchange is governed by the balance of hydrostatic and oncotic (colloid osmotic) pressures across the capillary wall — the Starling forces.
### Mechanism
During strenuous dynamic exercise, sympathetic activation and increased venous return raise cardiac output sharply, driving systolic blood pressure up substantially; diastolic pressure changes little, or falls slightly, because the exercising skeletal muscle vasodilates markedly, lowering total peripheral resistance enough to offset the higher cardiac output at the diastolic trough. At the capillary level, hydrostatic pressure inside the vessel — highest at the arteriolar end — pushes fluid and small dissolved solutes together, in bulk, out through the capillary wall's pores (filtration); this bulk flow is opposed by plasma's oncotic pressure, generated by large plasma proteins that cannot cross the wall, which pulls fluid back in (reabsorption). Because hydrostatic pressure falls progressively along the capillary while oncotic pressure stays roughly constant, filtration dominates at the arteriolar end and reabsorption typically dominates toward the venular end.
### Key determinants
The exam-relevant distinctions are: systolic pressure rises substantially with exercise while diastolic pressure is comparatively protected by muscle vasodilation (not both rising together); and bulk flow (mass movement of fluid through pores, driven by pressure) is mechanistically distinct from diffusion (movement of individual solute molecules down their own concentration gradient, through the lipid membrane or channels).
### Clinical significance
An exaggerated or blunted systolic pressure response to exercise is used clinically as a marker of cardiovascular fitness and risk. Disturbances of the Starling balance — reduced plasma oncotic pressure from hypoalbuminemia, or raised capillary hydrostatic pressure from venous congestion — are the mechanism behind edema in conditions such as nephrotic syndrome and heart failure.

## summary
Aldosterone and atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP) are a matched, opposing pair of hormones for renal sodium handling — aldosterone increases sodium reabsorption (decreasing excretion), coupled with increased potassium excretion, while ANP does the direct opposite, inhibiting sodium reabsorption to promote natriuresis.

## sections
### Definition
Aldosterone is a mineralocorticoid steroid hormone released from the adrenal cortex, principally in response to angiotensin II (via the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system) and to rising plasma potassium. Atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP) is secreted by atrial myocytes in direct response to atrial stretch from a rising central blood volume, and acts as aldosterone's physiological opposite.
### Mechanism
Aldosterone acts on principal cells of the late distal tubule and collecting duct, upregulating apical epithelial sodium channels and the basolateral Na+/K+-ATPase. The net effect is increased reabsorption of filtered sodium from the tubular fluid back into the blood — and because more sodium is reclaimed, less is lost in the urine, so aldosterone decreases sodium excretion. Water follows the reabsorbed sodium osmotically, expanding extracellular fluid volume. The same channels that reabsorb sodium are coupled to potassium secretion into the tubular fluid, so aldosterone's sodium-retaining action comes paired with increased potassium excretion in the opposite direction. ANP runs in the opposite direction on the renal front: it acts on the collecting duct to inhibit sodium reabsorption, increasing sodium (and secondarily water) loss in the urine, while also promoting vasodilation and suppressing renin and aldosterone secretion — a direct counterweight whenever the atria sense they are being overfilled.
### Key determinants
The pairing of aldosterone's own effects — sodium reabsorbed/excretion decreased, potassium secreted/excretion increased — is the detail exams test directly, and it is easy to invert one half of the pair by analogy with the other; they move in opposite net directions at the same set of channels, not the same direction. Between the two hormones, aldosterone increases sodium reabsorption while ANP inhibits it — opposite hormones producing opposite renal sodium effects, triggered by opposite stimuli (low perfusion/high potassium versus atrial overfilling).
### Clinical significance
Primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn's syndrome) produces hypertension from sodium/water retention alongside hypokalemia from excess potassium loss — the clinical fingerprint of aldosterone excess follows directly from this coupled mechanism, and aldosterone antagonists such as spironolactone are used clinically precisely to block this receptor and reverse both effects. ANP and related natriuretic peptides (such as BNP) are used as biomarkers of volume overload in heart failure, reflecting the same atrial-stretch-triggered natriuretic mechanism.

## summary
Mitosis, the division type of ordinary somatic cells, is driven forward through successive checkpoints by cyclin-dependent kinases and surveilled at the metaphase checkpoint for correct spindle attachment — the internal machinery that keeps growth and tissue repair genetically faithful.

## sections
### Definition
Mitosis is nuclear division in somatic cells, producing two daughter cells genetically identical to the parent and to each other, for the purposes of growth and the repair and replacement of damaged or lost tissue. Progression through the cell cycle that precedes and includes mitosis is controlled by cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) and surveilled by a series of checkpoints.
### Mechanism
CDKs are present at a roughly constant level through the cycle but are inactive alone; each requires binding its partner cyclin, whose concentration rises and falls in a stage-specific pattern, to become catalytically active and phosphorylate the targets that drive the cell past the relevant checkpoint. At the metaphase (spindle-assembly) checkpoint, the cell specifically verifies that every chromosome's kinetochore is correctly attached to spindle microtubules from opposite poles and aligned at the metaphase plate; even a single unattached kinetochore generates an inhibitory signal that halts the cell in metaphase, and only once every chromosome is properly bi-oriented is the block released, allowing sister chromatids to separate at anaphase.
### Key determinants
CDKs are the internal regulatory signal driving progression (distinguishing them from external cues such as growth factors or cytokines, and from environmental stresses, which trigger checkpoint arrest rather than drive normal progression); the metaphase checkpoint is specifically about spindle attachment, distinguishing it from the G1 checkpoint (cell readiness to replicate) and G2 checkpoint (DNA replication complete and undamaged).
### Clinical significance
Failure of the metaphase checkpoint allows chromosome missegregation (aneuploidy), a hallmark of many cancers; dysregulated cyclin-CDK activity, from mutated cyclins or CDK inhibitors, is one of the most common molecular lesions found in malignant cells, which is why several cancer therapies target CDK activity directly.

## summary
Meiosis I is the reductional division that pairs and then separates homologous chromosomes, using that pairing (synapsis) to enable genetic recombination; meiosis II then equationally separates sister chromatids, and applied symmetrically across both divisions in spermatogenesis this yields four sperm from every primary spermatocyte.

## sections
### Definition
Meiosis is a specialized two-division cell cycle restricted to gametogenesis, converting one diploid germ cell into four genetically varied haploid gametes. Its first division, meiosis I, is reductional (halves chromosome number); its second, meiosis II, is equational and mechanically resembles ordinary mitosis (separates sister chromatids without changing chromosome number).
### Mechanism
During prophase of meiosis I, each chromosome pairs closely with its homologous partner along its entire length — synapsis — held together by the synaptonemal complex, forming a bivalent. This close pairing is the structural prerequisite for crossing-over: only while homologous, non-sister chromatids are aligned in register can they exchange corresponding DNA segments at chiasmata, producing genetic recombination — new maternal-paternal allele combinations — the principal purpose synapsis serves. At anaphase I, the spindle then pulls the paired homologues apart (not sister chromatids, which remain joined), halving the chromosome number to 23 while each chromosome is still duplicated (23 duplicated chromosomes, 46 chromatids, in a secondary spermatocyte). Only at anaphase II — mechanically identical to mitotic anaphase — do sister chromatids finally separate, producing genuinely haploid, single-chromatid cells. Because spermatogenesis discards no products at either division (unlike oogenesis), one primary spermatocyte yields two secondary spermatocytes at meiosis I and then four spermatids (and ultimately four mature sperm) at meiosis II.
### Key determinants
Anaphase I separates homologues (sister chromatids stay joined); anaphase II separates sister chromatids (mechanically like mitosis) — this distinction, and the resulting chromosome/chromatid bookkeeping at each stage (46 duplicated -> 23 duplicated -> 23 single), is the recurring exam trap. Synapsis is pairing along the chromosome's full length, not merely at the centromere, and it is what makes recombination possible, not an incidental side effect of it.
### Clinical significance
Errors in this process — failure of homologous chromosomes to separate correctly at anaphase I (nondisjunction) — produce aneuploid gametes and are the mechanism behind conditions such as Down syndrome (trisomy 21); recombination frequency between linked genes, made possible by synapsis, is also the basis of genetic linkage mapping used in clinical genetics.

## summary
Because every oocyte begins meiosis with an XX chromosome pair and no Y chromosome to segregate, a mature human ovum can only ever carry an X — making the fertilizing sperm, not the egg, the determinant of a conceptus's chromosomal sex.

## sections
### Definition
Oogenesis, like spermatogenesis, uses the two meiotic divisions to halve the diploid chromosome number of 46 to the haploid number of 23 in the mature gamete. Unlike spermatogenesis, however, the starting germline sex chromosome complement is XX, not XY.
### Mechanism
Because every primary oocyte carries two X chromosomes and no Y chromosome at all, meiosis in the female can only ever segregate a single X chromosome into each product — there is no possible meiotic outcome that places a Y chromosome in an oocyte. A mature human ovum's normal chromosome complement is therefore 22 autosomes plus one X chromosome (22,X). This is the direct converse of spermatogenesis: a primary spermatocyte is XY, so its meiotic products split evenly into X-bearing and Y-bearing sperm, and it is this sperm-side asymmetry — not anything about the egg — that determines whether a conceptus is chromosomally XX or XY.
### Key determinants
The recurring exam trap is applying the sperm-side logic ('either X or Y') to the egg — that describes the XY spermatocyte's products, not the XX oocyte's, which can only ever yield an X-bearing gamete.
### Clinical significance
This asymmetry is the physiological basis for sex-selection technologies that separate X-bearing from Y-bearing sperm, and for understanding why maternal, not paternal, meiotic nondisjunction of the X chromosome (producing an XX or nullisomic oocyte) underlies conditions such as Turner syndrome (45,X) when combined with a normal sperm.

## summary
A nucleotide's 5' phosphate defines a nucleic acid strand's directionality, semiconservative replication of that strand always yields exactly two daughter molecules, telomerase (a self-templating reverse transcriptase) maintains the strand's ends, and mitochondria carry their own compact, roughly 16-kilobase genome distinct from all of this nuclear machinery.

## sections
### Definition
A nucleotide has a nitrogenous base attached to the 1' carbon of a pentose sugar and a phosphate group attached to the sugar's 5' carbon; this 5'-phosphate/3'-hydroxyl linkage chemistry underlies both how a DNA strand is built (replication) and how its ends are protected (telomerase). Mitochondria carry an entirely separate, much smaller genome from this nuclear DNA machinery, with its own replication and expression apparatus.
### Mechanism
Successive nucleotides are joined by a phosphodiester bond between one nucleotide's 5'-phosphate and the next's 3'-hydroxyl, giving every nucleic acid strand an intrinsic 5'-to-3' polarity. DNA replication itself is semiconservative: the parent double helix's two strands separate, and each templates synthesis of one new complementary strand, so a single replication event always produces exactly two daughter molecules, each with one original and one newly made strand. Because a linear DNA molecule cannot fully replicate its very end using this same primed mechanism, the ends (telomeres) shorten with each replication cycle unless restored — the job of telomerase, a reverse transcriptase that, unusually, carries its own built-in RNA template and uses it to add new telomeric repeats onto the chromosome's 3' end, active mainly in germ cells and stem cells. Mitochondria have their own small, circular, ~16-kilobase (16,569 bp) double-stranded DNA genome, maternally inherited and encoding 37 densely packed genes — 13 oxidative-phosphorylation proteins plus the rRNAs and tRNAs needed to translate them — independent of the nuclear genome and evidence of mitochondria's endosymbiotic bacterial origin.
### Key determinants
The recurring specifics tested are: the phosphate sits on the 5' (not 3') carbon; one replication event yields exactly two daughter molecules (not one, three or four); telomerase is classed as a reverse transcriptase (RNA-templated DNA polymerase), not a DNA-dependent RNA polymerase or an endonuclease; and the mitochondrial genome's size (~16 kb) should not be confused with the nuclear genome's (~3.2 billion bp).
### Clinical significance
Telomerase reactivation is a near-universal feature of cancer cells, allowing them to bypass the replicative senescence that limits normal somatic cells; mitochondrial DNA's small size, high mutation rate and strict maternal inheritance together explain the distinctive maternal inheritance pattern of mitochondrial disease.
