## summary
Synaptic transmission's exam facts cluster around two questions: what generates an IPSP, and what happens when a synapse is pushed too hard for too long. Two separate channel routes both hyperpolarise the membrane to inhibit firing, one presynaptic mechanism explains fatigue, and the same K+-efflux logic that ends an action potential also underlies inhibition — all against the backdrop of the peripheral nervous system's own organisation and its beta-2 receptors.

## sections
### Definition
An inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP) hyperpolarises the postsynaptic membrane, moving it further from the firing threshold, and it is generated by either of two distinct channel-opening events: ligand-gated potassium channels opening (letting K+ leave the cell) or chloride channels opening (letting Cl- enter the cell) — both are genuine, separately examinable mechanisms, not competing answers to the same question. Synaptic fatigue is the progressive decline in synaptic response during prolonged, intense stimulation, caused by depletion of the presynaptic neurotransmitter store. The peripheral nervous system (PNS) is the division linking the CNS to the rest of the body, with the autonomic and enteric nervous systems as its own subdivisions; within the autonomic system, beta-2 receptors are the dominant sympathetic receptor in bronchial smooth muscle.

### Mechanism
When voltage-gated K+ channels open — whether during an action potential's repolarisation phase or in the ligand-gated IPSP route — potassium flows out of the cell down its electrochemical gradient, and that outward positive-charge movement is what hyperpolarises the membrane. The chloride route works by the opposite ion but the same net effect: because extracellular Cl- exceeds intracellular Cl-, opening a chloride channel (via GABA-A or glycine receptors) lets negative charge flow in, again hyperpolarising the cell. Synaptic fatigue's mechanism is presynaptic rather than channel-based: the readily releasable vesicle pool empties faster than it refills under sustained high-frequency stimulation, so each successive action potential releases less neurotransmitter.

### Key determinants
The two IPSP mechanisms are told apart by which ion moves and through which channel: K+ leaving via a ligand-gated potassium channel, or Cl- entering via a GABA-A/glycine-linked chloride channel — an exam question naming the specific channel type is testing which route it means, not asking you to pick the "correct" IPSP mechanism as though only one exists. Voltage-gated K+ channel opening in a resting neuron is a separate but related idea: it is the mechanism of repolarisation/after-hyperpolarisation following an action potential, using the same K+-efflux logic as the ligand-gated IPSP route but triggered by voltage, not a neurotransmitter. Beta-2 receptors are determined by tissue and division: sympathetic, bronchial smooth muscle, relaxant — as opposed to beta-1 (cardiac), alpha-1 (vascular, constrictor), or muscarinic (parasympathetic, constrictor) receptors elsewhere in the autonomic system.

### Clinical significance
Because synaptic fatigue is a genuine physiological brake on runaway synaptic activity, it is protective against seizure-like over-excitation — a useful frame for understanding why prolonged, unchecked excitatory drive is dangerous in ways the nervous system has its own defences against. Beta-2 receptor dominance in the lung is directly exploited therapeutically: beta-2 agonists (salbutamol) are first-line bronchodilators in asthma/COPD, and non-selective beta-blockers are used cautiously in these patients because blocking beta-2 removes protective bronchodilator tone and can precipitate bronchospasm. Recognising that IPSPs and after-hyperpolarisation both use K+ efflux clarifies why drugs and channelopathies affecting K+ channels can broadly dampen neural excitability across multiple mechanisms at once.

## summary
General pathology's core vocabulary — etiology, pathogenesis, morphology, prognosis — describes different facets of a disease's story, and mixing them up is the commonest exam trap. Apoptosis and necrosis sit alongside this vocabulary as the two forms cell death can take, distinguished by mechanism (caspases versus none), appearance (shrinkage versus swelling), and consequence (no inflammation versus inflammation).

## sections
### Definition
Pathogenesis is the sequence of events in the response of cells or tissues to an etiologic agent, from the initial stimulus to the disease's ultimate expression — the mechanistic middle of the story. Prognosis is the expected further outcome of a disease, the forecast of what happens after it is established — the story's ending. Necrosis is only ever a pathological process, always the result of an external injurious insult, and it always triggers inflammation when the ruptured plasma membrane spills intracellular contents; apoptosis, by contrast, can be physiological (routine cell turnover) or pathological, is driven by an internal caspase cascade, and provokes no inflammation because the cell is packaged into apoptotic bodies before it is cleared.

### Mechanism
Caspase activation — a cascade of cysteine-aspartic proteases — is the defining biochemical signature that separates apoptosis from necrosis: caspases execute an ordered, energy-dependent dismantling of the cell (DNA fragmentation, chromatin condensation, membrane blebbing) without ever rupturing the plasma membrane. Necrosis has no such regulated programme; it is an unregulated, energy-independent failure that ends in membrane rupture. At the tissue level, this mechanistic difference produces different microscopy: coagulative necrosis (as in a hepatocyte injured by viral hepatitis) shows increased cytoplasmic eosinophilia (denatured, coagulated protein binding more eosin stain) together with nuclear pyknosis, one of necrosis's three characteristic nuclear changes alongside karyorrhexis and karyolysis.

### Key determinants
The four core general-pathology terms are told apart by which point in the disease's timeline they describe: etiology is the cause (start), pathogenesis is the mechanism (middle), morphology is the structural appearance (a snapshot), and prognosis is the outcome (end) — a question naming "the sequence of events ... to the ultimate expression" is asking for pathogenesis specifically, not any of the other three. Apoptosis-versus-necrosis questions are answered by picking the feature unique to one: caspase activation, chromatin fragmentation into sharply demarcated pieces, and no inflammation belong to apoptosis; cell swelling, membrane rupture, and inflammation belong to necrosis.

### Clinical significance
Distinguishing pathogenesis from etiology matters clinically because treatment often targets the pathogenic mechanism rather than the original cause (which may no longer be present or treatable) — understanding the mechanistic sequence is what allows intervention at any step along it. Recognising coagulative necrosis's histological signature (eosinophilia, pyknosis) is directly diagnostic in conditions like viral hepatitis, ischaemic injury, and infarction, where the pattern of cell death on biopsy informs both diagnosis and prognosis. Because apoptosis avoids triggering inflammation while necrosis provokes it, the clinical presentation of tissue affected by each differs markedly — a key reason apoptotic and necrotic cell death are managed and interpreted so differently.

## summary
Parasitology's foundational vocabulary is best anchored with one worked example: Strongyloides stercoralis, whose diagnostic stage (the rhabditiform larva) is different from its infective stage — proof that "diagnostic" and "infective" are not synonyms, and that each parasite needs its own answer.

## sections
### Definition
A parasite is a living organism that depends, temporarily or permanently, on another organism (the host) for its survival — a spectrum running from a mosquito's brief blood meal to a helminth's entire life cycle inside one host. The diagnostic stage is the specific parasite form — egg, larva, cyst, trophozoite, or adult — that a laboratory searches for in a specimen to confirm infection; this differs by species and is not automatically the same as the infective stage. The scolex is the head of a tapeworm, the attachment organ (bearing suckers, and in armed species hooks) that anchors it to the host gut wall.

### Mechanism
Most soil-transmitted helminths are diagnosed by finding their egg in stool, but Strongyloides stercoralis is diagnosed instead by the rhabditiform larva, because that species characteristically releases larvae (hatched internally) rather than eggs into the faeces — the exception that makes clear why "diagnostic stage" needs a species-specific answer, not a reflexive "egg". A tapeworm's whole body (the strobila) is a chain of segments (proglottids) trailing behind the scolex; passage of the scolex itself after treatment, not just of segments, is what confirms the whole worm — including its regenerative head — has been expelled.

### Key determinants
The diagnostic-versus-infective distinction is the single most examinable idea here: for Strongyloides, the rhabditiform larva is diagnostic (found in stool) while the filariform larva is infective (penetrates skin) — two different larval forms doing two different jobs. For tapeworm anatomy, scolex (head), strobila (whole segmented body), proglottid (one segment), and bothrium (a groove structure on some scolices) are four related but distinct terms, each naming a different level of the worm's structure.

### Clinical significance
Confirming successful antiparasitic treatment for a tapeworm infection specifically requires finding the scolex in a post-treatment stool sample — proglottids or eggs alone are not sufficient, because a retained scolex can regenerate the entire strobila and the infection can recur. Recognising that Strongyloides needs larval, not ova, stool microscopy is directly relevant to correctly diagnosing (and not missing) strongyloidiasis, which can become life-threatening hyperinfection in immunocompromised patients if undiagnosed.
