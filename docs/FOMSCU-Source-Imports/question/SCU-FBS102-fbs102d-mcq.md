Ventral as the synonym of anterior

## question
Which anatomical term has the exact same positional meaning as the word anterior?

## subject

## status

## answer_a
Inferior

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Inferior means toward the feet, describing the vertical axis, not the front-back axis that anterior describes.

## answer_b
Ventral

## explanation_b
Correct. Ventral is synonymous with anterior — both describe the front, or belly-side, surface or direction of the body in the standard anatomical position. This is the opposite of dorsal (posterior, the back surface), and distinct from superior/inferior, which describe the vertical axis rather than the front-back axis.

Superior

Incorrect. Superior means toward the head, describing the vertical axis, not the front-back axis that anterior describes.

## answer_d
Dorsal

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Dorsal means posterior (the back surface), the exact opposite of anterior, not its synonym.

## title
Synergist: assisting the prime mover

## question
What is the muscle called that assists the prime mover to produce a specific movement?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Antagonist

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The antagonist opposes the prime mover, producing the opposite movement, rather than assisting it.

## answer_b
Fixator

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The fixator stabilises the muscle's bony origin so the prime mover can act efficiently; it does not itself assist the movement's direction.

## answer_c
Synergist

## explanation_c
Correct. A synergist is a muscle that assists the prime mover (agonist) in producing a specific movement, either by adding force in the same direction or by preventing unwanted secondary movements. This is one of four standard muscle-action roles taught together with the agonist (prime mover), antagonist (opposes) and fixator (stabilises the origin).

## answer_d
Agonist

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The agonist (prime mover) is the muscle chiefly responsible for the movement itself, not the muscle that merely assists it.

## title
White matter is formed by nerve fibers

## question
Which of the following structural components forms the white matter in the nervous system?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Nerve cell bodies

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Nerve cell bodies form grey matter, not white matter.

## answer_b
Fibers

## explanation_b
Correct. White matter in the nervous system is formed by nerve fibers (axons), most of them myelinated — the myelin sheath's lipid content is what gives white matter its pale colour. This is distinct from grey matter, which is formed by nerve cell bodies, and from synaptic vesicles and dendritic trees, neither of which is the structural component that defines white matter as a tissue region.

## answer_c
Synaptic vesicles

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Synaptic vesicles are small membrane-bound sacs of neurotransmitter inside a nerve terminal, not the structural component that defines white matter.

## answer_d
Dendritic trees

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Dendritic trees are the branching receptive processes of a single neuron, found within grey matter regions, not the component that defines white matter.

## title
Medulla oblongata as a core part of the brainstem

## question
Which of the following structures is considered a core part of the brainstem?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Cerebral cortex

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The cerebral cortex is the cerebrum's outer grey-matter layer, a distinct structure from the brainstem.

## answer_b
Medulla oblongata

## explanation_b
Correct. The medulla oblongata is a core part of the brainstem, the stalk-like lower part of the brain that connects the spinal cord to the rest of the brain and houses the vital cardiorespiratory centres. The brainstem's other core parts are the pons and midbrain, distinguishing it from the cerebral cortex, cerebellar hemispheres and corpus callosum.

## answer_c
Cerebellar hemisphere

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The cerebellar hemispheres are paired lobes of the cerebellum, attached to the brainstem by the cerebellar peduncles but anatomically and developmentally distinct from it.

## answer_d
Corpus callosum

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The corpus callosum is the white-matter tract connecting the two cerebral hemispheres, a distinct structure from the brainstem.

The free morula's time in the uterine cavity

## question
The developing morula remains completely free in the uterine cavity for how many days before changing into a blastocyst?

## subject
dev

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Three days

## explanation_a
Correct. Per this FOMSCU source's own teaching timetable, the developing morula remains free (unattached) within the uterine cavity for about three days before it transforms into a blastocyst, ahead of implantation. The morula is a solid ball of blastomeres produced by cleavage of the fertilised zygote, with no internal cavity of its own. The fluid-filled cavity (blastocele) that defines the blastocyst stage only forms after this free period in the uterine cavity, per this source's teaching.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Seven days is too long an interval for the free-morula period in this source's own teaching timetable.

Almost twenty-four days

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Almost twenty-four days is far too long an interval, well past the first week of development during which this transition occurs.

Incorrect. Sixteen continuous days is far too long an interval, well past the first week of development during which this transition occurs.
