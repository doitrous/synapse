## title
Nerve injured causing lateral forearm sensory loss

## question
A 22-year-old male presents to the clinic with a loss of sensation on the lateral aspect of his forearm following a trauma. Which nerve is most likely injured?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Radial nerve

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The radial nerve supplies the posterior forearm and hand skin (via the posterior cutaneous nerve of the forearm and the superficial branch), not the lateral forearm, and its injury more typically produces wrist drop from motor loss to the extensor compartment.

## answer_b
Musculocutaneous nerve

## explanation_b
Correct. The musculocutaneous nerve, after supplying the anterior arm's flexor muscles, becomes superficial just above the elbow and continues as the lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm, supplying sensation to exactly this area. A trauma severing this nerve therefore produces sensory loss specifically over the lateral forearm, distinct from the sensory territories of the other three major arm nerves.

## answer_c
Ulnar nerve

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The ulnar nerve supplies the medial (not lateral) forearm and hand — the little finger and medial half of the ring finger — and does not contribute to lateral forearm sensation.

## answer_d
Median nerve

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The median nerve supplies most of the anterior forearm's motor compartment and the lateral palm/first three-and-a-half digits, but its own cutaneous forearm branch (the palmar cutaneous branch) is a separate, wrist-level territory, not the lateral forearm skin above the elbow.

Origin of the profunda brachii artery

## question
The profunda brachii (deep artery of the arm) typically arises as a major branch from which of the following arteries?

## subject
msk

Brachial artery

Correct. The profunda brachii is the first and largest branch of the brachial artery, arising just below teres major in the upper part of the arm. Ulnar artery

Incorrect. The ulnar artery is the brachial artery's other terminal branch at the elbow, likewise distal to the profunda brachii's own more proximal origin.

Which specialized bone cell is directly responsible for the mineralization and calcification of the bone matrix?

## subject

## explanation_a

Osteoclasts

Osteoblasts

Osteoprogenitor cells

Incorrect. Osteoprogenitor (osteogenic) cells are undifferentiated precursor cells lining the periosteum and endosteum that give rise to osteoblasts — they do not themselves secrete or mineralize matrix.

Contraction type with constant length and rising tension

## question
Which type of muscle contraction is characterized by high or increased tension while the muscle's overall length remains constant?

## subject
msk

## status

## vignette

C

## answer_a
Isotonic contraction

## explanation_a
Incorrect. In isotonic contraction, tension is held fixed once threshold is reached and the muscle instead shortens (or lengthens) — the opposite pairing from a contraction defined by fixed length and rising tension.

## answer_b
Eccentric contraction

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Eccentric contraction is a subtype of isotonic contraction in which the muscle actively lengthens under load (e.g. lowering a weight) — length is changing, not fixed.

Isometric contraction

## explanation_c
Correct. In isometric contraction the muscle's length is held fixed while tension rises. The muscle generates force without shortening. This is what happens when pushing against an immovable object.

## answer_d
Concentric contraction

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Concentric contraction is the other isotonic subtype, in which the muscle shortens while lifting a load — again a length change, not the fixed-length pattern being described.

## title
Sarcomere event during leg flexion (H band)

## question
A boy wants to play a football match. When he flexes his leg, which of the following events occurs in the contracting sarcomeres?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Actin filaments are pulled away from the center

## explanation_a
Incorrect. During contraction, actin (thin) filaments are pulled toward the center of the sarcomere (the M line), not away from it — the sliding-filament mechanism moves actin inward, not outward.

## answer_b
The H band shortens

## explanation_b
Correct. As actin filaments slide over myosin toward the M line during contraction, the H zone narrows and can be abolished at full contraction. The H zone is the region containing only myosin, with no actin overlap. Meanwhile the A band (myosin's own length) stays constant while the sarcomere as a whole shortens.

## answer_c
The A band disappears

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The A band, representing the full length of the myosin (thick) filaments, does not change length or disappear during contraction — only the I band and H zone narrow as filaments slide past each other.

## answer_d
Myosin filaments completely dissolve

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Myosin filaments do not dissolve during contraction; the sliding-filament mechanism involves myosin heads cyclically binding and releasing actin, not any breakdown of the filaments themselves.

## title
Most common organism causing acute hematogenous osteomyelitis

## question
What is the most common causative pyogenic organism responsible for acute hematogenous osteomyelitis in the general population?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Streptococcus pneumoniae

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Streptococcus pneumoniae is a leading cause of pneumonia, meningitis and otitis media, not the general population's leading cause of hematogenous osteomyelitis.

## answer_b
Escherichia coli

## explanation_b
Incorrect. E. coli is more typically associated with osteomyelitis in specific contexts (e.g. neonates, urinary tract-related seeding), not the general leading pathogen.

## answer_c
Staphylococcus aureus

## explanation_c
Correct. Staphylococcus aureus possesses surface adhesins that bind readily to bone matrix components. This makes it by far the most common organism responsible for acute hematogenous osteomyelitis across all age groups. Bloodborne bacteria seed the richly vascular, sluggish-flow metaphysis of a growing long bone, and S. aureus's adhesins give it a particular advantage there.

## answer_d
Pseudomonas aeruginosa

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Pseudomonas aeruginosa is classically associated with osteomyelitis in specific settings such as puncture wounds through footwear or in intravenous drug users, not the general population's leading cause.

## title
Mechanism of Protein A in staphylococcal osteomyelitis

## question
What is the primary mechanism of Protein A as a virulence factor in the pathogenesis of staphylococcal osteomyelitis?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Direct degradation of collagen fibers

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Direct collagen degradation is the mechanism of a separate enzymatic virulence factor (collagenase), not Protein A, which works through immunoglobulin binding rather than tissue-degrading enzymes.

## answer_b
Binding to the Fc portion of IgG antibodies

## explanation_b
Correct. Protein A binds the Fc (constant, non-antigen-binding) portion of host IgG antibodies backwards, leaving the antigen-binding Fab regions oriented outward and unable to engage Fc receptors on phagocytes. By preventing this normal opsonin-receptor interaction, Protein A blunts opsonization and protects Staphylococcus aureus from phagocytic clearance.

## answer_c
Cleavage of secretory IgA

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Cleavage of secretory IgA is the mechanism of IgA protease, a distinct virulence factor used by other organisms (e.g. Neisseria, Haemophilus), not Protein A's own Fc-binding mechanism.

## answer_d
Formation of large membrane pores in osteoblasts

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Pore formation in host cell membranes describes pore-forming toxins (e.g. alpha-hemolysin), a mechanistically different class of virulence factor from Protein A's antibody-misorientation strategy.

## title
Chemotherapy as an osteoporosis risk factor

## question
A 24-year-old obese woman (BMI 40) diagnosed with breast cancer is undergoing an active course of chemotherapy. Her physician informs her that she is at a significantly higher risk of developing osteoporosis. Which specific factor is most directly responsible for this heightened risk?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Age

## explanation_a
Incorrect. At 24 years old, age is not a significant background risk factor for osteoporosis on its own — the vignette's specific, treatment-attributable driver is a different, actively acting factor.

## answer_b
Female sex

## explanation_b
Incorrect. While female sex is a general background risk factor for osteoporosis, it does not explain why this particular patient's risk is described as significantly heightened at this age — an actively acting factor is doing that work.

## answer_c
Chemotherapy

## explanation_c
Correct. Certain chemotherapy regimens directly disrupt bone remodeling and, in patients of reproductive age, induce premature ovarian failure and hypogonadism; the resulting drop in gonadal hormones removes a key restraint on osteoclast activity. Together these effects sharply accelerate bone density loss, making the active chemotherapy course the specific, directly attributable driver of this patient's heightened risk.

## answer_d
Obesity

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Obesity is generally associated with modestly higher, not lower, bone density due to increased mechanical loading — it is not the factor explaining an elevated osteoporosis risk in this vignette.
