## title
Origin of the nerve supplying deltoid

## question
From where does the nerve supply of the deltoid muscle arise as a branch?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Roots of brachial plexus

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Deltoid's nerve, the axillary nerve, is a terminal branch of a cord, not a direct branch of the plexus's roots.

## answer_b
Trunks of brachial plexus

## explanation_b
Incorrect. No named branch to deltoid arises directly from a trunk; the axillary nerve is a cord-level branch, later in the plexus's formation.

## answer_c
Medial cord of brachial plexus

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The medial cord's branches supply flexor-side structures, the ulnar nerve, the medial root of the median nerve and the medial cutaneous nerves, and take no part in supplying deltoid.

## answer_d
Posterior cord of brachial plexus

## explanation_d
Correct. Deltoid is supplied by the axillary nerve, a terminal branch of the posterior cord of the brachial plexus, alongside the posterior cord's other terminal branch, the radial nerve. Both nerves supply the extensor/lateral side of the limb, consistent with the posterior cord's general role.

Origin of the nerve causing scapula winging after mastectomy

## question
What is the origin of the nerve whose injury is responsible for the scapula winging after mastectomy?

## subject
msk

Roots of brachial plexus

## answer_d
Lateral cord of brachial plexus

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The lateral cord's branches, the musculocutaneous nerve and the lateral root of the median nerve, supply the arm's anterior flexors, not serratus anterior.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The 'front' of the forearm is not the discrete sensory territory named for this injury pattern; the deficit is described by side, lateral, not surface.

Incorrect. Posterior forearm sensation is radial nerve territory, from the posterior cord, not the specific upper-trunk pattern being tested here.

Correct. The upper trunk carries C5 and C6 fibres, which reach the forearm's skin via the lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm, the musculocutaneous nerve's continuation. Upper trunk injury, Erb's palsy, therefore causes sensory loss along the lateral arm and forearm.

## subject

It crosses from medial to lateral at the level of the lower third of the arm

Correct — this is the false statement. The median nerve actually crosses the brachial artery from lateral to medial at around the middle to lower third of the arm, not from medial to lateral. Incorrect as the false statement. The median nerve does enter the forearm by passing between the two heads of pronator teres, so this statement is true.

It arises from C. 5,6,7,8, T. 1

## explanation_d
Incorrect as the false statement. The median nerve does carry root value C5 to T1, via its lateral root (C5-C7) and medial root (C8-T1), so this statement is true.

Structure commonly affected in supracondylar fracture of the humerus

## question
What structure is commonly affected in the supracondylar fracture of the humerus?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## explanation_a

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The musculocutaneous nerve lies well proximal and lateral by the time it becomes cutaneous, and is not the structure classically at risk here.

Correct. In a supracondylar fracture, the distal fragment typically displaces posteriorly, endangering the structures that cross the front of the elbow. Incorrect. It is the brachial artery, not the more distal radial artery, that is at risk in a supracondylar fracture, given its position directly anterior to the joint.

## answer_e
Medial cutaneous nerve of the arm

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The medial cutaneous nerve of the arm is a proximal, purely cutaneous branch of the medial cord that has already given off its territory well above the elbow, and is not the structure classically named at risk here.

## title
Vein found in the deltopectoral groove

## question
Which of the following veins is found within the deltopectoral groove?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Basilic vein

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The basilic vein ascends the medial side of the arm and pierces the deep fascia around mid-arm, running deep from there; it does not use the deltopectoral groove.

## answer_b
Median cubital vein

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The median cubital vein is a connecting vessel at the elbow, in the cubital fossa, well below the deltopectoral groove, which lies at the shoulder.

## answer_c
Cephalic vein

## explanation_c
Correct. The cephalic vein ascends in the deltopectoral groove, the natural furrow between deltoid and pectoralis major, before piercing the clavipectoral fascia to drain into the axillary vein. This superficial groove is exactly what makes the cephalic vein a useful surgical landmark for that region.

## answer_d
Axillary vein

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The axillary vein lies deep within the axilla itself, not in the superficial deltopectoral groove.

## title
Artery deep to the median cubital vein

## question
A medical student accidentally punctures an artery while attempting venipuncture in the cubital fossa. Which artery lies deep to the median cubital vein?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Radial artery

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The radial artery is a terminal branch of the brachial artery that begins more distally, at the level of the elbow, rather than being the vessel that lies directly deep to the median cubital vein.

## answer_b
Axillary artery

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The axillary artery lies proximal to the arm, in the axilla, well away from the cubital fossa.

## answer_c
Ulnar artery

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The ulnar artery, like the radial, is a terminal branch of the brachial artery arising at the elbow, not the vessel positioned directly beneath the median cubital vein.

## answer_d
Brachial artery

## explanation_d
Correct. In the cubital fossa, the median cubital vein lies superficially, separated from the brachial artery beneath it by the bicipital aponeurosis. This fascial layer is exactly what protects the brachial artery during routine venipuncture, so a needle placed too deep risks it directly.
