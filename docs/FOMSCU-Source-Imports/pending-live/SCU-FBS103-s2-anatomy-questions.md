QST-SCUFBS103-S2-ANATOMY-LATERALPTERYGOID

## title
Muscle of mastication responsible for mandibular protrusion

## question
Which of the following muscles of mastication is primarily responsible for the protrusion of the mandible?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Masseter

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The masseter elevates the mandible (closes the jaw) via its attachment from the zygomatic arch to the ramus and angle — it is a powerful elevator, not a protrusor.

## answer_b
Lateral pterygoid

## explanation_b
Correct. The lateral pterygoid is the principal protrusor of the mandible: acting bilaterally, its inferior head (arising from the lateral pterygoid plate) pulls the mandibular condyle and articular disc forward, out of the mandibular fossa and onto the articular eminence — the movement that opens the jaw and juts the chin forward. Acting alone, one side's lateral pterygoid instead produces the side-to-side grinding motion of chewing, making it the only muscle of mastication that pulls the mandible forward rather than closing it.

## answer_c
Temporalis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Temporalis elevates and retracts the mandible — its posterior, near-horizontal fibres pull the jaw backward, the opposite action to protrusion.

## answer_d
Buccinator

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Buccinator is a muscle of facial expression, not mastication — it compresses the cheek against the teeth (as in whistling or blowing) and plays no role in moving the mandible at the TMJ.

## title
Muscle of mastication responsible for mandibular retraction

## question
Which of the following muscles of mastication is responsible for the retraction of the mandible?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Medial pterygoid muscle

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Medial pterygoid works with masseter as a sling around the angle of the mandible to elevate the jaw, and it also assists mild protrusion — it does not pull the mandible backward.

## answer_b
Lateral pterygoid muscle

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Lateral pterygoid is the protrudor of the mandible (pulling the condyle forward), the opposite direction to retraction.

## answer_c
Masseter muscle

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Masseter's fibres run from the zygomatic arch down to the ramus and angle, so its main action is elevation (jaw closing), with only a minor retraction component compared to temporalis.

## answer_d
Temporalis muscle

## explanation_d
Correct. Temporalis is a broad fan-shaped muscle whose fibres converge from the temporal fossa onto the coronoid process; its anterior, near-vertical fibres are the primary elevators of the mandible, while its posterior, more horizontally oriented fibres are what retract the mandible — pulling the protruded jaw back into its resting position. This dual-direction fibre arrangement is exactly why temporalis, uniquely among the muscles of mastication, is credited with both elevation and retraction.

QST-SCUFBS103-S2-ANATOMY-MASSETERINSERTION

## title
Insertion site of the masseter muscle

## question
The masseter muscle primarily inserts into which part of the mandible?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Coronoid process

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The coronoid process is temporalis's insertion site, not masseter's — the two elevator muscles insert on opposite processes/surfaces of the mandible.

## answer_b
Medial surface of the ramus

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The medial surface of the ramus (and angle) is where the medial pterygoid inserts — masseter and medial pterygoid form a muscular "sling" around the angle, one on the lateral surface and one on the medial, not both on the same side.

## answer_c
Lateral surface of the ramus and angle

## explanation_c
Correct. The masseter arises from the zygomatic arch and passes down to insert onto the lateral surface of the ramus and the angle of the mandible. This lateral, external position — easily palpable when clenching the jaw — is what lets masseter act as a powerful elevator, and it is the mirror image of medial pterygoid's insertion on the ramus and angle's medial (internal) surface, the two together forming the sling that suspends and elevates the mandible.

## answer_d
Neck of the mandible

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The neck of the mandible is where the lateral pterygoid inserts (onto the pterygoid fovea just below the condyle) — a different site again from masseter's ramus/angle insertion.

## title
Muscle responsible for elevating the eyebrows

## question
Which of the following muscles is responsible for elevating the eyebrows?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer

## answer_a
Orbicularis oculi

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Orbicularis oculi is the sphincter muscle that closes the eyelids (as in blinking or squeezing the eyes shut) — its action lowers, not raises, the eyebrows and eyelids.

## answer_b
Frontalis

## explanation_b
Correct. Frontalis is the frontal belly of the occipitofrontalis muscle, a muscle of facial expression that runs vertically in the forehead with no bony attachment inferiorly — instead, it inserts into the skin of the eyebrows and the epicranial aponeurosis superiorly. When it contracts, it pulls the scalp forward and elevates the eyebrows, producing horizontal forehead wrinkles (the look of surprise); this is precisely the muscle Botox commonly targets to smooth those forehead lines.

## answer_c
Orbicularis oris

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Orbicularis oris is the sphincter muscle that closes and purses the lips — it has nothing to do with eyebrow position.

## answer_d
Temporalis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Temporalis is a muscle of mastication (it elevates and retracts the mandible), not a muscle of facial expression, and plays no role in eyebrow movement — a homonym-style trap based on both muscles sitting near the temple region.

## title
Location of the submandibular fossa

## question
The submandibular gland is located in the submandibular fossa, which is found on the:

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Lateral surface of the mandible

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The lateral surface of the mandibular body is a smooth external surface facing the cheek — it carries the mental foramen but not the submandibular fossa.

## answer_b
Inferior surface of the zygomatic bone

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The zygomatic bone forms the cheek prominence and part of the orbital rim; it plays no part in housing the submandibular gland, which sits at the mandible, well below the zygomatic region.

## answer_c
Medial surface of the mandible

## explanation_c
Correct. The submandibular fossa is a shallow depression on the medial (inner) surface of the body of the mandible, below the mylohyoid line, and it is exactly where the submandibular gland's larger, superficial part is lodged — the gland wraps around the posterior free edge of mylohyoid, with its smaller deep part continuing above the muscle. The mylohyoid line itself is the key landmark: structures above it (sublingual fossa/gland) sit in the floor of the mouth proper, while structures below it (submandibular fossa/gland) sit in the neck, outside the oral cavity.

## answer_d
Posterior surface of the maxilla

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The posterior surface of the maxilla (the infratemporal surface) faces the pterygopalatine and infratemporal fossae in the upper jaw — an entirely different bone and region from the mandible's submandibular fossa.

## title
Bony landmark midway between lambda and foramen magnum

## question
Which of the following bony landmarks lies midway between the lambda and the foramen magnum?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
External occipital protuberance

## explanation_a
Correct. The external occipital protuberance (inion) is a palpable bony prominence on the midline of the occipital bone's external surface, positioned midway between the lambda (the suture point where the two parietal bones meet the occipital bone above) and the foramen magnum below. It is a key surface landmark for locating the confluence of the venous sinuses on the internal surface directly opposite it, and it also gives attachment to the ligamentum nuchae and trapezius muscle.

## answer_b
External occipital crest and nuchal lines

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The external occipital crest and the superior/inferior nuchal lines radiate out from the external occipital protuberance itself — they are landmarks that extend from this point, not the midpoint landmark being described.

## answer_c
Glabella prominence of the frontal bone

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The glabella is a smooth elevation on the frontal bone between the eyebrows, at the front of the skull — nowhere near the lambda-to-foramen-magnum axis at the back of the skull.

## answer_d
Nasion point of the nasal bones

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The nasion is the midline point where the two nasal bones meet the frontal bone, again at the front of the skull, unrelated to the posterior lambda-foramen magnum landmark.

## title
Ophthalmic artery branch supplying the anterior scalp

## question
Which of the following arteries is a direct branch of the ophthalmic artery and supplies the anterior part of the scalp?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Occipital artery

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The occipital artery is a branch of the external carotid artery that supplies the back (occipital region) of the scalp — the opposite end of the head from the anterior scalp, and not an ophthalmic-artery branch.

## answer_b
Superficial temporal artery

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The superficial temporal artery, the terminal branch of the external carotid artery, supplies the lateral scalp above the ear — again arising from the external, not internal, carotid system (the ophthalmic artery is a branch of the internal carotid).

## answer_c
Supraorbital artery

## explanation_c
Correct. The supraorbital artery is a direct branch of the ophthalmic artery — itself a branch of the internal carotid artery — which it gives off inside the orbit before the ophthalmic artery continues on to supply the eyeball and surrounding structures. The supraorbital artery exits the orbit through the supraorbital notch or foramen, alongside the supraorbital nerve, and ascends onto the forehead to supply the anterior scalp, making it one of the few scalp vessels that ultimately traces back to the internal, not external, carotid circulation.

## answer_d
Posterior auricular artery

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The posterior auricular artery, a small branch of the external carotid artery, supplies the scalp behind and above the ear — a posterior-lateral territory, not the ophthalmic artery's anterior one.

Which of the following nerves provides sensory innervation primarily to the anterior part of the scalp?

## answer_a
Vagus nerve

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The vagus nerve (CN X) supplies motor and parasympathetic fibres to the thorax and abdomen and a small sensory patch around the external ear — it carries no sensory supply to the scalp.

## answer_b
Facial nerve

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The facial nerve (CN VII) is predominantly motor, supplying the muscles of facial expression (including frontalis); its sensory component is limited to taste on the anterior two-thirds of the tongue, not general sensation of the scalp skin.

## answer_c
Trigeminal nerve

## explanation_c
Correct. The anterior scalp — from the forehead back to roughly the vertex — is supplied by sensory branches of the ophthalmic (V1) and maxillary (V2) divisions of the trigeminal nerve (CN V): specifically the supraorbital and supratrochlear nerves (from V1) anteriorly. This is distinct from the posterior scalp, which is instead supplied by cervical spinal nerves (the greater and lesser occipital nerves) — a common exam split between anterior scalp (trigeminal, cranial) and posterior scalp (cervical) sensory territory.

## answer_d
Glossopharyngeal nerve

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX) supplies sensation to the posterior third of the tongue, the pharynx and the middle ear — no scalp territory at all.

## answer_a
Buccal branch of the facial nerve

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The buccal branch of the facial nerve (CN VII) is a motor branch that supplies the buccinator and other muscles of facial expression around the cheek and mouth — it carries no sensory (pain) fibres at all, a classic confusion the near-identical name is designed to test.

## answer_b
Buccal branch of the mandibular nerve

## explanation_b
Correct. The buccal nerve (long buccal nerve) is a sensory branch of the mandibular division (V3) of the trigeminal nerve, arising from the anterior trunk of V3. ## answer_c
Inferior alveolar nerve

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The inferior alveolar nerve, also a V3 branch, supplies sensation to the mandibular teeth and the skin of the chin and lower lip via the mental nerve — a different sensory territory from the cheek itself.

## answer_d
Lingual nerve

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The lingual nerve, another V3 branch, supplies general sensation to the anterior two-thirds of the tongue and the floor of the mouth — again a different territory from the cheek.

## title
Muscle classified as strap-like

## question
Which of the following muscles is anatomically classified as a strap-like muscle?

## subject
msk

## status

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Deltoid muscle

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The deltoid is a multipennate muscle, with fibres converging from three distinct heads (anterior, middle, posterior) toward a single tendon — a fan-like arrangement, not a long parallel strap.

## answer_b
Rectus abdominis muscle

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Rectus abdominis is a flat muscle interrupted by tendinous intersections, giving it a segmented rather than a single unbroken strap shape.

## answer_c
Sartorius muscle

## explanation_c
Correct. The sartorius is the longest muscle in the body and is architecturally classified as a strap muscle: a long, narrow, ribbon-like muscle whose fibres run largely parallel to each other along its whole length, from its origin at the anterior superior iliac spine to its insertion at the pes anserinus on the medial tibia. This parallel-fibre, uniform strap shape (as opposed to a fusiform, pennate or fan-shaped architecture) is what earns sartorius the classification, and it also explains its distinctive spiral course across the front and medial side of the thigh.

## answer_d
Biceps brachii muscle

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Biceps brachii is a fusiform (spindle-shaped) muscle — broad in its belly and tapering at both ends into tendons — a different architecture from the uniform-width strap muscle.

## title
Joint type at the epiphyseal growth plate

## question
What kind of joint is typically seen in the epiphyseal growth plate of a long bone?

## subject
msk

## status

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Fibrous suture joint

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Fibrous suture joints are the immobile joints of the skull vault, joined by fibrous tissue with no cartilage involved — an entirely different joint class from the growth plate.

## answer_b
Primary cartilaginous joint

## explanation_b
Correct. Primary cartilaginous joints are hyaline and temporary — bones united by hyaline cartilage, and the epiphyseal (growth) plate is the textbook example: it is the hyaline cartilage plate between the epiphysis and diaphysis of a growing long bone, permitting longitudinal bone growth, and it is temporary because it eventually ossifies and fuses once growth is complete (the epiphyseal line). Secondary cartilaginous joints, by contrast, are fibrocartilaginous and midline (like the pubic symphysis or intervertebral discs), and are permanent structures rather than temporary growth sites.

## answer_c
Secondary cartilaginous joint

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Secondary cartilaginous joints are fibrocartilaginous and midline, permanent structures (e.g. pubic symphysis, intervertebral discs) — not the hyaline, temporary growth-plate joint being described.

## answer_d
Synovial plane joint

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Synovial plane joints are freely mobile joints with a joint cavity and synovial fluid (e.g. the acromioclavicular joint) — the growth plate has no joint cavity and is not freely mobile at all.
