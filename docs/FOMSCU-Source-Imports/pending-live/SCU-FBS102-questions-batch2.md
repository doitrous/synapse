QST-SCUFBS102-PENDING-LIVE-REUSE-BATCH2-ANATOMY-BIPENNATE

## title
Fascicular arrangement of rectus femoris

## question
How is the rectus femoris muscle architecturally classified based on its fascicular arrangement?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The highly organized unipennate structure

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A unipennate muscle, such as flexor pollicis longus, has its fascicles running obliquely into only one side of a central tendon. Rectus femoris has fascicles converging onto its tendon from both sides, which is the bipennate pattern, not unipennate.

## answer_b
Bipennate

## explanation_b
Correct. Rectus femoris is bipennate: its fibres run obliquely to a central tendon that lies in the middle of the muscle, with fascicles approaching that tendon from both sides like the barbs of a feather. This arrangement packs a large number of short fibres into the muscle's cross-section, which trades range of movement for the greater force needed at the hip and knee that rectus femoris crosses. The classic textbook contrast is with sartorius, a long strap muscle built for range rather than force.

## answer_c
The extremely complex circumpennate form

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Circumpennate (or multipennate with fibres converging from all sides onto a central tendon, as in deltoid) is not how rectus femoris is described; the muscle has one central tendon with fascicles from two sides, which is the simpler bipennate pattern.

## answer_d
The massive highly branched multipennate design

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A multipennate muscle, like deltoid, has several tendinous septa with fascicles converging on each from multiple directions. Rectus femoris has a single central tendon, so it is described as bipennate rather than multipennate.

## title
Plane dividing the body into anterior and posterior parts

## question
Which precise anatomical plane is mathematically used to divide the body into front anterior and back posterior portions?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
The exactly completely centered median separating sagittal vertical plane

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The median (sagittal) plane is also vertical, but it runs in the body's midline and divides it into equal right and left halves, not into front and back. Confusing the median plane with the coronal plane is a common trap because both are vertical planes.

## answer_b
The completely horizontal dividing flat transverse level horizontal plane

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The horizontal (transverse) plane runs at right angles to the long axis of the body, dividing it into a superior (upper) and an inferior (lower) part rather than an anterior and a posterior part.

## answer_c
Coronal plane

## explanation_c
Correct. The coronal (frontal) plane is a vertical plane that runs at right angles to the median plane, cutting the body into an anterior part towards the front and a posterior part towards the back. It is one of the three standard anatomical planes, alongside the median/sagittal plane and the horizontal/transverse plane, each defined by which pair of parts it leaves behind.

## answer_d
The highly specialized precise completely targeted surgical cross sectional plane

## explanation_d
Incorrect. There is no separate 'surgical cross-sectional plane' among the standard anatomical planes; imaging cross-sections are usually taken along the transverse plane, and this option does not name any of the three recognised anatomical planes.

Short bone of the foot shaped like a cube

## question
Which of the following bones is anatomically classified as a short bone due to its cubical shape?

## subject
msk

## status

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
The flat protective posterior occipital cranial bone

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The occipital bone is one of the flat bones of the skull, built as two thin plates of compact bone around a spongy diploe, and it protects the posterior cranial fossa rather than fitting the short-bone class.

## answer_b
The highly complex irregular temporal skull bone

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The temporal bone is classed as an irregular bone because of its complex shape, not as a short bone; irregular bones share the internal spongy structure of short bones but do not have their compact, roughly cube-like external shape.

Cuboid bone

Correct. ## answer_d
The extremely long and slender fibula bone

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The fibula is a long bone: it has an elongated shaft with two expanded ends, the opposite shape from the compact, cube-like proportions that define a short bone.

## title
Classic example of a hinge joint

## question
Which of the following articulations is a typical example of a hinge joint?

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Elbow joint

## explanation_a
Correct. The elbow is a synovial hinge joint between the humerus, ulna and radius, held by collateral ligaments that restrict it to a single axis of movement. Like other hinge joints, it is uniaxial and permits flexion and extension only, never abduction, adduction or rotation, which is exactly the property that makes it the textbook example of the class.

## answer_b
The shoulder joint

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The shoulder is a ball-and-socket (spheroidal) synovial joint, which is multiaxial and allows flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, rotation and circumduction — the opposite of a hinge joint's single-axis restriction.

## answer_c
The complex hip joint

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The hip is also a ball-and-socket joint, deeper and more stable than the shoulder because of the acetabulum, but multiaxial in the same way, so it does not fit the hinge-joint class either.

## answer_d
The cranial sutures

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Cranial sutures are fibrous joints (synarthroses) with essentially no movement in the adult, joined directly by fibrous tissue rather than by a synovial cavity, so they belong to a different structural class altogether from the synovial hinge joint.

## title
Nerve cell body shape in a dorsal root ganglion

## question
What specific type of nerve cell body is characteristically found within a spinal dorsal root ganglion?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
The typical bipolar sensory neurons

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A true bipolar neuron has two separate processes leaving opposite poles of the cell body, as seen in the retina and the vestibulocochlear ganglia — a different, rarer pattern from the single stalk of a dorsal root ganglion cell.

## answer_b
The very large multipolar motor cells

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Multipolar neurons, with many dendrites and a single axon, are the shape of motor neurons in the anterior horn of the spinal cord, not of the sensory cell bodies packed inside a dorsal root ganglion.

## answer_c
Pseudounipolar

## explanation_c
Correct. Nerve cells are classed by how many processes leave the cell body. A dorsal root ganglion cell starts embryologically as bipolar, but its two processes fuse close to the cell body into a single stalk that then splits in a T shape into a peripheral and a central branch — so only one process appears to leave the soma, which is why the cell is called pseudounipolar rather than truly unipolar.

## answer_d
The extremely small simple unipolar cells

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A genuinely unipolar cell, with one process arising directly from the cell body and no fusion of two earlier processes, is essentially absent from the human nervous system; the dorsal root ganglion cell only resembles one at low magnification, which is precisely the distinction the term 'pseudounipolar' preserves.

Muscle type associated with the viscera

Which of the following types of muscle tissue is associated with the term viscera?

## subject
msk

## status

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Smooth muscles

## explanation_a
Correct. Smooth muscle is the involuntary muscle type that lines the walls of the hollow viscera — the gut tube, the airways, blood vessels, the bladder and the uterus — where it produces the slow, sustained or rhythmic contractions needed for functions such as peristalsis and vascular tone. It lacks the visible cross-striations of skeletal and cardiac muscle, is controlled by the autonomic nervous system rather than by voluntary command, and its spindle-shaped, single-nucleated fibres are the histological hallmark that sets it apart from the other two muscle types.

Cardiac muscles

## explanation_b

## answer_c
Skeletal muscles

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Skeletal muscle is voluntary and striated, attached to bone to move the joints, and it is not the muscle type found in the walls of the viscera.

## answer_d
Striated muscles

## explanation_d
Incorrect. 'Striated muscle' is a structural category covering both skeletal and cardiac muscle, which show visible cross-striations under the microscope; the viscera are lined by smooth muscle, which is specifically non-striated.

## title
Space between a presynaptic and postsynaptic neuron

## question
What is the anatomical term for the microscopic space located exactly between a presynaptic and a postsynaptic neuron?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Synaptic cleft

## explanation_a
Correct. Cells signal each other at a distance through neural communication, in which a neuron releases a neurotransmitter that must cross a narrow extracellular gap to reach a contiguous cell — that gap is the synaptic cleft. It is this space that the released neurotransmitter diffuses across before binding receptors on the postsynaptic membrane, which is what converts an electrical signal in the presynaptic neuron into a chemical, and then again electrical, signal in the postsynaptic one.

## answer_b
The completely enclosed synaptic vesicle area

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Synaptic vesicles are membrane-bound sacs inside the presynaptic terminal that store neurotransmitter before release; they are a structure within the presynaptic cell, not the extracellular space between the two neurons.

## answer_c
The very narrow myelin sheath gap

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The gaps in a myelin sheath are the nodes of Ranvier, which allow saltatory conduction along a single axon — an unrelated structure to the space between two separate neurons at a synapse.

## answer_d
The highly specialized postsynaptic density

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The postsynaptic density is a protein-dense specialisation on the inner face of the postsynaptic membrane where receptors cluster; it is part of the postsynaptic cell's membrane, not the gap between the two cells.

The low energy guanosine diphosphate

Incorrect. Guanosine diphosphate (GDP) is the inactive, GTP-bound Gα subunit's resting state before hormone binding triggers GDP-for-GTP exchange; it plays no part in the actual synthesis of cyclic AMP by adenylate cyclase.

Hydrogen bonds

The highly charged tightly bound completely electrostatic ionic bonds

The highly basic entirely totally strict simple linear peptide bonds

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Peptide bonds are the covalent bonds that link amino acids into the polypeptide chain in the first place, defining primary structure; they do not by themselves fold the chain into a helix or sheet, which is what secondary structure describes.

## explanation_a

## explanation_b

## question
Which cellular organelle is primarily responsible for the packaging of protein?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Mitochondria

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Mitochondria generate ATP through oxidative phosphorylation and have no role in modifying or packaging secretory protein.

## answer_b
Smooth endoplasmic reticulum

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Smooth endoplasmic reticulum lacks ribosomes and specialises in lipid and steroid synthesis and detoxification reactions, not in handling protein produced elsewhere in the cell.

## answer_c
Golgi complex

## explanation_c
Correct. On electron microscopy the Golgi complex is a stack of parallel flat saccules with a cis (entry) face receiving transfer vesicles from the rough endoplasmic reticulum and a trans (exit) face budding off finished products. The Golgi packs, concentrates and chemically modifies incoming protein — adding carbohydrate to build glycoproteins — before forming the secretory vesicles, primary lysosomes and coated vesicles that carry the finished, packaged product on to its destination.

## answer_d
Lysosomes

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Lysosomes are themselves one of the packaged products the Golgi forms and buds off from its trans face; they digest material with their hydrolytic enzymes rather than doing the packaging.

What is structurally considered the most common morphological shape of a typical motor neuron in the human body?

## subject
fnd

C

## explanation_a
Incorrect. True bipolar neurons, with two separate processes at opposite poles of the cell body, are a rare, specialised sensory shape found in the retina and vestibulocochlear ganglia, not the shape of a typical motor neuron.

## answer_b
The specialized pseudounipolar sensory shape

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Pseudounipolar neurons are the sensory cells of the dorsal root and cranial sensory ganglia, whose two embryonic processes fuse into a single stalk near the soma; this is a sensory pattern, not a motor one.

Multipolar

The very rare unipolar round shape

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A truly unipolar cell, with a single process arising directly from the soma and no history of fusion from two earlier processes, is essentially absent in humans; it is not a shape found among ordinary motor neurons.

## question
In a normal peripheral nerve trunk what is the specific connective tissue layer that closely surrounds a single nerve bundle?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The extremely delicate inner endoneurium layer

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The endoneurium is the finest of the three coverings, a delicate layer of connective tissue that surrounds each individual nerve fibre within a fascicle, one level deeper than the covering around the whole fascicle that this question asks about.

## answer_b
Perineurium

## explanation_b
Correct. A peripheral nerve trunk is wrapped, from outside in, by three layers: the epineurium around the whole trunk, the perineurium around each individual fascicle (bundle of nerve fibres), and the endoneurium around each single nerve fibre within a fascicle. The perineurium is built of layered flattened cells joined by tight junctions, which makes it the layer that forms the blood-nerve barrier around each fascicle — exactly the 'single nerve bundle' covering the question describes.

## answer_c
The massively thick outer epineurium layer

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The epineurium is the outermost, densest connective-tissue sheath, wrapping the whole nerve trunk (all of its fascicles together), not a single fascicle on its own.

## answer_d
The entirely distinct muscular outer epimysium

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Epimysium is the connective-tissue covering of a whole skeletal muscle, an entirely different tissue system from the coverings of a peripheral nerve, and it plays no part in wrapping nerve fascicles.

## title
Epithelium of the epidermis

## question
Which specific type of epithelial tissue exists in the epidermis of the skin?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Simple squamous epithelium

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Simple squamous epithelium is a single layer of flat cells, suited to sites needing rapid diffusion such as capillary walls and alveoli; it has none of the many stacked, protective layers the epidermis needs against abrasion and water loss.

## answer_b
Stratified squamous keratinized

## explanation_b
Correct. Both keratinized and non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium are many layers of polyhedral cells with rounded central nuclei, becoming progressively flatter towards the surface, resting on a basement membrane over connective tissue. In the keratinized form the surface cells are replaced by an acidophilic, anuclear horny layer of condensed keratin, and this is the type that covers the epidermis of the skin, protecting it against abrasion, friction and water loss in a way a non-keratinized wet lining, such as the oral mucosa, does not.

## answer_c
Stratified cuboidal tissue

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Stratified cuboidal epithelium, with two or more layers of roughly cube-shaped cells, lines structures such as sweat gland ducts, not the multilayered, flattening, keratin-forming epithelium of the skin.

## answer_d
Pseudostratified columnar

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Pseudostratified columnar epithelium looks layered because nuclei sit at different heights but every cell touches the basement membrane; it lines much of the respiratory tract, an entirely different structure and function from the true multilayered, keratinizing epidermis.

Elastic connective tissue of large arteries

## question
Which type of connective tissue has elasticity and exists in large arteries like the aorta?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Reticular connective tissue

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Reticular connective tissue is a fine meshwork of type III collagen fibres that forms the supporting stroma of organs such as the liver, spleen and lymph nodes; it provides a structural scaffold rather than the elastic recoil needed by a large artery wall.

## answer_b
White fibrous connective tissue

## explanation_b
Incorrect. White fibrous connective tissue is dense, mainly type I collagen, built for tensile strength in structures such as tendons and ligaments — strong but not markedly elastic, the opposite property from what a large artery's wall needs.

Yellow elastic CT

## explanation_c
Correct. Yellow elastic connective tissue is a dense field of thin, single, zigzagging elastic fibres, yellow in the fresh state because of their elastin content. Its defining property is elastic recoil, which is exactly why it is found in the walls of large (elastic) arteries such as the aorta, letting the vessel stretch during systole and spring back during diastole to keep blood flowing onward.

## answer_d
Mucoid connective tissue

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Mucoid connective tissue is the soft, gelatinous tissue of the umbilical cord (Wharton's jelly), built for cushioning rather than elastic recoil, and it is not the tissue found in the wall of a large artery.

## title
Transport mechanism for lipoprotein uptake

## question
Lipoproteins typically enter the target cells through which of the following cellular transport mechanisms?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Passive diffusion

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Passive (simple) diffusion moves small, lipid-soluble molecules directly across the membrane down their concentration gradient; a large lipoprotein particle is far too big to cross the membrane this way.

## answer_b
Facilitated diffusion

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Facilitated diffusion carries specific small solutes such as glucose through a dedicated carrier protein, still moving down a concentration gradient — a mechanism built for small solutes, not for taking in a large particle like a lipoprotein.

## answer_c
Endocytosis

## explanation_c
Correct. Endocytosis is the bulk movement of material into the cell by forming vesicles from the plasma membrane, and one of its three forms — receptor-mediated endocytosis — begins when a ligand binds its specific receptors on the cell surface; the receptors then cluster in a clathrin-coated patch of membrane that pinches off as a coated vesicle carrying the ligand inside. This is exactly the route lipoproteins such as LDL take, binding their receptor before being internalised whole, rather than crossing the membrane molecule by molecule.

## answer_d
Active diffusion

## explanation_d
Incorrect. 'Active diffusion' does not name a real transport mechanism; primary active transport moves solutes against their gradient using ATP directly, which is a different process from the vesicle-forming uptake that brings a whole lipoprotein particle into the cell.
