## title
Local response, EXCEPT: it does not propagate long distances

## question
Regarding the local response in a nerve fibre, the following is true, EXCEPT:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
it can result from application of subthreshold stimulus

## explanation_a
Incorrect as the exception -- this statement is true. A subthreshold stimulus is exactly what produces a local response: it opens some sodium activation gates, partially depolarising the membrane without reaching the firing level.

## answer_b
it is proportional to the strength of the stimulus

## explanation_b
Incorrect as the exception -- this statement is true. The local response is graded, so its size varies directly with the strength of the subthreshold stimulus that produces it, unlike the fixed, all-or-none action potential.

## answer_c
it can propagate along the nerve fiber for long distance

## explanation_c
Correct -- this is the exception (the false statement). The local response is non-propagated and fades within one to two millimetres. It does not propagate along the nerve fibre for any real distance. A true action potential, by contrast, regenerates itself node-to-node or point-to-point over the fibre's whole length, which is exactly the propagating behaviour the local response lacks.

## answer_d
it is accompanied by increased excitability

## explanation_d
Incorrect as the exception -- this statement is true. Excitability is increased during a local response, because the membrane has already moved partway toward the firing level, making a further stimulus more likely to trigger a full action potential.

## title
Local response is accompanied by increased excitability

## question
Local response :

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
is a state of passive depolarization at the anode

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Passive depolarization at the anode describes anodal break excitation, a separate phenomenon from the general local response; the local response itself is an active, graded event driven by sodium activation-gate opening at any stimulated point, not specifically anodal.

## answer_b
it is accompanied by increased excitability

## explanation_b
Correct. The local response is accompanied by increased excitability. The partial depolarization it produces moves the membrane potential closer to the firing level. A further stimulus is then more likely to reach threshold and trigger a full action potential, which is also why rapid subthreshold stimuli can summate to threshold.

## answer_c
it obeys all or non law

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The local response specifically does NOT obey the all-or-none law -- it is graded, varying in size and duration with the stimulus strength, which is the opposite of the fixed, all-or-none action potential.

## answer_d
it can propagate and generate another local response within a distance of 3-4 cm

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The local response does not propagate at all; it fades within one to two millimetres, nowhere near the 3-4 cm distance this option describes, and it does not generate a fresh local response further along the fibre.

## title
Excitability decreased by a fall in extracellular potassium

## question
Excitability of the nerve fiber is decreased in the following condition :

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
a decrease in ECF Na+ concentration

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A decrease in extracellular Na+ reduces the driving force for Na+ influx during depolarization (reducing action-potential amplitude), but this is not the factor named here as decreasing baseline excitability the way potassium changes do.

## answer_b
a decrease in ECF K+ concentration

## explanation_b
Correct. Extracellular potassium concentration sets the resting membrane potential. A decrease in ECF K+ hyperpolarizes the membrane, moving the resting potential further away from threshold, which decreases nerve fibre excitability.

## answer_c
an increase in ECF K+ concentration

## explanation_c
Incorrect. An increase in ECF K+ concentration instead depolarizes the resting membrane, moving it closer to threshold, which initially increases (not decreases) excitability -- the opposite direction from what this question asks about; sustained severe hyperkalaemia can later paralyse the nerve by inactivating Na+ channels, a different, later effect.

## answer_d
low Ca++ concentration in ECF

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Low extracellular calcium removes some of calcium's membrane-stabilising effect on Na+ channels, lowering the threshold and increasing, not decreasing, excitability (hypocalcaemic tetany).

## title
Myelinated fibres conduct faster than unmyelinated fibres

## question
Regarding conduction of action potential along a nerve fiber:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
propagation of action potential requires ATP

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Propagation of a single action potential itself is driven by passive ionic current flow down the axon, not a direct ATP-consuming step; ATP is instead needed afterward, to run the Na+-K+ pump that restores the ionic gradients the many action potentials have run down.

## answer_b
large myelinated nerve fibers conduct faster than unmyelinated nerve fibers

## explanation_b
Correct. Large myelinated nerve fibres conduct faster than unmyelinated fibres. In a myelinated axon the internodal membrane is insulated and only the nodes of Ranvier are exposed with voltage-gated Na+ channels, so the impulse jumps from node to node (saltatory conduction) instead of regenerating continuously along the whole membrane -- this increases conduction velocity up to fifty-fold over an unmyelinated fibre of similar diameter.

## answer_c
saltatory conduction is associated with slowing of action potential propagation

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Saltatory conduction is associated with speeding up, not slowing, action potential propagation -- jumping between nodes is exactly what makes myelinated conduction faster than the continuous, point-to-point conduction of an unmyelinated fibre.

## answer_d
the action potential magnitude declines as it propagates along the nerve fiber

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The action potential does not decline in magnitude as it propagates; it is regenerated at full size at each point (or each node, in myelinated fibres) along the way, which is why it can travel the full length of a long axon without decrement.
