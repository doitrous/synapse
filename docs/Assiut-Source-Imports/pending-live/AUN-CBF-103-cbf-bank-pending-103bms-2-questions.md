## title
Hypokalemia hyperpolarizes the cell and raises the threshold

## question
Hypokalemia reduces excitability of the nerve and muscle cells because decreased extracellular K+:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
increases activity of Na+-K+ ATPase which hyperpolarizes the cell.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. It is the fall in extracellular K+ itself, acting directly on the K+ diffusion potential across the resting membrane, that hyperpolarizes the cell -- not a separate, indirect rise in Na+-K+ ATPase activity.

## answer_b
hyperpolarizes the cell, which increases the action potential threshold.

## explanation_b
Correct. Extracellular potassium concentration is a major determinant of the resting membrane potential. A decrease in ECF K+ hyperpolarizes the membrane, moving the resting potential further from threshold, so a stronger depolarizing stimulus is now needed to reach threshold -- excitability falls.

## answer_c
decreases the number of Na+ channels.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Hypokalemia does not reduce the number of Na+ channels present in the membrane; it changes the resting potential's relationship to threshold, not the channel population itself.

## answer_d
depolarizes the cell, thus inactivating voltage-gated Na+ channels.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This describes a depolarizing block, the opposite of hypokalemia's usual, milder effect -- hyperpolarization, not depolarization, is the standard mechanism by which a fall in ECF K+ decreases excitability.

## title
Action potential smaller if extracellular Na+ is reduced

## question
The action potential:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
can be increased in size if the stimulus strength is increased.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The action potential obeys the all-or-none law: once threshold is reached, its size does not increase with a stronger stimulus -- a larger stimulus only makes the fibre fire sooner or more frequently, not with a bigger spike.

## answer_b
is smaller in size if the extracellular Na+ concentration is reduced.

## explanation_b
Correct. A fall in extracellular Na+ concentration reduces the electrochemical driving force for Na+ influx during depolarization, with little effect on the resting potential itself. Because the size of the depolarization tracks the size of the Na+ influx it produces, a lower extracellular Na+ concentration shrinks the resulting action potential.

## answer_c
has a depolarization phase which corresponds to increased K+ influx.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The depolarization phase corresponds to increased Na+ influx, not K+ influx; K+ moves outward (efflux), not inward, and this efflux drives repolarization rather than depolarization.

## answer_d
is of shorter duration in skeletal muscle than in nerve fiber.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Skeletal muscle's own action potential more closely resembles the nerve's in overall shape than this option implies, and duration comparisons of this kind are not the standard teaching point tested by the extracellular-sodium relationship this question is built around.

## title
Descending limb of action potential due to increased K+ efflux

## question
Concerning the nerve fibre:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
at rest, the inside of the nerve is positive to the outside.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. At rest, the inside of the nerve is negative relative to the outside, not positive -- that is the defining feature of the polarised resting state.

## answer_b
the resting potential becomes more negative if the Na+ permeability of the membrane rises.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A rise in Na+ permeability depolarizes the membrane, moving the potential toward a less negative (and eventually positive) value, not a more negative one.

## answer_c
during the rising phase of the action potential, there is an increase in efflux of K+ ions.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The rising phase (depolarization) is driven by increased influx of Na+, not efflux of K+; K+ efflux is the mechanism of the phase that follows, not the rising phase itself.

## answer_d
the descending limb of the action potential is largely due to increased K+ efflux.

## explanation_d
Correct. Two events together end the spike: inactivation of the voltage-gated Na+ channels stops the depolarizing Na+ influx, while the more slowly opening voltage-gated K+ channels carry K+ out of the cell and complete the return toward the resting level. This K+ efflux is what largely produces the descending limb of the action potential.

## title
Action potential is terminated by efflux of K+

## question
The action potential of a neuron:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
is initiated by efflux of Na+.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The action potential is initiated by influx, not efflux, of Na+ -- sodium moves into the cell down its electrochemical gradient once voltage-gated channels open.

## answer_b
is terminated by efflux of K+.

## explanation_b
Correct. Inactivation of the voltage-gated Na+ channels stops the depolarizing Na+ influx, and the more slowly opening voltage-gated K+ channels then carry K+ out of the cell, completing the return to the resting level. This K+ efflux is what terminates the spike.

## answer_c
decreases in amplitude as it moves along the axon.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The action potential obeys the all-or-none law and is regenerated at full size at each point (or node) along the axon; it does not decrease in amplitude as it propagates.

## answer_d
results in a transient reversal of concentration gradient of Na+ across the cell membrane

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The action potential reverses the membrane potential (voltage), not the underlying Na+ concentration gradient itself -- the concentration gradient for Na+ (higher outside than inside) is barely dented by a single action potential and is restored afterward by the Na+-K+ pump, not reversed by the spike.

## title
Na+ is actively transported out of the neuron

## question
Which of the following ions is actively transported out of the neuron?

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Na+.

## explanation_a
Correct. The Na+-K+ pump is the standard example of primary active transport in the neuron: it moves three sodium ions out of the cell for every two potassium ions it moves in, both against their electrochemical gradients, using energy from ATP hydrolysis. Because it moves more positive charge out than in, the pump is itself electrogenic, contributing directly to the resting membrane potential.

## answer_b
K+.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Potassium is actively transported into, not out of, the neuron by the same Na+-K+ pump -- the pump's two directions are opposite for the two ions.

## answer_c
Cl-.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Chloride is not the ion this pump moves; chloride distribution across the neuronal membrane is generally governed by passive forces (its own electrochemical gradient) rather than by primary active transport out of the cell.

## answer_d
HCO3.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Bicarbonate is not actively extruded from the neuron by this or any comparably prominent neuronal pump; it is not the ion this classic active-transport fact concerns.

## title
Na+-K+ pump requires energy from ATP

## question
Na+-K+ pump of neuron:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
it is the main cause of the resting membrane potential.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Selective ion permeability (the resting membrane's much greater permeability to K+ than Na+) accounts for the great majority of the resting potential; the pump's own direct, electrogenic contribution is a smaller additional part, not the main cause.

## answer_b
it couples extrusion of Na+ out of the cell in exchange with K+ into the cell at a ratio of 2:1.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The pump's ratio is reversed here: it moves three sodium ions out for every two potassium ions in (3:2), not two sodium ions out for every one potassium ion in (2:1).

## answer_c
it requires energy derived from ATP.

## explanation_c
Correct. The Na+-K+ pump is the classic example of primary active transport: its ATPase subunit splits ATP into ADP and phosphate, and the energy released drives sodium out of and potassium into the cell against their electrochemical gradients. This continuous, ATP-fuelled cycling is what restores the Na+ and K+ gradients that repeated action potentials run down.

## answer_d
activity is increased during descending limb of the spike potential.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The pump's activity is not specifically driven up during the descending limb; rather, it runs continuously to restore the Na+ and K+ gradients run down by repeated action potentials, a separate, ongoing job from the moment-to-moment phases of any single spike.

## title
Myelin sheath, EXCEPT: it does not itself transmit nerve impulses

## question
All of the following are true for myelin sheath EXCEPT:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
it provides insulation.

## explanation_a
Incorrect as the exception -- this statement is true. Myelin, secreted by Schwann cells, is an excellent insulator that decreases ion flow across the membrane wherever it wraps the axon.

## answer_b
it gives protection.

## answer_c
it is a lipoprotein material.
