## title
Amanita mushroom toxin and RNA polymerase II

## question
A child returns from a mushroom hunt in Northern Michigan, begins vomiting, and by the next morning is hospitalized with jaundice, hyperammonemia, and delirium. A toxicologist is called, who suggests poisoning from Amanita phylloides, a mushroom containing the toxin alpha-amanitin, which inhibits RNA polymerase II. She suggests that the child's liver failure is most likely due to inhibition of the synthesis of which of the following?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Large rRNA synthesis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Large ribosomal RNA (28S, 18S, 5.8S) is synthesised by RNA polymerase I, not RNA polymerase II, so alpha-amanitin's inhibition of Pol II would not directly explain a loss of large rRNA synthesis.

## answer_b
Small RNA and large rRNA synthesis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Large rRNA is made by Pol I; small nuclear RNAs are made partly by Pol II and partly by Pol III, so this option does not correctly isolate the Pol II-specific product.

## answer_c
mRNA

## explanation_c
Correct. Each eukaryotic RNA polymerase transcribes its own class of genes: RNA polymerase I makes the large ribosomal RNAs, RNA polymerase II makes messenger RNA (and some small nuclear RNAs), and RNA polymerase III makes tRNA and the small 5S rRNA. Alpha-amanitin is a potent, relatively selective inhibitor of RNA polymerase II, so poisoning with it collapses mRNA synthesis specifically, halting the production of new proteins genome-wide. Because the liver is a highly synthetically active organ constantly replacing its proteome, this abrupt loss of mRNA (and downstream protein) synthesis is exactly what drives the fulminant hepatic failure characteristic of Amanita phylloides poisoning.

## answer_d
tRNA and protein

## explanation_d
Incorrect. tRNA is made by RNA polymerase III, not RNA polymerase II, so alpha-amanitin's Pol II inhibition does not directly explain a loss of tRNA synthesis (though protein synthesis is affected indirectly, through the loss of new mRNA).

## answer_e
Mitochondrial RNA synthesis

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Mitochondrial RNA is synthesised by a separate, mitochondria-specific RNA polymerase encoded by the nuclear genome, distinct from the three nuclear RNA polymerases (I, II, III) and not the target alpha-amanitin's Pol II inhibition acts on.

## title
Removal of introns from mRNA precursors

## question
Which one of the following best describes the process of removal of introns from mRNA precursors?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
RNA catalyzed in the absence of protein

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Splicing is carried out by spliceosomes, ribonucleoprotein complexes that combine small nuclear RNAs with numerous associated proteins; it is not an RNA-alone reaction free of protein.

## answer_b
Requires no enzymes

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Splicing very much requires a dedicated enzymatic/catalytic machinery (the spliceosome); it does not proceed without enzymes.

## answer_c
Controlled by RNA polymerase

## explanation_c
Incorrect. RNA polymerase's job is transcription, synthesising the pre-mRNA in the first place; it is not the machinery that subsequently removes introns from that transcript.

## answer_d
Carried out by spliceosomes

## explanation_d
Correct. Introns are removed from a pre-mRNA transcript, and the flanking exons joined together, by the spliceosome, a large ribonucleoprotein complex built from small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs, such as U1, U2, U4, U5, U6) bound to their associated proteins (small nuclear ribonucleoproteins, snRNPs). The spliceosome recognises specific sequences at the intron-exon boundaries, precisely excises each intron, and ligates the exons, producing the mature mRNA that then leaves the nucleus. This spliceosome-mediated mechanism is what makes possible the mRNA processing step of intron removal, distinct from the earlier transcription step carried out by RNA polymerase.

## answer_e
Regulated by RNA helicase

## explanation_e
Incorrect. RNA helicase unwinds RNA secondary structure and assists various RNA-processing steps generally, but it is not itself the machinery that carries out intron removal; that is the spliceosome's role.

## title
Deriving the RNA sequence from a DNA template strand

## question
The sequence of the template DNA strand is 5'-GATATCCATTAGTGAC-3'. Which one of the following is the sequence of the RNA produced?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
5'-GTCACTAATGGATATC-3'

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This sequence uses thymine (T) instead of uracil (U), so it cannot be the RNA product; it also is not the correct base-pairing complement of the given template.

## answer_b
5'-CTATAGGTAATCACTG-3'

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This sequence also uses thymine (T) instead of uracil (U), so it cannot be an RNA sequence at all.

## answer_c
5'-CUAUAGGUAAUCACUG-3'

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This sequence correctly uses uracil, but its base order is not the antiparallel complement of the given template strand read in the correct 5' to 3' direction; it looks like a coding-strand-style sequence written the wrong way round rather than the actual RNA transcript.

## answer_d
5'-GUCACUAAUGGAUAUC-3'

## explanation_d
Correct. RNA polymerase reads the template strand 3' to 5' and synthesises RNA 5' to 3', with each RNA base complementary to the template base opposite it (A pairs with U, T pairs with A, G pairs with C, C pairs with G). Complementing the given template (5'-GATATCCATTAGTGAC-3') base by base and then expressing the result in the 5' to 3' direction of the newly made RNA strand gives 5'-GUCACUAAUGGAUAUC-3', which matches this option exactly. This antiparallel, complementary, thymine-to-uracil-substituted relationship between template and transcript is the basic rule of transcription that this question is testing.

## answer_e
5'-CAGUGAUUACCUAUAG-3'

## explanation_e
Incorrect. This sequence uses uracil correctly but its base order does not match the antiparallel complement of the given template strand.

## title
Properties of the genetic code

## question
Which of the following statements best describes the genetic code?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
There are 64 codons, all of which code for amino acids

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Of the 64 possible codons, 61 code for amino acids and 3 (UAA, UAG, UGA) are stop codons that do not code for an amino acid.

## answer_b
Information is stored as sets of dinucleotide repeats called codons

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A codon is a triplet (three nucleotides), not a dinucleotide -- the genetic code is read three bases at a time.

## answer_c
The code is degenerate

## explanation_c
Correct. The genetic code is degenerate: most amino acids are specified by more than one codon (up to six for some, such as leucine and serine), so a base change in the third ("wobble") position of a codon often still codes for the same amino acid. Alongside degeneracy, the code is also unambiguous (each codon specifies only one amino acid), non-overlapping (each base is read as part of only one codon), and universal (the same codon table applies across almost all organisms), with each codon exactly three nucleotides long.

## answer_d
Information is stored as sets of tetranucleotide repeats called codons

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A codon is a triplet, not a set of four nucleotides.

## answer_e
The sequence of codons that make up a gene exhibits an exact linear correspondence to the sequence of amino acids in the translated protein

## explanation_e
Incorrect (this describes colinearity, a separate true fact about mRNA and protein sequence, not a defining property of the genetic code itself as tested here). The printed key for this specific stem is degeneracy.

## title
What is unique about the caps on RNA molecules

## question
Which of the following best describes the so-called caps of RNA molecules?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Composed of poly A

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The poly-A tail is a separate mRNA-processing feature added at the 3' end; the cap is a 7-methylguanosine structure added at the 5' end, not composed of poly-A.

## answer_b
Allow correct translation of prokaryotic mRNA

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Prokaryotic mRNA is not capped; ribosome binding in prokaryotes uses the Shine-Dalgarno sequence instead, so caps are not what allows prokaryotic translation.

## answer_c
Allow tRNA to be processed

## explanation_c
Incorrect. tRNA processing (trimming, base modification, CCA addition) is a separate maturation pathway; the 5' cap is a feature of mRNA, not of tRNA.

## answer_d
Unique to eukaryotic mRNA

## explanation_d
Correct. mRNA processing in eukaryotes adds a 7-methylguanosine 5' cap (along with a 3' poly-A tail and, via splicing, removal of introns); this capping step does not occur in prokaryotic mRNA, so the cap is a feature unique to eukaryotic mRNA. The cap protects the 5' end from exonuclease degradation and is recognised by initiation factors during translation, both eukaryote-specific roles.

## answer_e
Occur at the 3' end of tRNA

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The cap is a 5' mRNA feature, not a 3' tRNA feature (the 3' end of tRNA instead carries the CCA sequence where the amino acid is attached).

## title
The hydrolytic step that releases the finished polypeptide

## question
The hydrolytic step leading to the release of a polypeptide chain from a ribosome is catalyzed by which of the following?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Peptidyl transferase

## explanation_a
Correct. Peptidyl transferase, a ribozyme activity carried by the large ribosomal subunit's rRNA, catalyses peptide bond formation throughout elongation. At termination, when a release factor (not a charged aminoacyl-tRNA) occupies the ribosomal A site in response to a stop codon, this same peptidyl transferase activity is redirected to hydrolyse the bond linking the finished polypeptide to its tRNA, releasing the completed protein from the ribosome.

## answer_b
UAA

## explanation_b
Incorrect. UAA is one of the three stop codons that signals termination; it is the trigger, not the enzyme that catalyses release.

## answer_c
Stop codons

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Stop codons (UAA, UAG, UGA) signal that termination should occur by recruiting a release factor; they do not themselves catalyse the hydrolysis that releases the polypeptide.

## answer_d
Dissociation of ribosomes

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Ribosomal subunit dissociation is a later step that follows polypeptide release, recycling the subunits for another round of translation; it is not the hydrolytic step itself.

## answer_e
Stop factors

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Release factors recognise the stop codon and trigger termination, but the hydrolysis of the peptide-tRNA bond is carried out by the ribosome's peptidyl transferase activity, not by the release factor itself.

## title
Xeroderma pigmentosum: which repair pathway is defective

## question
An 8-month-old child is brought to the pediatrician's office due to excessive sensitivity to the sun. Skin areas exposed to the sun for only a brief period of time were reddened with scaling. Irregular dark spots have also appeared. The pediatrician suspects xeroderma pigmentosa. Defect in which one of the following is most likely present in this case?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Translation

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Xeroderma pigmentosum is a DNA repair defect, not a translation defect; the child's protein-synthesis machinery is not what is impaired.

## answer_b
DNA replication

## explanation_b
Incorrect. DNA replication itself is not the defective process; the defect is specifically in repairing UV-induced DNA damage, not in copying DNA.

## answer_c
Transcription

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Transcription (RNA synthesis) is not the defective process in xeroderma pigmentosum.

## answer_d
Base excision repair

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Base excision repair removes single damaged bases (such as a deaminated cytosine); it is a different repair pathway from the one defective in xeroderma pigmentosum.

## answer_e
Nucleotide excision repair

## explanation_e
Correct. Xeroderma pigmentosum is caused by a defect in nucleotide excision repair, the pathway that removes bulky, helix-distorting lesions such as UV-induced pyrimidine dimers. Without functional nucleotide excision repair, UV-induced DNA damage accumulates unrepaired in sun-exposed skin, producing this child's marked photosensitivity, scaling and irregular pigmented spots, and predisposing to skin cancer and premature skin ageing over time.
