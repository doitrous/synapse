## title
Nerve excitability is maximum at the firing level

## question
Regarding excitability of a nerve:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
is maximum at the firing level.

## explanation_a
Correct. Excitability changes across the course of an action potential: during the initial depolarization, as the membrane approaches the firing level (threshold), excitability rises and peaks right at that point, since the membrane is then closest to the self-sustaining Na+ influx that triggers a full spike. Through the rest of the action potential the fibre becomes refractory instead.

## answer_b
is high during repolarization.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. During repolarization the fibre is within the absolute or relative refractory period, when excitability is reduced or abolished, not elevated.

## answer_c
can respond to a second stimulus during ARP.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. No stimulus, however strong, can excite the fibre during the absolute refractory period (ARP) -- the Na+ channels needed for a second spike are simply unavailable until they recover.

## answer_d
is zero during RRP.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. During the relative refractory period (RRP) excitability is reduced, not zero -- a stronger-than-threshold stimulus can still trigger a second action potential, unlike during the ARP.

## title
Chronaxie is the time by double rheobasic strength to stimulate the nerve

## question
The Strength-Duration curve:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
is the relationship between the duration of the stimulus and amplitude of response.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The strength-duration curve relates a stimulus's intensity to the duration it must be applied to excite the nerve, not to the amplitude of the resulting response -- the action potential's amplitude is fixed by the all-or-none law, not a variable the curve tracks.

## answer_b
has a rheobase which is 2 times chronaxie.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This reverses the two landmarks: chronaxie, not rheobase, is defined in terms of double an intensity -- specifically, double the rheobase intensity -- not the other way around.

## answer_c
has a chronaxie which is the time needed by double the rheobasic strength to stimulate the nerve.

## explanation_c
Correct. Rheobase is the minimum stimulus intensity that can excite the nerve, however long it is applied. Chronaxie is the duration needed for a stimulus at exactly twice that rheobase intensity to excite the nerve, and it is used as an index of excitability -- a shorter chronaxie means a more excitable fibre.

## answer_d
strong stimuli of extreme short duration can excite the nerve.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Within limits a stronger stimulus needs a shorter duration to excite the nerve, but an extremely short-duration stimulus will not excite the nerve no matter how intense it is -- there is a floor duration below which no stimulus strength suffices.

Voltage-gated Na+ channel inactivation gates are open at rest

## question
Voltage-gated Na+ channels:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
have activation gates on the inner surface of the membrane and inactivation gates on the outer surface.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This reverses the two gates' actual positions: the activation gate sits near the outer surface and the inactivation gate on the inner surface, not the other way around.

## answer_b
inactivation gates are opened at rest.

## explanation_b
Correct. At the resting membrane potential the voltage-gated Na+ channel's activation gate is closed and its inactivation gate is open, which together define the channel's resting state. Opening the outer (activation) gate on depolarization makes the channel activated; only later does the inner (inactivation) gate close, making it inactivated.

## answer_c
activation gates open during catelectrotonus.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Activation gates open on depolarization toward threshold, not specifically during catelectrotonus (the local hyperpolarization under a cathode-adjacent anode, or more generally an unrelated electrotonic phenomenon) -- the gate-opening event this question tests is tied to depolarization, not this specific named condition.

## answer_d
at threshold potential, the inactivation gates close.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Inactivation gates close shortly after the activation gates open, on a short delay following threshold, not exactly at the threshold potential itself.

## question
Chronaxie is:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

C

the minimum duration of a stimulus needed to excite the nerve.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. "The utilization time" names a different landmark on the strength-duration curve -- the duration needed at rheobase intensity itself, not at double that intensity.

## question
Resting membrane potential in medium sized nerve is normally about:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. -50 mV is not the standard teaching figure for any of the cell classes whose RMP magnitude is typically taught -- large nerve/muscle fibres, medium neurons, or less excitable cells.

## answer_b

Incorrect. +35 mV is not a resting value at all -- it is close to the overshoot value the membrane potential reaches at the peak of an action potential, not its resting state.

Naming the wrong cell class for a given RMP value is one of the commonest slips on this fact.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. -55 mV is not the standard teaching figure quoted for medium-sized nerve fibres; it falls between the taught values without corresponding to any of the three standard cell-type categories.

## title
Myelinated fibre stimulation produces reversal of membrane potential

## question
The stimulation of myelinated nerve fibers would produce:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
outward flow of sodium ions.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. An adequate stimulus produces an inward, not outward, flow of sodium ions -- Na+ moves down its electrochemical gradient into the cell, the mechanism of depolarization.

## answer_b
inward flow of potassium ions.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Potassium's movement during and after the action potential is outward (efflux), not inward, down its own concentration gradient.

## answer_c
reversal of membrane potential.

## explanation_c
Correct. An adequate stimulus to a myelinated fibre triggers a full action potential at the nearest node of Ranvier: depolarisation carries the potential from about -90 mV through the firing level to an overshoot of about +35 mV -- a genuine reversal of the membrane potential's sign, not merely a partial shift toward zero. This overshoot is the defining feature that separates a true action potential from a smaller, non-propagated local response.

## answer_d
displacement of myelin sheath.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Stimulation of a myelinated fibre does not physically displace the myelin sheath; the sheath's job is passive insulation of the internode, unaffected in position by the electrical events of the action potential.

## answer_e
none of the above are correct.

## explanation_e
Incorrect, since option C correctly describes what stimulation of a myelinated fibre produces.

## title
Local circuit conduction needs more energy than saltatory conduction

## question
Local circuit mechanism for conduction of action potential:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
is more rapid than saltatory conduction.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Local circuit (continuous, point-to-point) conduction is slower, not more rapid, than saltatory conduction -- saltatory conduction's node-to-node jumping is exactly what gives myelinated fibres their speed advantage.

## answer_b
occurs in the myelinated nerve fibers.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Local circuit conduction is the mechanism of unmyelinated, not myelinated, fibres; myelinated fibres instead use saltatory conduction between their nodes of Ranvier.

## answer_c
needs less energy than saltatory conduction.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This has the comparison backwards: local circuit conduction needs more, not less, energy than saltatory conduction, for the same reason option D gives.

## answer_d
needs more energy than saltatory conduction.

## explanation_d
Correct. In an unmyelinated axon, the action potential must be freshly regenerated at every point along the membrane via local circuit current flowing to the next resting patch, so the whole membrane's Na+-K+ pump has to restore ionic gradients continuously along its entire length. Saltatory conduction, by contrast, regenerates the impulse only at the sparse nodes of Ranvier, which is far more economical -- so local circuit conduction needs more energy overall than saltatory conduction does.

## answer_e
occurs in both myelinated and unmyelinated nerve fibers.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Local circuit conduction occurs specifically in unmyelinated fibres; myelinated fibres instead conduct by the separate saltatory mechanism, so this option's claim of occurring in both is not correct.

Relative refractory period coincides with second 2/3 of the descending limb

## question
Relative refractory period:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
is an increase in excitability.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The relative refractory period is a period of decreased, not increased, excitability -- only a stronger-than-threshold stimulus can excite the fibre during it.

## answer_b
is a complete loss of excitability.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Complete loss of excitability describes the absolute, not the relative, refractory period; during the relative refractory period some Na+ channels have already recovered and are available.

## answer_c
coincides with the second 2/3 of descending limb of spike.

Correct. The relative refractory period begins at the end of the absolute refractory period and ends when the membrane potential returns to its resting level -- a window that corresponds to roughly the second two-thirds of the spike's descending limb (repolarization), the first third of which is still part of the absolute refractory period. Only once enough Na+ channels have recovered, partway through repolarization, does the relative refractory period begin.

during it the nerve can't respond to any stimulus whatever its strength.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This describes the absolute, not the relative, refractory period -- during the relative refractory period a sufficiently strong stimulus CAN still excite the fibre, unlike during the absolute refractory period.

## answer_e
coincides with after hyperpolarization.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. After-hyperpolarization is a separate, later phase that follows repolarization, not the window the relative refractory period itself occupies.

## title
All-or-none law applies to a single nerve fiber

## question
All or none law can be applied to:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
whole skeletal muscle.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A whole skeletal muscle is made of many individual fibres with different thresholds, so its overall contraction is graded (by recruiting more fibres and raising stimulation frequency), not all-or-none, even though each single fibre within it does obey the law.

## answer_b

## title
Deep pressure decreases conduction mainly in A-type fibres

## question
Which of the following is correct for nerve fibers:

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This reverses the actual speed ranking: A fibres (2-20 micrometres, 20-120 m/sec) conduct faster than B fibres (1-5 micrometres, 5-15 m/sec), not the other way around.

## answer_c

## answer_d
preganglionic nerve fibers are of C type.

Incorrect. Preganglionic autonomic fibres are the example given for B fibres, not C fibres.

## answer_e
deep pressure decreases conduction mainly in A type.

## explanation_e
Correct. Beyond conduction speed, the fibre classes differ in their sensitivity to environmental factors: local anaesthetics depress transmission in C fibres before A fibres, B fibres are the most susceptible to hypoxia, and deep pressure on a nerve can abolish conduction in A fibres while C fibres stay relatively intact -- so deep pressure decreases conduction mainly in A-type fibres. This differential vulnerability is a classic bedside correlate: a limb "falling asleep" under sustained pressure loses touch and motor (A-fibre) sensation before it loses dull, aching pain (C-fibre) sensation.
