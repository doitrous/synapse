## explanation_a
Incorrect. "The effect of the environment on our bodies" describes neither branch of pharmacology taught here; it is not a recognised definition of pharmacodynamics, and confusing it with the drug-body relationship is a distractor, not a real alternative teaching point.

## answer_b
The effect of the kidney on drug molecules

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Renal handling of a drug is a pharmacokinetic detail (part of excretion), not a definition of pharmacodynamics, and it describes one organ's role in drug elimination rather than the drug's own effect on the body as a whole.

Correct. Pharmacodynamics is what the drug does to the body: the receptors it occupies, the effect it produces, and how that effect grows with concentration. This is the mirror-image half of pharmacokinetics, which instead asks what the body does to the drug (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion). Getting the direction right -- drug acting on body, versus body acting on drug -- is the single fact this whole vocabulary pair turns on.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. "The effect our body has on drugs" is the definition of pharmacokinetics, not pharmacodynamics -- this option reverses the direction of action that distinguishes the two terms.

## title
Competitive vs non-competitive antagonism: potency and efficacy

## question
A competitive antagonist affects the agonist's --------- and a non-competitive antagonist affects the agonist's ----------

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Duration, Speed

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Duration and speed are not the two properties this classic pharmacodynamics pair distinguishes; a competitive antagonist's effect is described by a rightward shift of the dose-response curve (potency), not a change in how fast or how long the agonist acts.

## answer_b
Efficacy, Potency

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This reverses the correct pairing: a competitive antagonist is the one that reduces potency (shifting the curve right), while a non-competitive antagonist is the one that reduces efficacy (flattening the maximum response), not the other way round.

## answer_c
Speed, Duration

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Speed and duration are not the properties tested; the standard teaching pair for competitive versus non-competitive antagonism is potency and efficacy, read from the shape of the shifted concentration-response curve.

## answer_d
Potency, Efficacy

## explanation_d
Correct. A competitive antagonist binds the same site as the agonist, so enough agonist can displace it: the concentration-response curve shifts to the right in parallel, reducing potency, while the maximum response is eventually still reached. A non-competitive antagonist binds elsewhere (or binds irreversibly), so agonist cannot displace it: the curve's maximum falls, reducing efficacy, and no amount of agonist restores it. Potency for competitive, efficacy for non-competitive is the pairing this teaching point isolates.

## title
Definition of an antagonist

## question
An antagonist is a substance that:

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Binds to the receptors without directly altering their functions

## explanation_a
Correct. An antagonist binds a receptor with affinity but has no efficacy of its own: it does not initiate the conformational change that produces a cellular response, and acts only by occupying the receptor and preventing an agonist from binding there. An antagonist alone at a quiet receptor does nothing at all -- what looks like an effect when an antagonist is given is really the removal of agonist tone that was already present, not a new action of its own.

## answer_b
Binds to the receptors and initiates changes in cell function, producing maximal effect

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Binding a receptor and producing a maximal effect is the definition of a full agonist, not an antagonist -- an antagonist by definition produces no effect of its own on binding.

## answer_c
Interacts with plasma proteins and does not produce any effect

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Plasma protein binding is a drug-distribution phenomenon (part of pharmacokinetics), not the definition of a pharmacodynamic antagonist, which specifically concerns receptor binding, not protein binding in the blood.

## answer_d
Binds to the receptors and initiates changes in cell function, producing submaximal effect

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Binding a receptor and producing a submaximal effect describes a partial agonist, which does have some efficacy, not an antagonist, which has none.

Definition of an agonist

## question
Which one of the following statements describes an agonist?

## subject
pharm

## status

## owner

## vignette

A

## answer_a
A drug that binds to a receptor and stimulates cellular activity

## explanation_a
Correct. An agonist is a drug that binds to a receptor and stimulates cellular activity -- it has both affinity (it binds) and intrinsic activity (the binding produces a response). Incorrect. Increasing the concentration of another drug at receptor sites is not the definition of an agonist; no standard pharmacodynamic term is defined this way, and it confuses a drug's own receptor action with a pharmacokinetic interaction between two drugs.

## answer_d
A drug that binds to a receptor but does not produce intrinsic activity

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Binding a receptor without producing intrinsic activity is the definition of an antagonist, not an agonist -- an agonist is specifically defined by producing a response, the opposite of this option.
