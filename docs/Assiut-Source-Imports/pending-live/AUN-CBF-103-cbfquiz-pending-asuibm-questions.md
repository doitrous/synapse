## title
Enzyme's substrate-binding surface

## question
In contrast to inorganic catalysts, enzymes have an intricately shaped surface called the:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Cofactor

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A cofactor is a non-protein helper molecule or metal ion an enzyme may require for activity; it is not the intricately shaped binding surface itself.

## answer_b
Substrate

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The substrate is the molecule the enzyme acts on, not the shaped surface of the enzyme that binds it.

## answer_c
Apoenzyme

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The apoenzyme is the protein portion of an enzyme that requires a cofactor to become catalytically active (the holoenzyme); it is not specifically the name for the intricately shaped binding surface.

## answer_d
Active site

## explanation_d
Correct. The active site is the enzyme's intricately shaped surface, a three-dimensional pocket or cleft formed by the folded protein. It binds the substrate with high specificity and carries out catalysis. This precisely shaped binding surface, tailored by the protein's fold to one substrate or a narrow family of substrates, is exactly what distinguishes an enzyme's specificity from the far less selective action of a simple inorganic catalyst.

## title
Enzyme-substrate relationship

## question
The relationship between an enzyme and a reactant molecule can best be described as:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
An association stabilized by a covalent bond

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Enzyme-substrate binding is overwhelmingly non-covalent (hydrogen bonds, ionic interactions, van der Waals forces), not stabilised by a covalent bond between enzyme and substrate.

## answer_b
A permanent mutual alteration of structure

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The substrate, not the enzyme, undergoes a lasting chemical change (conversion to product); the enzyme's own structure is not permanently and mutually altered.

## answer_c
One in which the enzyme is changed permanently

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The enzyme emerges from the reaction chemically unchanged and able to catalyse further reaction cycles; it is not permanently changed.

## answer_d
A temporary association

## explanation_d
Correct. The enzyme binds its substrate at the active site to form a transient enzyme-substrate complex, catalyses the conversion to product, and then releases the product, returning to its original state ready to bind another substrate molecule. This temporary, non-covalent association is what allows a single enzyme molecule to catalyse many reaction cycles in succession without being consumed or permanently altered itself. This transient nature of enzyme-substrate binding is a defining feature of enzyme catalysis, distinguishing it from a stoichiometric reactant that is consumed in a single use.

## title
Active site versus antibody binding site

## question
The active site of an enzyme differs from an antibody antigen binding site in that the enzyme active site:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Contains modified amino acids

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Both an enzyme active site and an antibody binding site can involve specific, sometimes unusual, amino acid arrangements; the presence of modified amino acids is not the distinguishing feature between them.

## answer_b
Catalyzes a chemical reaction

## explanation_b
Correct. Both an enzyme's active site and an antibody's antigen-binding site bind their target ligand with high specificity, but only the enzyme active site goes on to catalyse a chemical reaction, converting the bound substrate into product. An antibody binding site simply holds its antigen without chemically transforming it, which is the fundamental functional distinction between the two kinds of binding surface. This catalytic capability is precisely what defines an enzyme as a catalyst rather than merely a high-affinity binding protein.

## answer_c
Contains amino acids without side chains

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Amino acids by definition all have side chains (even glycine's is a single hydrogen), so this is not a real or distinguishing feature of either binding site.

## answer_d
Is complementary to a specific ligand

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Complementarity to a specific ligand is a shared feature of both an enzyme active site and an antibody binding site, not something that distinguishes the enzyme from the antibody.
