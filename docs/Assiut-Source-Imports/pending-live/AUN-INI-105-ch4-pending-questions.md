Transposons: mobile DNA and toxin genes

## question
Transposons?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Genetic elements that contain RNA

## explanation_a
Incorrect alone. Transposons are DNA elements, not RNA-containing genetic elements, so this statement alone is false and not part of the correct combined answer.

## answer_b
Carry genes that code for toxin production

## explanation_b
Incorrect alone (true but partial). Transposons can indeed carry genes that code for toxin production, but this is only one of two jointly true statements the key combines, not the complete answer by itself.

## answer_c
Autonomously replicate

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Transposons, unlike plasmids, do not replicate autonomously -- they depend on being part of a replicon (the chromosome or a plasmid) to be copied, which is exactly why this statement is excluded from the combined correct answer.

## answer_d
Mobile DNA sequence that can move from one site to another site.

## explanation_d
Incorrect alone (true but partial). A transposon is indeed a mobile DNA sequence that can move from one site to another (popularly called a "jumping gene"), but this is only one of two jointly true statements the key combines, not the complete answer alone.

## answer_e
B&D

## explanation_e
Correct. Transposons are best described by combining two of the listed statements: they are mobile DNA sequences able to move from one site to another (B, the defining "jumping gene" property) and they can carry genes that code for toxin production (D) -- while they do not contain RNA and, unlike plasmids, do not replicate autonomously. Since two separate listed statements are both true and the others are not, "B & D" is the correct composite answer rather than any single option alone.

## question
The following statements are correct regarding transposons EXCEPT?

## subject

## status

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect (true statement, so not the EXCEPT answer). Transposons can indeed carry genes that code for antibiotic resistance, one of the classic ways resistance genes spread between replicons.

## answer_b

## explanation_b

Transposons do not autonomously replicate -- unlike a plasmid, a transposon has no independent origin of replication of its own and depends entirely on being part of a chromosome or plasmid to be copied along with that larger replicon.

## title
Helicase: breaking hydrogen bonds between nucleotides

## question
Enzyme which breaks the hydrogen bonds between nucleotides?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Ligase

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Ligase seals nicks in the DNA backbone, joining fragments together (such as Okazaki fragments on the lagging strand) by forming a phosphodiester bond -- it does not break hydrogen bonds between base pairs.

## answer_b
Helicase

## explanation_b
Correct. Helicase is, by definition, the enzyme that breaks the hydrogen bonds between complementary nucleotide base pairs, unwinding the DNA duplex ahead of the replication fork and producing the two separated single strands that serve as templates for new DNA synthesis. Without helicase's unwinding action, the two other replication proteins -- single-strand binding proteins, which stabilise the separated strands, and DNA polymerase, which needs single-stranded template -- would have nothing to act on.

## answer_c
Polymerase

## explanation_c
Incorrect. DNA polymerase synthesizes a new DNA strand by adding nucleotides complementary to an already-single-stranded template; it does not itself break the hydrogen bonds holding the original duplex together.

## answer_d
Primase

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Primase synthesizes the short RNA primer needed to give DNA polymerase a starting point; it does not break hydrogen bonds to unwind the duplex.

## subject

Promotor

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A promoter is a regulatory DNA sequence upstream of a gene where RNA polymerase binds to begin transcription; it is not a 3-base unit of the mRNA reading frame.

Codon

Incorrect. An anticodon is the complementary 3-base sequence carried on a tRNA molecule that pairs with the mRNA codon; it is the tRNA's counterpart to a codon, not the codon on mRNA itself.
