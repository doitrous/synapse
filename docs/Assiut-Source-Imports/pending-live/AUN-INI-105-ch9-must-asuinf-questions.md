## title
Symbiosis: both organisms benefit

## question
Symbiosis is?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
One organism causes harm to other

## explanation_a
Incorrect. One organism causing harm to the other describes parasitism, not symbiosis in the mutualistic sense tested here.

## answer_b
One organism is benefited and the other is unaffected

## explanation_b
Incorrect. One organism benefiting while the other is unaffected describes commensalism, not symbiosis in this sense.

## answer_c
Essential association of two organisms and both are benefited

## explanation_c
Correct. Symbiosis, in the strict mutualistic sense used in this bank, is an essential association between two organisms. Both partners benefit from the relationship. This is distinct from parasitism, where the host is harmed, and commensalism, where the host is unaffected.

## answer_d
None of the above

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Since option C is the printed correct answer, "none of the above" is wrong.

Facultative parasites are those either free-living or parasitic

## question
Facultative parasites are those?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Exist in a host in which it does not ordinary live

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Existing in a host it does not ordinarily live in describes an incidental (accidental) parasite, not a facultative one.

## answer_b
Either free-living or parasitic

## explanation_b
Correct. Facultative parasites are those that can be either free-living or parasitic. They switch between the two lifestyles depending on conditions. This is unlike an obligate parasite, which has no free-living option at all.

## answer_c
Completely dependent upon its host

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Being completely dependent upon its host describes an obligate parasite, the opposite of facultative flexibility.

## answer_d
Remain all or most of its life in its host

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Remaining all or most of its life in its host describes an obligate (or at least a highly host-dependent) parasite, not the free-living-capable facultative pattern.

## question
Obligatory parasite are those?

## subject

## status

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Either free-living or parasitic

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Being either free-living or parasitic describes a facultative parasite, not an obligate one.

## answer_b
Occurs in one specific host

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Occurring in one specific host is a host-specificity fact, not the same axis as obligate-vs-facultative dependency (many obligate parasites can still use more than one host species across their life cycle).

## answer_c
A free-living that may live as parasitic

## explanation_c
Incorrect. "A free-living [organism] that may live as parasitic" restates the facultative pattern, not the obligate one.

## answer_d
Completely dependent upon its host

## explanation_d
Correct. An obligatory parasite is completely dependent upon its host. It cannot survive outside a host at any stage of its life cycle. This total dependency is the defining feature that distinguishes it from a facultative parasite.

## title
Opportunistic parasites cause disease in immunocompromised hosts

## question
Opportunistic parasite are those?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Produces disease in immunocompromised host

## explanation_a
Correct. Opportunistic parasites are those that produce disease specifically in an immunocompromised host. Their pathogenicity is normally kept in check by an intact immune system. Once that check is lost, disease that would not otherwise occur can develop.

## answer_b
Produces disease in immunocompotent host

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Producing disease in an immunocompetent host is the pattern of a true, primary pathogen, not an opportunistic one, which typically causes little or no disease when the immune system is intact.

## answer_c
Exists in a host in which it does not ordinary live

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Existing in a host it does not ordinarily live in describes an incidental (accidental) parasite, a different category from opportunism.

## answer_d
A free-living but seeks its host from time to time

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Being free-living but seeking a host occasionally describes a facultative parasite's behaviour, not the immune-status-dependent pattern that defines opportunism.
