## title
How fat-soluble molecules enter a cell

## question
How do fat-soluble molecules normally get into a cell?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
They never get in

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Fat-soluble molecules do routinely cross the cell membrane; the question is by which route.

## answer_b
They pass through protein pores in the cell membrane

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Protein pores/channels are the route for water-soluble ions and small polar molecules, not for fat-soluble ones, which do not need a protein channel to cross.

## answer_c
They dissolve in the fat layers of the membrane and enter the cell by diffusion

## explanation_c
Correct. The cell membrane's core is a lipid bilayer, so a fat-soluble (lipid-soluble) molecule simply dissolves into that lipid core and diffuses across it down its own concentration gradient, without needing a protein carrier or channel. This route -- simple diffusion directly through the lipid bilayer -- is how lipid-soluble substances such as oxygen, carbon dioxide, alcohols and steroid hormones normally cross cell membranes. This is a direct consequence of the membrane's basic phospholipid-bilayer architecture, which readily admits lipid-soluble molecules while excluding most water-soluble ones.

## answer_d
They are absorbed by phagocytosis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Phagocytosis is a bulk, vesicle-based uptake mechanism used for particles and large complexes, not the normal route for small fat-soluble molecules.

## title
What increases the rate of diffusion across a membrane

## question
The rate of diffusion of a particle across a membrane will increase if:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The size of the particle increases

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A larger particle diffuses more slowly, not faster, since diffusion rate is inversely related to particle (molecular) size.

## answer_b
The number of protein channels increases

## explanation_b
Correct. Facilitated diffusion of ions and polar molecules depends on the availability of protein channels (or carriers) in the membrane, so increasing the number of these channels increases the total cross-sectional pathway available for the particle to cross, raising the rate of diffusion. This is directly analogous to widening a doorway: more open channels mean more particles can cross per unit time for a given concentration gradient. This principle underlies physiological up- or down-regulation of channel/transporter number as a way of tuning membrane permeability.

## answer_c
The temperature decreases

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Lower temperature slows molecular motion and so decreases, rather than increases, the rate of diffusion.

## answer_d
The thickness of the membrane increases

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A thicker membrane increases the diffusion distance a particle must cross, which decreases, rather than increases, the rate of diffusion.
