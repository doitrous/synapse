## title
Enzymes that execute apoptosis

## question
Apoptosis is brought by a group of enzymes called:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Catalases

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Catalases break down hydrogen peroxide as part of the cell's antioxidant defence against oxidative stress; they play no role in executing programmed cell death.

## answer_b
Caspases

## explanation_b
Correct. Apoptosis is executed by caspases, a family of enzymes activated in a cascade once the cell commits to programmed death. Once active, caspases destroy the nuclear membrane and activate DNAases, which degrade nuclear DNA -- the ordered, energy-dependent dismantling that distinguishes apoptosis from necrosis. The bcl-2 protein family sits upstream of the caspases, controlling whether this cascade is triggered at all.

## answer_c
Proteases

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Caspases are technically a class of protease, but "proteases" alone is too broad an answer; the specific caspase family, not proteases in general, is what this teaching point names as apoptosis's executioner enzymes.

## answer_d
Lipases

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Lipases hydrolyse triglycerides and drive enzymatic fat necrosis (as in acute pancreatitis), a form of cell death unrelated to apoptosis's proteolytic execution machinery.

## title
Bcl-2's role in cell death

## question
Bcl2 gene plays an important role in:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Inflammation

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Inflammation is the vascular and cellular response to tissue injury or infection; the bcl-2 protein family does not regulate the inflammatory response, and apoptosis itself is characteristically non-inflammatory.

## answer_b
Necrosis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Necrosis is passive, uncontrolled cell death from severe injury, triggered by mechanisms like ATP depletion and membrane damage; bcl-2 does not control this pathway.

## answer_c
Autolysis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Autolysis is the self-digestion of a dead cell by its own released lysosomal enzymes after death has already occurred, not a process bcl-2 regulates.

## answer_d
Apoptosis

## explanation_d
Correct. Apoptosis is controlled by the bcl-2 protein family, which sets the threshold at which the caspase cascade that executes programmed cell death is triggered. Anti-apoptotic bcl-2 family members restrain this cascade, while pro-apoptotic family members promote it, so the balance between them decides whether a given cell survives or dies by apoptosis. This regulatory role is why bcl-2 overexpression is implicated in cancers that resist normal programmed cell death, such as follicular lymphoma.

Enzymatic fat necrosis and acute pancreatitis

## question
Enzymatic fat necrosis is characteristic of :

## subject

Incorrect. Moist gangrene is coagulative necrosis complicated by superadded bacterial putrefaction, not the lipase-driven enzymatic fat necrosis this question describes.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A pyogenic abscess is a localised collection of pus, the product of liquefactive necrosis, not of pancreatic lipase acting on adipose tissue.

## question
Pulmonary infarction is:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

A

## answer_a
Coagulative necrosis

## explanation_a
Correct. Pulmonary infarction, like most solid-organ infarcts (kidney, spleen, heart), follows coagulative necrosis: acute ischaemia denatures both the structural proteins and the enzymes that would digest them, so the cellular outline is held for a time even though the cells are dead. Caseation

Incorrect. Caseation necrosis is the cheese-like necrosis specific to tuberculosis, not the pattern seen in a vascular infarct.

## answer_d
Fat necrosis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Fat necrosis (traumatic or enzymatic) occurs in adipose tissue, not in the parenchyma of an infarcted lung.

## title
Metastatic calcification and diffuse skeletal metastasis

## question
Which is true regarding metastatic calcification:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Serum calcium level is normal

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Metastatic calcification occurs specifically because the serum calcium level is high (hypercalcaemia), not normal -- a normal serum calcium is the hallmark of dystrophic, not metastatic, calcification.

## answer_b
Due to increased phosphatase activity of the tissues

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Increased tissue phosphatase activity is not the mechanism named for metastatic calcification; the driver is a raised blood calcium level reaching otherwise-viable tissue.

## answer_c
Serum calcium level is low

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Serum calcium is high, not low, in metastatic calcification -- a low serum calcium would not drive calcium salts to precipitate in tissue.

## answer_d
Calcium is deposited on necrotic tissue

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Deposition on necrotic (already-damaged) tissue at a normal serum calcium describes dystrophic calcification; metastatic calcification instead deposits in viable, healthy tissue because the blood calcium level itself is elevated.

## answer_e
May occurs in patients with diffuse skeletal metastasis

## explanation_e
Correct. Metastatic calcification occurs in viable tissues whenever there is hypercalcaemia, and diffuse skeletal metastasis (such as from breast cancer) is one of its recognised causes, through the bone destruction it produces. Other causes include elevated parathyroid hormone, hypervitaminosis D, and Paget's disease; the deposits sit in gastric mucosa, kidney, lungs and blood vessel walls, usually without clinical dysfunction unless the kidney is heavily affected (nephrocalcinosis).

## title
Zenker's degeneration as hyaline change

## question
Zenker's degeneration is an example of:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Hydropic change

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Hydropic change is water accumulation giving a pale, vacuolated cytoplasm, a different appearance from the glassy pink change named here.

## answer_b
Fatty change

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Fatty change is lipid accumulation as cytoplasmic vacuoles, not the homogeneous pink appearance Zenker's degeneration describes.

## answer_c
Cloudy swelling

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Cloudy swelling is granular cytoplasmic swelling from water accumulation, not a glassy pink hyaline appearance.

## answer_d
Hyaline change

## explanation_d
Correct. "Hyaline" names a homogeneous, glassy, pink appearance on routine H&E staining rather than one specific substance. Zenker's (waxy) degeneration is hyaline change in skeletal muscle, classically described in the rectus abdominis in severe infections such as typhoid fever, and it sits alongside other hyaline-change examples such as Russell bodies, Mallory hyaline and arteriolar hyalinosis -- different substances that all happen to look alike under the microscope.

## answer_e
Amyloidosis

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Amyloidosis is extracellular beta-pleated protein deposition, a specific and distinct entity from the broader descriptive category of hyaline change, even though amyloid can also look glassy pink.
