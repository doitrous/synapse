## title
Irreversible inhibition and substrate concentration

## question
In irreversible inhibition, increasing the concentration of substrate:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Double the reaction rate

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Raising substrate concentration does not reverse an irreversible inhibitor's effect, so it cannot double the reaction rate.

## answer_b
Decreases the reaction rate

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Adding more substrate does not itself further decrease the rate; the inhibition, once irreversibly established, simply is not overcome, but the rate does not fall further either.

## answer_c
Has no effect on the reaction rate

## explanation_c
Correct. An irreversible inhibitor permanently disables the fraction of enzyme molecules it binds, whether by chelating an essential cofactor, denaturing the protein, or blocking a chemical group required for catalysis. Because this loss of active enzyme is not competed away by substrate the way a competitive inhibitor's binding is, raising substrate concentration cannot restore the lost activity, and the reaction rate stays exactly where the irreversible inhibition left it. This lack of reversal by excess substrate is the defining functional difference between irreversible and competitive (reversible) inhibition.

## answer_d
Overcomes the inhibition, increasing the reaction rate up to 1/2 Vmax

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Overcoming inhibition by raising substrate concentration is the hallmark of competitive (reversible) inhibition, where more substrate outcompetes the inhibitor for the active site; it does not happen with irreversible inhibition, where the enzyme is permanently disabled.

## title
False statement about enzymes

## question
Which of the statements regarding enzyme is false?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Enzymes are proteins that function as catalysts

## explanation_a
Incorrect as an answer (a true statement). Most enzymes are indeed proteins that function as biological catalysts, so this statement is true, not the false one being asked for.

## answer_b
Enzyme activity can be regulated

## explanation_b
Incorrect as an answer (a true statement). Enzyme activity is regulated in many ways, including allosteric effectors, covalent modification and changes in enzyme amount, so this statement is also true.

## answer_c
Enzymes are specific

## explanation_c
Incorrect as an answer (a true statement). Enzymes are highly specific for their substrate or a narrow family of related substrates, so this statement is true as well.

## answer_d
Enzymes provide activation energy for reaction

## explanation_d
Correct (the false statement). An enzyme does not provide or supply activation energy to a reaction; instead, it lowers the activation energy barrier that must be overcome, by stabilising the reaction's transition state, which is what allows the reaction to proceed faster at a given temperature. This distinction, between lowering a barrier and supplying energy, is exactly the point this false-statement item is testing. The reaction's own thermodynamics (its overall free energy change and equilibrium) are unaffected by the enzyme; only the rate at which equilibrium is reached changes.

Factor that does not affect enzymatic reaction rate

## question
On which of the following factors, does not affect the rate of enzymatic reaction?

## subject
fnd

## status

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Substrate, enzyme and cofactor concentration all directly affect the rate of an enzymatic reaction.

## answer_b

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Temperature affects reaction rate, rising toward an optimum before the enzyme denatures at higher temperatures.

Molecular weight

## explanation_c

Incorrect. pH affects reaction rate, since most enzymes have an optimum pH range outside of which their ionisation state and structure are disturbed.

## title
Enzyme concentration in a rate-versus-[S] plot

## question
When we discuss the relation between rate of reaction and [S], what should we do about the enzyme concentration?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Should increase rapidly

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Deliberately increasing enzyme concentration while plotting rate against [S] would confound the plot; it is held fixed, not increased.

## answer_b
No effect

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Enzyme concentration is not simply irrelevant to the plot; it must be deliberately fixed so that [S] is the only variable changing.

## answer_c
Remains constant

## explanation_c
Correct. When rate of reaction is plotted against substrate concentration [S] to study an enzyme's kinetics (as in deriving Km and Vmax), enzyme concentration must be held constant throughout, so that [S] is the only variable changing and the resulting curve reflects the enzyme's own kinetic behaviour rather than a confound from a shifting enzyme amount. If enzyme concentration were allowed to vary at the same time, the resulting rate-versus-[S] curve would not cleanly represent the enzyme's saturation kinetics. This controlled-variable principle is standard practice whenever a rate-versus-substrate curve is generated experimentally.

## answer_d
Should decrease rapidly

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Deliberately decreasing enzyme concentration during the plot would also confound the relationship being studied; it is held fixed, not decreased.

Factor giving a straight-line plot against reaction rate

## question
Which of the following factor when plotted against rate of reaction will form a straight line?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A plot of rate against substrate concentration [S] is a curve that rises and then plateaus toward Vmax as the enzyme becomes saturated, not a straight line.

## answer_b
pH

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Rate plotted against pH traces a bell-shaped curve around the enzyme's optimum pH, not a straight line.

## answer_c
Km

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Km is a fixed kinetic constant for a given enzyme-substrate pair, not a variable that is plotted against rate.

None of the other listed factors ([S], pH, Km) produce this simple linear relationship with rate.

## question
What is the effect of temperature on rate of reaction?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Increases till optimum temp and then decrease

## explanation_a
Correct. Raising temperature initially increases reaction rate, since molecules move faster and collide more frequently and energetically, up to the enzyme's optimum temperature (close to 37 degrees C for most human enzymes). Beyond that optimum, further heating denatures the enzyme's protein structure, disorganising the active site, and the rate falls sharply rather than continuing to rise. This rise-then-fall pattern, not a simple continuous increase or decrease, is the defining shape of an enzyme's temperature-rate curve.

Decreases

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Temperature does not simply decrease reaction rate throughout its range; rate rises initially before falling only past the optimum.

## answer_c
No effect on rate of reaction

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Temperature clearly affects reaction rate; it is not without effect.

## answer_d
Increases

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Rate does not simply keep increasing indefinitely with temperature; past the optimum, denaturation causes it to fall.

## title
Raised LDH1 relative to LDH2

## question
An increase in serum level of LDH1 relative to LDH2 is an indication of:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Leukemia

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Leukaemia is not the classic clinical setting associated with an LDH1-greater-than-LDH2 pattern (a "flipped" LDH ratio).

## answer_b
Myocardial infarction

## explanation_b
Correct. Lactate dehydrogenase is a tetramer of H (heart-type) and M (muscle-type) subunits, forming five isoenzymes, LDH1 (H4, cardiac-predominant) through LDH5 (M4, skeletal-muscle/liver-predominant). Normally LDH2 exceeds LDH1 in serum, so a rise in LDH1 to the point that it exceeds LDH2 (a "flipped" LDH1:LDH2 ratio) reflects cardiac muscle injury releasing its H-subunit-rich isoenzyme into the blood, classically myocardial infarction. This isoenzyme-ratio pattern is a tissue-specific diagnostic signal precisely because of LDH's differing subunit composition across tissues.

## answer_c
Muscular dystrophy

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Muscular dystrophy would more classically raise LDH5 (M4, skeletal-muscle-predominant), not specifically flip the LDH1:LDH2 ratio.

## answer_d
Hepatitis with jaundice

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Hepatitis with jaundice is more classically associated with a rise in LDH5 (liver/skeletal-muscle-predominant) rather than a rise in the cardiac-predominant LDH1 isoenzyme.

## title
Multiple forms of the same enzyme

## question
Multiple forms of the same enzyme is referred to as:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Isoenzyme

## explanation_a
Correct. Isoenzymes (isozymes) are multiple physically distinct forms of the same enzyme, each catalysing the same reaction but differing in their amino acid sequence, kinetic properties, or tissue distribution. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH1-LDH5, built from H and M subunits) and creatine kinase (CK-MM, CK-MB, CK-BB, built from M and B subunits) are the classic worked examples, and their differing tissue distribution is exactly what makes measuring a specific isoenzyme fraction diagnostically useful. This term specifically names the phenomenon of one catalytic function being carried out by more than one molecular form of enzyme.

## answer_b
Biosensor

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A biosensor is a device that uses a biological component (often an enzyme) to detect a chemical substance; it is not the term for multiple forms of one enzyme.

## answer_c
Allosteric enzyme

## explanation_c
Incorrect. An allosteric enzyme is one regulated by an effector binding away from its active site; it describes a regulatory property, not the existence of multiple forms of the same enzyme.

## answer_d
Effectors

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Effectors are molecules (activators or inhibitors) that bind an allosteric site and modulate enzyme activity; they are not the term for multiple forms of the same enzyme.
