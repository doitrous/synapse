## title
Facultative parasite can live outside or inside a host

## question
Type of parasite that can live outside the host and can also be parasitic?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Obligate

## explanation_a
Incorrect. An obligate parasite cannot live outside a host at all -- the opposite of the flexibility this stem describes.

## answer_b
Incidental

## explanation_b
Incorrect. An incidental parasite is a genuine infection of an unusual host species, not one defined by living either freely or parasitically.

## answer_c
Facultative

## explanation_c
Correct. A facultative parasite can live either freely in the environment or parasitically inside a host. It switches between the two lifestyles depending on conditions, rather than being locked into one. This flexibility distinguishes it from an obligate parasite, which has no free-living option at all.

## answer_d
Pseudoparasite

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A pseudoparasite is not a real parasite at all, merely an artefact mistaken for one.

Vector may act as intermediate, definitive or transfer host

## question
May act as intermediate host, or definitive host or transfer host?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Incidental host

## explanation_a
Incorrect. "Incidental host" names a single specific role (an unusual host species), not an organism that can play several different life-cycle roles.

## answer_b
Paratenic host

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A paratenic host is itself one specific role (arrested development, onward carriage), not the umbrella category being described.

## answer_c
Transfer host

## explanation_c
Incorrect. "Transfer host" is likewise one specific role, a synonym of paratenic host, not the broader category.

## answer_d
Vector

## explanation_d
Correct. A vector, typically an arthropod, actively shares in a parasite's life cycle. Depending on the organism, it can act biologically as an intermediate host that supports development, or even as a definitive host, as the mosquito is for the sexual stage of Plasmodium. It can also act as a purely mechanical transfer host, simply carrying the organism without any development at all.

## subject

## status

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Living in another habitat than normal describes an erratic (aberrant) parasite, not an opportunistic one.

## answer_b
Causes disease in immunocompromised people

## explanation_b
Correct. An opportunistic parasite is normally kept in check by an intact immune system. It causes disease specifically in immunocompromised hosts, when that check is lost. Cryptosporidium and Strongyloides are classic examples, both capable of severe, even life-threatening disease once immunity is impaired.

## answer_c
Passes through the alimentary canal without being noticed

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Passing through the alimentary canal unnoticed describes a coprozoic organism, not an opportunistic parasite.

## answer_d
Lives in another host than normal

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Living in another host than normal describes an incidental (accidental) parasite, a different category from opportunism, which is about the host's immune status rather than which species is infected.

## title
Parasitism: one benefits, the other is harmed

## question
Parasitism means relationship between two animals in which?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Two partners are harmed

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Two partners both being harmed does not describe any of the standard host-microbe relationship categories taught here.

## answer_b
Two partners are benefited

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Both partners benefiting describes mutualism, not parasitism.

## answer_c
One partner benefits and the other is harmed

## explanation_c
Correct. In parasitism, one partner, the parasite, benefits at the expense of the other, the host. The host is harmed by the relationship, not merely unaffected. This exploitative pattern is what distinguishes parasitism from commensalism, where the host is unaffected, and mutualism, where both benefit.

## answer_d
One partner benefits and the other not

## explanation_d
Incorrect. One partner benefiting while the other is simply unaffected (neither helped nor harmed) describes commensalism, not parasitism.
