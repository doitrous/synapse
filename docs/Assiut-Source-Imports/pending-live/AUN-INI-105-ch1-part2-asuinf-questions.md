## title
Pilus mediates bacterial adherence to mucous membranes

## question
The initial step in the process of many bacterial infections is adherence of the organism to mucous membranes. The bacterial component that mediates adherence is the?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Lipid A

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Lipid A is the toxic component of lipopolysaccharide in the Gram-negative outer membrane; it triggers inflammatory/septic effects once infection is established, but it does not mediate the initial attachment step.

## answer_b
Nucleoid

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The nucleoid is the unbound cytoplasmic region holding the bacterial chromosome; it plays no role in surface adherence.

## answer_c
Peptidoglycan

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Peptidoglycan gives the cell wall its rigidity and shape; it is not the specific structure bacteria use to attach to host mucosal surfaces.

## answer_d
Pilus

## explanation_d
Correct. The (ordinary/common) pilus is a thin, protein (pilin) appendage on the bacterial surface specialised for adherence -- it is the structure that lets a bacterium first attach to mucosal epithelium, the essential first step before an organism can colonise and go on to cause infection. This adherence function is distinct from the sex pilus, a related but separate structure used for conjugation, and distinct from the flagellum, which drives motility rather than attachment. Losing this adherence step (e.g. by blocking pilus binding) is one strategy used to prevent infection before it starts.

## answer_e
Plasmid

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A plasmid is extrachromosomal DNA carrying accessory genes (e.g. resistance genes); it is a genetic element, not a surface structure that mediates attachment.

## title
Thick vs thin peptidoglycan is the true gram-positive/negative wall difference

## question
Which one of the following is the most accurate description of the structural differences between gram-positive bacteria and gram-negative bacteria?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Gram-positive bacteria have a thick peptidoglycan layer whereas gram-negative bacteria have a thin layer

## explanation_a
Correct. The most accurate structural distinction between the two groups is peptidoglycan thickness: Gram-positive bacteria have a thick, multilayered peptidoglycan wall that retains the crystal violet stain, while Gram-negative bacteria have only a thin peptidoglycan layer sandwiched beneath an outer lipid membrane. This thickness difference is the direct mechanistic basis of the Gram stain result itself, and it also explains why Gram-positive walls can carry teichoic acid within their thick mesh while Gram-negative walls cannot. None of the other listed features (outer membrane presence, sex pilus, plasmids, capsules) actually differs systematically by Gram type the way peptidoglycan thickness does.

## answer_b
Gram-positive bacteria have an outer lipid-rich membrane whereas gram-negative bacteria do not

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This reverses the truth -- it is Gram-negative bacteria that have the outer lipid-rich membrane (carrying LPS), not Gram-positive bacteria, which lack an outer membrane entirely.

## answer_c
Gram-positive bacteria form a sex pilus that mediates conjugation whereas gram-negative bacteria do not

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The sex pilus, used for conjugative DNA transfer, is not restricted to Gram-positive bacteria -- conjugation occurs in both Gram-positive and Gram-negative organisms, and it is unrelated to the cell-wall structural difference the question is asking about.

## answer_d
Gram-positive bacteria have plasmids whereas gram-negative bacteria do not

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Plasmids (extrachromosomal DNA) occur in both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria; carrying plasmids is not a distinguishing wall-structure feature.

## answer_e
Gram-positive bacteria have capsules whereas gram-negative bacteria do not

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Capsules are found in both Gram-positive and Gram-negative species (e.g. pneumococcus is Gram-positive and encapsulated, while Klebsiella is Gram-negative and encapsulated); capsule presence does not track with Gram type.

Bacterial flagella are antigenic, not shared with human structures

## question
Each of the following statements concerning the surface structures of bacteria is correct EXCEPT?

## subject

## status

## vignette

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect (this is a true statement, so it is not the EXCEPT answer). Pili do mediate bacterial interaction with (adherence to) mucosal epithelium, the first step in establishing many infections.

## answer_b
Polysaccharide capsules retard phagocytosis

## explanation_b
Incorrect (true statement). Polysaccharide capsules genuinely retard phagocytosis, a major antiphagocytic virulence mechanism.

## answer_c
Both gram-negative rods and cocci have lipopolysaccharide endotoxin in their cell wall

They are also not chemically similar to any human structure: human cells carry no flagellum built from bacterial-type flagellin, so there is no structural basis for the claim that flagella are "nonantigenic in humans because they resemble human flagella."

## title
Peptidoglycan structure: what's true, and what isn't (thickness)

## question
Each of the following statements concerning peptidoglycan is correct EXCEPT?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
It has a backbone composed of alternating units of muramic acid and acetylglucosamine

## explanation_a
Incorrect (true statement). The peptidoglycan backbone is indeed built from alternating N-acetylmuramic acid and N-acetylglucosamine sugar units.

## answer_b
Cross-links between the tetrapeptides involve d-alanine

## explanation_b
Incorrect (true statement). The peptide cross-links between adjacent peptidoglycan strands do involve a D-alanine residue at the terminus of the pentapeptide side chain.

## answer_c
It is thinner in gram-positive than in gram-negative cells

## explanation_c
Correct. This is the false statement, so it is the EXCEPT answer. Peptidoglycan is, in fact, much THICKER in Gram-positive bacteria than in Gram-negative bacteria -- the reverse of what this statement claims. This thickness difference is exactly what makes Gram-positive walls retain the crystal violet stain (thick mesh traps the dye complex) while Gram-negative walls, with only a thin peptidoglycan layer, let the stain wash out.

## answer_d
It can be degraded by lysozyme

## explanation_d
Incorrect (true statement). Lysozyme does degrade peptidoglycan, cleaving the beta-1,4 glycosidic bond between its alternating sugar units -- this is the basis of protoplast/spheroplast formation when bacteria are treated with lysozyme.

Mycoplasma: the one prokaryote with membrane sterol

## question
Sterol is not a constituent of cytoplasmic membrane of all prokaryotes EXCEPT?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
E. Coli

## explanation_a
Incorrect. E. coli, like almost every other bacterium, lacks sterol in its cytoplasmic membrane.

## answer_b
Staphylococcus aureus

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Staphylococcus aureus's membrane, like other typical bacteria, contains no sterol.

## answer_c
Chlamydia

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Chlamydia is an obligate intracellular bacterium but does not have a sterol-containing membrane; it lacks classical peptidoglycan for a different reason (a distinct cell-wall biology), not a sterol-containing membrane.

Correct. Every other listed organism follows the general prokaryotic rule of a sterol-free membrane; Mycoplasma alone breaks it, which is also why its membrane needs this extra structural support -- it has no rigid wall to rely on instead.

## answer_e
Klebsiella

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Klebsiella, an ordinary Gram-negative rod, has no sterol in its cytoplasmic membrane.

Respiratory activities in human cells are associated with mitochondria while in bacteria they are associated with?

## subject
inf

## status

## explanation_a

Pili

Incorrect. Pili mediate adherence (or, as sex pili, conjugation); they play no role in respiration.

Cytoplasmic membrane

Incorrect. The cell wall provides shape and rigidity and is a site of action for some antibiotics; it has no respiratory enzyme function.

## title
Cell wall determines staining affinity

## question
The bacterial structure responsible for staining affinity is?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Cytoplasmic membrane

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The cytoplasmic membrane lies beneath the wall and is not what determines whether a cell retains or loses the crystal violet stain.

## answer_b
Cell wall

## explanation_b
Correct. Staining affinity in the Gram stain procedure is determined by the cell wall -- specifically, by how much peptidoglycan the wall contains and whether it is exposed to (Gram-positive, thick wall retains stain) or shielded by an outer membrane from (Gram-negative, thin wall loses stain) the decolourising step. This is the same underlying wall-thickness mechanism that produces the violet-vs-pink colour difference, so the cell wall -- not the membrane, Lipid A, mesosomes or endotoxin -- is the structure "responsible" for staining affinity.

## answer_c
Lipid A

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Lipid A is the toxic moiety of LPS; it plays no role in the mechanics of Gram staining.

## answer_d
Mesosomes

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Mesosomes are membrane invaginations with a respiratory role; they are not the structure responsible for stain retention.

## answer_e
