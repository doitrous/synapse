## title
The action potential is terminated by potassium efflux

## question
An action potential in a nerve fibre:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Is initiated by influx of K+

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The action potential is initiated by influx of sodium, not potassium; potassium's role is on the repolarising, not the initiating, side of the impulse.

## answer_b
Is terminated by influx of Na+

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Sodium influx is what depolarises and thereby initiates the impulse, not what terminates it; sodium channels inactivate rather than driving termination through further influx.

## answer_c
Is terminated by efflux of K+

## explanation_c
Correct. Repolarisation, which ends the action potential, is produced by sodium channel inactivation together with a delayed efflux of potassium through voltage-gated potassium channels; this potassium efflux restores the membrane toward its negative resting potential and is what specifically terminates the spike. The potassium channels close only slowly even after the membrane reaches its resting level, which is why a brief afterhyperpolarisation typically follows before the next impulse can be generated. This sequence -- sodium in to initiate, potassium out to terminate -- is the basic ionic grammar of every action potential.

## answer_d
Is initiated by efflux of Na+

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Sodium efflux is not how the impulse is initiated; depolarisation and initiation are driven by sodium influx, the opposite direction and the opposite phase from what this option states.

## title
Monophasic potential is called the current of injury

## question
A monophasic potential is called:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Depolarization wave

## explanation_a
Incorrect. "Depolarization wave" names a phase of the action potential itself, not the specific monophasic recording pattern produced when a nerve is damaged between two surface electrodes.

## answer_b
Negative after potential

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A negative after-potential is a small, separate deflection following the main spike, not the name given to a monophasic recording.

## answer_c
Spike potential

## explanation_c
Incorrect. "Spike potential" refers to the sharp depolarisation-repolarisation peak of the action potential itself, not to the specific monophasic recording produced by nerve damage between the electrodes.

## answer_d
Current of injury

## explanation_d
Correct. When a biphasic surface recording's second deflection is abolished because the nerve between (or under) the recording electrodes is crushed or otherwise damaged, the resulting single-deflection record is called a monophasic potential, also known clinically as the current (or demarcation potential) of injury; this is exactly the mechanism used to localise damage along a nerve pathway. The same single-deflection pattern is produced deliberately in the laboratory by placing one electrode inside the fibre, but when it appears unexpectedly in an otherwise biphasic surface recording it signals damage at or before the second electrode. Recognising this pattern is therefore a practical tool for locating a lesion along a peripheral nerve.
