## title
Enveloped viruses exit by budding or exocytosis

## question
How do enveloped viruses exit their host?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Rupturing the virus

## explanation_a
Incorrect. "Rupturing the virus" describes destroying the virion, not the mechanism by which an intact enveloped virus leaves the host cell.

## answer_b
Bursting the host cell

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Bursting (lysing) the host cell is instead the typical exit route for many naked (non-enveloped) viruses, which lack an envelope to bud through.

## answer_c
Budding or exocytosis

## explanation_c
Correct. Enveloped viruses exit the host cell by budding through the host's plasma or nuclear membrane (or via exocytosis), acquiring their lipid envelope from that host membrane in the same process. The viral envelope is lipoprotein in nature; its lipid portion is acquired from the host cell's plasma or nuclear membrane during the budding process, while its protein portion is coded by the viral genome. This exit route lets the host cell often survive and continue producing virus, unlike the lytic exit typical of many naked viruses.

## answer_d
None of these are correct

## explanation_d
Incorrect. "Budding or exocytosis" correctly describes enveloped-virus exit, so "none of these" is wrong.

## answer_e
Endocytosis

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Endocytosis is a mechanism of viral ENTRY into a host cell, not exit from one.

## title
Latent viral infection: occult, reactivating form

## question
What is a latent viral infection?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The virus initially presents with symptoms but then hides until it can be active and produce symptoms

## explanation_a
Correct. A latent viral infection is one in which the virus persists in an occult (hidden, non-productive) form inside the infected cell after the initial infection, later reactivating to produce symptoms again -- classic examples include herpes simplex and varicella-zoster virus reactivation. This occult, reactivating pattern is distinct from a chronic infection (ongoing, detectable low-level replication throughout) or a slow infection (long incubation, progressive disease with no true dormancy). Latency lets the virus persist in the body indefinitely, evading full immune clearance between reactivation episodes.

## answer_b
None of the above

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Option A correctly describes latent infection, so "none of the above" is wrong.

## answer_c
Infection develops rapidly but lasts for a short time

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Rapid development with a short overall duration describes an acute infection, not a latent one, which by definition persists for the long term with intervening dormancy.

## answer_d
Slow development, reactions to disease are less severe, likely to be present for a long time

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Slow, progressive development with milder but long-lasting reactions describes a slow virus infection (for example subacute sclerosing panencephalitis), a different persistence pattern from latency's occult-then-reactivating course.

## title
First line of defense against viral infection: interferon

## question
First Line of Defense against viral infection is?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Inflammatory response

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A general inflammatory response is a broader, slower-acting host reaction, not the specific rapid antiviral defense targeted here.

## answer_b
Type 1 interferon

## explanation_b
Correct. Type I interferons (IFN-alpha and IFN-beta) are produced early in the course of a viral infection, chiefly by virally infected cells themselves and by plasmacytoid dendritic cells, and act on neighbouring, still-uninfected cells to induce an intracellular antiviral state that slows the infection's spread. This rapid, non-specific innate response occurs well before the adaptive immune system (antibodies, cytotoxic T cells) has time to mount a specific response, making it the genuine first line of defense against viral infection. Type I interferon also increases NK-cell activity, adding a second early antiviral effect.

Incorrect. Type I interferon correctly answers this question, so "none of the above" is wrong.

## answer_d
Cell mediated immunity

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Cell-mediated immunity (cytotoxic T cells) is an adaptive response that takes days to develop after antigen presentation, acting later than the immediate interferon response, not as the first line of defense.

Correct. Pyemia

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Pyemia describes pus-forming bacteria spreading via the bloodstream to seed abscesses, an unrelated bacterial term.

## title
Capsid is the protein coat around the nucleic acid

## question
The protein coat which surrounds the nucleic acid of animal viruses is called?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Envelope

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The envelope is an additional, outer lipid layer found only in enveloped viruses, surrounding the nucleocapsid -- it is not itself the protein coat directly around the nucleic acid.

## answer_b
Nucleocapsid

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The nucleocapsid is the nucleic acid PLUS the capsid together, not the protein coat alone; describing it as just "the protein coat" omits the enclosed nucleic acid it also names.

## answer_c
Capsomer

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A capsomer is only one individual small protein subunit that assembles, together with many others, into the complete capsid -- not the whole protein coat itself.

## answer_d
Capsid

## explanation_d
Correct. The capsid (nucleocapsid) is the structural component found in all viruses -- the protein coat surrounding the viral genome, together forming the nucleocapsid. Its functions include protecting the viral genome, mediating attachment to host-cell receptors, and conferring antigenicity, but it does NOT confer sensitivity to 70% alcohol, a property instead associated with the lipid envelope. This is the one structural component every virus has, enveloped or not, distinguishing it from the envelope (present only in some viruses) or a fixed nucleic acid type.

Viroids are?

## subject
inf

## vignette

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This describes a defective virus (a genetically deficient virus unable to produce infectious daughter virions independently), a different infectious agent from a viroid.

Infective agents with low molecular weight RNA and no proteins

## explanation_b
Correct. Extrachromosomal genetic elements

## explanation_d
Incorrect. "Extrachromosomal genetic elements" describes a plasmid, a DNA element unrelated to a viroid's uncoated circular RNA composition.

## answer_e
DNA viruses

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A viroid's genome is RNA, not DNA, so it cannot be classed among DNA viruses; it is also not a true virus at all, lacking a capsid.

## question
Enveloped viruses obtain their envelopes by?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Modifying vesicles that may be used in the exporting of cellular proteins

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Modifying secretory-pathway vesicles is not the standard, general mechanism taught for how enveloped viruses acquire their envelope; the general rule is budding through a host membrane.

## answer_b
Breaking down the membrane of the cell they infect and incorporating the lipid into the capsids

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Enveloped viruses do not break down and destroy the host membrane; instead they bud through it intact, incorporating a portion of it as their own envelope while the host cell can often survive.

Budding through the cells or nuclear membrane as they leave the infected cell

Correct. The viral envelope is lipoprotein in nature; its lipid portion is acquired from the host cell's plasma or nuclear membrane during the budding process, while its protein portion is coded by the viral genome. Budding through the cell's plasma membrane or nuclear membrane as the virion leaves the infected cell is the general mechanism by which enveloped viruses obtain their envelope. This is also, correspondingly, the general mechanism by which enveloped viruses exit the host cell.

## answer_d
Encoding an enzyme which synthesizes the necessary lipids for the envelope

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A virus does not encode an enzyme to synthesize new lipids from scratch for its envelope; it instead acquires ready-made host membrane lipid during budding.

## answer_e
All of the above

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Since A, B and D are each individually incorrect, "all of the above" cannot be correct.
