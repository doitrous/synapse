## title
L-forms' resistance to penicillin is intrinsic (no cell wall)

## question
Resistance of L forms to penicillin is due to?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Absence of cell wall

## explanation_a
Correct. Penicillin's target is peptidoglycan cell-wall synthesis. L-forms are bacteria that have lost their cell wall (induced by an inducing agent), so there is simply no target for penicillin to act on -- an intrinsic, not acquired, resistance, the same logic that makes Mycoplasma naturally penicillin-resistant.

## answer_b
Production of enzymes that inactivate the drug

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Enzymatic drug inactivation (e.g. beta-lactamase) is the mechanism behind acquired resistance in organisms such as penicillinase-producing Staphylococcus aureus, not the mechanism behind L-forms' resistance, which is intrinsic due to the absent target.

## answer_c
Metabolic inactivity of the organism

## explanation_c
Incorrect. L-forms are not metabolically inactive; they remain viable and can still replicate despite lacking a cell wall, so metabolic inactivity is not the basis of their penicillin resistance.

## answer_d
All of the above

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Only the absence of a cell wall (option A) explains L-forms' resistance; options B and C do not apply to this organism, so "all of the above" incorrectly includes mechanisms that are not actually at play here.

Toxoid: a toxin that has lost toxicity but retains antigenicity

## question
Toxoid is?

## subject
inf

## status

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A toxoid retains its antigenicity (that is the whole point, so it can still be used as a vaccine); losing antigenicity while keeping toxicity would be both dangerous and useless as a vaccine, the opposite of what a toxoid is.

## answer_b
None of the above

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Option D correctly defines toxoid, so "none of the above" is factually wrong.

## answer_c

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A toxoid specifically retains antigenicity even as toxicity is destroyed; losing both properties would leave nothing capable of provoking a protective immune response, defeating the purpose of a toxoid vaccine.

## answer_d
Toxin lost its toxicity but still antigenic

## explanation_d
Correct. A toxoid is a toxin -- classically an AB-subunit exotoxin such as diphtheria toxin -- that has been treated, typically with formalin, to destroy its toxicity. Its antigenicity is preserved even as its toxicity is destroyed. This lets it be used safely as a vaccine that still provokes protective antibodies.

## title
Exotoxins are usually thermolabile

## question
The exotoxins are usually?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Thermolabile

## explanation_a
Correct. Exotoxins, being secreted proteins, are usually thermolabile, or heat-labile. Their protein structure denatures on heating, destroying their toxic activity. This contrasts with endotoxin (lipopolysaccharide), which is heat stable.

## answer_b
Thermostable

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Thermostability characterises endotoxin, not exotoxins; describing exotoxins as thermostable reverses the two toxin classes' defining property.

## answer_c
Not affected by formalin

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Exotoxins are, in fact, affected by formalin -- formalin treatment is exactly what converts an exotoxin into an antigenic but non-toxic toxoid, so "not affected by formalin" is the opposite of a defining exotoxin property.

## answer_d
Neurotoxins

## explanation_d
Incorrect. "Neurotoxin" describes the target/effect of certain specific exotoxins (e.g. tetanus, botulinum toxin), not a property shared by exotoxins in general.

## answer_e
Enterotoxins

## explanation_e
Incorrect. "Enterotoxin" likewise describes the target/effect of certain specific exotoxins (e.g. cholera toxin), not a general property of exotoxins as a class.

## question
A toxic material within the bacterial cell, non-diffusible usually lipopolysaccharide-protein complex, heat and formalin stable and poorly antigenic. This material is?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## explanation_a

Endotoxin

## explanation_b
Correct. Endotoxin is the lipopolysaccharide-protein complex forming part of the Gram-negative outer membrane. ## answer_e
B & C are correct

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Endogenous pyrogen (C) is a separate, host-derived entity from the bacterial structural material the stem defines, so "B & C" incorrectly folds in a substance this stem is not describing.

## title
Gentamycin's bactericidal effect is maximal in the log phase

## question
Gentamycin would have maximal bactericidal effect in the following phase of bacterial growth curve?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Log

## explanation_a
Correct. During the logarithmic (exponential) phase, bacteria are dividing at their maximal rate and are metabolically most active. Aminoglycosides such as Gentamycin depend on active protein synthesis for their ribosomal binding and killing action. This is why the log phase is when Gentamycin achieves its maximal bactericidal effect.

## answer_b
Lag

## explanation_b
Incorrect. In the lag phase, bacteria are adapting to their environment with no net division and comparatively low metabolic activity, giving an aminoglycoside far less of the active target process it needs to act maximally.

## answer_c
Stationary

## explanation_c
Incorrect. In the stationary phase, new-cell formation is balanced by cell death and net growth has plateaued, reducing the active division/protein-synthesis target Gentamycin's mechanism depends on for maximal effect.

## answer_d
Death

## explanation_d
Incorrect. In the death (decline) phase, cells are dying faster than new ones form and metabolic activity is winding down, well past the point of maximal target availability for an aminoglycoside.
