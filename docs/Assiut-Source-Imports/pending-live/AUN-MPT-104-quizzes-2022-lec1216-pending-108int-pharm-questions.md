## title
Effect of glucuronic acid conjugation on a drug

## question
The addition of glucuronic acid to a drug:

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Usually leads to inactivation of the drug

## explanation_a
Correct. Glucuronidation (conjugation with glucuronic acid) is a Phase II metabolic reaction, and Phase II conjugation reactions typically inactivate a drug by attaching a large, polar group that abolishes its pharmacological activity. That same conjugation also increases the drug's water solubility, preparing it for renal excretion.

## answer_b
Is an example of a Phase I reaction

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Glucuronidation is a Phase II reaction (conjugation), not Phase I -- Phase I reactions instead expose or add a reactive functional group by oxidation, reduction or hydrolysis, which typically precedes conjugation rather than being conjugation itself.

## answer_c
Occurs at the same rate in adults and newborns

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Glucuronide conjugation is markedly slower in newborns than in adults, because the neonatal liver has immature glucuronyl transferase activity. This is why neonates are especially vulnerable to toxicity from drugs cleared mainly by glucuronidation.

## answer_d
Decreases its water solubility

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Conjugation with glucuronic acid increases, not decreases, a drug's water solubility. That increase in polarity is exactly what allows the kidney to excrete the conjugated metabolite.

## title
What liver enzyme inducers increase the effect of

## question
Liver enzyme inducers increase the effect of:

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Lipid soluble drugs

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Enzyme induction speeds up metabolism, and for an ordinary active drug cleared by that pathway, faster metabolism means a reduced, not increased, effect. Being lipid-soluble does not, by itself, make a drug an exception to that.

## answer_b
Prodrugs

## explanation_b
Correct. A prodrug is inactive as administered and only becomes pharmacologically active after metabolic conversion. Enzyme induction speeds up the very reaction that generates the active drug, increasing rather than reducing its effect -- the one situation where faster metabolism raises, rather than lowers, a drug's action.

## answer_c
All drugs

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Enzyme induction reduces the effect of most ordinary drugs by clearing them faster, so "all drugs" is wrong. Prodrugs are the exception to that pattern, not the rule.

## answer_d
Water soluble drugs

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Enzyme induction does not selectively target water-soluble drugs. Induction's characteristic clinically relevant effect is on prodrug activation and on ordinary active drugs' clearance, not specifically on water-soluble drugs as a class.

## question
Biotransformation usually leads to:

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Increase in the lipid solubility of the drug

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Biotransformation generally moves a drug in the opposite direction -- toward lower, not higher, lipid solubility -- so that the kidney can eventually excrete it.

## answer_b
Decrease in water solubility of the drug

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Biotransformation generally increases, not decreases, a drug's water solubility, since a more water-soluble metabolite is more readily filtered and excreted by the kidney.

## answer_c
Decrease in the lipid solubility of the drug

## explanation_c
Correct. The overall purpose of biotransformation is to convert a lipid-soluble drug, which would otherwise be reabsorbed indefinitely in the renal tubule, into a more polar, water-soluble metabolite the kidney can excrete. So biotransformation usually decreases lipid solubility.

## answer_d
Increase in the activity of all drugs

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Biotransformation frequently inactivates a drug, particularly through Phase II conjugation, and even Phase I reactions do not increase the activity of every drug. Some Phase I products are inactive, so "all drugs" is too absolute to be correct.

## subject

Incorrect. Being water-soluble is not, by itself, what causes enterohepatic circulation -- the defining requirement is biliary excretion followed by intestinal reabsorption, not water solubility alone.
