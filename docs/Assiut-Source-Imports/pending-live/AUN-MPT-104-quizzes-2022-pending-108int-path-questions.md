## title
Cell death as a sign of irreversible cell injury

## question
Cell death is a sign of:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
General response to cellular stress

## explanation_a
Incorrect. "General response to cellular stress" is too broad and does not specify an outcome; a cell under stress may adapt, be reversibly injured, or die, and this option names none of those three specific endpoints.

## answer_b
Reversible cell injury

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Reversible cell injury is, by definition, an injury that resolves and returns the cell to normal if the stimulus is removed -- it does not end in cell death, which is the opposite endpoint.

## answer_c
Cellular adaptation

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Cellular adaptation is the response to modest, sustained stress that preserves cell viability (e.g. hypertrophy, atrophy, metaplasia) -- it is not a form of cell death.

## answer_d
Irreversible cell injury

## explanation_d
Correct. A cell meets stress with adaptation, reversible injury, or irreversible injury and death, forming one continuum of progressively worse impairment. Cell death itself only occurs once injury has crossed into the irreversible portion of that continuum -- by definition, a cell that dies has sustained damage beyond the point of recovery, which is exactly what "irreversible" means in this framework.

## title
Necrosis as an example of irreversible cell injury

## question
Irreversible cell injury includes:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Hydropic swelling

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Hydropic swelling (cell swelling from sodium and water accumulation) is a classic example of reversible cell injury -- it resolves if the stimulus is removed, which is the opposite of irreversible.

## answer_b
Necrosis

## explanation_b
Correct. Necrosis is the pathological process by which cells die following irreversible injury. It is the standard worked example of an irreversible outcome on the cell-stress continuum. Unlike adaptation or reversible injury, once necrosis has occurred the cell cannot be restored to normal, however promptly the injurious stimulus is removed.

## answer_c
Fatty change

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Fatty change (steatosis, lipid accumulation within a cell) is a reversible cellular accumulation, not a form of cell death, and resolves once the causative stimulus is removed.

## answer_d
Cloudy swelling

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Cloudy swelling is a mild, reversible cellular accumulation (an early, low-grade form of cell swelling), not an example of irreversible injury.

## title
Mechanisms by which stimuli cause cellular injury

## question
Stimuli causing cellular injury do so by:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Increase free radicals production

## explanation_a
Incorrect on its own. Increased free-radical production is a real mechanism of cell injury (it damages membranes, proteins and DNA), but it is not the only mechanism, and this quiz's own printed key marks all three named mechanisms together as correct, not this one alone.

## answer_b
All are correct

## explanation_b
Correct. Cell injury proceeds through several linked mechanisms together, not any single one alone: interference with mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation depletes ATP, which disables the energy-dependent pump that normally keeps intracellular calcium low, so calcium rises; free radicals, generated by the same injurious stimuli (ischaemia-reperfusion, toxins, radiation), independently damage membranes, proteins and DNA. All three -- ATP depletion, calcium rise, and free-radical accumulation -- are genuine, simultaneously-acting mechanisms of cell injury, which is why the printed key marks all of the individually-listed options as correct together.

## answer_c
ATP depletion

## explanation_c
Incorrect on its own. ATP depletion is a real mechanism of cell injury (it disables the sodium pump and the calcium-clearing pump), but it is not the only mechanism, and this quiz's own printed key marks all three named mechanisms together as correct, not this one alone.

## answer_d
Increased intracellular calcium

## explanation_d
Incorrect on its own. Increased intracellular calcium is a real and centrally important mechanism of cell injury, activating phospholipases, proteases and endonucleases, but it is not the only mechanism, and this quiz's own printed key marks all three named mechanisms together as correct, not this one alone.
