## title
Cell death as a sign of irreversible cell injury

## question
Cell death is a sign of:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
General response to cellular stress

## explanation_a
Incorrect. "General response to cellular stress" is too broad and does not specify an outcome; a cell under stress may adapt, be reversibly injured, or die, and this option names none of those three specific endpoints.

## answer_b
Reversible cell injury

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Reversible cell injury is, by definition, an injury that resolves and returns the cell to normal if the stimulus is removed -- it does not end in cell death, which is the opposite endpoint.

## answer_c
Cellular adaptation

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Cellular adaptation is the response to modest, sustained stress that preserves cell viability (e.g. hypertrophy, atrophy, metaplasia) -- it is not a form of cell death.

## answer_d
Irreversible cell injury

## explanation_d
Correct. A cell meets stress with adaptation, reversible injury, or irreversible injury and death, forming one continuum of progressively worse impairment. Cell death itself only occurs once injury has crossed into the irreversible portion of that continuum -- by definition, a cell that dies has sustained damage beyond the point of recovery, which is exactly what "irreversible" means in this framework.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Hydropic swelling (cell swelling from sodium and water accumulation) is a classic example of reversible cell injury -- it resolves if the stimulus is removed, which is the opposite of irreversible.

## answer_b
Necrosis

## explanation_b
Correct. Necrosis is the pathological process by which cells die following irreversible injury. It is the standard worked example of an irreversible outcome on the cell-stress continuum. Unlike adaptation or reversible injury, once necrosis has occurred the cell cannot be restored to normal, however promptly the injurious stimulus is removed.

Fatty change

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Fatty change (steatosis, lipid accumulation within a cell) is a reversible cellular accumulation, not a form of cell death, and resolves once the causative stimulus is removed.

## answer_d
Cloudy swelling

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Cloudy swelling is a mild, reversible cellular accumulation (an early, low-grade form of cell swelling), not an example of irreversible injury.

Mechanisms by which stimuli cause cellular injury

## question
Stimuli causing cellular injury do so by:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Increase free radicals production

## explanation_a
Incorrect on its own. Increased free-radical production is a real mechanism of cell injury (it damages membranes, proteins and DNA), but it is not the only mechanism, and this quiz's own printed key marks all three named mechanisms together as correct, not this one alone.

## answer_b
All are correct

## explanation_b
Correct. Cell injury proceeds through several linked mechanisms together, not any single one alone: interference with mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation depletes ATP, which disables the energy-dependent pump that normally keeps intracellular calcium low, so calcium rises; free radicals, generated by the same injurious stimuli (ischaemia-reperfusion, toxins, radiation), independently damage membranes, proteins and DNA. All three -- ATP depletion, calcium rise, and free-radical accumulation -- are genuine, simultaneously-acting mechanisms of cell injury, which is why the printed key marks all of the individually-listed options as correct together.

## answer_c
ATP depletion

## explanation_c
Incorrect on its own. ATP depletion is a real mechanism of cell injury (it disables the sodium pump and the calcium-clearing pump), but it is not the only mechanism, and this quiz's own printed key marks all three named mechanisms together as correct, not this one alone.

## answer_d
Increased intracellular calcium

## explanation_d
Incorrect on its own. Increased intracellular calcium is a real and centrally important mechanism of cell injury, activating phospholipases, proteases and endonucleases, but it is not the only mechanism, and this quiz's own printed key marks all three named mechanisms together as correct, not this one alone.

## question

## subject

## status

## vignette

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Atrophy is a decrease, not an increase, in cell size and number -- this option describes the opposite direction of change from what defines atrophy.

## answer_b
May be precancerous

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Being precancerous describes dysplasia, not atrophy -- atrophy is a benign shrinkage of tissue, not a step toward malignancy.

## answer_c
May result from a disuse of organ

## explanation_c
Correct. Atrophy is the adaptive shrinkage of a cell or organ that follows reduced functional demand, and disuse (reduced workload on a muscle, for example) is one of its classic causes, alongside reduced blood supply, reduced nutrition, reduced hormonal or nervous stimulation, and ageing. A disused limb muscle immobilised in a cast is the textbook worked example of disuse atrophy.

## answer_d
May be congenital

## explanation_d
Incorrect. "Congenital" describes a condition present from birth, which is not how atrophy is characterised -- atrophy is an acquired shrinkage of a previously normal-sized, previously formed tissue, not a developmental anomaly present at birth.

Definition of metaplasia

## question
Change of one type of tissue into another type is called:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Neoplasia

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Neoplasia is new, uncontrolled and excessive growth of cells, benign or malignant -- it does not describe one differentiated tissue type converting into another.

## answer_b
Hypoplasia

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Hypoplasia is underdevelopment of an organ (fewer cells than normal formed during development), not a conversion of one tissue type into another.

## answer_c
Dysplasia

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Dysplasia is disordered growth with loss of uniformity of individual cells and their architectural orientation -- an abnormal pattern within the same tissue type, not a change from one tissue type into another.

## answer_d
Metaplasia

## explanation_d
Correct. Metaplasia is the reversible replacement of one differentiated adult cell type by another, better able to withstand the new stress. The standard worked example is the columnar respiratory epithelium of a smoker's bronchus converting to stratified squamous epithelium -- a change of one tissue type into another type in response to a sustained irritant.

## answer_e
Hyperplasia

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Hyperplasia is an increase in the number of cells of the same type, not a change of one tissue type into a different one.

## title
A defining character of hyperplasia

## question
Which of the following is true as regard hyperplasia?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Can be caused by disuse

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Disuse causes atrophy (shrinkage from reduced demand), which is the opposite adaptive response to hyperplasia -- hyperplasia instead typically follows increased functional demand or hormonal stimulation.

## answer_b
Common in skeletal muscles

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Skeletal muscle cells are terminally differentiated and cannot ordinarily divide, so true hyperplasia (an increase in cell number by mitosis) is not a feature of skeletal muscle -- an enlarging skeletal muscle instead grows chiefly by hypertrophy of existing fibres.

## answer_c
Common in old age

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Old age is more classically associated with atrophy (senile atrophy) than with hyperplasia, which is instead a response to increased demand or stimulation, not a general feature of ageing tissue.

## answer_d
Defined as increase cell number

## explanation_d
Correct. Hyperplasia is defined as an increase in the number of cells in an organ or tissue, achieved by mitotic division of cells that retain the capacity to proliferate. It is the direct counterpart to atrophy, which is instead defined by decreased cell number and size, so the two sit at opposite ends of the same size-of-organ spectrum.
