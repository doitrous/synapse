# Item

## id
CON-INF-3160E700F96EB2

## label
Parasitic infection is acquired through several distinct modes: ingestion, injection (as by a biting arthropod vector), and autoinfection (the organism re-infects the same host without leaving it)

## canonical_key
teaching.parasitology.trematodes.modes-of-parasitic-infection

## aliases
Modes of parasitic infection
Autoinfection

## arabic_label
طرق انتقال العدوى الطفيلية

## arabic_aliases
العدوى الذاتية

## definition
A parasite can reach a new host through several distinct routes. Ingestion is ingestion of an infective stage (an egg, cyst or encysted larva) in contaminated food, water, or undercooked meat/fish. Injection describes an infective stage being introduced directly into the bloodstream or tissue, classically by the bite of a blood-feeding arthropod vector (as with malaria sporozoites injected by an Anopheles mosquito). Autoinfection is a distinct third mechanism in which the parasite completes part of its life cycle within the same host and re-infects that same individual without ever leaving the body or requiring a new exposure -- Strongyloides stercoralis and Hymenolepis nana are classic examples of parasites capable of autoinfection. All three modes can occur for different parasites, and some parasites are capable of more than one mode.

## explicit_objective
List ingestion, injection (via a vector bite) and autoinfection as the modes by which parasitic infection is acquired, and define autoinfection as re-infection of the same host without external exposure.

## pitfalls
Assuming every parasite is acquired the same way -- ingestion is the most commonly tested route, but injection (vector-borne) and autoinfection (self-perpetuating within one host) are equally valid, distinct mechanisms tested for specific organisms.

## definition
The number of intermediate hosts a platyhelminth (flatworm) requires varies by species and is a frequently tested distinguishing fact. Fasciola hepatica, Schistosoma haematobium and Fasciolopsis buski each require only one intermediate host (a snail, in each case). Diphyllobothrium latum (the fish tapeworm), by contrast, requires two intermediate hosts in sequence -- a copepod (first) and then a fresh-water fish (second) -- making it an exception among the trematode-adjacent flukes listed alongside it. Among the cestodes, most (such as Diphyllobothrium latum, Heterophyes heterophyes and Metagonimus yokogawai, tested elsewhere in this bank) require two intermediate hosts, but Taenia solium is itself an exception requiring only one (the pig, as its single intermediate host, harbouring the cysticercus larva).

## explicit_objective
State that Diphyllobothrium latum requires two intermediate hosts (unlike Fasciola hepatica, Schistosoma haematobium and Fasciolopsis buski, which each require one), and that Taenia solium requires only one intermediate host (unlike most other two-intermediate-host platyhelminths such as Diphyllobothrium latum).

## pitfalls
Assuming every fluke or tapeworm needs the same number of intermediate hosts -- the count is species-specific, and Diphyllobothrium latum (two hosts) and Taenia solium (one host, against the two-host pattern of most other cestodes tested alongside it) are the two classic exceptions worth memorising by name.

## label
The eggs, not the living or dead adult worms, are the main agent of pathology in schistosomiasis: eggs trapped in host tissue provoke a granulomatous inflammatory reaction that underlies the disease's fibrosis and organ damage

## canonical_key
teaching.parasitology.trematodes.schistosomiasis-egg-pathology

## aliases
Schistosomiasis pathology mechanism

## arabic_label
البيض هو العامل الرئيسي لأمراض داء البلهارسيات

## arabic_aliases

## definition
In schistosomiasis, the eggs -- not the living or dead adult worms -- are the main agent of pathology. Adult schistosome worms are relatively well tolerated by the host immune system, having evolved mechanisms to evade it, so they cause comparatively little direct damage while alive. Eggs, however, become trapped in the walls of the intestine, bladder, or other tissue (or are carried to the liver), and the host's immune response to egg antigens provokes a granulomatous inflammatory reaction around each trapped egg. Repeated egg deposition and granuloma formation over time is what drives the fibrosis, organ enlargement and functional damage characteristic of chronic schistosomiasis.

## explicit_objective
State that eggs, rather than living or dead adult worms, are the main agent of pathology in schistosomiasis, via the granulomatous reaction they provoke once trapped in tissue.

## pitfalls
Assuming the adult worms themselves are the main pathological driver -- they are comparatively well tolerated by the immune system, while the host's granulomatous response to trapped eggs is what actually produces most of the disease's tissue damage.

## label
Trematodes, as a group, require a snail as the intermediate host; not every species has operculated eggs or produces a fork-tailed cercaria, and being acoelomate (parenchyma-filled, no true body cavity) is a defining flatworm trait rather than "possessing parenchymal cells in the body cavity"

## canonical_key
teaching.parasitology.trematodes.general-characteristics

## aliases
General trematode characteristics
Snail as trematode intermediate host

## arabic_label
الخصائص العامة للديدان المثقوبة

## arabic_aliases
القوقع كعائل وسيط للديدان المثقوبة

## definition
As a general rule, trematodes (flukes) require a snail as their first intermediate host, a generalisation that holds across the group even though the exact snail genus differs by species. This does not mean every trematode shares every other feature: not all species have operculated eggs (Schistosoma is the standard exception), and not all species produce a fork-tailed (furcocercous) cercaria (that tail shape is specifically characteristic of Schistosoma's cercariae, not universal). Trematodes are also acoelomate flatworms, meaning they have no true body cavity; their internal spaces are instead filled with parenchymal tissue, so describing them as possessing "parenchymal cells in the body cavity" misstates their basic body plan.

## explicit_objective

## pitfalls
Over-generalising from Schistosoma's distinctive features (non-operculate eggs, fork-tailed cercaria) to the whole trematode group, or describing trematodes as having a body cavity at all -- they are acoelomate, with parenchyma filling the space instead.

## definition
Adult Schistosoma worms mature within, and largely reside in, the portal venous system before migrating against the direction of portal blood flow to their species-specific final site: the inferior mesenteric venules for Schistosoma mansoni, or the vesical venous plexus for Schistosoma haematobium. This portal-vein stage is why juvenile schistosomes (schistosomula) are described as maturing in the portal circulation/liver before their final migration.

## explicit_objective
State that adult Schistosoma worms reside in the portal venous system, from which they migrate against portal flow to their species-specific final venous site.

## pitfalls
Treating the portal vein as Schistosoma's single, fixed adult habitat -- it is instead a staging site the worms pass through before migrating on to their species-specific final venous location.

## definition
Schistosoma mansoni deposits its lateral-spined eggs in the small venules of the large intestinal (colonic) wall. The eggs then work their way through the bowel wall to be excreted in stool; eggs that instead embolise backward into the liver via the portal circulation, rather than passing outward through the gut wall, drive the periportal ("pipe-stem") fibrosis characteristic of chronic hepatosplenic schistosomiasis.

## explicit_objective
State that Schistosoma mansoni deposits its eggs in the venules of the large intestinal wall, and that eggs embolising to the liver instead drive hepatosplenic disease.

## pitfalls
Confusing Schistosoma mansoni's large-intestinal oviposition site with Schistosoma haematobium's bladder-wall site -- the two species' egg excretion routes (stool versus urine) and characteristic long-term complications (hepatosplenic fibrosis versus bladder pathology) follow from this difference in site.
