# Item

## id
CON-MSK-08751546A5D74E

## label
The hip joint is a multi-axial ball and socket synovial joint, like the shoulder

## canonical_key
anatomy.joint.hip-ball-and-socket

## aliases
Hip joint classification
Ball and socket joint

## arabic_label
مفصل الورك هو مفصل زليلي كروي الحُق متعدد المحاور، مثل مفصل الكتف

## arabic_aliases
مفصل كروي الحُق

## definition
Synovial joints are classified by the number of axes they move around, and the multi-axial class is the ball and socket joint, in which a rounded head fits into a cup-shaped socket and movement is possible in every plane -- flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, rotation and circumduction. The hip joint is the standard second example of this class alongside the shoulder: the near-spherical head of the femur fits into the deep, cup-shaped acetabulum of the hip bone. The hip trades some of the shoulder's very free range of movement for a deeper socket and stronger ligaments, since it is a weight-bearing joint, but it keeps the defining multi-axial, every-plane movement of the ball and socket class.

## explicit_objective
Classify the hip joint as a multi-axial ball and socket synovial joint, alongside the shoulder.

## pitfalls
Assuming a ball and socket joint must be as freely mobile as the shoulder. The hip is built to the same multi-axial plan but is deliberately more stable and less mobile than the shoulder, because it bears the weight of the body.

## label
The epidermis is the outer, avascular epithelial layer of the skin

## canonical_key
integument.epidermis.outer-layer-of-skin

## aliases

## arabic_label
البشرة هي الطبقة الظهارية الخارجية عديمة الأوعية الدموية من الجلد

## definition
The skin has two layers. The epidermis is the outer layer, a stratified keratinised epithelium with no blood vessels of its own, so it is nourished by diffusion from the vascular layer beneath it. The dermis is the inner layer, a layer of dense irregular connective tissue that does carry blood vessels, nerves and skin appendages, and it is the dermis's vascularity that supplies the avascular epidermis sitting on top of it. Naming the epidermis correctly as "outer" rather than "inner" is the whole of what this kind of question tests, since the two layers are otherwise easy to keep straight by their very different tissue types.

## explicit_objective
Identify the epidermis as the outer layer of the skin, in contrast to the inner dermis.

## pitfalls
Reversing epidermis and dermis. The epidermis is the outer, avascular epithelial layer; the dermis is the inner, vascular connective-tissue layer that nourishes it.

## definition
Teaching on the body wall's soft-tissue coverings orders them as a numbered sequence of envelopes, from the outside in. The skin is the first envelope. The superficial fascia -- the loose, fatty layer described elsewhere in this module's own fascia teaching -- is the second envelope, lying directly deep to the skin. The deep fascia -- the non-elastic, compact collagenous membrane that forms sheets, septa, retinacula, aponeuroses and vessel sheaths -- is the third envelope, lying deep to the superficial fascia and immediately superficial to the muscles it invests.

## explicit_objective
Number the body's soft-tissue envelopes in order from the skin inward: skin first, superficial fascia second, deep fascia third.

## pitfalls
Swapping the second and third envelope. Superficial fascia, the loose fatty layer, is always the second envelope, directly under the skin; deep fascia, the tough collagenous layer, is the third, one step further in.

## label
Medial rotation is one of the named rotational movements possible at the shoulder joint

## canonical_key

## definition
The shoulder, a multi-axial ball and socket joint, permits rotation about the long axis of the humerus in addition to flexion, extension, abduction and adduction (the four movements that, combined, produce circumduction). This rotation is named by direction: medial rotation turns the anterior surface of the arm inward, toward the midline, and lateral rotation turns it outward, away from the midline. Medial and lateral rotation are therefore a distinct pair of named movements at the shoulder, separate from the four movements that combine into circumduction and separate from the forearm's own pronation and supination.

## explicit_objective
Name medial rotation as one of the shoulder joint's own rotational movements, distinct from circumduction's component movements and from forearm pronation/supination.

## pitfalls
Confusing shoulder rotation with forearm pronation and supination, or with the four movements (flexion, extension, abduction, adduction) that combine into circumduction. Medial and lateral rotation are a separate pair of movements at the shoulder itself.

## definition
Muscles are also classified by their overall shape, alongside the fibre-arrangement classification (parallel/strap, unipennate, bipennate, multipennate) taught elsewhere in this module. A fusiform muscle is spindle-shaped: it has a thick, rounded belly that tapers at both ends into a tendon, giving it a shape like a spindle rather than a flat strap or a feather. Biceps brachii, with its prominent central belly narrowing into the long and short tendons of origin proximally and the biceps tendon distally, is the standard teaching example of a fusiform muscle.

## explicit_objective
Identify biceps brachii as the type example of a fusiform (spindle-shaped) muscle.

## pitfalls
Confusing fusiform with parallel/strap-shaped. A strap muscle such as sartorius keeps a roughly uniform width along its whole length; a fusiform muscle's belly is distinctly thicker in the middle and tapers at both ends, like a spindle.

## label
An antagonist is the muscle that opposes the action of the prime mover

## canonical_key
anatomy.muscle.antagonist-opposes-prime-mover

## aliases
Antagonist muscle
Prime mover and antagonist

## arabic_label
العضلة المضادة هي العضلة التي تعاكس عمل العضلة المحركة الرئيسية

## arabic_aliases
العضلة المضادة

## definition
Muscles acting at a joint are named by their functional role in a given movement. The prime mover (agonist) is the muscle whose contraction produces the movement directly. The antagonist is the muscle on the opposite side of the joint that produces the opposite movement; it relaxes in a controlled, coordinated way as the prime mover contracts, and it is this reciprocal relaxation, not simple inactivity, that keeps the movement smooth. A fixator, by contrast, stabilises a nearby joint so the prime mover has a fixed base to pull from, which is a third and different functional role from either the prime mover or its antagonist.

## explicit_objective
Define the antagonist as the muscle that opposes the prime mover's action, and distinguish it from the prime mover (agonist) and from a fixator.

## pitfalls
Confusing the antagonist with a fixator. A fixator stabilises a different joint so the prime mover has something to pull against; the antagonist acts at the same joint as the prime mover, producing the opposite movement.

## pitfalls

## definition
The centrosome is a small, non-membrane-bound region of cytoplasm, usually located near the nucleus, that contains a pair of centrioles surrounded by pericentriolar material. It is the cell's main microtubule-organising centre: microtubules nucleate from it and radiate outward, forming the interphase cytoskeleton's radial array and, during mitosis, the two poles of the mitotic spindle that segregates the chromosomes. Because organising the microtubule network is the centrosome's defining role, distinguishing it from other cytoplasmic organelles such as the Golgi apparatus, mitochondria and lysosomes, which have their own separate functions, is what a question naming it as "the organising centre" is testing.

## explicit_objective
Identify the centrosome as the cell's own microtubule-organising centre.

## pitfalls
Confusing the centrosome with the Golgi apparatus or another organelle that also sits near the nucleus. Only the centrosome organises the microtubule network and the mitotic spindle.
