## label
The mouth is divided into the vestibule and the mouth proper

## canonical_key
mouth.regions.vestibule-and-mouth-proper

## aliases
Oral cavity regions
Vestibule of the mouth

## arabic_label
ينقسم الفم إلى الدهليز والفم الحقيقي

## arabic_aliases
دهليز الفم

## definition
The mouth (oral cavity) is divided into two regions. The vestibule is the slit-like space outside the teeth and gums and inside the lips and cheeks, while the mouth proper is the space inside the teeth and gums that extends back to the oropharyngeal isthmus, where the mouth continues into the pharynx. This two-part division is the standard description of the mouth's regions taught in gross anatomy, and both regions communicate with each other behind the last molar tooth.

## explicit_objective
Divide the mouth into its vestibule and mouth-proper regions, and state the boundary of each.

## pitfalls
Treating the mouth as a single undivided cavity, or as divided into three parts rather than the standard two (vestibule and mouth proper).

## definition
The third permanent molar, popularly called the wisdom tooth, is the last permanent tooth to erupt, typically around 17 to 21 years of age, well after the rest of the permanent dentition (32 teeth in total) is in place. Its late eruption is often crowded against the second molar, which is why it is singled out for special clinical attention among the permanent teeth. Eruption of the permanent dentition as a whole begins around 6 years of age with the first permanent molar, not with the wisdom tooth.

## explicit_objective
State that the third permanent molar (wisdom tooth) is the last permanent tooth to erupt, around 17-21 years of age.

## pitfalls
Confusing the wisdom tooth's late eruption (17-21 years) with the eruption of the permanent dentition overall, which begins around 6 years of age with the first permanent molar.

## pitfalls
Assuming the oesophagus descends through the thorax alone, or misplacing the level of the oesophageal hiatus (T10) or the organ's overall length (about 25 cm).

The stomach has two orifices, two borders (curvatures) and two surfaces. Identify the greater curvature as the stomach's longer, left, convex border, in contrast to the shorter, right, concave lesser curvature.

## definition
The interphase nucleus contains a fine, interwoven network of nuclear material called chromatin, made of DNA wound around histone proteins and visible as clumps and threads with basic (basophilic) nuclear stains. This same chromatin material condenses into the individually visible chromosomes once the cell enters mitosis, which is why chromosomes are described as being made up of chromatin. ## explicit_objective
Name chromatin as the DNA-and-histone material forming the interphase nucleus's network and the substance chromosomes are made of.

## pitfalls
Treating the interphase chromatin network and the condensed mitotic chromosome as different materials, rather than the same chromatin at different stages of coiling.

The nucleus contains histones, heterochromatin and nucleosomes; the endosome is a cytoplasmic, not nuclear, structure

## definition
The nucleus contains histones (the basic proteins DNA is wound around), heterochromatin (the condensed, inactive portion of chromatin) and nucleosomes (the DNA-wound-around-histone-octamer packaging units), all genuine components of its own chromatin. An endosome, by contrast, is a cytoplasmic membrane-bound compartment that sorts material taken in by endocytosis on its way to the lysosome or back to the plasma membrane, and so belongs to the cytoplasm rather than the nucleus. Distinguishing genuine nuclear chromatin components from cytoplasmic organelles such as the endosome is the point of grouping them this way.

## explicit_objective
List histones, heterochromatin and nucleosomes as genuine nuclear components, and distinguish them from the cytoplasmic endosome.

## pitfalls
Placing the endosome, a cytoplasmic sorting compartment for endocytosed material, among the nucleus's own chromatin components.

## pitfalls
Confusing the centromere (primary constriction, chromatid-joining and spindle-attachment site) with a secondary constriction elsewhere on the chromosome or with the telomeres at its ends.

State that germ cells alone carry the haploid chromosome number, in contrast to the diploid number of somatic cells.

## pitfalls
Assuming any specialised somatic cell type (sensory, skin, brain) might be haploid; only the mature germ cells are.

## definition
A stem cell is an undifferentiated cell that divides asymmetrically: one of its two daughter cells remains an undifferentiated stem cell, preserving the stem-cell population, while the other daughter goes on to differentiate and replace the damaged or worn-out cells of the adult tissue or organ. This self-renewing, asymmetric pattern of division is what lets a small, stable pool of stem cells continuously supply replacement cells throughout adult life without depleting itself. A stem cell is by definition undifferentiated and keeps dividing throughout life, unlike a postmitotic, already-differentiated cell.

## explicit_objective
Describe a stem cell's asymmetric division into one self-renewing daughter and one differentiating daughter, and state that stem cells are undifferentiated and not postmitotic.

## pitfalls
Describing a stem cell as already differentiated, as postmitotic, or as simply a differentiated cell capable of dividing when needed (which instead describes a progenitor cell or a stable/labile differentiated cell type).

## definition
Progenitor cells are daughter cells of stem cells that have reduced potentiality, being already committed to a single cell lineage rather than able to give rise to multiple different cell types. Unlike stem cells, which divide relatively rarely to preserve the long-term stem-cell pool, progenitor cells divide frequently, rapidly amplifying their numbers before their progeny go on to terminally differentiate. This frequent, transit-amplifying division is exactly what lets a small stem-cell pool ultimately supply large numbers of mature, differentiated cells.

## explicit_objective
State that progenitor cells, unlike stem cells, are lineage-committed and divide frequently before their progeny terminally differentiate.

## pitfalls
Assuming progenitor cells divide infrequently like stem cells; the opposite is true, as their frequent division is what rapidly amplifies cell numbers for a differentiating tissue.
