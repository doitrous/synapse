## label
Endoderm gives rise to the epithelial lining of the gut tube and its derivatives (respiratory tract, thyroid parenchyma) but not to the mammary gland

## canonical_key
embryology.germ-layers.endoderm-derivatives

## aliases
Endoderm derivatives
Germ layer derivatives
Epithelial lining of the respiratory tract

## arabic_label
مشتقات الأديم الباطن

## arabic_aliases
مشتقات الطبقة الجرثومية الباطنة

## definition
The endoderm is the innermost of the three primary germ layers, and it forms the epithelial lining of the gut tube and the epithelial component of every structure that buds from it: the epithelial lining of the respiratory tract (from the laryngotracheal groove), the parenchyma of the thyroid, parathyroid, liver and pancreas, and the epithelial lining of the urinary bladder and urethra. The mammary gland, by contrast, is an ectodermal derivative (a specialised skin appendage arising from the ectodermal mammary ridge), not an endodermal one, despite sitting alongside several endoderm-derived glands in a typical "which germ layer" exam list.

## explicit_objective
Name the endoderm as the germ layer giving rise to the gut epithelium and its budded derivatives (respiratory epithelium, thyroid parenchyma, liver, pancreas, bladder/urethral epithelium), and identify the mammary gland as an ectodermal, not endodermal, derivative.

## pitfalls
Assuming every gland near the neck or trunk midline is endodermal. The mammary gland is the classic exception tested here -- it is ectodermal, arising from the mammary ridge, not from a gut-tube outpouching.

## label
During head folding, the septum transversum, heart, prochordal plate and pericardial cavity all turn under onto the embryo's ventral surface; the notochord does not

## canonical_key
embryology.folding.head-fold-ventral-structures

## aliases
Head folding
Ventral turning of structures during folding
Cephalocaudal folding

## arabic_label
انطواء الرأس والبنى المنطوية بطنياً

## arabic_aliases
انطواء الرأس

## definition
Folding of the trilaminar embryo (cephalocaudal and lateral) converts a flat disc into a cylindrical body and carries several structures that started out on or beyond the embryo's dorsal/cranial margin around onto its ventral surface: the septum transversum (future central tendon of the diaphragm), the developing heart, the prochordal plate/oropharyngeal membrane, and the pericardial cavity all end up ventral after head folding. The notochord, by contrast, stays in its dorsal, axial position throughout folding -- it is the structure that does not turn under, since it is the fixed midline rod folding takes place around, not one of the structures being repositioned by it.

## explicit_objective
State that head folding brings the septum transversum, heart, prochordal plate and pericardial cavity onto the embryo's ventral surface, while the dorsally-fixed notochord does not turn under.

## pitfalls
Assuming the notochord, as a central embryonic landmark, must also be repositioned by folding. It is the axial structure folding occurs around, so it keeps its dorsal position rather than being carried ventrally like the other four.

## label
The cloacal membrane is derived from both ectoderm and endoderm, at the site where these two layers meet without intervening mesoderm

## canonical_key

## definition
The cloacal membrane is one of the embryo's two bilaminar membranes (with the oropharyngeal/buccopharyngeal membrane), formed where ectoderm and endoderm come into direct contact with no mesoderm intervening between them. It sits at the caudal end of the embryonic disc, closing off the cloaca, and later ruptures to establish the anal and urogenital openings. Because it is bilaminar by definition, its correct germ-layer origin is both ectoderm and endoderm together, not either layer alone.

## explicit_objective
State that the cloacal membrane is derived from both ectoderm and endoderm, as one of the embryo's two bilaminar (mesoderm-free) membranes.

## pitfalls
Naming only ectoderm or only endoderm. The defining feature of the cloacal (and oropharyngeal) membrane is that it is bilaminar -- both layers, fused with no mesoderm between them.

## label
The midgut communicates with the yolk sac through the vitelline (omphalomesenteric) duct

## canonical_key
embryology.yolk-sac.vitelline-duct-midgut

## aliases
Vitelline duct
Omphalomesenteric duct

## arabic_label
القناة المحّية والاتصال بالمعي المتوسط

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Once the embryo folds and the dorsal part of the yolk sac is incorporated into the embryo as the primitive gut tube, the midgut remains in open communication with the remaining, extra-embryonic part of the yolk sac through a narrow connecting channel: the vitelline duct (also called the omphalomesenteric duct), which runs through the umbilical cord. The duct normally obliterates later in development; its persistence is the embryological basis of Meckel's diverticulum, a true diverticulum on the antimesenteric border of the distal ileum.

## explicit_objective
Name the vitelline (omphalomesenteric) duct as the structure connecting the midgut to the yolk sac.

## pitfalls
Confusing the vitelline duct with the allantois, the other duct running through the connecting stalk/umbilical cord -- the allantois connects to the hindgut/cloacal region and contributes to bladder development, not to the midgut-yolk sac connection.

## definition
Primordial germ cells (PGCs), the precursors of the gametes, first become identifiable in the wall of the yolk sac near the origin of the allantois, before migrating along the dorsal mesentery of the hindgut to reach the developing gonadal ridges. ## explicit_objective
State that primordial germ cells arise in the wall of the yolk sac before migrating to the gonadal ridges.

## pitfalls
Assuming PGCs arise directly in the gonadal ridge because that is where they end up. The gonadal ridge is their destination after migration, not their site of origin.

## label
An embryo with a crown-rump length (CRL) of 5-8 mm is approximately 5 weeks old, and embryonic age within the first month is expressed by somite count

## canonical_key

## definition
Two complementary methods date an early embryo. Crown-rump length (CRL), the greatest length measured from the crown (top of the head) to the rump (buttocks), tracks age through the embryonic and early fetal period; a CRL of 5-8 mm corresponds to an embryo of approximately 5 weeks. Before CRL becomes practical, and specifically during the first month, embryonic age is instead expressed by counting somites -- the paired blocks of paraxial mesoderm that appear in a predictable, near-daily sequence and give a more precise age estimate than external form alone at that very early stage.

## explicit_objective
State that a CRL of 5-8 mm corresponds to an embryo of about 5 weeks, and that embryonic age during the first month is expressed by somite count rather than CRL.

## pitfalls
Assuming CRL is used to age the embryo from the very earliest stages. Somite counting is the first-month method; CRL becomes the standard once the embryo is large enough to measure reliably.

## id

## label
The fetal period runs from the beginning of the ninth week to birth, and fetal length is indicated by crown-rump length (CRL)

## canonical_key
embryology.fetal-period.definition-and-crl

## aliases
Fetal period definition
Crown-rump length in the fetal period

## arabic_label
تعريف الفترة الجنينية وطول الجالس

## arabic_aliases
الفترة الجنينية

## definition
Development is divided into the embryonic period (fertilisation to the end of the eighth week, when the basic body plan and organ systems are laid down) and the fetal period (from the beginning of the ninth week until birth, dominated by growth and maturation of the already-established structures). Throughout the fetal period, fetal size is conventionally expressed as crown-rump length (CRL), the same measurement used for aging embryos, rather than by femur length, head circumference or somite count, which are either used for other specific purposes or (for somites) no longer present as discrete countable structures by this stage.

## explicit_objective

## definition
Cri du chat ("cry of the cat") syndrome is caused by a partial deletion of the short arm of chromosome 5 (5p-), producing intellectual disability, microcephaly, characteristic facial features and, in infancy, a distinctive high-pitched, cat-like cry that gives the syndrome its name. It is a structural chromosomal aberration (a deletion), not caused by absence of an X chromosome, non-disjunction of chromosome 19, or an extra copy of chromosome 21 -- each of those describes the mechanism of a different, unrelated syndrome (Turner syndrome, no standard syndrome, and Down syndrome, respectively).

## explicit_objective
State that cri du chat syndrome results from a partial deletion of chromosome 5.

## pitfalls
Confusing cri du chat's deletion mechanism with the numerical chromosomal abnormalities (monosomy, trisomy) tested alongside it in the same question set -- cri du chat is specifically a structural deletion, not a change in chromosome number.
