## label
A clone is a population of genetically identical cells all derived from a single parent cell

## canonical_key
teaching.microbiology.bacterial-genetics.clone-definition

## aliases
Clonal population
Monoclonal population
Population derived from a single cell

## arabic_label
المستنسخ هو مجموعة خلايا مشتقة من خلية واحدة

## arabic_aliases
مجموعة خلوية أحادية النسيلة

## definition
A clone is the population of cells produced when a single bacterial (or other) cell divides repeatedly -- every member is genetically identical to the original parent cell, since ordinary binary fission is not a source of new genetic variation. "Monoclonal" is the adjective used for something derived from, or specific to, one such clone (e.g. a monoclonal antibody comes from one clone of antibody-producing cells), while "clone" itself names the population. A protoplast (a bacterium stripped of its cell wall) and a subculture (cells transferred to fresh medium) are unrelated concepts that do not describe this single-ancestor relationship.

## explicit_objective
Identify "clone" as the term for a population of cells all derived from one parent cell, and distinguish it from protoplast (wall-less cell) and subculture (a transfer to new medium).

## pitfalls
Choosing "monoclonal cells" instead of "clones" as the name for the population itself -- monoclonal is the adjective describing derivation from a single clone, not the standard noun for the population.

## label
Gram-negative bacteria stain pink/red (safranin counterstain); Gram-positive bacteria stain violet/purple (crystal violet retained)

## canonical_key
teaching.microbiology.gram-stain.color-by-cell-wall-type

## aliases
Gram stain colour result
Crystal violet retention
Safranin counterstain

## arabic_label
لون صبغة الجرام حسب نوع الجدار الخلوي

## arabic_aliases
احتباس صبغة الكريستال البنفسجي

## definition
The Gram stain's final colour depends on whether a cell retains the primary stain (crystal violet-iodine complex) through the decolourisation step. Gram-positive bacteria have a thick peptidoglycan wall that traps the crystal violet-iodine complex, so they resist decolourisation and appear violet/purple. Gram-negative bacteria have a thin peptidoglycan layer and an outer lipid membrane that the decolouriser (alcohol/acetone) disrupts, so the complex washes out and the cell instead takes up the safranin counterstain, appearing pink/red. The colour result is therefore a direct readout of cell-wall thickness and structure, not an arbitrary label.

## explicit_objective
State the Gram-stain colour each cell-wall type produces (Gram-positive violet/purple via retained crystal violet; Gram-negative pink/red via the safranin counterstain) and explain why wall thickness determines the result.

## pitfalls
Reversing the two colours, or treating the colour as a memorised label rather than a consequence of whether the thick (Gram-positive) or thin (Gram-negative) peptidoglycan layer retains the primary stain.

A prokaryotic cell has no membrane-bound nucleus or organelles and stores its DNA as a single circular chromosome in the nucleoid; eukaryotic genetic material is instead enclosed in a true nucleus

## canonical_key
teaching.microbiology.cell-biology.prokaryote-vs-eukaryote-genetic-material

## aliases
Prokaryote defining features
Nucleoid vs nucleus

## arabic_label
الفرق بين الخلية بدائية النواة وحقيقية النواة في تخزين المادة الوراثية

## arabic_aliases

## definition
A prokaryotic cell (bacterium) lacks a true, membrane-bound nucleus and lacks membrane-bound organelles (mitochondria, ER, Golgi); its genetic material is instead a single circular chromosome held in an unbound cytoplasmic region called the nucleoid. It divides by binary fission, not mitosis, and its flagella/cilia (where present) are structurally simpler than the eukaryotic cytoskeletal versions. A eukaryotic cell, by contrast, encloses its genetic material inside a true nucleus bounded by a nuclear envelope -- the general answer to "where is a cell's genetic information stored" among nucleus/ER/Golgi/lysosome options is the nucleus, since none of the other three organelles store DNA.

## explicit_objective
List the defining differences between a prokaryotic and a eukaryotic cell (no true nucleus, no membrane-bound organelles, single circular chromosome in a nucleoid, binary fission, simpler flagella/cilia), and identify the nucleus as the organelle that stores a eukaryotic cell's genetic material.

## pitfalls
Assuming "no genetic material" describes a prokaryote (it has DNA, just not enclosed in a nucleus) or assuming a prokaryote has some organelles (mitochondria, an organized nucleus) that it in fact lacks entirely.

Bacterial flagellar arrangement is named by position: one flagellum at one pole (monotrichous), one at each pole (amphitrichous), a tuft at one pole (lophotrichous), or flagella all around the cell (peritrichous)

## definition
Bacterial flagellar arrangement is classified purely by where the flagella sit on the cell. Monotrichous means a single flagellum at one pole. Amphitrichous means a single flagellum at each of the two poles. Lophotrichous means a tuft (group) of several flagella at one pole. Peritrichous means flagella distributed all around the cell surface, not confined to a pole. These four terms describe position and number only -- they say nothing about motility speed or pathogenicity by themselves.

## explicit_objective
Given a description of where flagella are located on a bacterial cell (one pole, both poles, a tuft at one pole, or all around), name the matching arrangement term (monotrichous, amphitrichous, lophotrichous, peritrichous).

## pitfalls
Confusing "amphitrichous" (one flagellum at each of two poles) with "lophotrichous" (a tuft/group of flagella at one single pole) -- the distinguishing facts are number-per-pole and how many poles are involved, not overall flagellar count.

The plasma membrane is the cell's selectively permeable barrier, controlling what enters and leaves

## canonical_key
teaching.microbiology.plasma-membrane.selective-barrier-function

## aliases

## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases

## definition
The plasma (cytoplasmic) membrane is the structure that controls the passage of molecules into and out of the cell -- a selectively permeable barrier, not an absolute one, and not a rigid structure (it is a fluid phospholipid bilayer). In a bacterium it sits internal to the cell wall, so it is not the cell's outermost structure; that distinction belongs to the cell wall (or capsule, where present). This selective-barrier role is distinct from the cell wall's own role of giving the cell shape and rigidity.

## explicit_objective
State that the plasma membrane's defining function is controlling molecular traffic into and out of the cell (selective permeability), and distinguish this from the cell wall's shape/rigidity role and from the false idea that the membrane is rigid or the cell's outermost layer.

## pitfalls
Assuming the plasma membrane is the bacterial cell's outermost structure (the cell wall is) or that it is rigid (it is a fluid bilayer) -- both are the wrong-option traps this fact is tested against.

## label
Bacterial virulence factors include the capsule, pili, extracellular enzymes and endotoxin -- not storage structures such as inclusion granules

## canonical_key
teaching.microbiology.pathogenesis.virulence-factors-overview

## aliases

Factors of pathogenicity

## arabic_label
عوامل الضراوة البكتيرية

## arabic_aliases

## definition
A bacterium's virulence factors are the structures and products that let it establish infection and cause disease: the antiphagocytic capsule, pili/fimbriae (adherence), secreted extracellular enzymes (tissue invasion) and endotoxin (LPS-driven inflammatory/septic effects in Gram-negative organisms) are all standard examples. Inclusion granules (intracellular storage deposits of nutrients such as glycogen or polyphosphate) are a bacterial structure but not a virulence factor -- they store reserves for the organism's own metabolism and play no direct role in causing host damage or evading host defence.

## explicit_objective
List the standard bacterial virulence factors (capsule, pili, extracellular enzymes, endotoxin) and exclude inclusion granules, a storage structure with no pathogenic role.

## pitfalls
Assuming every named bacterial structure is automatically a virulence factor -- inclusion granules exist for the organism's own nutrient storage, not for attacking or evading the host, which is the distinguishing fact this record isolates.
