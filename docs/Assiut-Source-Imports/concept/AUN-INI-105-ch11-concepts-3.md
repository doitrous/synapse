## label
Trichinella spiralis: acquired from undercooked pork, infective stage is the encysted larva, newborn larvae are carried to striated muscle

## canonical_key
trichinella-spiralis.life-cycle.pork-encysted-larva-striated-muscle

## aliases
Trichinella spiralis life cycle
Trichinosis transmission and muscle invasion

## arabic_label
دورة حياة الترايكينيلا سبيراليس

## arabic_aliases


## definition
Man is infected with Trichinella spiralis by eating undercooked pork containing encysted larvae in the animal's striated muscle; the encysted larva is the infective stage swallowed. In the human small intestine the larva matures to an adult, and the female releases newborn larvae directly (Trichinella is viviparous) that penetrate the gut wall, enter the bloodstream and are carried throughout the body, finally encysting selectively in active striated (skeletal) muscle -- not liver, lung or the intestinal lumen -- where they can persist and eventually calcify.

## explicit_objective
State that Trichinella spiralis is acquired from undercooked pork, that the encysted larva is the infective stage, and that newborn larvae are carried to striated muscle.

## pitfalls
Confusing Trichinella's pork-borne transmission with the fish-borne transmission of Capillaria philippinensis or Heterophyes heterophyes; assuming newborn larvae lodge in the liver or lung rather than striated muscle, their actual final destination.

## definition
The egg of Capillaria philippinensis is peanut- (barrel-)shaped with flattened, slightly protruding bipolar plugs resembling opercula at each end, and is immature (unembryonated) when passed in stool -- these three descriptive facts are jointly true and often combined into one "all of the above" exam item testing egg recognition, distinguishing it from the smoothly barrel-shaped, non-operculated Trichuris egg.

## explicit_objective
State that the Capillaria philippinensis egg is peanut-shaped, has flattened bipolar plugs, and is immature when passed.

## pitfalls
Confusing the Capillaria philippinensis egg's flattened, operculum-like bipolar plugs with the smoothly rounded bipolar mucoid plugs of the similarly barrel-shaped Trichuris trichiura egg.

## label
Dracunculus medinensis inhabits subcutaneous tissue, uses Cyclops as intermediate host, and is not treated with niclosamide

## canonical_key
dracunculus-medinensis.habitat-vector-and-treatment.subcutaneous-cyclops-not-niclosamide

## aliases
Guinea worm biology
Dracunculiasis habitat intermediate host treatment

## arabic_label
موطن دودة غينيا وعائلها الوسيط وعلاجها

## arabic_aliases


## definition
The adult Dracunculus medinensis (guinea worm) inhabits subcutaneous tissue, most classically of the lower limb, where the gravid female eventually forms a painful blister that ruptures on contact with water to release larvae. Its intermediate host is the copepod Cyclops, ingested in contaminated drinking water. Historical drug treatment for dracunculiasis has included metronidazole, diethylcarbamazine and thiabendazole (mainly as adjuncts to slow, gradual mechanical extraction of the worm), but not niclosamide, which is instead an anticestode (tapeworm) drug with no role against this nematode.

## explicit_objective
State that Dracunculus medinensis lives in subcutaneous tissue, uses Cyclops as its intermediate host, and is not treated with niclosamide.

## pitfalls
Assuming niclosamide, a standard antihelminthic, works against every helminth; it is specifically an anticestode drug and has no activity against the nematode Dracunculus medinensis.

## label
The infective stage of Wuchereria bancrofti is the filariform larva found in the labium (proboscis) of the mosquito vector

## canonical_key
wuchereria-bancrofti.infective-stage.filariform-larva-in-mosquito-labium

## aliases

## arabic_label
الطور المعدي لدودة الفخريا في خرطوم البعوضة

## arabic_aliases


## definition
The infective stage of Wuchereria bancrofti is the third-stage (filariform) larva, which develops from the ingested microfilaria within the mosquito's thoracic flight muscles and then migrates forward to the labium (the sheath around the mosquito's proboscis), from where it is deposited onto the skin at the next blood meal and actively penetrates the bite wound -- not injected directly like a virus, and not the circulating blood-stage microfilaria itself, which is the diagnostic, not the infective, stage in man.

## explicit_objective
State that the infective stage of Wuchereria bancrofti is the filariform larva found in the mosquito's labium, ready for transmission at the next bite.

## pitfalls
Confusing the circulating microfilaria (the diagnostic stage found in human blood) with the filariform larva (the infective stage found in the mosquito vector, ready for transmission).

## label
The microfilaria of Wuchereria bancrofti is sheathed, has a nucleus-free posterior end, shows nocturnal periodicity, and is ingested by Culex, not Simulium

## canonical_key
wuchereria-bancrofti.microfilaria-morphology.sheathed-nucleus-free-tip-periodic-culex-vector

## aliases
Wuchereria bancrofti microfilaria description
Microfilaria morphology and periodicity

## arabic_label
صفات المتَيَقة الدقيقة لدودة الفخريا

## arabic_aliases


## definition
The microfilaria of Wuchereria bancrofti is sheathed (it retains the egg-shell membrane as a loose covering), and its posterior end is tapered and free of nuclei -- the opposite of the "posterior end full of nuclei" distractor sometimes offered. It circulates with nocturnal periodicity (most numerous in peripheral blood between roughly 10 pm and 2 am, matching the night-biting habit of its vector), and it is ingested during a blood meal by Culex mosquitoes, not by Simulium (the Onchocerca volvulus vector, which does not transmit W. bancrofti).

## explicit_objective
State that the W. bancrofti microfilaria is sheathed, nucleus-free at the posterior tip, nocturnally periodic, and taken up by Culex rather than Simulium.

## pitfalls
Assuming the microfilaria's posterior end is "full of nuclei" (that describes a different microfilarial species pattern, not W. bancrofti); assuming it is aperiodic or that Simulium, not Culex, is its vector.

The microfilaria of Wuchereria bancrofti differs from that of Loa loa by a loose sheath, gentle body curves, and a posterior end free of nuclei -- all true

wuchereria-bancrofti.microfilaria-vs-loa-loa.loose-sheath-gentle-curves-nucleus-free-tip

## arabic_label
اختلاف متيقة الفخريا عن متيقة لوا لوا

## definition
The microfilaria of Wuchereria bancrofti differs from that of Loa loa in three linked morphological features, often combined into one "all of the above" exam item: W. bancrofti's sheath is loose (versus Loa loa's tightly fitting sheath); its body shows gentle, smooth curves (versus Loa loa's kinky, irregular curves); and its posterior end is free of nuclei (versus Loa loa's nuclei extending to the very tip of the tail) -- a standard three-point comparison used to differentiate the two filarial species on a blood film.

## explicit_objective
State that W. bancrofti microfilaria differs from Loa loa's by a loose sheath, gentle body curves and a nucleus-free posterior tip.

## pitfalls
Reversing the sheath tightness, curve pattern or posterior-nuclei assignment between the two species; all three features move together as one consistent comparison.

## id
CON-INF-1CE00268409419

## label
Onchocerca volvulus microfilariae are the exception among filariae in not showing blood periodicity, since they reside in skin, not blood

## canonical_key
onchocerca-volvulus.microfilaria.no-blood-periodicity-skin-dwelling

## aliases
Onchocerca volvulus periodicity exception
Non-periodic microfilaria

## arabic_label
عدم وجود دورية دموية لمتيقة الأونكوسركا فولفولوس

## arabic_aliases


## definition
Wuchereria bancrofti, Brugia malayi and Loa loa microfilariae all circulate in peripheral blood with a defined periodicity (nocturnal for W. bancrofti and Brugia malayi, diurnal for Loa loa), timed to match each species' respective biting-vector activity. Onchocerca volvulus microfilariae are the exception: they live in the skin and subcutaneous tissue rather than in the bloodstream, so they show no blood periodicity at all -- a fact that also explains why Onchocerca is diagnosed by skin snip, not by a timed blood film.

## explicit_objective
Identify Onchocerca volvulus as the exception among filariae without blood periodicity, since its microfilariae reside in skin rather than blood.

## pitfalls
Assuming every filarial species shows a timed blood periodicity; Onchocerca volvulus's skin-dwelling microfilariae are the standard exception, which is also why skin snip (not blood film) is its diagnostic sample.
