## label
Most amino acids classed as nonessential become conditionally essential under stress -- growth, surgical recovery, or severe illness -- and arginine is the textbook example

## canonical_key
teaching.biochemistry.protein.conditionally-essential-amino-acid

## aliases

## arabic_label

## arabic_aliases
الأرجينين

## definition
An amino acid is classed as nutritionally essential if the body cannot synthesise it fast enough to meet demand and it must come from the diet, and nonessential if the body's own biosynthetic pathways normally keep pace. Several amino acids that are ordinarily nonessential -- arginine foremost among them -- become "conditionally essential" when demand rises sharply: during rapid growth (infancy, childhood), pregnancy, or the catabolic stress of surgery, trauma or severe illness, the body's own synthetic capacity for arginine cannot keep pace with the increased need (arginine feeds directly into protein synthesis, the urea cycle, nitric oxide production and wound healing), so dietary/supplemental arginine becomes necessary the way a true essential amino acid's intake always is.

## explicit_objective
Identify arginine as the textbook example of a conditionally essential amino acid, one normally classed as nonessential that must be supplied by diet during growth or catabolic stress.

## pitfalls
Treating "nonessential" as an absolute, fixed label. The essential/nonessential distinction is a statement about ordinary demand, and several nonessential amino acids (arginine is the one this question bank tests) cross into functionally essential territory whenever synthesis cannot keep up with a sharply raised demand.

## label
An alpha-helix is held together by hydrogen bonds between the backbone carbonyl oxygen of one peptide bond and the backbone amide hydrogen of another peptide bond further along the same chain

## canonical_key
teaching.biochemistry.protein.alpha-helix-hydrogen-bonding

## aliases
Alpha helix
Secondary structure hydrogen bonding
Backbone hydrogen bonds
Protein secondary structure

## arabic_label
اللولب الألفا والروابط الهيدروجينية في العمود الفقري للبروتين

## arabic_aliases
الروابط الهيدروجينية للعمود الفقري الببتيدي

## definition
Protein secondary structure -- the alpha-helix and the beta-pleated sheet alike -- is stabilised by hydrogen bonds between backbone (main-chain) atoms, not side-chain atoms and not bonds within a single peptide bond. In the alpha-helix specifically, each hydrogen bond forms between the carbonyl oxygen atom of one peptide bond and the amide (N-H) hydrogen of a peptide bond that lies about four residues further along the same polypeptide chain, and the repetition of this bonding pattern along the chain's whole length is what coils it into the helix's regular, rod-like shape. This is a fixed, sequence-independent geometry (any residue's backbone carbonyl and amide groups can form it), which is why the alpha-helix does not require any particular amino acid composition, only that residues bulky enough or rigid enough (proline, with no free amide hydrogen; glycine, too flexible) not to be present in excess.

## explicit_objective
State that the alpha-helix's hydrogen bonds run between the backbone carbonyl oxygen of one peptide bond and the backbone amide hydrogen of a different peptide bond, not between side chains and not within a single peptide bond.

## pitfalls
Locating the alpha-helix's hydrogen bonds within a single peptide bond, or between two adjacent amino acids in the primary sequence. The bonding partners are backbone atoms from peptide bonds separated by about four residues along the chain, not neighbouring residues and not a single bond's own two atoms.

## definition
Two of the twenty standard amino acids carry sulphur in their side chain: methionine, whose side chain ends in a thioether (-S-CH3), and cysteine, whose side chain ends in a free thiol (-SH) that lets two cysteine residues form a disulphide bond. Methionine has two further roles distinct from cysteine's: as N-formylmethionine (in bacteria) or methionine itself (in eukaryotes), it is the initiating amino acid of every new polypeptide chain, and once activated to S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) it is the cell's principal methyl-group donor, methylating DNA, proteins, phospholipids and small molecules such as noradrenaline in its conversion to adrenaline.

## explicit_objective
Identify methionine as a sulphur-containing amino acid, alongside cysteine, distinguishing it from the other listed amino acids by its side-chain sulphur.

## pitfalls
Forgetting that sulphur content is a side-chain property shared by exactly two amino acids (methionine, cysteine) and confusing it with unrelated classifications such as aromaticity (tryptophan, in the same option list, is aromatic and heterocyclic, not sulphur-containing) or basicity (histidine is a basic, imidazole-containing amino acid, again not sulphur-containing).

## aliases

## definition
Amyloid is the name given to abnormal, insoluble protein deposits found in a range of unrelated diseases -- Alzheimer's disease (neuritic plaques and neurofibrillary tangles), multiple myeloma and other causes of systemic amyloidosis (immunoglobulin light-chain deposits), and several other conditions -- each with a chemically different precursor protein. What unites them structurally, regardless of the precursor, is that the deposited protein has misfolded into a high content of beta-pleated sheet secondary structure, in place of its normal fold; this is what gives amyloid deposits their characteristic birefringent staining with Congo red and their resistance to normal proteolytic clearance.

## explicit_objective
State that amyloid deposits, whatever disease or precursor protein produces them, share a high content of beta-pleated sheet as their common secondary structure.

## pitfalls
Assuming each amyloid-forming disease has its own distinct structural signature. The precursor protein differs by disease (tau/amyloid-beta in Alzheimer's, immunoglobulin light chains in myeloma-associated amyloidosis, and others elsewhere), but the misfolded secondary structure that actually defines "amyloid" as a category is the same beta-pleated sheet in every case.

## label
The ubiquitin-proteasome pathway degrades proteins the cell has tagged with polyubiquitin chains; the proteasome inhibitor bortezomib blocks this route specifically, sparing proteins degraded by other mechanisms

## canonical_key
teaching.biochemistry.protein.ubiquitin-proteasome-degradation

## aliases

## arabic_label
مسار اليوبيكويتين والبروتيازوم لتحليل البروتينات

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Intracellular proteins destined for controlled degradation are first tagged with a chain of several ubiquitin molecules (polyubiquitination), a small-protein label the cell's ubiquitin-conjugating machinery attaches covalently to the target. The 26S proteasome, a large multi-subunit protease complex, recognises this polyubiquitin tag specifically and unfolds and degrades the tagged protein into short peptides, in an ATP-dependent process. Bortezomib is a drug that inhibits the proteasome itself, blocking degradation of polyubiquitinated proteins -- which is exploited therapeutically in multiple myeloma, where malignant plasma cells are especially dependent on proteasome activity to clear the large burden of misfolded immunoglobulin they produce. Because bortezomib acts specifically on the proteasome's own catalytic activity, proteins that are degraded by routes other than polyubiquitination and the proteasome (for example, most extracellular or lysosomally degraded proteins, and non-ubiquitinated intracellular proteins) are not specifically affected by it.

## explicit_objective
State that bortezomib inhibits the proteasome and therefore specifically blocks degradation of polyubiquitinated proteins, while proteins degraded by other routes are not specifically affected.

## pitfalls
Assuming a proteasome inhibitor blocks all intracellular protein degradation indiscriminately. Bortezomib's action is specific to the ubiquitin-proteasome route; a protein class not marked for degradation by polyubiquitination in the first place is not the class whose clearance this drug specifically prevents.

## label
Most acute-phase reactants (CRP, haptoglobin, transferrin's usual pairing, alpha-1 antitrypsin) rise in inflammation, but albumin is a negative acute-phase reactant and falls

## canonical_key

## arabic_label
بروتينات الطور الحاد الإيجابية والسلبية

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Acute-phase reactants are plasma proteins whose concentration changes characteristically during the systemic inflammatory response, chiefly under the drive of interleukin-6 acting on hepatocytes. Most are positive acute-phase reactants, rising during inflammation -- C-reactive protein (CRP), haptoglobin and alpha-1 antitrypsin among them, each supporting host defence or tissue repair in its own way. Albumin, by contrast, is a negative acute-phase reactant: its hepatic synthesis is down-regulated during the acute-phase response (partly to free up amino-acid and synthetic capacity for the positive reactants), so its plasma concentration falls rather than rises, which is why a low albumin in a systemically unwell patient is not automatically read as chronic malnutrition or liver failure without considering acute inflammation as a cause.

## explicit_objective
Identify albumin as a negative acute-phase reactant (falls in inflammation), in contrast to CRP, haptoglobin and alpha-1 antitrypsin, which are positive acute-phase reactants that rise.

## pitfalls
Assuming every major plasma protein behaves the same way during inflammation. Albumin's fall is a specific, clinically important exception to the general "acute-phase reactants rise" pattern, driven by hepatic synthesis being redirected toward the positive reactants.

## label
Denaturation of a protein decreases its solubility and biological activity but increases its viscosity, the reverse of what an intuitive guess based on "unfolding" might suggest

## canonical_key
teaching.biochemistry.protein.denaturation-effects

## aliases
Protein denaturation
Denaturation and viscosity

## arabic_label
تمسخ البروتين وتأثيره على اللزوجة والذوبانية

## arabic_aliases
تمسخ البروتين

## definition
Denaturation is the loss of a protein's native three-dimensional folded structure (secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure) without breaking its primary peptide-bond sequence, typically from heat, extremes of pH, or chaotropic agents. Several of its effects on measurable protein properties are: solubility decreases, because hydrophobic residues normally buried in the folded interior become exposed and drive aggregation; diffusibility decreases, for the same aggregation-related reason; and biological activity (enzymatic, hormonal, binding) is lost, because activity depends on the precise folded shape. Viscosity, however, increases on denaturation, not decreases -- an unfolded or aggregated polypeptide occupies a larger, more irregular hydrodynamic volume and disrupts solvent flow more than the compact native fold did, the opposite of what an intuitive guess based on "the protein has come apart" might suggest.

## explicit_objective
State that protein denaturation increases viscosity, in contrast to its other characteristic effects (decreased solubility, decreased diffusibility, decreased biological activity).

## pitfalls
Assuming every physical property changes in the "expected" direction of decline once a protein denatures. Viscosity is the counter-intuitive exception among this list of properties: it rises, because the unfolded/aggregated molecule takes up more effective volume, while solubility, diffusibility and biological activity all fall.

## label
The endoplasmic reticulum synthesises proteins (rough ER) and lipids (smooth ER); the Golgi body then packages these products and exports them out of the cell

## canonical_key
teaching.cellbiology.organelles.er-golgi-function

## aliases
Endoplasmic reticulum function
Golgi body function
Golgi apparatus
Protein synthesis and export pathway

## arabic_label
وظيفة الشبكة الإندوبلازمية وجهاز جولجي

## arabic_aliases
الشبكة الإندوبلازمية
جهاز جولجي

## definition
The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is a membranous network continuous with the nuclear envelope, divided functionally into rough ER, studded with ribosomes and specialised for synthesising and initially folding secreted and membrane proteins, and smooth ER, specialised for lipid synthesis and (in some cells) detoxification. Proteins synthesised in the rough ER are packaged into transport vesicles and sent to the Golgi body (Golgi apparatus), a stack of flattened membranous sacs that further modifies these proteins (glycosylation, proteolytic processing), sorts them, and packages them into vesicles for export -- either secretion out of the cell, or delivery to the plasma membrane or other organelles such as lysosomes. The two organelles thus work in a fixed sequence: the ER synthesises, the Golgi packages and directs traffic onward.

## definition

## definition
Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is built around a corrin ring, a macrocyclic structure related to the porphyrin ring of haem but with one fewer carbon bridge, whose centre coordinates a cobalt atom -- the metal that gives cobalamin its name and that is essential to both of its coenzyme forms (methylcobalamin, adenosylcobalamin). Vitamin B12 deficiency produces a megaloblastic anaemia; when the specific cause is loss of intrinsic factor (needed for B12 absorption in the terminal ileum) rather than simple dietary lack, this clinical picture is given the specific name pernicious anaemia. Because methylmalonyl-CoA mutase is one of only two human enzymes that require a B12 coenzyme (adenosylcobalamin), B12 deficiency causes methylmalonyl-CoA to accumulate and be converted to methylmalonic acid, so a raised urinary (or serum) methylmalonate is a specific diagnostic marker of B12 deficiency, one that folate deficiency (which produces an identical blood picture) does not share. Folate (folic acid) is structurally a member of the same B-vitamin, single-carbon-metabolism family as B12 in function, but is built differently: a pteridine ring joined to para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA), which is in turn joined to one or more glutamate residues -- the pteridine-PABA-glutamate combination is folate's defining structure.

## explicit_objective
State that vitamin B12's corrin ring is centred on cobalt, that B12-deficiency anaemia due to lost intrinsic factor is specifically termed pernicious anaemia, that raised urinary methylmalonate specifically marks B12 (not folate) deficiency, and that folate is built from a pteridine base, PABA and glutamate.

## pitfalls
Naming iron, rather than cobalt, as the metal at the centre of B12's corrin ring -- that confuses corrin with the structurally related but iron-centred haem/porphyrin ring. Using "pernicious anaemia" as a synonym for any B12 deficiency rather than specifically the intrinsic-factor-loss cause. Treating methylmalonate as a marker of general megaloblastic anaemia rather than specifically B12 deficiency, since folate deficiency does not raise it.
