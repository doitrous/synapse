## label
Taenia saginata's scolex is unarmed and man is never its intermediate host

## canonical_key
taenia-saginata.host-specificity-and-scolex

## aliases
Taenia saginata scolex
Beef tapeworm host specificity

## arabic_label
التينيا ساجيناتا: الرأس غير المسلح ونوعية العائل

## arabic_aliases
دودة الشريط البقرية

## definition
Taenia saginata (the beef tapeworm) belongs to the order Cyclophyllidea and inhabits the small intestine of its only definitive host, man -- never the dog, which is the definitive host for other cestodes such as Echinococcus granulosus. Its scolex is globular with four suckers but, unlike Taenia solium's armed scolex, carries no hooks. Cattle, not pigs, serve as its sole intermediate host, and man cannot act as an intermediate host for T. saginata: ingesting its eggs produces no infection at all, because human tissue does not support the parasite's oncosphere the way it does for T. solium.

## explicit_objective
State that Taenia saginata's scolex is unarmed, that cattle (not pigs) are its sole intermediate host, and that man cannot act as its intermediate host even after ingesting its eggs.

## pitfalls
Assuming T. saginata behaves like T. solium and can cause cysticercosis in man from ingested eggs (it cannot -- man is exclusively its definitive host); confusing its unarmed, hookless scolex with T. solium's armed, hooked one; assigning pig instead of cattle as its intermediate host.

## label
Taenia solium uniquely serves as both definitive and intermediate host in man

## canonical_key

## definition
Taenia solium (the pork tapeworm) uses the pig as its intermediate host, where ingested eggs develop into the larval cysticercus cellulosae in muscle tissue. Man acquires taeniasis, the adult intestinal worm, by eating undercooked pork containing cysticercus cellulosae -- the parasite's human-infective stage for taeniasis -- and is thereby its definitive host. Uniquely among the human cestodes, man can also act as T. solium's intermediate host, so that a single person may carry both the adult worm in the intestine and larval cysts in tissue.

## explicit_objective
State that Taenia solium's intermediate host is the pig, that its human-infective larval stage for taeniasis is cysticercus cellulosae acquired by eating undercooked pork, and that man is unusual in being able to serve as both its definitive and intermediate host.

## pitfalls
Naming cysticercus bovis instead of cysticercus cellulosae as T. solium's infective larval stage (bovis belongs to T. saginata); confusing which meal (raw pork vs. contaminated vegetables/water) produces taeniasis versus cysticercosis; forgetting that man is one of the few cestode hosts able to serve as both definitive and intermediate host for the same species.

Diphyllobothrium latum's life cycle runs coracidium to procercoid in Cyclops to plerocercoid in fish

## arabic_label
دورة حياة الدفيلوبوثريوم لاتوم

## arabic_aliases

## definition
Diphyllobothrium latum, the fish tapeworm and a pseudophyllidean cestode, has a three-stage life cycle spanning two intermediate hosts: its egg hatches in water to release a free-swimming coracidium, which is ingested by a first intermediate host, the copepod Cyclops, and develops there into a procercoid larva; when the infected Cyclops is eaten by a second intermediate host, a fresh-water fish, the procercoid develops further into the plerocercoid larva, the stage infective to man and to other fish-eating mammals such as dogs, which act as reservoir hosts. Cyclops, in the same way, is also the sole intermediate host of the unrelated Dracunculus medinensis (Guinea worm) and the first intermediate host of Diphyllobothrium mansoni, which goes on to use a second vertebrate host as well.

## explicit_objective
Sequence Diphyllobothrium latum's life cycle -- coracidium in water, procercoid in Cyclops, plerocercoid in fresh-water fish -- and state that Cyclops also acts as an intermediate host for Dracunculus medinensis and Diphyllobothrium mansoni, and that fish-eating mammals such as dogs are D. latum's reservoir hosts.

## pitfalls
Reversing the order of D. latum's larval stages (coracidium in water, procercoid in Cyclops, plerocercoid in fish); assuming Cyclops alone is sufficient to complete D. latum's cycle, when a second, fish, intermediate host is also required; classifying D. latum as cyclophyllidean instead of pseudophyllidean.

## label
Man is only an accidental intermediate host for Echinococcus granulosus, which never lives as an adult in man

## canonical_key
echinococcus-granulosus.tissue-distribution-and-mans-accidental-host-role

## aliases
Echinococcus granulosus hydatid cyst
Hydatid disease liver
Man accidental intermediate host

## arabic_label
الإكينوكوكس جرانيولوسس: توزيع الكيسة العدارية ودور الإنسان العرضي

## arabic_aliases
مرض الكيسة العدارية

## definition
Echinococcus granulosus's adult tapeworm lives in the intestine of the dog, its definitive host, which is why the species is excluded whenever a question asks which cestodes are found as adults in man's own intestine -- unlike Taenia and Hymenolepis species, man never harbours the adult E. granulosus worm. Man becomes infected instead by ingesting the parasite's eggs, shed in dog faeces and contaminating food or water, never by eating a hydatid cyst itself; the ingested egg's oncosphere then forms a hydatid cyst in human tissue, most frequently in the liver, followed by the lung. In this role man is only ever an accidental, dead-end intermediate host for the tissue (hydatid) form of the disease.

## explicit_objective
State that Echinococcus granulosus's adult worm lives only in the dog, that man is infected by ingesting its eggs (not by eating a cyst) and becomes only an accidental intermediate host, and that the liver is the most frequent hydatid cyst site.

## pitfalls
Listing Echinococcus granulosus among the intestinal cestodes of man, when its adult lives only in the dog; assuming hydatid infection follows eating an existing cyst rather than ingesting eggs; forgetting that the liver, not the lung, is the most frequent hydatid cyst site.

## definition
Cestodes (tapeworms) are segmented, hermaphroditic flatworms -- distinct from the leaf-shaped, unsegmented trematodes and the cylindrical, unsegmented nematodes -- whose body is divided into a head, the scolex, used for attachment, followed by a chain of proglottid segments. The intestinal cestodes of man are found in the small intestine, in the jejunum and ileum, rather than the large intestine or appendix.

## explicit_objective
Describe cestodes as segmented, hermaphroditic flatworms with a scolex head, and state that the intestinal cestodes of man inhabit the small intestine (jejunum and ileum).

## pitfalls
Describing cestodes as leaf-shaped (a trematode feature) or rounded (a nematode feature) instead of segmented; placing the intestinal tapeworms in the large intestine or colon instead of the small intestine (jejunum/ileum).

## definition
The number and identity of intermediate hosts a platyhelminth requires varies by species and is a common source of EXCEPT-style exam questions. Diphyllobothrium latum, Heterophyes heterophyes and Metagonimus yokogawai all require two intermediate hosts, but Taenia solium is the exception among them, needing only the single pig as its sole intermediate host. Pig is a source of human infection for Taenia solium, Trichinella spiralis and Balantidium coli, but not for Taenia saginata, whose source is cattle instead.

## explicit_objective
State that Taenia solium needs only one intermediate host (the pig) while Diphyllobothrium latum, Heterophyes heterophyes and Metagonimus yokogawai each need two, and that pig is a source of infection for Taenia solium, Trichinella spiralis and Balantidium coli but not Taenia saginata.

## pitfalls
Assuming every cestode needs the same number of intermediate hosts; picking Taenia saginata instead of Taenia solium as the platyhelminth needing only one intermediate host; forgetting that Metagonimus yokogawai's fish/snail intermediate hosts are not herbivorous animals; naming Taenia saginata instead of Taenia solium as the parasite not transmitted through pigs.

## definition
Sparganosis is the infection of human tissue by the plerocercoid larva (sparganum) of Diphyllobothrium mansoni, a related but distinct species from Diphyllobothrium latum -- whose own plerocercoid instead causes an ordinary intestinal infection when eaten in fish -- and is caused by the larval, not the adult, worm form.

## explicit_objective
State that sparganosis is tissue infection by the plerocercoid larva of Diphyllobothrium mansoni, distinct from Diphyllobothrium latum's intestinal infection.

## pitfalls
Attributing sparganosis to Diphyllobothrium latum instead of Diphyllobothrium mansoni, or to the adult worm instead of the plerocercoid larva.
