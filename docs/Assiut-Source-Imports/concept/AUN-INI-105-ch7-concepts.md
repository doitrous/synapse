## label
Fungi reproduce by different mechanisms depending on growth form: yeasts multiply by budding, while molds propagate by fragmentation of the mycelium, each broken fragment regrowing into a new mycelium

## canonical_key
teaching.mycology.fungal-reproduction.budding-and-fragmentation

## aliases
Yeast reproduction by budding
Mold propagation by fragmentation

## arabic_label
طرق تكاثر الفطريات: التبرعم والتفتت

## arabic_aliases
تكاثر الخميرة بالتبرعم
تكاثر العفن بالتفتت

## definition
Fungal reproduction differs by growth form. Yeasts (unicellular fungi) multiply asexually by budding: a small outgrowth forms on the parent cell, enlarges, and eventually separates as a new, genetically identical daughter cell. Molds (filamentous fungi), by contrast, propagate their mycelium by fragmentation: mechanical or physiological breakage of the mycelium produces fragments, and each broken fragment is capable of growing into a whole new mycelium, since fungal hyphae retain the capacity for indefinite apical growth from almost any point. Neither mechanism involves spore formation or binary fission, which are separate fungal or bacterial processes respectively.

## explicit_objective
Identify budding as the reproductive mechanism of yeasts and fragmentation of the mycelium as a propagation mechanism of molds, each fragment regrowing into a new mycelium.

## pitfalls
Assuming all fungi reproduce the same way, or confusing fungal budding with bacterial binary fission -- budding produces an unequal outgrowth that pinches off, unlike the equal, symmetric split of binary fission.

## label
Fungi are eukaryotes: they possess a true, membrane-bound nucleus and chromosomes, unlike prokaryotic bacteria

## canonical_key
teaching.mycology.fungi.eukaryotic-nucleus-chromosomes

## aliases
Fungi are eukaryotic organisms
Fungal nucleus and chromosomes

## arabic_label
الفطريات كائنات حقيقية النواة

## arabic_aliases
امتلاك الفطريات لنواة حقيقية وكروموسومات

## definition
Fungi are eukaryotic organisms: each fungal cell has a true, membrane-bound nucleus containing chromosomes, along with membrane-bound organelles such as mitochondria -- unlike bacteria, which are prokaryotes with no nuclear membrane and a single circular chromosome lying free in the cytoplasm. This eukaryotic organisation is a defining feature that separates the fungal kingdom from the bacterial domain, even though both are studied together in medical microbiology. Fungi are also not photosynthetic (they lack chloroplasts and obtain nutrition heterotrophically, by absorption), and not every fungus grows in both yeast and mold forms -- only dimorphic species switch between the two.

## explicit_objective
State that fungi are eukaryotes possessing a true nucleus and chromosomes (and mitochondria), distinguishing them from prokaryotic bacteria, and reject the false claims that fungi are photosynthetic, lack cell membranes, or that every fungus grows as both yeast and mold.

## pitfalls
Assuming fungi lack mitochondria because they are sometimes taught alongside bacteria, or assuming every fungal species is dimorphic (able to grow as both yeast and mold) rather than this being a property of only a subset of medically important fungi.

## label
Mold hyphae may be septate (cross-walled) or non-septate (coenocytic); a mold's hyphae are not uniformly one or the other type across all species

## canonical_key
teaching.mycology.mold-hyphae.septate-vs-nonseptate

## aliases
Septate vs non-septate hyphae
Mold hyphal cross-walls

## arabic_label
الخيوط الفطرية المقسمة وغير المقسمة

## arabic_aliases
الخيوط ذات الحواجز مقابل الخيوط بدون حواجز

## definition
Mold hyphae are not built to a single template: depending on the species, hyphae may be septate (divided into individual cellular compartments by regularly spaced cross-walls, or septa, often perforated to allow cytoplasmic streaming) or non-septate/coenocytic (a single continuous, multinucleate cytoplasmic mass with no cross-walls at all, as in the lower fungi such as the Zygomycetes). A mold's hyphae are therefore correctly described as "may or may not be partitioned with cross walls," not as uniformly one type -- this variability is itself the medically testable fact, used to distinguish fungal groups (for example, the classically non-septate Mucor/Rhizopus from the classically septate Aspergillus).

## explicit_objective
State that mold hyphae may be either septate or non-septate (coenocytic) depending on the species, rather than uniformly one type.

## pitfalls
Assuming all mold hyphae are septate, or that septation status is irrelevant to fungal classification -- septate vs. non-septate hyphae is a standard first distinguishing feature used in fungal identification.

## label
Ergosterol is the sterol unique to the fungal cytoplasmic membrane, and its absence from human cell membranes (which use cholesterol instead) is the basis for the selective toxicity of most antifungal drugs

## canonical_key
teaching.mycology.fungal-membrane.ergosterol-selective-toxicity

## aliases
Ergosterol and selective antifungal toxicity
Fungal cell membrane ergosterol

## arabic_label
الإرغوستيرول والسمية الانتقائية لمضادات الفطريات

## arabic_aliases
الإرغوستيرول في الغشاء الفطري

## definition
The fungal cytoplasmic membrane contains ergosterol as its characteristic sterol, in place of the cholesterol found in human (and other mammalian) cell membranes. Because ergosterol is a fungus-specific target that human cells simply do not have, most major classes of antifungal drugs are built around it: polyenes (for example amphotericin B) bind ergosterol directly and form membrane pores, while azoles inhibit the fungal enzyme that synthesises ergosterol in the first place. This ergosterol-vs-cholesterol difference is what allows these drugs to damage the fungal membrane selectively while sparing human cell membranes -- the textbook explanation for "selective toxicity" of antifungal therapy.

## explicit_objective
State that ergosterol is the sterol characteristic of the fungal cell membrane (replacing cholesterol), and that this fungus-specific target is the basis of the selective toxicity of major antifungal drug classes.

## pitfalls
Assuming antifungal selective toxicity works the same way as antibacterial selective toxicity (which typically exploits a structure absent from human cells altogether, such as peptidoglycan) -- for antifungals it specifically hinges on a chemically different sterol occupying an analogous membrane role in both fungal and human cells.

## definition
Among the microorganism types compared in introductory microbiology teaching, a sterol-containing cytoplasmic membrane is characteristic of fungi (which use ergosterol). Viruses have no cytoplasmic membrane of their own synthesis at all -- an enveloped virus's envelope is instead derived from a host cell membrane during budding, not independently built with a virus-specific sterol. Chlamydia, though bacterial, has an atypical cell envelope and does not synthesise its own membrane sterol either. Fungi are therefore the standard answer when a question asks which of these three groups normally has sterol as a cytoplasmic membrane component.

## explicit_objective
Identify fungi as the group, among fungi/viruses/Chlamydia, whose cytoplasmic membrane normally contains sterol.

## pitfalls
Confusing an enveloped virus's host-cell-derived envelope with an independently synthesised, sterol-containing membrane -- the virus does not make its own membrane or its own sterol.

## label
Mycoses are classified by the depth of tissue involved: superficial (outermost keratin layer only, no inflammation, e.g. tinea versicolor), cutaneous (keratinized skin/hair/nail, dermatophytes), subcutaneous (dermis and subcutaneous tissue), and systemic (deep organs)

## canonical_key
teaching.mycology.mycoses.classification-by-depth

## aliases

## arabic_label
تصنيف الأمراض الفطرية حسب العمق

## arabic_aliases
النخالية المبرقشة كعدوى فطرية سطحية

## definition
Fungal infections (mycoses) are classified by how deep into the tissue the fungus involves. Superficial mycoses affect only the outermost, non-living keratin layer of skin or hair, provoking essentially no host inflammatory response -- tinea versicolor, caused by Malassezia, is the classic example. Cutaneous mycoses (dermatophytoses) invade the living keratinized structures themselves (stratum corneum, hair, nails) and do provoke inflammation, caused by dermatophytes. Subcutaneous mycoses involve the dermis and subcutaneous tissue, typically following traumatic inoculation. Systemic mycoses involve deep organs, often via inhalation, and can be caused by either true pathogens or opportunists in the immunocompromised. Tinea versicolor is specifically a superficial mycosis, not a cutaneous, systemic or subcutaneous one.

## explicit_objective
Classify tinea versicolor as a superficial mycosis, and place it within the broader superficial / cutaneous / subcutaneous / systemic depth-based classification of mycoses.

## pitfalls
Confusing tinea versicolor (superficial, Malassezia, no inflammation) with a dermatophyte cutaneous infection (also affects skin, but invades living keratinized tissue and does provoke inflammation) -- the names sound similar ("tinea") but the two lie in different depth categories.

Candida albicans is recognized in infected tissue by the co-occurrence of yeast cells and pseudohyphae on microscopic examination

## canonical_key

## definition
Candida albicans is a dimorphic yeast that, on microscopic examination of infected tissue, is recognized by the simultaneous presence of budding yeast cells and pseudohyphae -- elongated, budding yeast cells that remain attached end-to-end, mimicking true hyphae but lacking their parallel-walled, continuously branching structure. This dual yeast-plus-pseudohyphae picture is characteristic of Candida invading tissue and distinguishes it from organisms that show only one form (a pure yeast such as Cryptococcus, or true septate/non-septate hyphae as in molds). Pseudohyphae specifically are the structure most closely associated with Candida among the fungi commonly tested in introductory mycology.

## explicit_objective
Identify the combination of yeasts and pseudohyphae, on microscopic examination, as characteristic of Candida albicans in infected tissue, and recognize pseudohyphae as a structure associated with Candida.

## pitfalls
Confusing pseudohyphae (chains of elongated, still-separable budding yeast cells) with true hyphae (a continuous, branching filamentous structure) -- Candida's invasive form is pseudohyphal, not truly hyphal.

## label
Candida albicans is a budding yeast that forms pseudohyphae when it invades tissue and causes thrush; impaired cell-mediated immunity is an important predisposing factor, and transmission is endogenous/mucosal rather than primarily respiratory aerosol

## canonical_key
teaching.mycology.candida-albicans.pathogenesis-and-transmission

## aliases
Candida albicans pathogenesis
Candida albicans predisposing factors

## arabic_label
إمراضية المبيضات البيضاء وطرق انتقالها

## arabic_aliases
العدوى المخاطية بالمبيضات وليست بالاستنشاق

## definition
Candida albicans is a budding yeast that, when it invades tissue, forms pseudohyphae, and it causes thrush (oral/mucosal candidiasis) as one of its classic presentations. Impaired cell-mediated immunity (as in HIV/AIDS, or with immunosuppressive therapy) is an important predisposing factor to invasive or persistent disease, since T-cell-mediated immunity is central to controlling Candida at mucosal surfaces. Unlike many respiratory fungal pathogens, Candida albicans is not primarily transmitted by respiratory aerosol -- it is instead part of the normal endogenous flora of the mucosa (mouth, gut, vagina) in most people, and disease typically arises from overgrowth of this endogenous organism (an opportunistic infection) rather than from acquiring it anew by inhalation.

## explicit_objective
