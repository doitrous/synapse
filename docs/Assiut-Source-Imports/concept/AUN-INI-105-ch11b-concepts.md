## label
Adult Loa loa lives in subcutaneous and connective tissue

## canonical_key
loaloa.habitat.adult-subcutaneous-tissue

## aliases


## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases


## definition
The adult Loa loa worm inhabits subcutaneous and connective tissue, migrating slowly through these planes over the course of the infection rather than remaining fixed in one site; it is this migration that produces transient Calabar swellings wherever the worm currently passes and that occasionally lets the worm be seen crossing the subconjunctiva of the eye. This subcutaneous, migratory habitat contrasts with the fixed lymphatic habitat of Wuchereria bancrofti and Brugia malayi, and with the fixed subcutaneous nodules of Onchocerca volvulus.

## explicit_objective
State that the adult Loa loa worm inhabits subcutaneous and connective tissue, not lymphatics, blood or a serous cavity.

## pitfalls
Confusing Loa loa's migratory subcutaneous habitat with the fixed lymphatic habitat of Wuchereria bancrofti and Brugia malayi, or with the fixed nodular habitat of Onchocerca volvulus.

## label
Calabar swelling is transient, unlike the fixed Onchocerca nodule

## canonical_key
filariae.calabar-swelling-vs-onchocerca-nodule.transient-vs-fixed

## aliases


## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases


## definition
Calabar swelling, the localized subcutaneous oedema produced by the migrating adult Loa loa, is transient -- it appears suddenly, lasts a few days, and resolves spontaneously only to recur elsewhere as the worm continues to migrate. This is unlike the fixed, persistent subcutaneous nodule formed around an adult Onchocerca volvulus worm, which remains in one location for as long as the worm survives there, since Onchocerca adults become sedentary within fibrous nodules rather than continuing to migrate through the tissue.

## explicit_objective
State that Calabar swelling (Loa loa) is transient, unlike the fixed nodule of Onchocerca volvulus, because Loa loa's adult worm continues to migrate through subcutaneous tissue while Onchocerca's adult becomes sedentary within a nodule.

## pitfalls
Assuming both filarial tissue reactions behave the same way; Calabar swelling moves and resolves because the causative Loa loa worm keeps migrating, while the Onchocerca nodule persists because its worm does not.

## canonical_key

## definition
Dracunculus medinensis, Trichostrongylus colubriformis and Trichinella spiralis all infect man by ingestion of their respective larval stage -- infected Cyclops for Dracunculus, contaminated vegetation carrying the filariform larva for Trichostrongylus, and encysted larvae in undercooked meat for Trichinella. ## pitfalls
Reaching for a generic food-hygiene or vector-control measure instead of the skin-barrier measure that specifically interrupts hookworm's own skin-penetration transmission route.

## label
Baermann's technique detects Ancylostoma larvae in soil

## canonical_key
ancylostoma.diagnosis.baermann-technique-soil-larvae

## aliases


## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases


## definition
Baermann's technique is the method used to detect and recover Ancylostoma larvae from soil samples, exploiting the larvae's own active motility and thermotropism: a soil sample is suspended over warm water, and living larvae actively migrate out of the soil, through a filter, and sediment in the water below, where they can be collected and counted. Knott's technique is instead used to concentrate microfilariae from a blood sample, and Stoll's technique is an egg-counting dilution method applied to faeces, so neither is the soil-larvae recovery method being tested here.

## explicit_objective
Identify Baermann's technique as the method used to detect Ancylostoma larvae in soil, distinguishing it from Knott's technique (blood microfilariae) and Stoll's technique (faecal egg counting).

## pitfalls
Confusing Baermann's soil-larvae technique with Knott's blood-microfilaria concentration technique or Stoll's faecal egg-counting technique, which sample different specimens for different organisms' stages.

Nematodes are characterised, among other features, by organs of attachment that are teeth or cutting plates -- the buccal capsule of hookworms, for example, bears teeth or plates that grip the intestinal mucosa. ## explicit_objective
State that nematodes are characterised by organs of attachment that are teeth or cutting plates, distinguishing this from the false claims that they are rectangled in cross-section, possess a circulatory system, or are hermaphrodites.

## pitfalls
Attributing a circulatory system or hermaphroditism to nematodes; nematodes lack a circulatory system and have separate sexes, unlike cestodes and most trematodes.

## label
Retrofection (Enterobius) differs from internal autoinfection (Strongyloides)

## canonical_key
enterobius.retrofection.vs-strongyloides-internal-autoinfection

## aliases


## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases


## definition
Retrofection is the term for Enterobius vermicularis's own form of autoinfection: eggs laid on the perianal skin by the gravid female can hatch there, and the newly hatched larvae migrate back through the anus into the rectum to mature, without ever leaving the body's exterior surface. This is distinct from Strongyloides stercoralis's internal autoinfection, in which rhabditiform larvae shed within the intestinal lumen itself moult directly to the infective filariform stage before ever reaching the perianal skin, then re-penetrate the bowel wall or perianal skin from inside.

## explicit_objective
Distinguish Enterobius vermicularis's retrofection (external, via the perianal skin) from Strongyloides stercoralis's internal autoinfection (within the bowel lumen itself).

## pitfalls
Treating "retrofection" and "internal autoinfection" as interchangeable; retrofection is Enterobius's own perianal-skin route, while internal autoinfection is the intraluminal route that makes Strongyloides stercoralis infection potentially lifelong.

## definition
Rats act as a definitive host for all three of Hymenolepis nana, Hymenolepis diminuta and Trichinella spiralis: rodents sustain their own direct or arthropod-mediated Hymenolepis cycles independent of man, and rats also participate in the sylvatic rat-to-rat (and rat-to-pig) transmission cycle that maintains Trichinella spiralis in nature alongside its pork-borne cycle in man.

## explicit_objective
State that rats can act as a definitive host for Hymenolepis nana, Hymenolepis diminuta and Trichinella spiralis together.

## pitfalls
Assuming only one of these three parasites uses a rodent reservoir; all three have an independent rodent-sustained cycle, distinct from the human-only cycles of parasites such as Enterobius vermicularis.

## label
Taenia saginata eggs are not infective to man, unlike T. solium, Echinococcus granulosus and Enterobius eggs

## canonical_key
cestodenematode.eggs-infective-to-man.taenia-saginata-exception

## aliases


## arabic_label


## arabic_aliases


## definition
Taenia solium eggs, Echinococcus granulosus eggs and Enterobius vermicularis eggs are all directly infective to man when passed in (or, for Enterobius, deposited near) the faeces -- causing cysticercosis, hydatid disease and enterobiasis respectively. Taenia saginata eggs are the exception: man is not T. saginata's intermediate host at all, so its eggs cannot establish infection in man even if swallowed, and are instead infective only to cattle.

## explicit_objective
Identify Taenia saginata eggs as the exception among these four species' eggs, since man cannot act as its intermediate host.

## pitfalls
Assuming any tapeworm or nematode egg passed by man is automatically infective back to man; Taenia saginata eggs are infective only to its cattle intermediate host, not to man.
