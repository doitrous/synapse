Heterophyes heterophyes has a restricted, not cosmopolitan, geographical distribution

## canonical_key
heterophyes-heterophyes.geographical-distribution.not-cosmopolitan

## aliases
Heterophyes heterophyes distribution
Cosmopolitan vs restricted parasite distribution

## arabic_label
التوزيع الجغرافي المحدود لديدان الهتيروفييس

## arabic_aliases


## definition
Heterophyes heterophyes, a small intestinal fluke acquired by eating undercooked brackish-water fish, has a geographically restricted distribution -- the Far East, the Middle East (including Egypt) and parts of the Balkans, wherever its snail and fish intermediate hosts and local raw-fish-eating habits coincide. This is unlike several intestinal helminths tested in the same chapter -- Ascaris lumbricoides, Hymenolepis nana and Fasciola hepatica -- whose distribution is instead cosmopolitan (worldwide), reflecting transmission routes (direct faecal-oral egg ingestion, or a single widely available intermediate host) that do not depend on a specific brackish-water snail-fish complex.

## explicit_objective
Identify Heterophyes heterophyes as geographically restricted rather than cosmopolitan, unlike the cosmopolitan Ascaris lumbricoides, Hymenolepis nana and Fasciola hepatica.

## pitfalls
Assuming every helminth tested alongside cosmopolitan soil/faecal-oral parasites shares the same worldwide distribution; Heterophyes needs a specific fish-snail transmission chain that is not available everywhere.

## label
Hydatid disease is not characteristically a disease of childhood, unlike several other parasites tested alongside it

## canonical_key
hydatid-disease.epidemiology.not-characteristically-childhood

## aliases

Parasites commonly affecting children

## arabic_label
داء الكيس المائي ليس مرضًا شائعًا في الأطفال

## arabic_aliases


## definition
Hydatid disease (Echinococcus granulosus cystic hydatidosis) is acquired by ingesting eggs shed by an infected dog, and because the resulting cyst grows slowly over years before becoming clinically apparent, it is not characteristically a disease of childhood -- unlike Schistosoma mansoni, Enterobius vermicularis and Hymenolepis nana, which commonly and characteristically affect children through play-related soil/water contact, close-contact autoinfection and poor hand hygiene respectively. The distinction tested here is epidemiological (who is typically affected), not transmission route.

## explicit_objective
Identify hydatid disease as the exception among these four parasites in not characteristically affecting children, unlike Schistosoma mansoni, Enterobius vermicularis and Hymenolepis nana.

## pitfalls
Assuming any parasite acquired from dog contact must behave like the other "commonly affects children" options in the same list; hydatid cysts grow silently for years, so clinical presentation is usually delayed well past early childhood.

## label
Nematode reproduction: most intestinal species are oviparous (lay eggs), but Trichinella spiralis, Dracunculus medinensis and Loa loa are viviparous (release live larvae)

## canonical_key
nematodes.reproduction-mode.oviparous-vs-viviparous

## aliases
Oviparous vs viviparous nematodes
Trichinella viviparity

## arabic_label
التكاثر البيوضي مقابل الولودي في الديدان الخيطية

## arabic_aliases


## definition
Most intestinal nematodes tested in this bank -- Ascaris lumbricoides, Trichuris trichiura and Enterobius (Oxyuris) vermicularis -- are oviparous: the adult female lays eggs, which pass out of the body (or, for Enterobius, onto perianal skin) to develop further. Trichinella spiralis, Dracunculus medinensis and Loa loa are instead viviparous: the adult female releases live first-stage larvae directly, with no egg stage passed to the environment, which is why stool or skin examination for these three looks for larvae or adult worms rather than eggs.

## explicit_objective
Classify Ascaris, Trichuris and Enterobius as oviparous, and Trichinella spiralis, Dracunculus medinensis and Loa loa as viviparous.

## pitfalls
Assuming all nematodes share one reproductive pattern; the oviparous/viviparous split correlates with which stage (egg vs larva) is the diagnostic finding for each species.

Ingested nematode eggs hatch at a species-specific site: Enterobius in the duodenum, Ascaris in the small intestine

## canonical_key

## arabic_label
مكان فقس بيض الديدان الخيطية بعد الابتلاع

## definition
After a human host swallows an infective Enterobius vermicularis egg, the larva hatches in the duodenum and migrates to the caecum to mature. After swallowing an infective Ascaris lumbricoides egg, the larva instead hatches more generally within the small intestine, from where it penetrates the gut wall to begin its hepato-pulmonary migratory phase before returning to the small intestine as an adult. ## explicit_objective
State that ingested Enterobius eggs hatch in the duodenum, while ingested Ascaris eggs hatch in the small intestine.

## pitfalls
Assuming every ingested-egg nematode hatches at the same anatomical site; the duodenum-vs-small-intestine distinction between Enterobius and Ascaris is a specific, testable fact rather than an interchangeable detail.

## label
Enterobius vermicularis has no cycle requiring development outside the human body, unlike Ascaris, hookworm and Trichuris

## canonical_key
enterobius-vermicularis.life-cycle.no-obligatory-external-cycle

## aliases
Enterobius short life cycle
Worm without external soil cycle

## arabic_label
دودة الدبوس بلا طور إلزامي خارج الجسم

## arabic_aliases


## definition
Enterobius vermicularis has an unusually short, direct life cycle: eggs deposited on perianal skin become infective within hours, and can be swallowed again by the same host (retroinfection) or by a close contact almost immediately, with no obligatory period of development in soil. This contrasts with Ascaris lumbricoides, Ancylostoma duodenale and Trichuris trichiura, whose eggs must all spend days to weeks maturing in soil under suitable temperature and moisture before becoming infective -- an external, environment-dependent cycle Enterobius does not require.

## explicit_objective
Identify Enterobius vermicularis as the nematode without an obligatory external (soil) developmental cycle, unlike Ascaris, Ancylostoma duodenale and Trichuris trichiura.

## pitfalls
Confusing "no obligatory external cycle" with "the egg never leaves the body" -- Enterobius eggs are still deposited outside the body (on perianal skin), they simply do not require a soil-maturation period the way the other three species do.

## arabic_label
تصنيف شعبة الديدان الخيطية مقابل الديدان الشريطية

## arabic_aliases


## definition
Trichuris trichiura and Necator americanus are both members of class Nematoda (roundworms) -- elongated, cylindrical, unsegmented worms with a complete digestive tract and separate sexes. Dipylidium caninum, by contrast, is a cestode (tapeworm): a flat, ribbon-shaped, segmented hermaphroditic worm, placing it in an entirely different class (Cestoidea), not Nematoda -- a classification distinction this bank tests repeatedly by asking which organism in a mixed list does or does not belong to Nematoda.

## explicit_objective
Classify Trichuris trichiura and Necator americanus as Nematoda, and Dipylidium caninum as Cestoidea, not Nematoda.

## pitfalls
Assuming every worm-shaped human parasite listed in an exam question belongs to the same class; the roundworm-vs-tapeworm (Nematoda vs Cestoidea) distinction is a basic but frequently tested classification point.

A fertilized Ascaris lumbricoides egg is passed in faeces while still immature (unembryonated)

## canonical_key

## aliases
Ascaris egg maturity at passage
Fertilized Ascaris egg

## arabic_label
بيضة الأسكاريس المخصبة غير ناضجة عند طرحها

## arabic_aliases


## definition
A fertilized Ascaris lumbricoides egg is unembryonated (immature, a single-cell stage) at the moment it is passed in the host's faeces; it is mamillated (has a rough, bile-stained outer coat, not smooth/without mamillation) and brownish in colour rather than colourless. Further embryonation to the infective larvated stage happens afterward in soil, not before or at the time of passage, which is why stool-passed Ascaris eggs are not yet infective to a new host.

## explicit_objective
State that the fertilized Ascaris lumbricoides egg is immature (unembryonated) when passed in faeces, and that further development occurs afterward in soil.

## pitfalls
Assuming a fertilized egg is already infective at the point it leaves the body; Ascaris eggs (like most soil-transmitted nematode eggs) need an external maturation period first.

Enterobius vermicularis is called the pin-worm (not the whip-worm), has no larval migratory phase, and its eggs are laid immature

## arabic_label

## definition
Enterobius vermicularis is called the pin-worm (its slender, pin-like tapering tail gives the common name), not the whip-worm -- that name belongs to Trichuris trichiura, whose anterior end is thin and whip-like. Enterobius has no larval migratory phase through the lungs (unlike Ascaris, hookworm and Strongyloides), and its eggs are laid immature (unembryonated) on perianal skin by the gravid female, becoming infective only after several hours of further development in the open air.

## explicit_objective
Identify Enterobius vermicularis as the pin-worm (not whip-worm), with no larval migratory phase and eggs laid immature.

## pitfalls
Swapping the pin-worm/whip-worm common names between Enterobius and Trichuris; assuming Enterobius eggs are already mature (embryonated, infective) at the moment they are laid.

## pitfalls

## label
Enterobius vermicularis is the exception among common intestinal nematodes that lay their eggs in the intestine

## canonical_key
nematodes.egg-laying-site.enterobius-exception-perianal

## aliases
Where nematodes lay eggs
Enterobius perianal egg-laying

## arabic_label
استثناء دودة الدبوس في مكان وضع البيض

## arabic_aliases


## definition
Ascaris lumbricoides, Trichuris trichiura and Ancylostoma duodenale all lay their eggs directly into the intestinal lumen, from where the eggs pass out with the faeces. Enterobius vermicularis is the exception: the gravid female migrates out through the anus at night and deposits her eggs on the perianal skin, not into the intestinal lumen, which is why routine stool examination is an unreliable diagnostic method for Enterobius and a perianal (cellophane tape) swab is used instead.

## explicit_objective
Identify Enterobius vermicularis as the exception that does not lay its eggs in the intestine, unlike Ascaris, Trichuris and Ancylostoma duodenale.

## pitfalls
Assuming every intestinal nematode's diagnostic egg is deposited inside the gut lumen; Enterobius eggs are deposited externally, on perianal skin.
