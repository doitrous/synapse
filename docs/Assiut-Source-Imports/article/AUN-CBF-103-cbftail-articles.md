## summary
A nerve impulse is electrochemical, not purely electrical or purely chemical: it is the rapid, self-propagating movement of ions -- sodium in during depolarisation, potassium out during repolarisation -- across the excitable membrane, producing a recordable voltage change that travels the length of the fibre at full amplitude once threshold is crossed.

## sections
### Definition
The nerve impulse (action potential) is defined as electrochemical because it combines a chemical event, the movement of ions through voltage-gated channels down their electrochemical gradients, with an electrical consequence, a measurable and propagating reversal of membrane potential. It is not a flow of free electrons along the axon the way current runs through a wire, and it is not a simple diffusing chemical secretion either; both mechanisms alone would fail to explain the impulse's speed, its self-regeneration along the fibre, and its recordability as a voltage.

### Mechanism
At threshold, enough voltage-gated sodium channels open that sodium influx becomes self-sustaining, driving rapid depolarisation; this local change in membrane potential is large enough to open the same channels in the immediately adjacent membrane patch, so the impulse regenerates itself along the fibre rather than fading with distance the way a passive electrical or chemical signal would. Repolarisation follows as sodium channels inactivate and voltage-gated potassium channels open, letting potassium efflux restore the resting potential.

### Key determinants
The impulse obeys the all-or-none law: once threshold is reached, it propagates at full, unchanging amplitude, in contrast to a graded local response or a passively decaying electrical/chemical signal.

### Clinical significance
Because the impulse depends on the orderly sequence of sodium influx then potassium efflux through specific channels, drugs and toxins that block either channel type (local anaesthetics on sodium channels, certain toxins on potassium channels) abolish or distort nerve conduction, which is the pharmacological basis of local anaesthesia and several channelopathies.

### Common misconceptions
Describing the impulse as purely "electrical" (like current in a wire) or purely "chemical" (like a diffusing substance) rather than as the combination of ion movement (chemical) producing a propagating voltage change (electrical) that "electrochemical" specifically names.

## summary
Blood glucose is held steady by several cooperating organs -- pancreatic beta cells (insulin), the kidney (reabsorption), thyroid hormone (intestinal absorption) and the liver (the body's glucostat) -- while glycogen itself is built onto a glycogenin primer by insulin-activated glycogen synthase and broken down by phosphorylase; inherited defects in these pathways produce the glycogen storage diseases, including Von Gierke's (glucose 6-phosphatase) and Cori's (debranching enzyme).

## sections
### Definition
Glucose homeostasis depends on distinct organ contributions: pancreatic islet beta cells secrete insulin (alpha cells secrete glucagon), the renal tubules reabsorb filtered glucose up to a threshold, thyroid hormone raises intestinal glucose absorption, and the liver buffers blood glucose by storing or releasing it, earning it the name "glucostat." Glycogen synthesis (glycogenesis) begins with the protein primer glycogenin, extended by insulin-activated glycogen synthase; glycogen breakdown (glycogenolysis) is carried out by glycogen phosphorylase, which releases glucose 1-phosphate, plus a debranching enzyme that clears each branch point.

### Mechanism
Insulin promotes glycogen synthase's active (dephosphorylated) state, so glycogen accumulates in the fed, high-insulin state; glucagon and epinephrine promote phosphorylase's active (phosphorylated) state, mobilising glycogen in the fasted or stressed state. Phosphorylase alone cannot clear a branch point -- it stops about four residues short -- so the debranching enzyme's glucosyl-transferase and glucosidase activities are needed to finish the job and release the residue at the branch as free glucose.

### Key determinants
In the glycogen storage diseases, the specific missing enzyme determines the clinical picture: Von Gierke's disease (glucose 6-phosphatase deficiency) blocks the final step of both glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis, causing severe fasting hypoglycaemia, lactic acidosis and hyperuricaemia; Cori's disease (debranching enzyme deficiency) is milder, since phosphorylase can still strip the outer chains and only the branch-point glucose is trapped.

### Clinical significance
Recognising which enzyme is missing (glucose 6-phosphatase vs debranching enzyme vs muscle phosphorylase vs lysosomal acid maltase vs branching enzyme) distinguishes Von Gierke's, Cori's, McArdle's, Pompe's and Andersen's disease respectively, each with a different severity and management approach.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming every glucose-regulation question is about insulin and glucagon alone, when the kidney's reabsorptive threshold and thyroid hormone's absorptive effect at the gut are separately tested. Confusing glycogenin (the primer), glycogen synthase (the synthesis enzyme) and glycogen phosphorylase (the breakdown enzyme) with one another.

## summary
Two thermoregulatory adaptations extend beyond ordinary shivering and sweating: sympathetically driven brown fat gives the neonate a non-shivering source of heat that is more important than shivering at that age, and repeated heat exposure produces acclimatization, chiefly an increased capacity to lose heat by sweating with progressively lower sweat salt content.

## sections
### Definition
Brown adipose tissue is a mitochondria-rich fat depot, densely supplied by sympathetic nerves, that generates heat directly through uncoupled mitochondrial oxidation rather than through muscular work -- non-shivering thermogenesis. Heat acclimatization is the set of adaptations, chiefly sweat gland hypertrophy and increased sweat output, that develops with repeated exposure to a hot climate.

### Mechanism
Sympathetic stimulation of brown fat activates the mitochondrial uncoupling protein thermogenin (UCP1), which lets protons leak across the inner mitochondrial membrane without producing ATP, dissipating the proton gradient's energy as heat instead; this mechanism dominates in the neonate, whose shivering reflex is immature and whose high surface-area-to-mass ratio makes heat loss especially dangerous. With heat acclimatization, sweat glands hypertrophy and rising aldosterone reabsorbs more sodium in the sweat duct, so total sweat output rises while its salt concentration falls, protecting the acclimatized person from salt depletion during heavy sweating.

### Key determinants
Non-shivering thermogenesis is sympathetic, not parasympathetic, matching the sympathetic nervous system's general role in mobilising cold defence; heat acclimatization specifically increases sweating capacity rather than basal metabolic rate, muscle tone or urinary output.

### Clinical significance
An infant's dependence on brown fat rather than shivering explains why premature or hypothermic neonates, who have less brown fat reserve, are especially vulnerable to cold stress; heat acclimatization protocols (used, for example, before military or athletic exposure to hot climates) work by driving exactly this sweat gland adaptation over days to weeks.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming brown fat's heat production works the same way shivering does (via mechanical muscle contraction) rather than via uncoupled mitochondrial oxidation. Assuming heat acclimatization raises metabolic rate generally, rather than specifically improving the sweating mechanism.

## summary
Five related lipid facts recur across this exam block: pulmonary surfactant (mainly DPPC) is deficient, not excessive, in premature infant respiratory distress syndrome; glycerophospholipids share a glycerol backbone that sphingomyelin lacks, and phosphatidylinositol specifically feeds intracellular second-messenger signalling; essential fatty acids (linoleic, linolenic) cannot be made by the body while oleic, palmitic and stearic acid can; fatty acids are named by both chain length and double-bond count (palmitoleic 16:1, arachidonic 20:4); and gangliosides are the sphingolipid cholera toxin binds in the gut.

## sections
### Definition
Pulmonary surfactant, secreted by type II pneumocytes and composed mainly of dipalmitoyl phosphatidylcholine (DPPC), lowers alveolar surface tension. Glycerophospholipids (lecithin, phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylserine, phosphatidylinositol, plasmalogens, cardiolipin) share a glycerol backbone, unlike the sphingosine-backboned sphingolipids such as sphingomyelin and the gangliosides. Fatty acids are classified as essential (cannot be synthesized -- linoleic, linolenic) or non-essential (can be synthesized -- oleic, palmitic, stearic), and are further identified by carbon-chain length and double-bond count.

### Mechanism
DPPC production rises through gestation, so a premature infant (for example, born at 28 weeks) has too little accumulated surfactant, and its concentration is lower, not higher, than in a full-term baby -- the direct cause of alveolar collapse in respiratory distress syndrome. Phosphatidylinositol, once cleaved by phospholipase C after receptor activation, yields IP3 (releasing calcium from the endoplasmic reticulum) and diacylglycerol (activating protein kinase C), making it the specific second-messenger-precursor glycerophospholipid. Essential fatty acids require a desaturase enzyme the human body lacks (to place a double bond beyond carbon 9), which is why linoleic and linolenic acid, but not oleic, palmitic or stearic acid, must come from the diet; arachidonic acid (20:4), derived from linoleic acid, is the eicosanoid precursor. Gangliosides carry sialic-acid-bearing oligosaccharide head groups on a ceramide backbone, and it is this structure that cholera toxin's GM1 receptor recognises in the intestinal epithelium.

### Key determinants
Two numbers (chain length and double-bond count) are needed together to identify a fatty acid; a glycerol backbone (vs a sphingosine one) is what defines a glycerophospholipid; and a lower-than-normal DPPC concentration, not an elevated lecithin/sphingomyelin ratio, is the hallmark of neonatal surfactant deficiency.

### Clinical significance
Antenatal corticosteroids accelerate fetal surfactant production and are given when preterm delivery is anticipated; essential fatty acid deficiency produces a recognisable dermatitis and growth-impairment syndrome; cholera's profuse diarrhoea follows directly from toxin binding at the ganglioside GM1 receptor and the resulting persistent adenylyl cyclase activation.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming a higher lecithin/sphingomyelin ratio, rather than a lower DPPC concentration, defines respiratory distress syndrome. Classifying sphingomyelin as a glycerophospholipid because it is also a membrane phospholipid. Assuming any polyunsaturated fatty acid must be essential, rather than specifically the ones (linoleic, linolenic) the body's desaturases cannot make.

## published_sections

## hold_these

Linoleic and linolenic acid are essential; oleic, palmitic and stearic acid are not.

## summary
Transaminases transfer amino groups between an amino acid and a keto acid without net nitrogen loss, and serum GOT (AST) is a classic marker of tissue damage including myocardial infarction; downstream, the urea cycle disposes of that nitrogen, with ornithine transcarbamylase forming citrulline from ornithine, and any urea-cycle enzyme deficiency, OTC included, backing up nitrogen as blood ammonia.

## sections
### Definition
A transaminase (aminotransferase) transfers an amino group from an amino acid to an alpha-keto acid acceptor, using pyridoxal phosphate as coenzyme, converting the donor into a new keto acid and the acceptor into a new amino acid -- forming keto acids from amino acids, with the nitrogen relocated rather than removed. Ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC) is the urea-cycle enzyme that combines ornithine and carbamoyl phosphate to form citrulline, the second step of the cycle.

### Mechanism
Because transamination relocates nitrogen rather than removing it, it is oxidative deamination (via glutamate dehydrogenase), not transamination, that actually releases free ammonia from the amino-acid pool for urea synthesis. Serum glutamate-oxaloacetate transaminase (GOT/AST) is abundant in cardiac and liver tissue and leaks into the blood when either is damaged, so a rise in its activity is a classic, if non-specific, marker of myocardial infarction. The urea cycle is a strictly linear pathway: ammonia and bicarbonate form carbamoyl phosphate, OTC combines this with ornithine to form citrulline, and the remaining steps convert citrulline to urea via argininosuccinate and arginine -- a block at any one enzyme, OTC included, stops the whole sequence and lets ammonia and the upstream intermediates accumulate.

### Key determinants
Distinguishing transamination (amino-group transfer, no net nitrogen loss) from oxidative deamination (net release of free ammonia) is essential to correctly answering "what does a transaminase do." OTC's direction is specifically ornithine + carbamoyl phosphate to citrulline, not the reverse.

### Clinical significance
GOT/AST elevation historically helped diagnose myocardial infarction before troponin and CK-MB became standard, and still helps flag hepatocellular damage. Urea cycle enzyme deficiencies, including OTC deficiency (X-linked, the most common), cause life-threatening neonatal or later-onset hyperammonaemia, managed by restricting protein intake and providing nitrogen-scavenging drugs.

### Common misconceptions
Describing a transaminase as removing nitrogen from the body (that is oxidative deamination's job, not transamination's). Reversing OTC's reaction direction, or assuming only one specific urea-cycle enzyme's deficiency raises ammonia rather than any block in the linear pathway.
