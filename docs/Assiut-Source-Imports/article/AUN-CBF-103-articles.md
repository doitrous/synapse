## summary
Total body water and its two subcompartments -- intracellular fluid (ICF) and extracellular fluid (ECF) -- are described both as fractions of body weight and as litre values for a 70 kg reference adult, with the extracellular compartment splitting further into plasma and interstitial fluid. Each compartment boundary has a distinct physical barrier: the cell membrane between ICF and ECF, the capillary wall between plasma and interstitial fluid. Across the capillary wall, plasma's higher protein content generates the colloidal osmotic pressure that reabsorbs fluid from the interstitial space, and when the lymphatics that normally drain that space are obstructed, the result is a distinctively non-pitting oedema.

## sections
### Definition
Total body water (TBW) is about 60% of body weight in a normal adult male -- 42 L for a 70 kg reference adult -- and is lower in adult females, obese people (both due to higher fat content, which holds relatively little water) and higher in infants. TBW divides into the intracellular fluid (ICF) compartment, two-thirds of TBW (28 L, 40% of body weight), and the extracellular fluid (ECF) compartment, one-third of TBW (14 L, 20% of body weight). The ECF itself splits into plasma, about 3 L, and interstitial fluid, about 10 L. A small transcellular fluid compartment (about 0.5 L: CSF, synovial and similar specialised fluids) sits apart from this basic ICF/ECF scheme.

### Mechanism
The cell membrane is the physical barrier between ICF and ECF; the capillary wall is the separate barrier, one level up within the ECF, between plasma and interstitial fluid. The capillary wall is freely permeable to water and small solutes but restricts large plasma proteins, so plasma carries a much higher protein concentration than interstitial fluid despite both being ECF. That protein difference generates the plasma colloidal osmotic pressure, the force that reabsorbs fluid from the interstitial space back into the capillary, balancing the capillary hydrostatic pressure that pushes fluid the opposite way. Lymphatic vessels normally drain any interstitial protein and fluid that escapes this balance; when they are obstructed, protein-rich fluid accumulates in the tissue and organises there, producing oedema that is characteristically non-pitting rather than the ordinary pitting oedema seen with hydrostatic- or oncotic-pressure-driven fluid excess.

### Key determinants
The ECF total (14 L) is never measured directly -- only plasma volume is measured by tracer dilution, and interstitial fluid volume is calculated as the remainder (ECF minus plasma). Whether an oedema pits or not is itself diagnostic: pitting points toward a hydrostatic- or oncotic-pressure cause, non-pitting points toward lymphatic obstruction.

### Clinical significance
Recognising non-pitting oedema on examination narrows the differential toward lymphatic obstruction (lymphoedema) rather than the far more common pitting causes (heart failure, hypoalbuminaemia, venous obstruction), directly changing what work-up is pursued next.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming plasma and interstitial fluid differ broadly in composition -- they are nearly identical in small-ion content, and the one major difference (protein) is exactly what generates the reabsorbing osmotic force. Confusing the cell membrane and capillary wall as the same barrier, when they sit at different levels of the compartment hierarchy.

## summary
The peripheral axon carries a neurilemmal sheath as its outermost covering, essential for regeneration after injury. At rest, the nerve membrane's much greater permeability to potassium than sodium sets a resting potential well negative to threshold, and how close that resting potential sits to threshold determines excitability -- shifted toward threshold by a fall in extracellular calcium (hypocalcaemic tetany), shifted away by a rise. Depolarization is driven by a rise in sodium permeability, in proportion to how large that rise is, and the absolute refractory period that follows is caused by inactivation of the same voltage-gated sodium channels' own inactivation gates -- unresponsive to any stimulus, however strong, until they recover.

## sections
### Definition
Working outward from the axon, a peripheral nerve fibre's coverings are the axolemma, the myelin sheath (where present), and outermost of all the neurilemmal sheath (sheath of Schwann) -- the Schwann cell's own cytoplasm and nucleus, surrounding every peripheral fibre whether myelinated or not. At rest, the nerve membrane is far more permeable to potassium than to sodium, through leak channels, which is why the resting potential sits close to the potassium equilibrium potential. Depolarization is the shift toward a less negative potential, caused by a rise in sodium permeability as voltage-gated Na+ channels open.

### Mechanism
The size of a depolarization tracks the size of the sodium-permeability rise that causes it -- a bigger rise in Na+ permeability produces a bigger depolarization. How easily that depolarization reaches threshold (i.e. how excitable the fibre is) depends on how far the resting potential already sits from threshold: shifting the resting potential toward threshold (less negative) increases excitability, shifting it away (hyperpolarization) decreases it. Extracellular calcium sets part of that distance by a membrane-stabilising effect on Na+ channels -- a fall in extracellular calcium removes stabilisation and increases excitability (hypocalcaemic tetany), a rise increases stabilisation and decreases it. Once Na+ channels open, their separate inactivation gates close on a short delay regardless of continued depolarization; while enough of them stay closed (the absolute refractory period), no stimulus of any strength, however large, can trigger a second action potential, because the channels needed for one are simply unavailable.

### Key determinants
The neurilemmal sheath is what makes peripheral (but not central) nerve regeneration possible after injury -- it provides a physical guide for a regrowing axon. The absolute refractory period is qualitatively different from reduced excitability: it is a hard block from channel unavailability, not a raised threshold.

### Clinical significance
Hypocalcaemic tetany (spontaneous, repetitive nerve/muscle firing from lowered extracellular calcium) and the local anaesthetic's exploitation of sodium-channel block (preventing the same depolarizing permeability rise this article describes) are both direct clinical extensions of the mechanisms taught here. Peripheral nerve injury's recovery potential, unlike a central nervous system lesion's, rests on the neurilemmal sheath's guiding role.

### Common misconceptions
Naming potassium instead of sodium as the depolarizing ion -- potassium efflux drives repolarization/hyperpolarization, not depolarization. Treating the absolute refractory period as simply "less excitable" the way the relative refractory period is, rather than as a hard, strength-independent block.
