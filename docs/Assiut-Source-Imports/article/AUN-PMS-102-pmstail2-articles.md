## summary
Five long bones of the limbs, each with one defining feature this module's own quiz bank tests: the clavicle's whole length is subcutaneous; the scapular spine's lateral end forms the acromion; the humerus carries a surgical neck and two tuberosities just below its head; the femur is the body's longest and heaviest bone; and the fibula, unlike the tibia, takes no part in the knee joint.

## sections
### Definition
The upper limb's bony girdle and arm are the clavicle, scapula and humerus; the lower limb's thigh and leg are the femur, tibia and fibula. Each is a long bone with a shaft and two expanded ends, but each also carries features specific to its own site and function.

### Mechanism
The clavicle is the one long bone with no overlying muscle anywhere along its length, which is why it can be palpated in full and why it fractures easily on a fall. The scapula's posterior spine divides it into two fossae and flattens laterally into the acromion, the roof of the shoulder joint and the clavicle's own articulating partner. The humerus narrows below its rounded head into the surgical neck -- a common fracture site -- and carries the greater and lesser tuberosities, separated by the bicipital groove that guides the long head of biceps tendon. The femur, transmitting the full weight of the trunk from hip to knee, is built larger and heavier than any other bone in the body to do that job. The fibula, carrying almost no weight, is free to serve a purely muscle-attachment and ankle-stabilising role, which is exactly why it plays no part in the knee joint that the weight-bearing tibia forms with the femur.

### Key determinants
The clavicle is subcutaneous throughout its whole extent.

The lateral end of the scapular spine forms the acromion, which articulates with the clavicle at the acromioclavicular joint.

Immediately below the humeral head lies the surgical neck; below that, the greater and lesser tuberosities are separated by the bicipital (intertubercular) groove.

The femur is the longest and heaviest bone in the body.

The fibula takes no part in the knee joint, which is formed by the femur and tibia alone.

### Clinical significance
The clavicle's subcutaneous, unprotected course is exactly why it is one of the most commonly fractured bones, typically from a fall onto the shoulder or an outstretched hand. The humeral surgical neck is a classic fracture site in older patients with osteoporotic bone. The femur's size and the force needed to break it are why a femoral shaft fracture is associated with substantial blood loss into the thigh.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming a bone described as easily fractured must be deeply placed and protected; the clavicle fractures easily precisely because it lies unprotected just under the skin.

Placing the femur's head, neck and trochanters at its lower end; those landmarks are proximal, at the hip, while the femoral condyles are the distal, knee-articulating landmarks.

Assuming every bone of a limb segment shares in every joint at both ends of that segment; the fibula's own joint contribution is at the ankle, not the knee.

## summary
Spermatogenesis is the whole process from spermatogonium to spermatozoon, made of meiosis (the reduction divisions) and spermiogenesis (the subsequent non-dividing remodelling), regulated jointly by FSH and LH; aspermia, a failure of the process's end product, is the absence of semen itself rather than of sperm within a semen sample.

## sections
### Definition
Spermatogenesis begins with a type A spermatogonium, which by mitosis produces a type B spermatogonium and, from it, a primary spermatocyte. The primary spermatocyte undergoes meiosis I to form two secondary spermatocytes, each of which undergoes meiosis II to form two spermatids -- so meiosis converts one diploid primary spermatocyte into four haploid spermatids. Spermiogenesis is the further remodelling of each round spermatid into a streamlined, motile spermatozoon, without any further cell division: the Golgi apparatus packages hydrolytic enzymes into the acrosomal cap, the nucleus condenses, most cytoplasm is shed, and a flagellar tail forms from one centriole.

### Mechanism
Aspermia is the complete absence of semen (the ejaculate fluid itself) on ejaculation, a different fact from azoospermia (semen present, but with no live sperm within it) or from reduced sperm motility; it is a failure of ejaculation or semen production, not a defect confined to the sperm cells. Hormonally, luteinising hormone (LH) acts mainly on Leydig (interstitial) cells to drive testosterone production, while follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) acts mainly on Sertoli cells: it supports the production of testicular (seminiferous tubule) fluid, the medium in which developing sperm are carried, and it promotes the Sertoli cell's own synthesis of testosterone receptors, which is what lets the high local testosterone that LH drives actually act on spermatogenesis. Once formed, a mature spermatozoon remains viable and capable of fertilising an ovum for only about 3-4 days within the female genital tract.

### Key determinants
Spermatogenesis includes both meiosis (division) and spermiogenesis (non-dividing remodelling).

The primary spermatocyte arises by mitosis of the spermatogonium; the primary spermatocyte's own division is meiotic.

The acrosomal cap forms from the spermatid's Golgi apparatus during spermiogenesis.

FSH and LH together regulate spermatogenesis; FSH specifically supports testicular fluid production and testosterone-receptor synthesis on the Sertoli cell.

Mature sperm remain viable for about 3-4 days.

Aspermia is the complete absence of semen, distinct from azoospermia (no sperm within a present semen sample).

### Clinical significance
Aspermia and azoospermia lead to different diagnostic pathways in male infertility, since one is a failure of ejaculation/semen production and the other a failure of sperm production or transport within an otherwise normal ejaculate. The sperm's own short 3-4 day viability window is part of why the fertile window around ovulation is time-limited from the male side as well as the female side.

### Common misconceptions
Confusing aspermia with azoospermia -- aspermia is no semen at all, azoospermia is semen with no sperm in it.

Assuming the primary spermatocyte divides mitotically. It arises by mitosis of the spermatogonium before it, but its own division, into secondary spermatocytes, is meiotic.

Crediting LH alone with regulating spermatogenesis. FSH's own separate Sertoli-cell actions, testicular fluid production and testosterone-receptor synthesis, are equally part of the hormonal regulation.

## summary
The oocyte released at ovulation is still incomplete, arrested at metaphase of meiosis II until a sperm triggers its completion at fertilisation, and it degenerates within 1-2 days if that trigger never comes; fertilisation itself is marked by fusion of the male and female pronuclei, after which cleavage increases the conceptus's cell number without increasing its size; separately, the corpus luteum's own luteinised granulosa cells are the ovarian cycle's main source of progesterone.

## sections
### Definition
At ovulation the secondary oocyte has completed the first meiotic division but is arrested part-way through the second, at metaphase of meiosis II. Fertilisation, sperm penetration of the oocyte, is the trigger that releases this arrest, letting meiosis II complete with extrusion of the second polar body; oogenesis is therefore only finished after fertilisation. Each gamete's own haploid nucleus at this point is called a pronucleus, and fusion of the male and female pronuclei restores the diploid number and completes fertilisation. The fertilised zygote then begins cleavage, defined as an increase in cell (blastomere) number without any increase in the conceptus's overall size.

### Mechanism
If fertilisation does not occur, the ovulated oocyte has no way to sustain itself indefinitely and degenerates about 1-2 days after ovulation -- a short window that, together with the roughly similar few-day viability of sperm, sets the practical fertile period around ovulation. Once fertilisation has occurred, cleavage divides the zygote's fixed volume of cytoplasm into progressively smaller cells at each round, rather than growing the conceptus, which is why the early cleavage stages stay roughly the same overall size as they divide; true growth only begins later, after implantation. Separately, within the corpus luteum that forms from the ruptured follicle after ovulation, it is specifically the luteinised granulosa cells, not the thecal cells alone, that are credited as the principal source of the corpus luteum's progesterone output.

### Key determinants
The ovulated oocyte is arrested at metaphase of meiosis II until fertilisation triggers its completion.

An unfertilised ovum degenerates about 1-2 days after ovulation.

Haploid pronuclei fuse at fertilisation.

Early cleavage is defined by an increase in cell number, not an increase in size.

Granulosa cells are the corpus luteum's main source of progesterone.

### Clinical significance
The 1-2 day post-ovulatory ovum lifespan, combined with the sperm's own few-day viability, defines the narrow fertile window that natural-family-planning and fertility-timing methods are built around. Failure of the corpus luteum's granulosa-cell progesterone output in early pregnancy, before the placenta takes over, is a recognised cause of early miscarriage.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming oogenesis is complete at ovulation; the released oocyte is still arrested at metaphase of meiosis II, awaiting fertilisation to finish the second meiotic division.

Describing cleavage as growth. Cleavage increases the conceptus's cell number while its overall size stays essentially constant.

Crediting thecal or thecal-and-lutein cells jointly with the corpus luteum's progesterone output. Granulosa cells are credited as the principal source once luteinised.

## summary
Ground substance is built mainly of glycosaminoglycans, of which hyaluronic acid is the most abundant and the only non-sulfated, non-protein-bound one; the mast cell, a separate fixed connective-tissue cell packed with metachromatic granules, is the tissue's own resident histamine-secreting cell.

## sections
### Definition
Connective tissue's ground substance is the amorphous gel filling the space between its cells and fibres, built mainly of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) together with proteoglycans and glycoproteins. The mast cell is a large, oval, fixed connective tissue cell, distinct from the fibroblast and the macrophage, whose cytoplasm is packed with metachromatic granules.

### Mechanism
Of the several GAGs present in ground substance -- hyaluronic acid, dermatan sulfate, heparan sulfate and chondroitin sulfate -- hyaluronic acid is both the most abundant and structurally distinctive: unlike the others, it is very large, non-sulfated, and not covalently bound to a protein core, and it works by trapping enormous amounts of water, which is what gives ground substance its characteristic viscous, gel-like consistency. The mast cell's own granules hold heparin, histamine and other mediators; on an appropriate stimulus, most often an IgE-mediated allergic trigger, the cell degranulates and releases its histamine, causing local vasodilatation and increased capillary permeability -- the basis of an immediate hypersensitivity reaction.

### Key determinants
Hyaluronic acid is the most common glycosaminoglycan in ground substance, and the only one that is non-sulfated and not protein-bound.

Mast cells secrete histamine, stored together with heparin in metachromatic cytoplasmic granules.

### Clinical significance
Mast-cell histamine release is the immediate effector of type I (immediate) hypersensitivity reactions, from urticaria to anaphylaxis, which is why antihistamines target this pathway.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming every glycosaminoglycan is sulfated and protein-bound like dermatan, heparan and chondroitin sulfate. Hyaluronic acid is the exception, and that exception is part of why it is the most abundant.

Confusing the mast cell with the basophil. Both store histamine and heparin in metachromatic granules and carry IgE receptors, but the mast cell is a fixed tissue cell and the basophil a circulating blood granulocyte, differing in size and life span.
