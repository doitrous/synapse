## summary
AUN-INI-105's fifth microbiology chapter pairs two closely related themes: how antimicrobial drugs work, resist being defeated by bacteria, and are used clinically, and how bacteria themselves cause disease once an antimicrobial (or the host's own defences) has not stopped them. It walks through each major antimicrobial mechanism class (cell wall, protein synthesis, nucleic acid, and membrane-active agents), the main routes bacteria use to resist those drugs, the clinical judgment calls around combining drugs and giving surgical prophylaxis, and the pathogenesis vocabulary -- virulence, invasiveness, transmission routes, carriers, latent infection, opportunistic pathogens -- that describes how an infection actually unfolds in a patient.

## sections

### Cell wall synthesis inhibitors
Cell-wall-synthesis-inhibiting drugs -- the beta-lactams (penicillins, cephalosporins, monobactams such as aztreonam) and the glycopeptide vancomycin -- are the most selective class of antibacterial agents, because bacterial peptidoglycan has no eukaryotic counterpart: a drug that blocks its synthesis has essentially nothing to disrupt in a human cell, which lacks a cell wall altogether. Penicillin's mechanism is inhibition of cell wall synthesis, and cephalosporins share that same target, not the protein-synthesis target some students mistakenly assign them. This selective-toxicity principle -- harming the pathogen without significantly harming the host by targeting a microbe-unique structure -- is the organizing idea behind why cell-wall-active drugs remain a first-line antimicrobial class.

### Protein synthesis inhibitors
Protein synthesis inhibitors split by which ribosomal subunit they target. Aminoglycosides (e.g. gentamycin) and tetracyclines both bind the 30S subunit -- aminoglycosides irreversibly, causing mRNA misreading and a bactericidal effect; tetracyclines reversibly, blocking aminoacyl-tRNA binding and producing a bacteriostatic effect instead. Chloramphenicol binds the different, 50S subunit, blocking peptidyl transferase; its clinical use is now limited chiefly by the risk of bone marrow suppression, which can progress to aplastic anaemia, rather than by any GI-absorption problem.

### Nucleic acid synthesis inhibitors
Two distinct nucleic-acid targets recur in this chapter. Quinolones inhibit DNA gyrase (topoisomerase II), blocking DNA replication; rifampicin, an antituberculous agent, instead inhibits RNA polymerase, blocking transcription. Sulfonamides take a third route entirely -- not acting on DNA or RNA directly, but blocking the folate-synthesis pathway bacteria need to make the nucleotide precursors DNA and RNA are built from, by competitively antagonising PABA's conversion to folic acid. Because human cells absorb pre-formed folic acid from the diet and do not depend on this bacterial pathway, sulfonamides are selectively toxic to bacteria; a bacterium that can also absorb pre-formed folic acid from its environment bypasses the block and becomes resistant. Trimethoprim blocks the very next step of the same pathway, which is why the sulfonamide-trimethoprim combination is synergistic rather than merely additive.

### Membrane-active agents
Polymyxin disrupts the bacterial cytoplasmic membrane, producing a bactericidal effect against Gram-negative organisms; Nystatin, an antifungal rather than antibacterial agent, disrupts the ergosterol-containing fungal cell membrane instead and is the standard treatment for Candida infections. The two are not interchangeable across kingdoms, since bacterial membranes lack the ergosterol Nystatin targets.

### Mechanisms of antibiotic resistance
Bacteria acquire resistance chiefly by mutation or by gaining an R-factor (resistance) plasmid; because a plasmid is mobile, it is specifically the R-plasmid route -- transferred between bacterial cells by conjugation -- that spreads multidrug resistance through a population, including resistance to beta-lactams. Once resistance is present, it typically operates through one or more of several broad mechanism categories: enzymatic drug inactivation (beta-lactamase hydrolysing and inactivating beta-lactam drugs is the classic example), an altered target-site receptor, and altered drug permeability -- categories that frequently combine within the same resistant organism.

### Clinical use: spectrum, combinations, and prophylaxis
Antibiotic spectrum classifies a drug by how many bacterial groups it acts against -- narrow-spectrum agents (vancomycin, clindamycin) target a limited range, while broad-spectrum agents (levofloxacin) act against a wide range of both Gram-positive and Gram-negative organisms; broader spectrum comes at the cost of disrupting the normal bacterial flora more severely, which is why broad-spectrum antibiotic use is the classic setup for Clostridium difficile-associated pseudomembranous enterocolitis once competing flora is suppressed. Combining two antimicrobials can go either way: penicillin plus gentamycin against enterococcal endocarditis is synergistic, because cell-wall damage from the penicillin lets the aminoglycoside reach its ribosomal target more effectively, while penicillin plus tetracycline against pneumococcal pneumonia is antagonistic, because the bacteriostatic tetracycline halts the active division and cell-wall synthesis the bactericidal penicillin needs in order to act. Surgical antimicrobial prophylaxis follows its own logic too: it is indicated when a possible infection would carry a catastrophic consequence, not routinely for every operation or on an arbitrary fixed schedule.

### Pathogenesis: virulence, invasiveness, and transmission
Virulence is the quantitative measure of a pathogen's pathogenicity, often expressed as the number of organisms needed to cause disease; because virulence genes are frequently carried on a plasmid or phage, often clustered in pathogenicity islands, virulence -- like antibiotic resistance -- can be transmitted between bacteria, not fixed to a species. Once established, a pathogen's ability to spread through host tissue (invasiveness) is aided by extracellular spreading enzymes such as collagenase, distinct from coagulase (which tends to localise infection by clot formation) or catalase (which protects against host oxidative killing rather than aiding spread). Pathogens reach a new host chiefly by vertical transmission (mother to child during pregnancy) or by horizontal routes such as airborne spread, of which influenza is the classic epidemic example. When bacteria do reach the bloodstream, the terminology narrows further: bacteraemia names the presence of viable bacteria circulating in the blood, septicaemia adds systemic toxic illness on top of that bacteraemia, and viraemia is the equivalent term for viruses rather than bacteria.

### Pathogenesis: persistence states and diagnosis
Three related but distinct persistence patterns recur in this chapter's questions: a carrier is an apparently healthy individual harboring a pathogen who can transmit it at any time, not only briefly or only during epidemics; a latent infection is a state in which a microbe persists for years causing minimal disease while retaining the ability to reactivate; and an opportunistic pathogen is usually a normal-flora member that turns pathogenic specifically in an immunocompromised host, rather than frequently causing disease in healthy subjects. Diagnosing a bacterial disease in the first place relies on two complementary methods -- direct microscopic demonstration of the organism in pathological fluids, and isolation by culture from exudates or blood -- treated as jointly valid rather than as competing alternatives. Phage therapy, proposed as an antimicrobial alternative, specifically requires virulent (lytic) phages that kill their bacterial host outright; lysogenic or temperate phages integrate into the host genome instead and are unsuitable, since they do not kill the cell and can even worsen pathogenicity by transferring virulence genes. Attenuation -- deliberately reducing a microorganism's virulence while it remains viable -- is the basis of live attenuated vaccines, distinct from inactivation (killing the organism outright) or mere avirulence.

## hold_these
Cell-wall-synthesis inhibitors (penicillins, cephalosporins, vancomycin, monobactams) are the most selective antibacterial class because bacterial peptidoglycan has no eukaryotic counterpart -- this is the general principle of selective toxicity.
Aminoglycosides and tetracyclines both target the bacterial 30S ribosomal subunit; chloramphenicol targets 50S instead, and its main clinical limitation is bone marrow toxicity, not GI absorption.
Quinolones inhibit DNA gyrase (blocking DNA replication); rifampicin inhibits RNA polymerase (blocking transcription) -- these are different nucleic-acid targets.
Sulfonamides competitively block PABA-to-folic-acid conversion; trimethoprim blocks the next step of the same pathway, making the combination synergistic.
Resistance is chiefly acquired by mutation or an R-plasmid, and it is the R-plasmid route, spread by conjugation, that moves resistance between bacteria; beta-lactamase enzymatic inactivation is the classic single-enzyme resistance example.
Virulence is the quantitative measure of pathogenicity and its genes -- often plasmid- or phage-encoded -- can be transmitted between bacteria.
A carrier, a latent infection, and an opportunistic pathogen name three distinct patterns of microbial persistence, not synonyms.

## lose_the_mark
Assuming any protein-synthesis inhibitor shares one ribosomal target -- 30S (aminoglycosides, tetracyclines) and 50S (chloramphenicol) are different subunits with different downstream effects.
Confusing DNA gyrase inhibition (quinolones) with RNA polymerase inhibition (rifampicin).
Treating mutation and R-plasmid acquisition as equally transmissible between bacteria -- only the plasmid route spreads by conjugation.
Assuming virulence is a fixed, non-transmissible trait rather than one that (like resistance) can move on mobile genetic elements.
Using "carrier," "latent infection," and "opportunistic pathogen" interchangeably.
