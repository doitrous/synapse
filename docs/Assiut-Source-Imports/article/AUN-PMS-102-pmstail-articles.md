## summary
The mouth is divided into the vestibule (outside the teeth/gums, inside the lips/cheeks) and the mouth proper. The permanent dentition erupts progressively from age 6, with the third molar (wisdom tooth) last, around 17-21 years. The oesophagus is a muscular tube extending from the pharynx, through the neck, thorax and a short abdominal segment, to the stomach. The stomach's greater curvature is its longer, left, convex border; the lesser curvature its shorter, right, concave one.

## sections
### Definition
The oral cavity (mouth) has two named regions: the vestibule, the slit-like space outside the teeth and gums and inside the lips and cheeks, and the mouth proper, the space inside the teeth and gums extending back to the oropharyngeal isthmus. The oesophagus is the muscular tube, about 25 cm long, that continues the alimentary canal from the pharynx to the stomach. The stomach has two orifices (cardiac and pyloric), two borders (the greater and lesser curvatures) and two surfaces (anterior and posterior).

### Mechanism
Food and fluid pass from the mouth proper, through the oropharyngeal isthmus into the pharynx, and down the oesophagus by peristalsis, crossing the neck, thorax (where it lies posterior to the trachea and left atrium) and a short abdominal segment before it reaches the stomach through the oesophageal hiatus of the diaphragm at the level of T10. In the stomach, food is churned along the greater curvature's longer, distensible arc while the lesser curvature's shorter, straighter border directs it toward the pylorus and, ultimately, the duodenum.

### Key determinants
The permanent dentition (32 teeth total: 2 incisors, 1 canine, 2 premolars, 3 molars per quadrant) begins erupting around age 6 with the first permanent molar; the third permanent molar (wisdom tooth) is the last to erupt, typically 17-21 years, and is often crowded against the second molar. On the stomach, the greater curvature is left-sided and convex; the lesser curvature is right-sided and concave -- getting this pairing backward is the classic error. The pyloric canal (narrow) should not be confused with the pyloric antrum (wider) that precedes it.

### Clinical significance
Impacted or crowded eruption of the third molar (wisdom tooth) is a common source of dental pain and infection in young adults. Peptic ulcers occur more often along the lesser curvature, where the mucosa is thinner and blood supply relatively less abundant than along the greater curvature. Oesophageal constrictions at the cricopharyngeus, the aortic arch/left main bronchus crossing, and the diaphragmatic hiatus are sites where swallowed foreign bodies or an endoscope characteristically lodge or meet resistance.

### Common misconceptions
Treating the mouth as one undivided cavity rather than the vestibule/mouth-proper pair. Assuming eruption of the permanent dentition as a whole begins with the wisdom tooth rather than the first permanent molar around age 6. Reversing the stomach's greater and lesser curvatures, or its pyloric canal and pyloric antrum.

## summary
The paranasal sinuses are air-filled extensions of the nasal cavity's mucosa into the frontal, ethmoid, sphenoid and maxillary bones. The lung is the respiratory system's gas-exchanging organ, entered and left through the hilum on its medial surface, where the structures forming its root pass. The right lung is larger and heavier than the left, which is narrowed by the cardiac notch. The trachea bifurcates into the right and left principal bronchi at the level of the sternal angle.

## sections
### Definition
The paranasal sinuses (frontal, ethmoid, sphenoid, maxillary) are air-filled bony spaces that communicate with the nasal cavity through a small ostium and develop as outgrowths of its respiratory mucosa. The hilum is the triangular area on each lung's medial (mediastinal) surface through which its root -- the bronchus, pulmonary vessels, bronchial vessels, nerves and lymphatics -- enters and leaves. The trachea is a fibrocartilaginous tube, kept patent by 16-20 incomplete (C-shaped) cartilage rings, extending from C6 in the neck to its bifurcation at the sternal angle (T4/T5).

### Mechanism
Air passes through the conducting passages (nose, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi/bronchioles) before reaching the lung's own respiratory portion, where gas exchange occurs; the paranasal sinuses are ventilated and drained through their ostia as air moves through the adjacent nasal cavity. At the sternal angle the trachea splits into the right and left principal bronchi, the right one shorter, wider and more vertical than the left, before each further subdivides within its own lung, entering and leaving all structures through the hilum.

### Key determinants
The right lung is larger, heavier and has three lobes across two fissures (oblique and horizontal); the left lung is smaller, longer and narrower, with only two lobes across one (oblique) fissure and a cardiac notch on its anterior border where the heart projects into it. The trachea's incomplete C-shaped rings are closed posteriorly by the trachealis muscle, not by a full ring of cartilage, allowing the oesophagus (which lies immediately posterior) room to distend during swallowing.

### Clinical significance
Because the right principal bronchus is shorter, wider and more vertical than the left, an inhaled foreign body more often lodges in the right lung. The sternal angle is a reliable external landmark for locating the tracheal bifurcation (carina) clinically, for example when confirming endotracheal tube position. Sinusitis reflects inflammation of the paranasal sinus mucosa, continuous with and draining into the nasal cavity through the same ostia used for normal ventilation.

### Common misconceptions
Treating the lung's whole substance, rather than its own alveoli-bearing branches, as the site of gas exchange, or treating every airway structure as if it exchanged gas. Confusing the hilum (the lung-surface area of entry/exit) with the root (the bundle of structures passing through it). Assuming the trachea's cartilage rings are complete rather than C-shaped and posteriorly incomplete.

## summary
Centrioles organise the mitotic spindle apparatus that carries out cell division. The cell cycle's S phase is when DNA is replicated (doubled); cell death is the irreversible cessation of a cell's vital activities. Adult tissues are renewed by stem cells, which divide asymmetrically to self-renew and produce a differentiating daughter, and by progenitor cells, their lineage-committed, rapidly dividing descendants that go on to terminally differentiate.

## sections
### Definition
The cell cycle runs through G1 (pre-replication growth), S phase (DNA replication, doubling the DNA content), G2 (post-replication growth) and M phase (mitosis, when the doubled chromosomes segregate into two daughter cells), organised at each division by the centriole-derived centrosome's mitotic spindle. Cell death is the irreversible cessation of a cell's vital activities, whether by injury (necrosis) or a programmed process (apoptosis). A stem cell is an undifferentiated cell able to divide asymmetrically; a progenitor cell is its lineage-committed daughter.

### Mechanism
Centrioles duplicate before mitosis and migrate to opposite cell poles, each pair organising a centrosome that nucleates the spindle microtubules; these attach to the chromosomes at their centromeres and pull the sister chromatids, produced during S phase, apart into the two daughter cells. In a self-renewing tissue, a stem cell divides asymmetrically so that one daughter remains a stem cell while the other becomes a progenitor cell; the progenitor cell then divides frequently (transit amplification) before its own progeny terminally differentiate into the tissue's mature, functional cell type.

### Key determinants
DNA content doubles specifically in S phase, not G1, G2 or M -- G1 cells have the unreplicated (2n DNA) amount and G2 cells the already-doubled (4n DNA) amount, both still counted as the same chromosome number until M phase segregates them. Stem cells divide relatively infrequently, preserving the long-term stem-cell pool, while their progenitor-cell daughters divide frequently to rapidly amplify cell numbers; reversing this frequency pairing is the standard exam trap. A stem cell is always undifferentiated and never postmitotic, unlike the terminally differentiated cells a progenitor cell eventually produces.

### Clinical significance
Tissues with a high, ongoing cellular turnover -- skin, gut epithelium, bone marrow -- depend on exactly this stem-cell/progenitor-cell renewal hierarchy to replace cells lost to normal wear. Drugs and radiation that target actively dividing cells (chemotherapy) act preferentially on the rapidly dividing progenitor-cell compartment, which is why such tissues show early toxicity, while the more quiescent stem-cell pool is comparatively protected. Loss of the irreversibility that defines true cell death, for example in some cancers evading apoptosis, allows damaged cells to persist and divide when they normally would not.

### Common misconceptions
Placing DNA replication in G2 rather than S phase. Assuming progenitor cells, like stem cells, divide infrequently -- the opposite is true. Describing a stem cell as differentiated, or as postmitotic, rather than as an undifferentiated cell that keeps dividing throughout life.
