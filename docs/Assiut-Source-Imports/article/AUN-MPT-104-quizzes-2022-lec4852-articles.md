## summary
Antiviral drugs are grouped by the virus family they treat. Against herpesviruses, acyclovir and its oral prodrug valacyclovir inhibit herpes simplex and varicella-zoster, while ganciclovir extends cover to cytomegalovirus (CMV) and is used for CMV retinitis prophylaxis. Hepatitis C is eradicated with sofosbuvir, interferon-alpha and ribavirin — vidarabine is not an HCV drug. Influenza is treated with the M2 blockers amantadine and rimantadine and the neuraminidase inhibitor oseltamivir, which stops viral release from infected cells — lamivudine is an anti-HBV/HIV nucleoside, not an influenza drug. Interferons are protective glycoproteins used in refractory condylomata acuminata, chronic hepatitis B and (with ribavirin) hepatitis C, but not for CMV retinitis, which needs ganciclovir.

## sections
### Definition
Antiviral agents are drugs that suppress a specific step of a virus's replication cycle. The herpesvirus agents (acyclovir, valacyclovir, ganciclovir) are guanosine nucleoside analogues activated by viral thymidine kinase and then incorporated by the viral DNA polymerase to terminate the growing DNA chain. Valacyclovir is the L-valyl ester prodrug of acyclovir, giving much better oral bioavailability. The neuraminidase inhibitor oseltamivir blocks the influenza surface enzyme neuraminidase. Interferons are naturally occurring glycoproteins given as recombinant drugs to induce an antiviral state in neighbouring cells.

### Mechanism
Acyclovir and valacyclovir depend on herpesvirus-encoded thymidine kinase for the first phosphorylation step, which is why they are selectively toxic to infected cells and effective against herpes simplex and varicella-zoster. Ganciclovir adds meaningful activity against cytomegalovirus, making it the drug used to suppress herpetic infection and to provide CMV-retinitis prophylaxis in an immunocompromised patient. Oseltamivir inhibits the viral neuraminidase enzyme so newly assembled H1N1 (and other influenza) virions cannot be cleaved free from the host-cell surface, halting spread — it does not act on viral RNA/DNA replication or uncoating. Interferons bind cell-surface receptors and switch on intracellular enzymes (e.g. a protein kinase and an oligoadenylate synthetase) that degrade viral mRNA and block viral protein synthesis.

### Key determinants
Match the drug to the virus family. Herpesviruses: acyclovir/valacyclovir for HSV and VZV, ganciclovir when CMV must also be covered. Hepatitis C: sofosbuvir (a direct-acting NS5B polymerase inhibitor), interferon-alpha and ribavirin are all effective; vidarabine — an old herpesvirus agent — is not an HCV drug and is the "EXCEPT" answer. Influenza: amantadine and rimantadine (M2 ion-channel blockers) and oseltamivir (neuraminidase inhibitor) are effective; lamivudine is a hepatitis-B/HIV nucleoside and is the influenza "EXCEPT" answer. Interferon indications are condylomata acuminata (genital warts), chronic HBV and HCV (with ribavirin) — but CMV retinitis is treated by ganciclovir, not interferon.

### Clinical significance
A nursing mother with recurrent genital herpes previously controlled topically is stepped up to oral valacyclovir, whose prodrug design gives reliable oral levels. An immunocompromised patient needing both herpetic suppression and CMV-retinitis prophylaxis is given ganciclovir. Oseltamivir is prescribed early in influenza to shorten illness by blocking viral release. HCV therapy is built from sofosbuvir-based direct-acting regimens with interferon/ribavirin in older schedules.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming any nucleoside analogue treats any virus — vidarabine and lamivudine are the classic distractors placed among HCV and influenza drugs respectively. Believing interferon covers CMV retinitis; it does not, ganciclovir does. Thinking oseltamivir blocks viral replication or uncoating rather than the neuraminidase-mediated release step.

## summary
A carcinoma is a malignant tumour of epithelial origin; when that epithelium is glandular the tumour is an adenocarcinoma. Signet-ring carcinoma — malignant cells whose nucleus is pushed to the rim by intracellular mucin — is a form of glandular (adeno)carcinoma. Two independent descriptors are combined for prognosis: grade (how differentiated the cells are microscopically; Broder's grading applies to squamous cell carcinoma) and stage (how far the tumour has spread), so a grade I, stage I neoplasm is both well-differentiated and localized. The malignant ulcer is recognised by raised, everted edges and an indurated base, and a carcinoma is distinguished from a sarcoma at the microscope by whether stroma separates the individual tumour cells.

## sections
### Definition
Carcinoma is the general term for a malignant neoplasm arising from epithelium — covering (surface) or glandular. Adenocarcinoma is specifically the malignant tumour of glandular epithelium. Signet-ring carcinoma is a histological variant of adenocarcinoma in which abundant intracytoplasmic mucin displaces the nucleus to one edge of the cell, giving the "signet ring" appearance, classically in the stomach. Grade describes the degree of cytological differentiation/atypia; stage describes the anatomical extent of spread.

### Mechanism
Grading and staging answer different questions. Grade (I-IV) reads the microscope: a grade I tumour is well-differentiated, closely resembling the parent tissue, whereas a grade IV tumour is anaplastic. Broder's classification grades squamous cell carcinoma by the proportion of differentiated cells. Stage (e.g. TNM, or I-IV) reads the spread: tumour size, nodal involvement and metastasis. Because the two are independent, a neoplasm reported as grade I and stage I is simultaneously well-differentiated (favourable microscopy) and localized (no spread). At the bedside a malignant epithelial ulcer shows raised and everted edges with an indurated (hard) base, unlike the punched-out or sloping edges of benign ulcers. Under the microscope, carcinoma cells are cohesive and arranged in nests with stroma around the groups, whereas in sarcoma the malignant cells are diffuse and stroma runs between individual cells — this stromal relationship is the key differentiating feature.

### Key determinants
Epithelial origin defines carcinoma; glandular epithelial origin defines adenocarcinoma; mucin-displaced nuclei define the signet-ring adenocarcinoma variant. Grade is microscopic differentiation, stage is extent of spread — never conflate them. The malignant ulcer's everted, raised edge and indurated base are the clinical signature. Stroma separating individual cells is the histologic clue that a tumour is a sarcoma rather than a carcinoma.

### Clinical significance
An elderly man with painless haematuria and an ulcerated bladder mass showing atypical cells has a carcinoma (here urothelial). Reporting a tumour as grade I / stage I tells the clinician it is both well-differentiated and localized — the most favourable combination. Recognising the everted edge and indurated base of a malignant ulcer prompts biopsy rather than reassurance.

### Common misconceptions
Treating grade and stage as the same thing; a well-differentiated (low-grade) tumour can still be advanced-stage, and vice versa. Assuming Broder's grading applies to all carcinomas — it is specifically for squamous cell carcinoma. Forgetting that signet-ring carcinoma is glandular (adeno)carcinoma, not a squamous or transitional type.

## summary
Cytotoxic anticancer drugs are classified by whether they act only at a particular phase of the cell cycle (cell-cycle specific) or throughout it (cell-cycle non-specific), and each carries a signature toxicity. Bleomycin complexes with iron and oxygen to cause DNA strand breaks, acts in the G2 phase, and its dose-limiting toxicity is pulmonary fibrosis. Paclitaxel targets microtubules, stabilising them so the mitotic spindle cannot disassemble. Cisplatin's distinguishing adverse effect is nephrotoxicity rather than bone-marrow suppression. Methotrexate is a folic-acid analogue that inhibits dihydrofolate reductase. Cyclophosphamide is an alkylating agent and the classic cell-cycle non-specific drug.

## sections
### Definition
Cell-cycle specific agents kill cells only in a defined phase (e.g. bleomycin in G2, the vinca alkaloids and taxanes in M, antimetabolites in S), so their effect depends on cells passing through that phase. Cell-cycle non-specific agents — the alkylating agents such as cyclophosphamide — damage DNA in any phase, including resting (G0) cells. Each agent also has a signature organ toxicity used to recognise it in a vignette.

### Mechanism
Bleomycin chelates iron and, with molecular oxygen, generates free radicals that produce single- and double-strand DNA breaks; it acts principally in G2 and accumulates the risk of pulmonary fibrosis, so lung function is monitored. Paclitaxel binds beta-tubulin and hyper-stabilises microtubules, freezing the mitotic spindle so cells arrest in mitosis. Cisplatin forms intrastrand DNA cross-links; unusually among cytotoxics its dose-limiting toxicity is nephrotoxicity (managed with hydration), not myelosuppression. Methotrexate, structurally a folic-acid analogue, competitively inhibits dihydrofolate reductase, depleting the reduced folate needed for thymidylate and purine synthesis (an S-phase, antimetabolite effect). Cyclophosphamide is a nitrogen-mustard prodrug activated in the liver; it alkylates DNA guanine independent of cell-cycle phase — the prototype cell-cycle non-specific agent — and can cause haemorrhagic cystitis.

### Key determinants
Recognise the agent by its mechanism-plus-toxicity pair: iron/oxygen DNA breaks + pulmonary toxicity + G2 = bleomycin; microtubules = paclitaxel; nephrotoxicity over marrow suppression = cisplatin; folic-acid analogue = methotrexate; cell-cycle non-specific alkylator = cyclophosphamide. Cell-cycle specific versus non-specific is the top-level sorting question.

### Clinical significance
A patient on a chemotherapy drug that complexes iron and oxygen to break DNA and must be watched for lung side effects is on bleomycin, acting in G2. Choosing cisplatin means anticipating renal, not primarily marrow, toxicity. Knowing methotrexate is a folate analogue explains folinic-acid rescue. Classifying cyclophosphamide as cell-cycle non-specific predicts activity against slowly dividing tumour cells.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming every cytotoxic drug's main toxicity is bone-marrow suppression — cisplatin's is nephrotoxicity. Calling paclitaxel a DNA-binding drug rather than a microtubule agent. Treating cyclophosphamide as cell-cycle specific.

## summary
Mesenchymal (connective-tissue) tumours are named from their tissue of origin plus "-oma" when benign: lipoma (fat), haemangioma (blood vessels), leiomyoma (smooth muscle), chondroma (cartilage) and osteoma (bone). Their malignant counterparts take the suffix "-sarcoma". Two developmental categories sit alongside them: a hamartoma is a mass of disorganised but mature cells indigenous to the site, while a teratoma is a true neoplasm derived from all three germ cell layers (the ovarian dermoid cyst with hair, epithelium and cartilage is the classic example). Embryonic ("blastoma") tumours such as neuroblastoma, retinoblastoma and medulloblastoma arise from immature precursor cells; a seminoma, by contrast, is a germ-cell tumour and is not an embryonic tumour. A haematoma is a collection of blood, not a neoplasm at all.

## sections
### Definition
Benign mesenchymal tumours are named "tissue + -oma": lipoma (adipose tissue), haemangioma (blood vessels), leiomyoma (smooth muscle, classically uterine), chondroma (cartilage) and osteoma (bone). The malignant versions are liposarcoma, angiosarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, chondrosarcoma and osteosarcoma. A hamartoma is an excess of tissue elements normally found at that site but arranged in a disorganised way — mature, indigenous, disorganised. A teratoma is a genuine neoplasm containing tissues from all three germ layers (ectoderm, mesoderm, endoderm). Embryonic/blastoma tumours arise from immature blast cells.

### Mechanism
Mesenchymal naming is mechanical: identify the parent connective tissue, then add "-oma" (benign) or "-sarcoma" (malignant). A congenital reddish cutaneous nodule stable since birth is a haemangioma; a soft, painless, mobile, size-stable subcutaneous mass is a lipoma; a firm, solid, circumscribed, slowly enlarging uterine nodule is a leiomyoma. Developmental tumours turn on cell maturity and germ-layer content: the hamartoma's cells are mature but disorganised and indigenous (so it is not truly neoplastic in the malignant sense), whereas the teratoma is derived from all three germ layers and can be benign (mature cystic, e.g. ovarian dermoid) or malignant. "Malignant tumours arising from glandular cells are called adenocarcinoma" is the naming rule that ties the epithelial and mesenchymal schemes together. Blastomas (neuroblastoma, retinoblastoma, medulloblastoma) are embryonic tumours of immature precursors; seminoma is a testicular germ-cell tumour, not a blastoma. A haematoma is simply extravasated, clotted blood — no neoplastic cells — so it is not a benign tumour even though its name ends in "-oma".

### Key determinants
Tissue of origin plus suffix gives the mesenchymal name. Hamartoma = disorganised but mature indigenous tissue. Teratoma = all three germ layers. Embryonic tumours = neuroblastoma, retinoblastoma, medulloblastoma; seminoma is the odd one out (germ-cell, not embryonic). Haematoma and granuloma are "-oma" words that are not neoplasms — do not classify a haematoma as a benign tumour.

### Clinical significance
Vignettes are solved by matching the described mass to the naming rule: dermoid cyst with hair and mixed tissues on the ovary = teratoma; rubbery fixed uterine nodule = leiomyoma; lifelong stable red skin nodule = haemangioma. Recognising a hamartoma prevents overtreatment of a benign disorganised mass. Knowing seminoma is not a blastoma avoids the "embryonic tumour" trap.

### Common misconceptions
Reading every "-oma" as a benign tumour — haematoma (blood collection) and granuloma (inflammatory) are not neoplasms. Confusing hamartoma (disorganised mature indigenous tissue) with choristoma (normal tissue in an ectopic site) or teratoma (all three germ layers). Grouping seminoma with the embryonic blastomas.

## summary
Cyclosporine is the prototype calcineurin-inhibitor immunosuppressant and the drug of choice for preventing post-transplant rejection. It binds cyclophilin and the complex inhibits calcineurin, blocking the dephosphorylation of NFAT and so the transcription of interleukin-2 in T cells. Its recognisable adverse effects are gingival (gum) hyperplasia, nephrotoxicity, hypertension, hyperuricemia and hyperkalemia — but not hypokalemia. It is metabolised mainly by the hepatic enzyme CYP3A4, which is the basis of many of its drug interactions.

## sections
### Definition
Cyclosporine (ciclosporin) is a cyclic polypeptide immunosuppressant of the calcineurin-inhibitor class. It is used to prevent and treat allograft rejection and in some autoimmune diseases. Calcineurin is the calcium-calmodulin-dependent phosphatase that normally activates the transcription factor NFAT in T lymphocytes.

### Mechanism
Cyclosporine enters the T cell and binds the immunophilin cyclophilin; the cyclosporine-cyclophilin complex inhibits calcineurin. With calcineurin blocked, NFAT is not dephosphorylated and cannot enter the nucleus, so transcription of interleukin-2 (and other cytokines driving T-cell activation) falls. The result is selective suppression of the cell-mediated immune response responsible for graft rejection — distinct from simply inhibiting all white-cell division. Cyclosporine is metabolised chiefly by hepatic CYP3A4, so CYP3A4 inhibitors and inducers markedly change its blood level, and its narrow therapeutic index makes those interactions clinically important.

### Key determinants
Mechanism = inhibition of calcineurin (not calcineurin activation, not blocking IL receptors, not simply inhibiting WBC division). Signature toxicities = gingival hyperplasia, nephrotoxicity, hypertension, hyperuricemia and hyperkalemia; hypokalemia is NOT a cyclosporine effect and is the "does not include" answer. Metabolism = mainly CYP3A4. Role = drug of choice for prevention of post-transplant rejection.

### Clinical significance
A transplant patient on cyclosporine is monitored for renal function, blood pressure, potassium and urate, and counselled about gum overgrowth. Because CYP3A4 governs its clearance, co-prescribing a CYP3A4 inhibitor (e.g. some azoles or macrolides) risks toxicity, while an inducer risks rejection. Cyclosporine remains a first-choice agent for preventing rejection after solid-organ transplantation.

### Common misconceptions
Saying cyclosporine activates calcineurin or blocks interleukin receptors, rather than inhibiting calcineurin. Listing hypokalemia among its toxicities — it tends to raise, not lower, potassium. Assuming it is cleared by CYP2D6 or CYP2C9 rather than CYP3A4.
