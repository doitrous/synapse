## summary
Primordial germ cells are ordinary diploid cells with 46 single-strand chromosomes before meiosis begins. Ploidy is the number of chromosomes per cell, not a measure of DNA content, ribosome number or nuclear number. Synapsis and crossing over between homologous chromosomes occur specifically during prophase of the first meiotic division. Mature gametes carry a haploid number of chromosomes, restoring the diploid number at fertilisation.

## sections
### Definition
Ploidy is the number of complete chromosome sets -- and by extension chromosomes -- present in a cell. Human somatic cells and primordial germ cells are diploid, carrying 46 chromosomes; mature gametes are haploid, carrying 23. Primordial germ cells, which arise in the wall of the yolk sac and migrate to the gonadal ridges, are diploid cells with 46 single-strand (unreplicated) chromosomes -- the same baseline number as any other body cell -- before meiosis begins.

### Mechanism
Meiosis is two successive divisions that reduce the chromosome number from diploid to haploid while generating genetic variation. During prophase of the first meiotic division specifically, homologous chromosomes pair (synapsis) and exchange segments (crossing over), producing genetically varied haploid gametes by the end of the second division. No synapsis or crossing over occurs in prophase of the second meiotic division, since homologous pairs no longer exist once meiosis I has separated them, nor in mitotic prophase, which produces genetically identical daughter cells.

### Key determinants
Ploidy tracks chromosome number, not DNA content -- a cell in G2 or in meiotic prophase I has double the DNA of a G1 cell at the same ploidy, because replication duplicates each chromosome's DNA without yet changing how many chromosomes are counted. Whether a cell is still diploid (primordial germ cells, primary spermatocytes/oocytes before division) or already haploid (secondary spermatocytes/oocytes onward, mature gametes) depends on how many meiotic divisions have been completed, not on which meiotic stage a cell happens to be arrested in.

### Clinical significance
Errors in this system -- non-disjunction during meiosis I or II, producing aneuploid gametes -- underlie the numerical chromosomal aberrations (trisomies, monosomies) responsible for conditions such as Down syndrome. Correct fertilisation of a haploid ovum by a haploid sperm restores the diploid number; fertilisation involving a gamete that failed to reduce properly produces an abnormal ploidy state such as triploidy.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming any germ-line cell must already show a reduced chromosome number simply because it is destined to become a gamete -- primordial germ cells and primary spermatocytes/oocytes are still diploid. Placing crossing over in meiosis II because it "sounds like" the reduction division, when homologous pairing is only possible in meiosis I. Reading "haploid" as meaning only a sex chromosome is present, when a haploid gamete still carries a full set of 22 autosomes plus one sex chromosome.

## summary
Implantation begins once the conceptus reaches the blastocyst stage, roughly six days after fertilisation, and requires a progestational endometrium and an intact trophoblast -- but not the zona pellucida, already shed before this stage. The blastocyst's inner cell mass gives rise to the embryo proper, while its trophoblast differentiates into the amniotic cavity, the chorion and the placenta over the course of the second week. Pregnancy tests detect the hCG secreted by the syncytiotrophoblast soon after implantation.

## sections
### Definition
The blastocyst, reached about six days after fertilisation, is organised into an outer trophoblast and an inner cell mass (embryoblast). Implantation, the embedding of the blastocyst into the endometrium, begins at this stage, once the zona pellucida has already been shed and the trophoblast is competent to invade. It requires a receptive, progestational endometrium with an intact functional layer, and a differentiated trophoblast providing both invasive syncytiotrophoblast and underlying cytotrophoblast.

### Mechanism
The inner cell mass gives rise to the embryo proper and, by around day eight, a small slit-like space -- the amniotic cavity -- appears within it, between the trophoblast and the embryoblast, bounded on the embryoblast side by amnioblasts. Over the same window the trophoblast differentiates into cytotrophoblast and syncytiotrophoblast, and the inner cell mass into epiblast and hypoblast. The chorion, the outermost fetal membrane, forms from the fusion of extraembryonic somatopleuric mesoderm with the trophoblast -- distinct from the amnion (ectoderm plus somatopleuric mesoderm) and the yolk sac wall (endoderm plus splanchnopleuric mesoderm).

### Key determinants
Timing separates the zona pellucida's fertilisation-stage role (blocking polyspermy, protecting cleaving blastomeres) from implantation's own requirements, since the zona is shed before implantation begins. Which extraembryonic mesoderm layer -- somatopleuric or splanchnopleuric -- fuses with which extraembryonic ectoderm or endoderm layer determines whether the resulting membrane is chorion/amnion or yolk sac wall.

### Clinical significance
The syncytiotrophoblast secretes human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) from soon after implantation, which maintains the corpus luteum and its progesterone output until the placenta itself takes over; hCG in maternal urine or serum is the analyte standard pregnancy tests detect. Implantation outside the endometrial cavity produces an ectopic pregnancy, most commonly tubal, presenting with abdominal pain and bleeding after a missed period.

### Common misconceptions
Listing the zona pellucida among implantation's requirements, when it has already been shed by this stage. Assuming the whole blastocyst becomes the embryo, when only the inner cell mass does -- the trophoblast becomes placenta and fetal membranes. Confusing the fabricated distractor "human chorionic corticotropin" with the real hormone, human chorionic gonadotropin.

## summary
Gastrulation converts the bilaminar disc into a trilaminar one during the third week, beginning with the primitive streak; the resulting intraembryonic mesoderm is absent only at the buccopharyngeal and cloacal membranes. The notochord, a mesodermal midline rod lying beneath the neural tube and flanked by the somites, induces the overlying neural plate. Placental attachment is anchored by the outer cytotrophoblastic shell, while chorionic villi are staged from primary through secondary (mesodermal core) to tertiary (vascularised).

## sections
### Definition
Gastrulation is the transformation of the bilaminar embryonic disc into a trilaminar one, the characteristic event of the third gestational week. It begins with the primitive streak, formed in the caudal epiblast; epiblast cells invaginate through it to form endoderm, then intraembryonic mesoderm, so that all three germ layers arise from the epiblast. Intraembryonic mesoderm is present between ectoderm and endoderm everywhere except at the buccopharyngeal membrane and the cloacal membrane, where the two layers remain fused with no mesoderm between them -- the embryo stays bilaminar at exactly these two sites.

### Mechanism
The notochord, a mesodermal, rod-like midline structure, lies beneath the developing neural tube and is flanked on either side by the somites. Its position beneath the neuroectoderm is what lets it induce the overlying ectoderm to thicken into the neural plate, the first step of neurulation. Meanwhile the placenta anchors itself: cytotrophoblast at the villous periphery proliferates through the syncytiotrophoblast to form a continuous outer cytotrophoblastic shell that firmly attaches the chorionic sac to the maternal endometrium, and chorionic villi progress from primary (cytotrophoblast plus syncytiotrophoblast, no core) to secondary (once extraembryonic mesoderm invades the core) to tertiary (once that mesodermal core is vascularised).

### Key determinants
Whether intraembryonic mesoderm is present or absent at a given point of the disc determines whether that point stays bilaminar (buccopharyngeal and cloacal membranes) or becomes trilaminar (everywhere else). Which villous structure does the work of attachment (the outer cytotrophoblastic shell) versus exchange (the tertiary villi themselves) is a frequently confused pair of related but distinct roles.

### Clinical significance
Persistence of the bilaminar buccopharyngeal or cloacal membrane beyond its normal rupture time underlies specific congenital anomalies at each site. Failure of adequate cytotrophoblastic shell attachment or of villous vascularisation contributes to placental insufficiency.

### Common misconceptions
Classifying the notochord as ectodermal because of its close relationship to the neural tube, when it is mesodermal in origin. Crediting the chorionic villi themselves, rather than the outer cytotrophoblastic shell that surrounds them, with the placenta's firm attachment to the endometrium. Crediting syncytiotrophoblast coverage or villous branching -- rather than the appearance of a mesodermal core specifically -- for the primary-to-secondary villous transition.

## summary
Neurulation converts the ectodermal neural plate into the neural tube; fusion begins in the cervical region and proceeds toward both ends, so the cranial neuropore closes (day ~25) before the caudal neuropore. Failure of caudal neuropore closure produces spina bifida, distinct from anencephaly (cranial neuropore failure). The embryonic period, spanning the third to eighth weeks, is organogenesis; by its end the major features of external body form are already recognisable.

## sections
### Definition
Neurulation is the process by which the neural plate -- ectoderm thickened by the underlying notochord's inductive signal -- folds into the neural tube, the primordium of the central nervous system. The neural folds rise on either side of the neural groove, meet, and fuse; fusion begins in the cervical region and proceeds bidirectionally, leaving the cranial and caudal neuropores as the last points to close. The embryonic period as a whole spans the third to the eighth week and is the period of organogenesis, during which organ systems are formed rather than merely growing.

### Mechanism
The cranial neuropore closes at approximately day 25, several days ahead of the caudal neuropore at approximately day 27 to 28. As the folds fuse, cells at their crest separate as the neural crest and migrate to form much of the peripheral nervous system. By the end of the embryonic period (end of the second month), the major features of external body form -- a relatively large head, a more human facial appearance, formed limbs with digits, and early external genital differentiation -- are already recognisable, even though the embryo is still only about 3 cm in crown-rump length.

### Key determinants
Which neuropore fails to close determines which defect results: cranial neuropore failure produces anencephaly, caudal neuropore failure produces spina bifida. Whether a feature is newly formed (embryonic period, organogenesis) or simply maturing and growing (fetal period, from the ninth week) is the key distinction between the two halves of intrauterine development.

### Clinical significance
Spina bifida, from failure of caudal neuropore closure, and anencephaly, from failure of cranial neuropore closure, are the two principal neural tube defects, both reduced in incidence by periconceptional folate supplementation. Recognising that the "major features" milestone falls at the end of the embryonic period, not the fetal period, matters for correctly timing prenatal counselling about organogenesis-sensitive teratogen exposure.

### Common misconceptions
Attributing spina bifida to failure of neural tube formation in general, rather than specifically to failure of caudal neuropore closure. Assuming the two neuropores close at the same time, when the cranial neuropore closes first. Confusing "recognisable by the end of the embryonic period" with "fully mature" -- the features are present in outline by week 8 and continue maturing through the fetal period.

## summary
Ectoderm (surface and neuro-) gives rise to the epidermis and appendages, the nervous system, and sense-organ epithelium -- but not to mesoderm-derived organs such as the kidneys, nor to the endoderm-derived upper anal canal. The neural crest, separating from the fusing neural folds, contributes sensory and autonomic ganglia, Schwann cells, melanocytes and the suprarenal medulla -- but not mesodermal structures such as the bones of the hand or the dura mater.

## sections
### Definition
Surface ectoderm gives rise to the epidermis and its appendages (including the mammary gland), and to the sensory epithelium of the ear, nose and eye; neuroectoderm gives rise to the entire central nervous system via the neural tube. The neural crest, a population of cells that separates from the crest of the fusing neural folds and migrates widely, is also ectodermal in origin, and contributes the sensory, sympathetic and parasympathetic ganglia, Schwann cells, melanocytes and the suprarenal medulla.

### Mechanism
Two germ-layer boundaries repeatedly trip up a "which layer" question built from ectodermal derivatives. First, mesoderm-derived organs that sit near ectodermal structures in a mixed list -- most classically the kidneys, arising from intermediate mesoderm -- are not ectodermal despite the company they keep. Second, the gut tube is endodermal along almost its whole length, with only two ectodermal exceptions at its two ends: the mouth cavity (from the stomodeum) cranially, and the lower anal canal, below the pectinate line (from the proctodeum), caudally -- the upper anal canal above the pectinate line remains endodermal hindgut.

### Key determinants
Whether a structure derives from the neural tube (central nervous system only) or the neural crest (almost everything peripheral -- ganglia, Schwann cells, melanocytes, suprarenal medulla) determines which "except" list it belongs to; bones of the hand and the dura mater are the standard non-crest exceptions used to test this boundary. Whether a gut-tube structure sits above or below the pectinate line of the anal canal determines whether it is endodermal or ectodermal, with matching differences in blood supply, lymphatic drainage and epithelial type.

### Clinical significance
The suprarenal medulla's neural crest origin is why it is functionally and embryologically distinct from the mesodermal suprarenal cortex it sits within, relevant to the pathology of phaeochromocytoma versus adrenocortical tumours. The pectinate line's germ-layer switch in the anal canal underlies the differing presentation, spread and pain sensitivity of tumours above versus below it.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming every organ near the body surface is ectodermal, when the kidneys (mesoderm) are a classic counter-example. Assuming the whole gut tube, including both ends, is uniformly endodermal, when the mouth cavity and lower anal canal are ectodermal exceptions. Assuming the whole suprarenal gland or the whole anal canal shares one germ-layer origin, when each is a fusion of tissues from two different layers.

## summary
Mesoderm differentiates regionally: paraxial mesoderm forms the ~42-44 pairs of somites (sclerotome, myotome, dermatome), intermediate mesoderm forms the kidneys and gonads, and lateral plate mesoderm forms the body wall, limbs, cardiovascular system, genital system and spleen. The suprarenal medulla is the standard exception in a mesoderm-derivatives list, since it is a neural crest, not mesodermal, structure.

## sections
### Definition
Mesoderm, once formed by gastrulation, differentiates regionally along the mediolateral axis. Paraxial mesoderm segments into somites, appearing in craniocaudal sequence through the fourth and fifth weeks and totalling approximately 42 to 44 pairs -- more than the roughly 30-35 vertebrae that ultimately persist, because several of the most caudal somites regress. Intermediate mesoderm, the narrow strip connecting paraxial to lateral plate mesoderm, differentiates into the urogenital ridge and gives rise to the kidneys and gonads. Lateral plate mesoderm forms the body wall, limbs, the linings of the body cavities, and -- through its splanchnic component -- the cardiovascular system, the connective tissue and musculature of the respiratory and genital systems, and the spleen.

### Mechanism
Each somite splits into a ventromedial sclerotome, whose cells migrate to surround the notochord and neural tube and form the vertebrae, and a dorsolateral dermomyotome, which further splits into a dermatome (dermis) and a myotome (skeletal muscle of the trunk and limbs). This is how "somites differentiate into bone, dermis and muscle -- but not epidermis", since epidermis is ectodermal, not somitic.

### Key determinants
Which mesodermal subdivision a structure comes from (paraxial → axial skeleton/muscle/dermis; intermediate → kidneys/gonads; lateral plate → body wall/limbs/cardiovascular/spleen) determines which "except" list it belongs to. The suprarenal medulla is the recurring trap: it sits inside a mesoderm-derived organ (the suprarenal gland) but is itself a neural crest derivative, making it the ectodermal exception in an otherwise-mesodermal list.

### Clinical significance
Errors in somite segmentation or in intermediate mesoderm's differentiation into the urogenital ridge underlie a range of vertebral and renal/gonadal congenital anomalies, and the shared intermediate-mesoderm origin of kidneys and gonads explains their frequently combined embryological pathology (e.g. in renal agenesis with associated gonadal anomalies).

### Common misconceptions
Equating the total somite count (42-44 pairs) with the adult vertebral count, when several caudal somites regress and never contribute a vertebra. Assuming the suprarenal medulla is mesodermal because the suprarenal cortex around it is. Crediting a somite with forming epidermis, when epidermis is ectodermal and only the dermis comes from the dermatome.
