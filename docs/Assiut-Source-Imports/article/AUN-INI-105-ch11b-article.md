## summary
The sixth article of AUN-INI-105's Nematoda chapter covers the adult Loa loa's subcutaneous/connective-tissue habitat and its transient Calabar swelling (contrasted with Onchocerca volvulus's fixed nodule), a set of comparative transmission-route facts (which nematodes infect by ingestion of a larval stage rather than skin penetration; how Enterobius vermicularis is and is not transmitted), practical control and diagnostic techniques (wearing shoes to control hookworm; Baermann's technique for soil larvae), class-level nematode characteristics, a synthesised overview of Wuchereria bancrofti, and several cross-topic comparisons that reach into the cestode and general-parasitology material already taught elsewhere in this bank (retrofection vs internal autoinfection, rodent reservoir hosts, and which helminth eggs are truly infective back to man).

## sections
### Definition
The adult Loa loa worm inhabits subcutaneous and connective tissue, migrating through these planes rather than remaining fixed, which is why it can occasionally be seen crossing the subconjunctiva of the eye and why it produces Calabar swelling wherever it currently passes. This differs from the fixed lymphatic habitat of Wuchereria bancrofti and Brugia malayi and from the fixed subcutaneous nodule of Onchocerca volvulus. Nematodes as a class are characterised by, among other features, organs of attachment that are teeth or cutting plates, such as the hookworm's toothed buccal capsule, a round (not rectangled) cross-section, the absence of any circulatory system, and separate sexes rather than hermaphroditism.

### Mechanism
Dracunculus medinensis, Trichostrongylus colubriformis and Trichinella spiralis all infect man by ingestion of a larval stage, whether an infected Cyclops, larva-carrying vegetation, or encysted larvae in undercooked meat, while Ancylostoma duodenale is the exception among them, infecting instead by skin penetration of its filariform larva. Enterobius vermicularis is transmitted by ingestion of the mature egg, by inhalation of airborne eggs, and by autoinfection (retrofection, in which larvae hatching on the perianal skin migrate back through the anus), but never by skin penetration of a larval stage, unlike the hookworms and Strongyloides stercoralis. Retrofection in Enterobius vermicularis and internal autoinfection in Strongyloides stercoralis are distinct mechanisms, since retrofection occurs entirely at and outside the perianal skin surface while internal autoinfection occurs within the intestinal lumen itself, before the larvae ever reach the perianal skin.

### Key determinants
Because Ancylostoma duodenale infects man by skin penetration of its filariform larva from contaminated soil, the standard control recommendation is wearing shoes. Baermann's technique, which exploits the larvae's own active motility toward warm water, is the method used to detect Ancylostoma larvae in soil, distinct from Knott's technique for blood microfilariae or Stoll's technique for faecal egg counts. Calabar swelling is transient because the causative adult Loa loa continues migrating through subcutaneous tissue, unlike the fixed Onchocerca volvulus nodule, whose worm becomes sedentary within it.

### Clinical significance
Wuchereria bancrofti is transmitted by culicine mosquitoes, chiefly Culex but also Aedes and Anopheles in some endemic settings, its infective stage is the filariform larva deposited during a mosquito bite, its chronic lymphatic disease classically produces oedema and elephantiasis of the lower limbs, and it is diagnosed by demonstrating microfilariae in a peripheral blood film timed to its nocturnal periodicity. Rats act as a definitive host for Hymenolepis nana, Hymenolepis diminuta and Trichinella spiralis together, sustaining an independent rodent reservoir cycle for all three that is distinct from the human-only cycle of a parasite such as Enterobius vermicularis.

### Common misconceptions
Man is a 'blind' (dead-end) intermediate host for Echinococcus granulosus in hydatid disease, and this same dead-end pattern applies to Trichinella spiralis in trichiniasis, since neither the hydatid cyst nor the encysted muscle larva is, in ordinary circumstances, eaten onward by a host that would carry the infection forward. Taenia solium eggs, Echinococcus granulosus eggs and Enterobius vermicularis eggs are all directly infective to man when passed, but Taenia saginata eggs are the exception, since man cannot act as its intermediate host at all and its eggs are instead infective only to cattle.

## hold_these
Adult Loa loa habitat = subcutaneous/connective tissue (migratory); Calabar swelling is transient because the worm keeps moving, unlike the fixed Onchocerca nodule.
Ingestion-of-larva infections: Dracunculus (Cyclops), Trichostrongylus (vegetation), Trichinella (meat) -- Ancylostoma is the EXCEPT (skin penetration instead).
Enterobius transmission = ingestion, inhalation, autoinfection (retrofection) -- never skin penetration.
Retrofection (Enterobius, perianal-skin route) is distinct from internal autoinfection (Strongyloides, intraluminal route).
Hookworm control = wearing shoes; Baermann's technique detects soil larvae (vs Knott's for blood microfilariae, Stoll's for faecal egg counts).
Nematode class characteristics: round in cross-section, no circulatory system, separate sexes, attachment organs = teeth/plates.
Wuchereria bancrofti overview: culicine vector, filariform infective stage, lower-limb elephantiasis, nocturnal blood-film microfilaria diagnosis.
Rats are a definitive host for Hymenolepis nana, Hymenolepis diminuta AND Trichinella spiralis.
Taenia saginata eggs are NOT infective to man (man is not its intermediate host), unlike T. solium, Echinococcus granulosus and Enterobius eggs.

## lose_the_mark
Mixing up Loa loa's migratory subcutaneous habitat with Onchocerca's fixed nodule or the lymphatic-dwelling filariae.
Forgetting that Ancylostoma is the skin-penetrating exception in an otherwise ingestion-of-larva list.
Assuming Enterobius can be transmitted by skin penetration like hookworm or Strongyloides.
Treating retrofection and internal autoinfection as the same mechanism.
Reaching for a food-hygiene or insecticide answer instead of "wearing shoes" for hookworm control.
Confusing Baermann's (soil larvae), Knott's (blood microfilariae) and Stoll's (faecal egg count) techniques.
Attributing a circulatory system or hermaphroditism to nematodes.
Assuming Taenia saginata eggs are infective back to man the way T. solium's are.
