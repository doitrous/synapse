## summary
The first article of AUN-INI-105's Nematoda chapter covers the luminal intestinal roundworms most heavily tested in this bank -- Ascaris lumbricoides, Enterobius vermicularis and Trichuris trichiura -- their classification against Cestoidea, comparative distribution and epidemiology, reproduction mode, egg-hatching sites, and egg/worm descriptive facts.

## sections
### Definition
Trichuris trichiura and Necator americanus are both members of class Nematoda (roundworms) -- elongated, cylindrical, unsegmented worms with a complete digestive tract and separate sexes; Dipylidium caninum, a flat, segmented, hermaphroditic tapeworm, belongs instead to class Cestoidea, not Nematoda. Most intestinal nematodes tested in this bank -- Ascaris lumbricoides, Trichuris trichiura and Enterobius (Oxyuris) vermicularis -- are oviparous: the adult female lays eggs, which pass out of the body (or, for Enterobius, onto perianal skin) to develop further; Trichinella spiralis, Dracunculus medinensis and Loa loa are instead viviparous, releasing live larvae directly.

### Mechanism
After a human host swallows an infective Enterobius vermicularis egg, the larva hatches in the duodenum and migrates to the caecum to mature; after swallowing an infective Ascaris lumbricoides egg, the larva instead hatches more generally within the small intestine. Enterobius vermicularis has an unusually short, direct life cycle: eggs deposited on perianal skin become infective within hours, and can be swallowed again by the same host (retroinfection) or by a close contact almost immediately, with no obligatory period of development in soil, unlike Ascaris, hookworm and Trichuris, whose eggs all require days to weeks maturing in soil first. Ascaris lumbricoides, Trichuris trichiura and Ancylostoma duodenale all lay their eggs directly into the intestinal lumen, from where the eggs pass out with the faeces, but Enterobius vermicularis is the exception, since its gravid female deposits eggs on perianal skin rather than into the gut lumen.

### Key determinants
A fertilized Ascaris lumbricoides egg is unembryonated (immature, a single-cell stage) at the moment it is passed in the host's faeces; it is mamillated (has a rough, bile-stained outer coat, not smooth/without mamillation) and brownish in colour rather than colourless. The egg of Trichuris trichiura is barrel- (lemon-) shaped with bipolar mucoid plugs at each end, brownish in colour (bile-stained), and immature (a single-cell, unembryonated ovum) at the time it is passed in the host's faeces -- these three descriptive facts are jointly true and are often combined into one "all of the above" exam item testing egg recognition. Ascaris lumbricoides is correctly described by three separate facts often combined into one "all of the above" exam item: its egg requires a period of maturation in soil before becoming infective; the adult worm, at up to 20-35 cm in the female, is the largest (giant) intestinal nematode infecting man; and its larva undergoes an obligatory migratory phase (gut wall to liver to lungs to trachea, then swallowed back to the small intestine) before developing into the adult worm.

### Clinical significance
Enterobius vermicularis is called the pin-worm (its slender, pin-like tapering tail gives the common name), not the whip-worm -- that name belongs to Trichuris trichiura, whose anterior end is thin and whip-like. Enterobius (pin-worm) and Trichuris trichiura (whip-worm) both inhabit the caecum and adjacent large-bowel segments; Dracunculus medinensis (guinea worm), despite sharing the same "helminth infecting man" grouping in an exam list, does not inhabit the intestine at all -- its adult female lives in the subcutaneous tissue, most classically of the lower limb, so it is the correct exception whenever a caecum-habitat list includes it as a distractor. Hydatid disease (Echinococcus granulosus cystic hydatidosis) is acquired by ingesting eggs shed by an infected dog, and because the resulting cyst grows slowly over years before becoming clinically apparent, it is not characteristically a disease of childhood -- unlike Schistosoma mansoni, Enterobius vermicularis and Hymenolepis nana, which commonly and characteristically affect children through play-related soil/water contact, close-contact autoinfection and poor hand hygiene respectively.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming every parasite tested alongside cosmopolitan soil/faecal-oral parasites shares the same worldwide distribution -- Heterophyes heterophyes, a small intestinal fluke acquired by eating undercooked brackish-water fish, has a geographically restricted distribution -- the Far East, the Middle East (including Egypt) and parts of the Balkans, wherever its snail and fish intermediate hosts and local raw-fish-eating habits coincide -- unlike the cosmopolitan Ascaris lumbricoides, Hymenolepis nana and Fasciola hepatica. Swapping the pin-worm/whip-worm common names between Enterobius and Trichuris; assuming a fertilized Ascaris egg or a hatching-site fact applies interchangeably across species when each is in fact species-specific.

## summary
The second article of AUN-INI-105's Nematoda chapter covers the hookworms (Ancylostoma duodenale, Necator americanus), Strongyloides stercoralis and Trichostrongylus colubriformis -- their habitat, transmission route, larval morphology, egg description, and the specific points that distinguish Strongyloides from the ordinary skin-penetrating hookworms.

## sections
### Definition
Ancylostoma duodenale, despite its species name suggesting the duodenum specifically, inhabits the small intestine broadly -- attaching by its buccal capsule to the mucosa, most heavily in the jejunum and upper ileum as well as the duodenum -- not the gall bladder or the large intestine. Strongyloides stercoralis is named the dwarf (thread) worm, reflecting the adult female's small, slender size (about 2 mm) compared with other intestinal nematodes. Trichostrongylus colubriformis infects the human host when the infective filariform (third-stage) larva, present on contaminated vegetation, is ingested -- unlike Ancylostoma duodenale, Strongyloides stercoralis and Schistosoma mansoni, whose portal of entry is instead the skin, penetrated directly by their own infective larvae.

### Mechanism
The filariform (third-stage, infective) larva of Ancylostoma duodenale is sheathed -- it retains the cuticle of the preceding (second-stage) moult as a loose outer sheath -- and shows positive thermotropism and negative geotropism (it moves toward warmth and away from gravity, upward on moist soil or vegetation), the behaviour that brings it into contact with skin for penetration. The filariform larva of Strongyloides stercoralis differs from that of Ancylostoma duodenale in three linked morphological features, often tested together as an "all of the above" item: it is unsheathed (has shed the second-stage cuticle, unlike Ancylostoma's retained sheath); it has a distinctively notched (forked) tail tip; and it is shorter overall, at roughly 500-600 micrometres, compared with Ancylostoma's longer filariform larva.

### Key determinants
Ancylostoma duodenale infects man by skin penetration of its filariform larva, not by ingestion of eggs, and its adult female lays eggs that pass out with faeces in an immature (unsegmented, unembryonated) state -- not already infective. The egg itself is thin-shelled and translucent (colourless), a standard triad of descriptive features (thin-shelled, translucent, immature) tested together as an "all of the above" item, distinguishing it from the thicker-shelled, mamillated, bile-stained Ascaris egg. The egg of Trichostrongylus colubriformis is larger than, but otherwise resembles, the egg of Ancylostoma duodenale (both are oval, thin-shelled and immature when passed) -- the key distinguishing feature tested is that the Trichostrongylus egg already contains a morula (an early cluster of several blastomeres/dividing cells) at the time it is passed, whereas the Ancylostoma egg is still at an earlier, less-developed cleavage stage.

### Clinical significance
Ancylostoma duodenale (the "Old World" hookworm) and Necator americanus (the "New World" hookworm) are both hookworms causing the same disease (ancylostomiasis/necatoriasis) by the same general mechanism, but they differ chiefly in geographical distribution: A. duodenale predominates in the Mediterranean, Middle East, North Africa and parts of Asia, while N. americanus predominates in the Americas, sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Asia. Strongyloides stercoralis differs from Ancylostoma duodenale in three linked ways, often combined into one "all of the above" exam item: it alone is capable of autoinfection (its own rhabditiform larvae can moult to the infective filariform stage within the host's bowel or perianal skin, re-penetrating without ever leaving the body); its diagnostic larval stage recovered in fresh stool is the rhabditiform (not filariform) larva; and it alone has a free-living (indirect) generation capable of completing an entire life cycle in soil, independent of a human host.

### Common misconceptions
Ascaris lumbricoides, Ancylostoma duodenale and Strongyloides stercoralis larvae all migrate through the lungs as part of their normal life cycle, so their larvae can occasionally be recovered from sputum during heavy or acute infection (Loeffler's syndrome); Hymenolepis nana, a cestode with a direct life cycle confined to the intestine (no obligatory tissue or pulmonary migratory phase), is the exception -- its larvae are never found in sputum. Visceral larva migrans (VLM) follows ingestion of embryonated eggs of Toxocara canis or Toxocara cati -- the ascarid roundworms of dogs and cats -- not eggs of the human-specific Ascaris lumbricoides, Trichuris trichiura or Enterobius vermicularis.

## summary
The third article of AUN-INI-105's Nematoda chapter covers the tissue-invasive and lymphatic nematodes -- Trichinella spiralis, Capillaria philippinensis, Dracunculus medinensis and Wuchereria bancrofti -- their transmission routes, infective and diagnostic stages, and the microfilarial features used to differentiate the filarial species.

## sections
### Definition
Man is infected with Trichinella spiralis by eating undercooked pork containing encysted larvae in the animal's striated muscle; the encysted larva is the infective stage swallowed. In the human small intestine the larva matures to an adult, and the female releases newborn larvae directly (Trichinella is viviparous) that penetrate the gut wall, enter the bloodstream and are carried throughout the body, finally encysting selectively in active striated (skeletal) muscle -- not liver, lung or the intestinal lumen -- where they can persist and eventually calcify. The egg of Capillaria philippinensis is peanut- (barrel-)shaped with flattened, slightly protruding bipolar plugs resembling opercula at each end, and is immature (unembryonated) when passed in stool.

### Mechanism
The adult Dracunculus medinensis (guinea worm) inhabits subcutaneous tissue, most classically of the lower limb, where the gravid female eventually forms a painful blister that ruptures on contact with water to release larvae. Its intermediate host is the copepod Cyclops, ingested in contaminated drinking water. Historical drug treatment for dracunculiasis has included metronidazole, diethylcarbamazine and thiabendazole (mainly as adjuncts to slow, gradual mechanical extraction of the worm), but not niclosamide, which is instead an anticestode (tapeworm) drug with no role against this nematode. The infective stage of Wuchereria bancrofti is the third-stage (filariform) larva, which develops from the ingested microfilaria within the mosquito's thoracic flight muscles and then migrates forward to the labium (the sheath around the mosquito's proboscis), from where it is deposited onto the skin at the next blood meal and actively penetrates the bite wound.

### Key determinants
The microfilaria of Wuchereria bancrofti is sheathed (it retains the egg-shell membrane as a loose covering), and its posterior end is tapered and free of nuclei -- the opposite of the "posterior end full of nuclei" distractor sometimes offered. It circulates with nocturnal periodicity (most numerous in peripheral blood between roughly 10 pm and 2 am, matching the night-biting habit of its vector), and it is ingested during a blood meal by Culex mosquitoes, not by Simulium (the Onchocerca volvulus vector, which does not transmit W. bancrofti). The microfilaria of Wuchereria bancrofti differs from that of Loa loa in three linked morphological features, often combined into one "all of the above" exam item: W. bancrofti's sheath is loose (versus Loa loa's tightly fitting sheath); its body shows gentle, smooth curves (versus Loa loa's kinky, irregular curves); and its posterior end is free of nuclei (versus Loa loa's nuclei extending to the very tip of the tail).

### Clinical significance
Wuchereria bancrofti, Brugia malayi and Loa loa microfilariae all circulate in peripheral blood with a defined periodicity (nocturnal for W. bancrofti and Brugia malayi, diurnal for Loa loa), timed to match each species' respective biting-vector activity. Onchocerca volvulus microfilariae are the exception: they live in the skin and subcutaneous tissue rather than in the bloodstream, so they show no blood periodicity at all -- a fact that also explains why Onchocerca is diagnosed by skin snip, not by a timed blood film.

### Common misconceptions
Confusing Trichinella's pork-borne transmission with the fish-borne transmission of Capillaria philippinensis or Heterophyes heterophyes; confusing the Capillaria philippinensis egg's flattened, operculum-like bipolar plugs with the smoothly rounded bipolar mucoid plugs of the similarly barrel-shaped Trichuris trichiura egg. Assuming niclosamide, a standard antihelminthic, works against every helminth; it is specifically an anticestode drug and has no activity against the nematode Dracunculus medinensis. Confusing the circulating microfilaria (the diagnostic stage found in human blood) with the filariform larva (the infective stage found in the mosquito vector, ready for transmission).

## hold_these
Trichinella: pork -> encysted larva (infective) -> newborn larvae to striated muscle.
Capillaria philippinensis egg: peanut-shaped, flattened bipolar plugs, immature.
Dracunculus: subcutaneous habitat, Cyclops intermediate host, not treated with niclosamide.
W. bancrofti infective stage: filariform larva in mosquito labium.
W. bancrofti microfilaria: sheathed, nucleus-free tip, nocturnal periodicity, Culex vector.
W. bancrofti vs Loa loa microfilaria: loose sheath/gentle curves/nucleus-free tip vs tight sheath/kinky curves/nuclei to the tip.
Onchocerca volvulus microfilariae: skin-dwelling, no blood periodicity (the periodicity exception).

## lose_the_mark
Confusing pork-borne Trichinella with fish-borne Capillaria or Heterophyes.
Confusing Capillaria and Trichuris egg morphology.
Assuming niclosamide treats Dracunculus medinensis.
Confusing the infective filariform larva with the diagnostic microfilaria.
Reversing sheath tightness, curve pattern or posterior-nuclei assignment between W. bancrofti and Loa loa.
Assuming Onchocerca volvulus microfilariae show blood periodicity like the other filariae.
