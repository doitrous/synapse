## summary
A solution's tonicity toward a red blood cell is set by its non-penetrating solute concentration relative to plasma, not by osmolarity in the abstract. Normal saline (0.9% NaCl) is isotonic and leaves cell volume unchanged; a more concentrated solution such as 1.0% NaCl is hypertonic and draws water out, shrinking (crenating) the cell, while a more dilute solution is hypotonic and swells or lyses it.

## sections
### Definition
Tonicity is the effect a solution has on cell volume once osmotic equilibrium is reached, determined by the concentration of solute that cannot cross the cell membrane. Isotonic saline, 0.9% NaCl (about 154 mmol/L), matches the red blood cell's own effective osmotic pressure, so no net water movement occurs and the cell keeps its normal volume and shape. A hypertonic solution -- one with a higher non-penetrating solute concentration than the cell's interior, such as 1.0% NaCl relative to the 0.9% isotonic reference -- draws water out of the cell by osmosis, shrinking it, a change termed crenation. A hypotonic solution has a lower non-penetrating solute concentration than the cell, so water enters, and the cell swells and may rupture (haemolyse) if the solution is markedly hypotonic.

### Mechanism
Water crosses the red cell membrane freely through aquaporin channels, moving down its own concentration gradient (equivalently, up the solute concentration gradient) until the osmotic pressures on both sides equalise. Because NaCl in the extracellular fluid does not freely cross the membrane in the short timescale relevant to a bench test, its concentration alone sets the direction of water movement for a saline test solution: more concentrated than 0.9% pulls water out (hypertonic, crenation), less concentrated pushes water in (hypotonic, swelling/haemolysis), and exactly 0.9% produces no net movement (isotonic).

### Key determinants
The comparison that matters is always against the cell's own effective (isotonic) reference, not an absolute concentration value in isolation -- 1.0% NaCl is only hypertonic because it is read relative to the 0.9% isotonic benchmark, and the same logic ranks any other percentage.

### Clinical significance
Isotonic (0.9%) saline is the standard intravenous fluid precisely because it does not shift fluid into or out of red blood cells; giving a markedly hypotonic intravenous fluid risks red cell swelling and haemolysis, while a markedly hypertonic one risks crenation and cell shrinkage, both clinically relevant complications of fluid therapy.

### Common misconceptions
Treating "more concentrated" and "hypertonic" as automatically synonymous without the explicit comparison to the isotonic reference. Confusing tonicity (the physiological effect on cell volume) with total osmolarity measured in a laboratory sense, which can differ when some solute freely crosses the membrane -- though for NaCl and red blood cells the simple concentration ranking against 0.9% holds directly.

## summary
A free radical is any species carrying an unpaired electron, which makes it highly reactive. The oxygen-derived free radicals (ROS) are superoxide, the hydroxyl radical and hydrogen peroxide; nitric oxide is a separate, nitrogen-centred radical, and carbohydrates cannot themselves form radicals (unlike oxygen, lipids and xenobiotics). Free radicals damage cell membranes by lipid peroxidation -- polyunsaturated fatty acids are most vulnerable -- cause DNA mutation and predispose to cancer, and inactivate enzymes and other proteins by oxidising their functional groups; protein glycosylation is a separate, non-oxidative process and not itself a free-radical effect.

## sections
### Definition
A free radical is any atom, ion or molecule carrying an unpaired electron in its outer orbital, which makes it intensely reactive as it seeks to pair that electron by attacking another molecule. The oxygen-derived free radicals, collectively called reactive oxygen species (ROS), are the superoxide anion (O2·-), the hydroxyl radical (OH·, the most reactive of the group) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), which is grouped with the true radicals because it readily generates the hydroxyl radical through the Fenton reaction. Oxygen itself, lipids and xenobiotics (foreign chemicals, including many drugs and toxins) can all generate or propagate free radicals; carbohydrates cannot themselves form free radicals.

### Mechanism
Free radicals attack polyunsaturated fatty acids in cell membranes by lipid peroxidation, because the multiple double bonds of a polyunsaturated chain carry easily abstractable allylic hydrogens, making PUFAs the lipid class most vulnerable to this chain-reaction damage. Free radicals also attack DNA directly, producing mutations that predispose to cancer, and attack proteins, oxidising functional groups (including active-site residues of enzymes) and causing loss of biological activity. Protein glycosylation -- the non-enzymatic attachment of a sugar to a protein, as seen in glycated hameoglobin (HbA1c) -- proceeds by a separate, non-oxidative chemical mechanism and is not itself one of the free radical's direct effects.

### Key determinants
Classifying a given reactive species correctly matters for exam purposes: nitric oxide is a genuine radical but is nitrogen-centred, not one of the oxygen-derived ROS (superoxide, hydroxyl radical, hydrogen peroxide) this topic tests as a set. Among the four major biomolecule classes commonly listed as radical sources or targets (oxygen, lipids, xenobiotics, carbohydrates), carbohydrates are the one that cannot itself generate a free radical.

### Clinical significance
Lipid peroxidation of membrane PUFAs underlies much of the tissue injury attributed to oxidative stress in ischaemia-reperfusion injury, chronic inflammation and ageing, which is why antioxidant defences (enzymatic and dietary) are of ongoing clinical interest. DNA damage from free radicals is one recognised route to carcinogenesis, alongside other mutagenic mechanisms.

### Common misconceptions
Classifying nitric oxide as an oxygen-derived free radical (ROS) rather than as a separate, nitrogen-centred radical. Assuming any organic molecule, including carbohydrates, can form a free radical. Attributing protein glycosylation to free-radical damage rather than recognising it as a distinct, non-oxidative process.
