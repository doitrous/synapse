## summary
AUN-INI-105's tenth chapter opens the Cestode (tapeworm) topic with the vocabulary and host relationships a student needs before any single species is tested in detail: the segmented, hermaphroditic body plan and scolex anatomy shared by all cestodes, the dog as definitive host for three separate parasites, and the contrasting host specificity of the two medically important Taenia species -- T. saginata, which never uses man as an intermediate host, and T. solium, which uniquely can use man as both definitive and intermediate host.

## sections
### Definition
Cestodes (tapeworms) are segmented, hermaphroditic flatworms -- distinct from the leaf-shaped, unsegmented trematodes and the cylindrical, unsegmented nematodes -- whose body is divided into a head, the scolex, used for attachment, followed by a chain of proglottid segments. The intestinal cestodes of man are found in the small intestine, in the jejunum and ileum, rather than the large intestine or appendix.

### Mechanism
The dog is the definitive host for three medically important helminths taught together for this reason: Toxocara canis, the dog roundworm whose eggs cause visceral larva migrans in man; Echinococcus granulosus, whose adult tapeworm lives in the dog intestine while its larval hydatid cyst develops in sheep or, accidentally, man; and Multiceps multiceps (Taenia multiceps), whose adult lives in the dog while its larval coenurus cyst develops in sheep.

### Key determinants
Taenia saginata (the beef tapeworm) belongs to the order Cyclophyllidea and inhabits the small intestine of its only definitive host, man -- never the dog, which is the definitive host for other cestodes such as Echinococcus granulosus. Its scolex is globular with four suckers but, unlike Taenia solium's armed scolex, carries no hooks. Cattle, not pigs, serve as its sole intermediate host, and man cannot act as an intermediate host for T. saginata: ingesting its eggs produces no infection at all, because human tissue does not support the parasite's oncosphere the way it does for T. solium.

### Clinical significance
Taenia solium (the pork tapeworm) uses the pig as its intermediate host, where ingested eggs develop into the larval cysticercus cellulosae in muscle tissue. Man acquires taeniasis, the adult intestinal worm, by eating undercooked pork containing cysticercus cellulosae -- the parasite's human-infective stage for taeniasis -- and is thereby its definitive host. Uniquely among the human cestodes, man can also act as T. solium's intermediate host, so that a single person may carry both the adult worm in the intestine and larval cysts in tissue.

### Common misconceptions
Describing cestodes as leaf-shaped or rounded instead of segmented; assigning the dog as definitive host for only one of Toxocara canis, Echinococcus granulosus or Multiceps multiceps instead of all three; assuming T. saginata can cause cysticercosis in man the way T. solium does; naming cysticercus bovis instead of cysticercus cellulosae as T. solium's pork-borne infective larva.

### Related concepts (reused)
Several facts this window tests are already covered by pending Helwan HU-GIT-301 parasitology concepts, reused here via overlay rather than re-minted: Hymenolepis nana's ability to complete its life cycle without an intermediate host, through autoinfection via its own cysticercoid larva, and its status as the commonest childhood cestode; Taenia solium's own autoinfection route to cysticercosis; the gravid-proglottid lateral-uterine-branch count that best differentiates T. saginata from T. solium; and cysticercus bovis as T. saginata's infective larva in undercooked beef.

## summary
This article covers the two-intermediate-host life cycle of Diphyllobothrium latum (the fish tapeworm), the flea-borne cysticercoid larva of Dipylidium caninum (the dog tapeworm), and sparganosis, the tissue infection caused by the plerocercoid of the related but distinct species Diphyllobothrium mansoni.

## sections
### Definition
Diphyllobothrium latum, the fish tapeworm and a pseudophyllidean cestode, has a three-stage life cycle spanning two intermediate hosts: its egg hatches in water to release a free-swimming coracidium, which is ingested by a first intermediate host, the copepod Cyclops, and develops there into a procercoid larva; when the infected Cyclops is eaten by a second intermediate host, a fresh-water fish, the procercoid develops further into the plerocercoid larva, the stage infective to man and to other fish-eating mammals such as dogs, which act as reservoir hosts. Cyclops, in the same way, is also the sole intermediate host of the unrelated Dracunculus medinensis (Guinea worm) and the first intermediate host of Diphyllobothrium mansoni, which goes on to use a second vertebrate host as well.

### Mechanism
Dipylidium caninum, the double-pored dog tapeworm, uses the flea as its intermediate host: flea larvae ingest D. caninum eggs, and the parasite develops within the flea into a cysticercoid larva, the stage infective to the definitive host (typically a dog or cat, occasionally a child) that swallows the infected flea.

### Clinical significance
Sparganosis is the infection of human tissue by the plerocercoid larva (sparganum) of Diphyllobothrium mansoni, a related but distinct species from Diphyllobothrium latum -- whose own plerocercoid instead causes an ordinary intestinal infection when eaten in fish -- and is caused by the larval, not the adult, worm form.

### Common misconceptions
Reversing D. latum's larval sequence (coracidium, procercoid, plerocercoid) or its host sequence (Cyclops, then fish); naming cysticercus instead of cysticercoid as Dipylidium caninum's infective larva; attributing sparganosis to D. latum or to an adult worm instead of D. mansoni's plerocercoid larva.

### Related concepts (reused)
Two further facts this window tests are already covered by pending Helwan HU-GIT-301 parasitology concepts, reused here via overlay rather than re-minted: Diphyllobothrium latum lays the only operculated egg among the cestodes, and its infective stage for man is the plerocercoid larva carried in fresh-water fish.

## summary
This article covers Echinococcus granulosus's dog-only adult habitat, egg-borne (not cyst-borne) transmission to man and liver-predominant hydatid distribution, then steps back to compare how many and which intermediate hosts different platyhelminths need -- a recurring EXCEPT-style exam pattern across this chapter.

## sections
### Definition
Echinococcus granulosus's adult tapeworm lives in the intestine of the dog, its definitive host, which is why the species is excluded whenever a question asks which cestodes are found as adults in man's own intestine -- unlike Taenia and Hymenolepis species, man never harbours the adult E. granulosus worm. Man becomes infected instead by ingesting the parasite's eggs, shed in dog faeces and contaminating food or water, never by eating a hydatid cyst itself; the ingested egg's oncosphere then forms a hydatid cyst in human tissue, most frequently in the liver, followed by the lung. In this role man is only ever an accidental, dead-end intermediate host for the tissue (hydatid) form of the disease.

### Mechanism
The number and identity of intermediate hosts a platyhelminth requires varies by species and is a common source of EXCEPT-style exam questions. Diphyllobothrium latum, Heterophyes heterophyes and Metagonimus yokogawai all require two intermediate hosts, but Taenia solium is the exception among them, needing only the single pig as its sole intermediate host. Herbivorous animals such as sheep serve as intermediate hosts for Multiceps multiceps and Echinococcus granulosus, and cattle for Taenia saginata, but Metagonimus yokogawai's intermediate hosts (a snail, then a fish) are not herbivores, making it the exception in that grouping. Pig is a source of human infection for Taenia solium, Trichinella spiralis and Balantidium coli, but not for Taenia saginata, whose source is cattle instead.

### Common misconceptions
Listing Echinococcus granulosus among the intestinal cestodes of man; assuming hydatid infection follows eating a cyst rather than ingesting eggs; assuming every platyhelminth needs the same number of intermediate hosts, or that every herbivore-borne or pig-borne parasite list has no exception.

### Related concepts (reused)
One further fact this window tests is already covered by a pending Helwan HU-GIT-301 parasitology concept, reused here via overlay rather than re-minted: sheep, not man or the dog, is Echinococcus granulosus's commonest intermediate host, with man an accidental intermediate host and never the definitive host.
