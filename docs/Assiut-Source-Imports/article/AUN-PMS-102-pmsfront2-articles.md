## summary
The body wall is built as a numbered sequence of soft-tissue envelopes -- skin first, superficial fascia second, deep fascia third -- and the skin itself is two layers, an outer avascular epidermis over an inner vascular dermis. Individual organs carry their own local version of a fascial covering, named separately: the kidney's is the renal (fibrous) capsule, functionally its own deep fascia.

## sections
### Definition
Teaching on the coverings of the body wall numbers them as three envelopes, from outside in: the skin, the superficial fascia immediately deep to it, and the deep fascia deep to that, immediately superficial to the muscles it invests. The skin itself divides into two layers: the epidermis, an outer stratified keratinised epithelium with no blood supply of its own, and the dermis, an inner layer of dense irregular connective tissue that is vascular and nourishes the epidermis sitting on top of it. Superficial fascia is loose, fatty connective tissue; deep fascia is a non-elastic, compact collagenous membrane, described fully in this module's own sibling fascia article.

### Mechanism
The envelope sequence follows tissue density inward: loose and fatty first (superficial fascia), then dense and inelastic (deep fascia), because a body wall needs a compressible layer to let skin move and carry small vessels, and then a tough anchoring layer to fix muscles and vessels in place. The epidermis-over-dermis arrangement follows the same logic at a smaller scale -- an avascular barrier layer (epidermis) sitting on the vascular layer (dermis) that feeds it by diffusion, since a barrier layer with its own blood supply would be a poor barrier.

Individual organs extend the same idea locally. The kidney sits within peri-renal fat and is wrapped in renal fascia, but immediately against its own surface is a thin fibrous sheath, the renal (or true) capsule -- the organ's own local equivalent of deep fascia, holding its shape the same way deep fascia holds a limb's muscles in place.

### Key determinants
Order of the body wall's three envelopes, outside in: skin, superficial fascia, deep fascia.

The skin's own two layers, outside in: epidermis (outer, avascular, epithelial) and dermis (inner, vascular, connective tissue).

An organ's own capsule is that organ's local example of deep fascia; for the kidney this is specifically the renal (fibrous) capsule, distinct from the peri-renal fat and the renal fascia that lie further out.

### Clinical significance
Because the epidermis has no blood supply, a wound confined to it (a graze) does not bleed and heals without scarring; a wound reaching the vascular dermis does bleed and can scar. Because deep fascia -- and an organ's own capsule -- is inelastic, swelling confined within it (for example within the renal capsule in an inflamed or obstructed kidney) produces pain from the pressure the unyielding sheath applies, rather than accommodating the swelling freely.

### Common misconceptions
Naming the dermis as the outer layer of the skin. It is the epidermis that is outer and avascular; the dermis is inner and vascular.

Treating an organ's capsule as unrelated to deep fascia. Functionally, an organ's own fibrous capsule is that organ's local instance of the same non-elastic, shape-holding tissue that deep fascia is elsewhere in the body.
