## summary
AUN-INI-105's sixth chapter opens general virology with the vocabulary a student needs before any specific virus family is taught: what a virus's genome can and cannot be, what distinguishes a naked virus from an enveloped one, which named DNA-virus families break the expected rule, what a bacteriophage is, and how capsid symmetry (icosahedral, helical or complex) is classified.

## sections
### Definition
A virus's genetic material is either DNA or RNA, never both together -- one of the defining structural features separating a virus from any cellular organism. A naked (non-enveloped) virus consists of the nucleocapsid only, with no surrounding envelope, distinguishing it from an enveloped virus, whose nucleocapsid is wrapped in a host-membrane-derived lipoprotein envelope. A bacteriophage (phage) is, at its most basic definition, a virus that lives as a parasite specifically on bacteria, infecting and often lysing them.

### Mechanism
Among the DNA virus families, most (Polyomaviridae, Poxviridae, Papillomaviridae, Herpesviridae, Adenoviridae) carry double-stranded DNA, but Parvoviridae is the single-stranded DNA exception -- the only DNA virus family with a single-stranded genome. Separately, when a mixed list of "DNA viruses" is presented for recognition (for example Parvo, Herpes and Pox), Paramyxoviridae is the odd one out because it is an RNA virus family, not a DNA one, despite superficially resembling the others in exam-list company. Viral capsid symmetry is classified as icosahedral (most DNA and many RNA viruses), helical (most enveloped RNA viruses, wrapping the nucleic acid in a coil) or complex (an irregular, brick-shaped structure not reducible to either simple form) -- poxviruses, including smallpox virus (variola), are the classic example of complex symmetry among the medically important virus families.

### Key determinants
Because naked viruses lack the fragile lipid envelope, they are more resistant than enveloped viruses to lipid solvents, detergents and drying -- a structural fact with practical consequences for disinfection and transmission. The single-nucleic-acid-type rule (DNA or RNA, never both) and the DNA-family strandedness exception (Parvoviridae) are both tested by asking students to spot the one item that breaks an otherwise consistent pattern.

### Clinical significance
Recognising which virus family a pathogen belongs to -- its genome type, envelope status and capsid symmetry -- predicts practical properties: environmental resistance (naked viruses survive fecal-oral transmission routes better), disinfectant susceptibility (enveloped viruses are inactivated by alcohol and detergents), and even vaccine strategy (structural classification underlies antigen choice).

### Common misconceptions
Selecting "DNA and RNA" as if a virus carries both types of nucleic acid at once, when in fact every virus has exactly one. Confusing "naked virus" with "incomplete virus" (a defective particle missing genes) or a loosely used, non-standard term such as "simple virus." Assuming all DNA virus families share the same strandedness, when Parvoviridae is the single-stranded exception. Assigning poxvirus icosahedral or helical symmetry instead of its actual complex, brick-shaped morphology. Confusing "bacteriophage" with "fungus" or "commensal," neither of which names a virus that infects bacteria.

## summary
This second article of AUN-INI-105's General virology chapter covers what the capsid does and does not do, how the envelope's larger glycoprotein spikes differ from the capsid's small capsomere subunits, the general size and parasitism properties shared by all viruses, and the two size extremes -- poxvirus (largest) and parvovirus (smallest) -- that anchor the viral size range.

## sections
### Definition
The capsid -- the protein coat surrounding the viral genome -- performs several true structural functions: its tightly packed surface proteins protect the enclosed viral genome against inactivation by host nucleases, and it participates in attachment of the virus to receptors on a susceptible host cell. Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites -- they can only replicate inside a living host cell, never independently -- typically ranging in size from about 20 to 300 nm, small enough to require electron microscopy for direct visualization. Among medically important viruses, poxvirus (for example vaccinia) is the largest, at roughly 200-450 nm and visible even by light microscopy, while parvovirus is the smallest, at roughly 18-26 nm, among the smallest DNA viruses known.

### Mechanism
The viral envelope's protein component consists of larger glycoprotein spikes (peplomers) embedded in the lipid bilayer, which is a structurally different arrangement from the capsid's own small protein subunits, the capsomeres -- describing the envelope as made of "small protein subunits" confuses it with the capsid. For enveloped viruses specifically, antigenic variation that produces different serotypes is driven by variation in the envelope's own surface glycoproteins, not by variation in the underlying capsid protein, since the envelope is the outermost, host-facing, immunologically exposed layer. Absorbing nutrients by active transport is NOT a function of the capsid, or of any part of a virus, because a virus has no independent metabolism -- it is an obligate intracellular parasite that depends entirely on the host cell's own metabolic machinery once inside.

### Key determinants
Because viruses lack the bacterial-specific cell-wall and metabolic targets that antibiotics act on, they are not susceptible to antibiotics; growth on a bacteriological medium such as blood agar likewise does not apply to viruses, which require living host cells rather than an inert nutrient medium. Poxvirus sitting near the upper end of the viral size range and parvovirus near the lower end are the two size extremes most frequently paired in exam questions.

### Clinical significance
Distinguishing envelope glycoprotein variation from capsid protein variation matters clinically because vaccine and diagnostic-antigen choice for enveloped viruses (for example influenza's hemagglutinin/neuraminidase) targets the envelope spikes, the site of the antigenic variation that drives new serotypes and periodic re-infection.

### Common misconceptions
Attributing metabolic functions such as active nutrient transport to the capsid or to any viral structure, forgetting that viruses have no independent metabolism outside a host cell. Describing the envelope as composed of "small protein subunits" (that phrase describes capsomeres, part of the capsid, not the envelope). Attributing serotype differences among enveloped viruses to capsid protein variation instead of envelope glycoprotein variation. Calling viruses "obligatory extracellular parasites" (the correct term is intracellular). Reversing the size pairing (calling parvovirus the largest or poxvirus the smallest).

### Related structures
A capsomere is the individual, small protein subunit that assembles together with many others to build the capsid -- distinct from a nucleic acid unit or a replication-related viral protein, and distinct from the envelope's own larger glycoprotein spikes described above. A virion is the complete, extracellular, infectious virus particle -- for a naked virus, the nucleocapsid alone; for an enveloped virus, the nucleocapsid plus its surrounding envelope -- the unit a plaque assay or hemagglutination test ultimately counts.

## summary
This third article covers where viral replication happens (nucleus vs. cytoplasm, with poxvirus the exception), what viral replication does and does not require (host macromolecules and living tissue, not binary fission, antibiotics or synthetic media), the host origin of the tRNA and ribosomes a virus borrows to translate its own mRNA, and antigenic drift, the point-mutation mechanism behind a virus's gradual antigenic change.

## sections
### Definition
As a general rule, DNA viruses assemble and replicate in the host cell's nucleus (where host DNA polymerase and other nuclear machinery are available), while RNA viruses replicate in the host cell's cytoplasm. Viral replication requires the host cell's own macromolecules (ribosomes, enzymes, nucleotide precursors, energy) because a virus has no independent metabolic or protein-synthesis machinery of its own, and it can therefore only proceed inside living tissue (living host cells), never in dead tissue, soil, or any cell-free synthetic culture medium. Antigenic drift is a gradual change in a virus's surface antigens caused by the accumulation of point mutations in the genes encoding those surface proteins, most classically described for influenza's hemagglutinin and neuraminidase.

### Mechanism
Poxvirus is the standard exception on the DNA side, replicating entirely in the cytoplasm despite being a DNA virus, because it is large enough to encode its own complete replication machinery rather than depending on nuclear enzymes. A virus multiplies inside a living cell by directing the synthesis of viral proteins using its own viral mRNA, but that mRNA is translated using the HOST cell's own tRNA and ribosomes -- a virus encodes neither its own tRNA nor its own ribosomes, and must borrow the host's translation apparatus entirely. Because replication depends on hijacked host processes rather than the bacterial-type target antibiotics act on, viral replication is not prevented by antibiotics; and because a virus is assembled from pre-made components inside the cell rather than growing and splitting as a whole organism, it does not replicate by binary fission the way a bacterium does.

### Key determinants
Antigenic drift is distinct from antigenic shift, which is instead produced by reassortment -- the exchange of whole genome segments between two different viral strains co-infecting the same cell -- and which requires a segmented genome, unlike drift. Without a host cell's ribosomal machinery, viral proteins simply cannot be made, which is part of why a virus is an obligate intracellular parasite.

### Clinical significance
The site of replication (nucleus vs. cytoplasm) and the dependence on host machinery both explain why antiviral drugs are harder to design selectively than antibacterial drugs: a virus's replication steps are deeply intertwined with the host cell's own, leaving fewer virus-only targets. Antigenic drift is the reason influenza vaccines require near-annual reformulation, since accumulated point mutations gradually erode the match between circulating strains and last season's vaccine antigen.

### Common misconceptions
Applying the DNA-virus-in-nucleus rule to poxvirus without exception. Assuming a virus supplies its own tRNA and ribosomes for translating its mRNA, when in fact only the mRNA is viral and the translation apparatus is entirely host-derived. Assuming viruses replicate by binary fission like bacteria, that antibiotics can prevent viral replication, or that a virus can multiply in dead tissue, soil, or a cell-free synthetic medium. Confusing antigenic drift (mutation, gradual, can occur in any virus) with antigenic shift (reassortment, abrupt, requires a segmented genome).

## summary
This fourth article gathers the chapter's non-virus-classification content that still sits within the same page range of the bank: prions (protein-only infectious particles), herpesvirus structure, two stray fungal-biology facts (ergosterol, opportunistic infection risk factors) the source bank places within this chapter, HIV's classification as a Retroviridae retrovirus with reverse transcriptase as its hallmark, and the plaque assay used to quantitate infectious virus titer.

## sections
### Definition
A prion is an infectious protein particle that contains no detectable nucleic acid of any kind (no DNA, no RNA, no plasmid) -- it is protein-only, distinguishing it from a virus (which always has DNA or RNA), a viroid (uncoated circular RNA, no protein) and a defective virus (which does have nucleic acid, just an incomplete genome). Herpesviruses are classified as icosahedral-symmetry, enveloped, double-stranded DNA viruses -- a combination that distinguishes them from RNA viruses with helical symmetry, from non-enveloped DNA viruses, and from the "brick-shaped" complex-symmetry morphology of poxviruses. HIV belongs to the family Retroviridae, and as such it is correctly described both as an RNA virus and, more specifically, as a retrovirus.

### Mechanism
Because a prion lacks nucleic acid, it cannot "multiply" in the sense a virus does through nucleic acid replication and protein synthesis; instead it propagates by inducing a conformational (misfolding) change in the host's own normal cellular protein, a mechanism responsible for transmissible spongiform encephalopathies. The special, defining feature of all retroviruses -- including HIV -- is reverse transcriptase, an RNA-directed DNA polymerase that copies the virus's RNA genome into DNA before integration into the host chromosome, and detecting reverse transcriptase activity in a patient's blood serves as a laboratory marker of retroviral (including HIV) infection. The plaque assay quantitates the titer of infectious virus particles by counting the discrete zones of cell lysis ("plaques") that form in a susceptible cell monolayer, each plaque arising from a single infectious virion -- unlike electron microscopy (visualizes but does not by itself quantitate infectivity), hemagglutination (measures total viral particle count including non-infectious ones) or PCR (measures genome copies, not infectious particles).

### Key determinants
The fungal cell membrane's characteristic sterol is ergosterol, the fungal counterpart of the cholesterol found in animal cell membranes -- not cholesterol itself, and not the lipopolysaccharide that instead belongs to the Gram-negative bacterial outer membrane; chitin, another commonly confused fungal component, is a cell-wall polysaccharide, not a membrane constituent. Opportunistic fungal infections are initiated when host defenses are compromised: defective cell-mediated immunity (fungi are classically controlled by T-cell-mediated responses), prolonged broad-spectrum antibiotic use (which disrupts competing normal flora and allows fungal overgrowth), and chemotherapy (which is both immunosuppressive and neutropenic) are all recognized predisposing factors; anaerobic wound conditions are instead a classic predisposing factor for anaerobic bacterial infection, not for opportunistic fungal infection.

### Clinical significance
Recognizing that prions propagate by protein misfolding, not nucleic-acid replication, explains why prion diseases resist the sterilization methods (including standard autoclaving) that inactivate viruses and bacteria, and why no nucleic-acid-targeted antiviral strategy can touch them. Detecting reverse transcriptase activity in blood is used as a practical laboratory marker of retroviral infection, and identifying ergosterol as the fungal-specific membrane sterol is the structural basis for azole and polyene antifungal drug selectivity, since these drugs exploit a target (ergosterol) absent from human cell membranes.

### Common misconceptions
Attributing a nucleic acid (even a small one) to prions, which by definition have none; describing prion propagation as "multiplication" or "replication" in the viral sense rather than as protein-misfolding conversion. Assigning herpesviruses a "brick shape" or complex symmetry (that describes poxviruses, not herpesviruses). Classifying HIV under an unrelated virus family (Rhabdoviridae, Togaviridae, Paramyxoviridae); describing reverse transcriptase as anything other than an RNA-directed DNA polymerase. Assuming fungal membranes use cholesterol like animal cells, or confusing the membrane sterol (ergosterol) with the cell-wall polysaccharide (chitin). Including anaerobic wound conditions among fungal-infection risk factors by confusing it with anaerobic bacterial infection risk. Confusing plaque assay (counts infectious units) with hemagglutination or PCR (which count total particles or genome copies without distinguishing infectious from non-infectious virions).

## summary
This fifth and final article of the chapter covers the named interactions that can occur between two viruses co-infecting the same cell (reassortment, phenotypic masking, complementation, exaltation) and the standard classification of viral mutants by phenotype (conditional-lethal, plaque-size).

## sections
### Definition
Genetic reassortment is the exchange of whole genome segments between two different strains of a segmented-genome virus co-infecting the same host cell, producing a hybrid progeny virus -- it can only happen in viruses whose genome is naturally divided into separate segments. Phenotypic masking (phenotypic mixing) occurs when two related viruses co-infect the same cell and the genome of one virus becomes packaged inside capsid proteins contributed by the other. A defective virus is a virus particle missing one or more genes necessary for a complete, independent replication cycle; such a virus can only replicate and spread if the same cell is co-infected with a helper virus that supplies the missing function.

### Mechanism
This rescue process -- a defective virus replicating because a helper virus's gene products complement its own missing ones -- is called complementation, and it is distinct from recombination (exchange of genetic sequence) or interference (one virus suppressing another's replication). Exaltation is the phenomenon in which one virus, through co-infection, enhances the growth (replication) of a second, different virus in the same cell -- the reverse of interference, in which one virus instead suppresses another's replication. A conditional-lethal mutant is a virus mutant that can multiply under some (permissive) conditions but not under other (restrictive) conditions -- temperature-sensitive mutants are the classic example, replicating at a lower permissive temperature but not at a higher restrictive one. A plaque-size mutant, by contrast, is a mutant that induces a change in the diameter of the zone of viral lysis (the plaque) it produces in an infected cell monolayer, a phenotype used to distinguish mutant from wild-type virus without reference to temperature sensitivity at all.

### Key determinants
Reassortment produces a genuinely hybrid genome and is the mechanism underlying antigenic shift; phenotypic masking changes only the outer coat borrowed for one generation, leaving the genome unchanged; complementation and exaltation are both cooperative interactions between two viruses that leave each virus's own genome unchanged, differing only in which virus's replication is being helped (the defective virus's, in complementation, versus a fully competent second virus's, in exaltation).

### Clinical significance
Reassortment between animal and human influenza strains co-infecting the same host is the mechanism behind pandemic-causing antigenic shift, making this genetic interaction directly relevant to pandemic preparedness. Recognizing defective viruses and their dependence on helper-virus complementation is clinically relevant for hepatitis D virus, a defective virus that can only establish infection in a patient already infected (or simultaneously co-infected) with hepatitis B virus, its helper.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming reassortment can occur in any virus regardless of genome structure; confusing reassortment (segment exchange, abrupt) with mutation-driven antigenic drift (gradual, does not require a segmented genome). Confusing phenotypic masking (borrowed coat, unchanged genome) with reassortment (genome segments actually exchanged) or with a point mutation. Confusing complementation (helper virus supplies a missing function, allowing the defective virus to replicate) with recombination or interference. Confusing exaltation (growth enhancement) with interference (growth suppression, the opposite phenomenon), or mistaking it for a type of mutation or genetic reassortment. Confusing a conditional-lethal mutant (defined by permissive-vs-restrictive replication) with a plaque-size mutant (defined by an altered lysis-zone diameter).

## hold_these
Reassortment = segment exchange, needs segmented genome, underlies antigenic shift.
Phenotypic masking = one virus's genome packaged in another's capsid, genome unchanged.
Defective virus needs a helper virus; complementation = the rescue process.
Exaltation = one virus enhances another's growth (opposite of interference).
Conditional-lethal mutant = permissive-vs-restrictive replication; plaque-size mutant = altered lysis-zone diameter.

## lose_the_mark
Applying reassortment to a non-segmented-genome virus.
Confusing phenotypic masking with an actual genome exchange.
Confusing complementation with recombination or interference.
Confusing exaltation with interference or with a mutation.
Confusing conditional-lethal and plaque-size mutant definitions.
