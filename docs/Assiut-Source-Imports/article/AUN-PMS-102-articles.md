## summary
Endoderm forms the gut epithelium and its budded derivatives (respiratory epithelium, thyroid parenchyma, liver, pancreas, bladder/urethral epithelium) but not the ectodermal mammary gland. The cloacal membrane is bilaminar, derived from both ectoderm and endoderm. Fourth-week folding carries the septum transversum, heart, prochordal plate and pericardial cavity onto the ventral surface while the notochord stays dorsal, and the midgut keeps communicating with the yolk sac through the vitelline duct, in whose wall primordial germ cells first form.

## sections
### Definition
The endoderm is the innermost primary germ layer, lining the primitive gut tube and giving rise to the epithelial component of every structure that buds from it: the respiratory tract epithelium, the parenchyma of the thyroid, parathyroid, liver and pancreas, and the epithelial lining of the urinary bladder and urethra. The mammary gland, despite often appearing in the same exam list, is an ectodermal derivative, arising from the ectodermal mammary ridge. The cloacal membrane, at the disc's caudal end, is bilaminar -- derived from both ectoderm and endoderm fused with no intervening mesoderm.

### Mechanism
Cephalocaudal and lateral folding during the fourth week converts the flat trilaminar disc into a cylindrical body: the septum transversum, developing heart, prochordal plate/oropharyngeal membrane and pericardial cavity are all carried onto the ventral surface by this process, while the notochord -- the fixed axial structure folding takes place around -- stays in its dorsal position throughout. As folding proceeds, the dorsal part of the yolk sac is incorporated into the embryo as the primitive gut, and the midgut remains connected to the remaining, extra-embryonic yolk sac through the vitelline (omphalomesenteric) duct, running through the umbilical cord. Primordial germ cells, the precursors of the gametes, first become identifiable in the wall of this same yolk sac before migrating to the gonadal ridges.

### Key determinants
Whether a structure "turns ventral" during folding or stays fixed distinguishes the notochord from its neighbouring structures. Whether a membrane is bilaminar (cloacal, oropharyngeal) or trilaminar determines its site and later fate. The vitelline duct's persistence, rather than its normal presence, is what turns this same connection into a clinical finding (Meckel's diverticulum).

### Clinical significance
A persistent vitelline duct produces Meckel's diverticulum, a true diverticulum on the antimesenteric border of the distal ileum, the most common congenital gastrointestinal anomaly. Failure of cloacal membrane rupture or of folding itself underlies a range of ventral body-wall and cloacal anomalies.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming every gland near the trunk midline is endodermal (the mammary gland is the classic ectodermal exception). Assuming the notochord, as a central landmark, must itself be repositioned by folding, when it is in fact the fixed axis folding occurs around. Assuming primordial germ cells arise directly in the gonadal ridge, when that is their migratory destination, not their site of origin.

## summary
Embryonic age during the first month is expressed by somite count; a CRL of 5-8 mm corresponds to about 5 weeks. By the end of the fourth week the main external features are the somites and pharyngeal arches, and limb buds first appear during the second month. The fetal period runs from the beginning of the ninth week to birth, with fetal length tracked by CRL throughout; the head is about half the CRL at the start of the third month, when the face also becomes more human-looking. Quickening is felt around the fifth month, and viability is classically placed at the seventh month.

## sections
### Definition
Two methods date an early embryo: somite counting during the first month, and crown-rump length (CRL) once the embryo is large enough to measure -- a CRL of 5-8 mm corresponds to about 5 weeks. By the end of the fourth week the main external features are the somites and pharyngeal arches; limb buds appear later, during the second month. The fetal period (beginning of the ninth week to birth) follows the embryonic period, and fetal size throughout it is conventionally expressed as CRL.

### Mechanism
Early in the fetal period the head is disproportionately large: at the start of the third month it constitutes about half the CRL, a ratio that falls steadily as trunk and limb growth outpaces head growth. Over the same third month the face changes from its more primitive early appearance to a distinctly more human-looking profile as the eyes migrate toward the front of the face. Fetal movements begin well before they are perceptible, but the mother's first perception (quickening) classically occurs during the fifth month. By the seventh month, fetal lung and CNS maturation (including the start of surfactant production) has advanced enough that a fetus born during or after this month has a good chance of survival.

### Key determinants
Which dating method applies depends on gestational stage -- somites in the first month, CRL from then through the fetal period. The head:CRL ratio and facial appearance are markers specifically anchored to the third month; quickening to the fifth; the viability threshold to the seventh.

### Clinical significance
Quickening remains a rough clinical check on gestational age, alongside ultrasound dating. The seventh-month viability threshold underlies obstetric decisions around threatened preterm birth and antenatal corticosteroid timing.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming CRL is used to age the embryo from the earliest stages, when somite counting is the first-month method. Assuming limb buds are present by the end of the fourth week, when they are a second-month feature. Confusing the timing of the fetus's first actual movements (much earlier) with quickening, the mother's first perception of them (fifth month).

## summary
The chorion is the embryonic contribution to the human, haemochorial placenta; maternal and fetal blood never mix directly across the placental barrier. hCG maintains the corpus luteum through the first trimester. The amnion (ectodermally related, non-vascular) and yolk sac (endodermal, disappearing by the fifth month) are the two principal fetal membranes; the allantois contributes to the bladder, and the umbilical cord carries 2 arteries and only 1 vein in Wharton's jelly. The placenta itself develops from chorion frondosum and decidua basalis, gains blood vessels at the tertiary-villus stage, and at full term is a discoid, ~500 g, upper-segment structure -- placenta accreta and previa are its two classic abnormal-attachment variants.

## sections
### Definition
The definitive placenta has a fetal part, the chorion (chorion frondosum, from trophoblast and extraembryonic mesoderm), and a maternal part, the decidua basalis; the human placenta is haemochorial, meaning maternal blood bathes the trophoblast directly in the intervillous space. The amnion is a thin, tough, transparent, non-vascular membrane (ectodermally related) containing pale watery fluid; the yolk sac is its endodermal counterpart, disappearing by about the fifth month. The allantois contributes to the urinary bladder (as the urachus), and the umbilical cord contains 2 umbilical arteries, 1 umbilical vein and Wharton's jelly.

### Mechanism
Chorionic villi mature through three stages -- primary (trophoblast only), secondary (mesodermal core added) and tertiary (blood vessels differentiate within that core) -- with vessel presence specifically marking the tertiary stage. Villi on the embryonic pole proliferate into the chorion frondosum, which together with the decidua basalis forms the placenta proper; villi on the abembryonic pole degenerate into the smooth chorion laeve. Human chorionic gonadotropin, secreted by the syncytiotrophoblast, rescues and maintains the corpus luteum's progesterone output through the first trimester, before the placenta itself takes over. Exchange between mother and fetus happens entirely across the placental barrier -- fetal vessels never attach directly to maternal ones, so "mixing of fetal and maternal blood" is never a normal feature of placental development.

### Key determinants
A full-term placenta is discoid, weighs about 500 g, covers about 25% of the internal uterine surface, and implants in the upper uterine segment; lower-segment attachment is placenta previa, not a normal variant. Placenta accreta is a distinct abnormality of depth, not position -- villi invading the myometrium because the decidua basalis is deficient.

### Clinical significance
Placenta previa (abnormal lower-segment attachment) and placenta accreta (abnormal myometrial invasion) are the two placental-attachment abnormalities most tested at this stage, and are mechanistically distinct from one another (position vs depth). A persistent, patent allantois/urachus produces urachal cysts or fistulae. Loss of corpus luteum function (and the hCG that maintains it) before the placenta can take over progesterone production causes early pregnancy loss.

### Common misconceptions
Treating "haemochorial" as meaning fetal and maternal blood mix -- it means maternal blood directly bathes the trophoblast surface, not that the two circulations connect. Assuming the amnion, like the yolk sac, is endoderm-derived. Assuming the umbilical cord is symmetric with 2 veins and 2 arteries, when it normally carries only 1 vein. Confusing placenta previa (abnormal site) with placenta accreta (abnormal depth of invasion).

## summary
Conjoined twins are named by their site of union -- craniopagus for twins joined at the head -- and arise from very late, incomplete division of monozygotic twins, distinct from the unrelated vanishing-twin phenomenon. Rubella, varicella and cytomegalovirus are all recognised teratogenic viruses. Amniocentesis is a standard prenatal diagnostic method. Cri du chat syndrome, a partial deletion of chromosome 5, is a structural chromosomal aberration distinct from the numerical aberrations (monosomy, trisomy) taught alongside it.

## sections
### Definition
Conjoined twins result from incomplete division of the embryonic disc in monozygotic twinning occurring very late, and are named by site of union: craniopagus (head), thoracopagus (chest), pygopagus (buttocks/sacrum). A "vanishing twin" is an unrelated, early-pregnancy resorption phenomenon, not a conjoined-twin type. Rubella virus, varicella (chickenpox) virus and cytomegalovirus are each independently established teratogens capable of crossing the placenta and disrupting development.

### Mechanism
Amniocentesis -- transabdominal aspiration of amniotic fluid, typically from about the fifteenth week -- yields fetal cells for karyotyping and biochemical markers such as alpha-fetoprotein, and is the standard, specifically-named method of prenatal diagnosis among options that also include non-specific imaging (X-ray, CT), which is not the tool used for this purpose in pregnancy. Cri du chat syndrome is caused by a partial deletion of the short arm of chromosome 5 (5p-) -- a structural aberration, mechanistically distinct from numerical aberrations such as an extra chromosome 21 (Down syndrome) or absence of an X chromosome (Turner syndrome).

### Key determinants
Site of conjoined union (head/chest/buttocks) determines the specific term used. Which teratogenic mechanism applies (structural deletion vs numerical non-disjunction) determines which syndrome is being described.

### Clinical significance
Recognising the classic teratogenic viruses (rubella, varicella, CMV) informs counselling and immunity screening in pregnancy. Amniocentesis remains a core prenatal diagnostic tool for chromosomal and neural tube abnormalities. Cri du chat's characteristic cry and craniofacial features are a recognised clinical presentation distinct from trisomy or monosomy syndromes.

### Common misconceptions
Treating "vanishing twin" as a conjoined-twin variant. Assuming only one virus among rubella/varicella/CMV is "the" correct teratogen when a question groups all three. Confusing cri du chat's deletion mechanism with a numerical chromosome-count abnormality.

## summary
The nervous system divides into the CNS (brain and spinal cord) and PNS. The spinal cord gives rise to 31 pairs of spinal nerves (8C, 12T, 5L, 5S, 1Co). The neuron is the structural and functional unit of the nervous system. Gray matter is composed of nerve cell bodies embedded in neuroglia, distinct from white matter's myelinated axon tracts. ## sections
### Definition
The nervous system is divided into the central nervous system (CNS) -- the brain and the spinal cord -- and the peripheral nervous system (PNS) -- the cranial and spinal nerves and their ganglia. The spinal cord, continuous with the brain through the foramen magnum, gives rise to 31 pairs of spinal nerves, one per spinal cord segment: 8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral and 1 coccygeal. This 31-pair count is distinct from the 33 vertebrae (sacral fusion and a single coccyx account for the difference) and from the 12 pairs of cranial nerves.

### Mechanism
The neuron is the structural and functional unit of the nervous system: it has a cell body (soma), dendrites, and an axon, and every nervous system task is carried out by neurons acting alone or in circuits, supported but not substituted for by glial cells. Nervous tissue is organised into gray matter, composed of nerve cell bodies embedded in neuroglia, and white matter, composed of myelinated axons collected into tracts. Each spinal nerve forms from a dorsal (sensory, afferent) root and a ventral (motor, efferent) root, making it mixed; efferent fibres carry impulses away from the CNS to effector organs, while afferent fibres carry impulses from peripheral receptors toward the CNS.

### Key determinants
Whether a fibre carries impulses toward the CNS (afferent) or away from it (efferent) determines its functional class. Whether a structure is a complete neuron or one of its processes, or a supporting glial cell, determines whether it is the nervous system's functional unit. Whether tissue is composed of cell bodies (gray matter) or myelinated tracts (white matter) determines its colour and its role as processing centre versus conduction pathway.

### Clinical significance
Damage confined to a single spinal nerve root produces a predictable dermatomal or myotomal deficit, because each of the 31 pairs supplies a defined segment. Lesions of gray matter (e.g. anterior horn cell disease) and white matter (e.g. demyelinating disease) produce different clinical pictures because they damage cell bodies versus conducting tracts respectively.

### Common misconceptions
Naming the brainstem and cerebrum, rather than the brain and spinal cord, as the two CNS subdivisions. Naming a neuron's own axon, or a glial cell, as the nervous system's functional unit instead of the complete neuron. Confusing efferent (motor, away from CNS) with afferent (sensory, toward CNS) direction.

## summary
The base of the heart is formed mainly by the left atrium, distinct from the apex (left ventricle). Arterioles are the final distributing vessels, delivering oxygenated blood to capillaries, where exchange with extracellular fluid actually occurs. The pulmonary artery is a classic anatomic end artery. Lymphatic vessels have a beaded appearance from their numerous valves.

## sections
### Definition
The heart is pyramid-shaped, with an apex formed by the left ventricle and a base, facing posteriorly, formed mainly by the left atrium (with a smaller right-atrial contribution), receiving the four pulmonary veins. Arteries carry blood away from the heart under pulsatile pressure; arterioles, their smallest branches, are the final distributing vessels that regulate flow into capillary beds and deliver oxygenated blood to them. Veins carry blood back toward the heart under low, non-pulsatile pressure, draining via venules from the capillary bed. The lymphatic system is a parallel one-way drainage network of vessels with a beaded, or nodular, appearance produced by their numerous valves.

### Mechanism
An anatomic (true) end artery, such as the pulmonary artery, supplies its territory with no effective anastomosis to a neighbouring artery's supply, so its occlusion causes ischaemia of the tissue it feeds; well-anastomosed arteries such as the femoral, common carotid and lingual arteries are not end arteries in this strict sense. Because arterial pressure is pulsatile and venous pressure is not, a severed artery pulsates and spurts blood while a severed vein does neither; arterial walls are correspondingly thicker than venous walls, and it is the medium and large veins of the limbs specifically -- not arteries -- that carry valves enforcing one-directional flow toward the heart. Actual exchange of oxygen and metabolic waste with the extracellular fluid takes place at the capillary bed, between the arteriole and venule ends, not within either vessel type itself.

### Key determinants
Which chamber forms a named heart landmark (base = left atrium, apex = left ventricle) determines the correct answer to "which chamber" questions. Whether an artery has collateral anastomoses determines whether it is a true end artery. Wall thickness, pulsatility and the presence of valves distinguish arteries from veins; the presence of numerous valves gives lymphatics their beaded appearance.

### Clinical significance
Occlusion of a true end artery, such as a pulmonary artery branch (pulmonary embolism), causes infarction because no collateral supply rescues the downstream tissue -- unlike occlusion of a well-anastomosed artery. Recognising pulsatile, spurting bleeding as arterial versus non-pulsatile venous bleeding guides emergency haemorrhage control. Lymphoedema and lymphangitis both reflect disease of the beaded, valved lymphatic network.

### Common misconceptions
Assigning the heart's base to the left ventricle (that is the apex) or the apex to the left atrium. Believing venules, not capillaries, are where oxygen/waste exchange with extracellular fluid occurs. Reversing the wall-thickness comparison between arteries and veins, or placing valves in arteries instead of the limb veins. Assuming lymphoid tissue is absent from the digestive tract wall or that lymphatics penetrate every tissue without exception.

## summary
The kidneys extend from T12 to L3, retroperitoneally; the right kidney's anterior relations include the liver. Renal pyramids lie in the medulla, not the cortex. The ureter is continuous superiorly with the renal pelvis and inferiorly with the bladder. The male urethra's four parts, from proximal to distal, are intramural (preprostatic, surrounded by the internal urethral sphincter), prostatic, membranous (the narrowest part) and spongy.

## sections
### Definition
The kidneys are retroperitoneal organs extending from the level of T12 to L3, with the right kidney sitting slightly lower than the left because of the liver above it. On coronal section each kidney shows an outer cortex and inner medulla; the renal (Malpighian) pyramids, cone-shaped with bases toward the cortex and apices (papillae) projecting into the minor calyces, lie in the medulla. The right kidney's anterior relations include the right suprarenal gland, the liver, the second part of the duodenum and the hepatic flexure; the left kidney's differ (spleen, stomach, pancreas, jejunum, splenic flexure).

### Mechanism
Urine passes from the minor calyces to the major calyces to the funnel-shaped renal pelvis, which is continuous inferiorly with the ureter; the ureter itself is continuous inferiorly with the bladder, which it enters obliquely, and has three points of physiological constriction along its course. In the male, the urethra has four parts: intramural (preprostatic), surrounded by the internal urethral sphincter (smooth muscle, autonomic, prevents retrograde ejaculation); prostatic, running through the prostate; membranous, the narrowest part, passing through the urogenital diaphragm and surrounded by the external urethral sphincter (skeletal muscle, voluntary); and spongy (penile), the longest part, running through the corpus spongiosum.

### Key determinants
Whether an anterior relation belongs to the right kidney (liver, duodenum, hepatic flexure) or the left (spleen, pancreas, splenic flexure) determines the correct answer to "anterior relation" questions. Whether a urethral part is proximal (intramural, internal sphincter) or distal (membranous, external sphincter, narrowest) determines which sphincter and calibre apply. Cortex versus medulla location distinguishes cortical structures (glomeruli, convoluted tubules) from medullary ones (pyramids, loops of Henle, collecting ducts).

### Clinical significance
The membranous urethra's narrow calibre and fixed position make it a common site of injury in pelvic fracture and a point of resistance during catheterisation. The kidney's retroperitoneal position and vertebral-level relations guide surgical and radiological localisation (e.g. counting ribs/vertebrae on imaging). Ureteric constriction points are the classic sites where renal calculi impact.

### Common misconceptions
Placing renal pyramids in the cortex rather than the medulla. Assigning the liver as an anterior relation of the left kidney (it is the right) or the spleen to the right (it is the left). Naming the membranous, prostatic or spongy part as surrounded by the internal urethral sphincter instead of the intramural part, or naming a part other than membranous as narrowest. Believing the ureter drains into the urethra rather than the bladder.

## summary
The ovary is suspended from the lateral pelvic wall by the suspensory ligament of the ovary, carrying the ovarian vessels, distinct from the medially-anchoring ovarian ligament. The normal uterine position combines two angulations, anteversion and anteflexion, described together as "anteverted and anteflexed."

## sections
### Definition
The ovary is anchored by two separate ligaments: the suspensory ligament of the ovary (infundibulopelvic ligament), a peritoneal fold suspending it from the lateral pelvic wall and carrying the ovarian vessels, nerves and lymphatics, and the ovarian ligament proper, which anchors it medially to the uterus. The uterus's normal position combines anteversion -- the forward angle between the cervix's long axis and the vagina's long axis -- and anteflexion -- the forward angle between the uterine body's long axis and the cervix's long axis -- described together as "anteverted and anteflexed."

### Mechanism
The ovarian medulla, not the cortex, is the more vascular region of the two, and the ovary itself is almond-shaped rather than diamond-shaped; its surface is covered by the "germinal" epithelium, a historical name from an earlier, now-rejected belief that it was the source of germ cells. The combined anteverted-and-anteflexed position tips the uterine fundus forward over the bladder; the opposite, abnormal position is retroversion (backward tilt), which can be associated with symptoms such as pelvic pain or dyspareunia in some individuals.

### Key determinants
Which ligament suspends the ovary from the pelvic wall (suspensory ligament, carrying vessels) versus which anchors it to the uterus (ovarian ligament) determines the correct answer to "ovarian attachment" questions. Whether both uterine angulations (ante-version and ante-flexion) or only one is named determines whether a description of uterine position is complete.

### Clinical significance
The suspensory ligament of the ovary is deliberately clamped and divided during oophorectomy because it carries the ovarian vessels. A retroverted uterus is a recognised anatomical variant that is usually benign but can occasionally correlate with symptoms, and is distinguished on examination from the normal anteverted-anteflexed position.

### Common misconceptions
Confusing the suspensory ligament of the ovary (lateral, to pelvic wall, vascular) with the ovarian ligament (medial, to uterus). Describing normal uterine position with only one of the two angulations (anteversion or anteflexion) rather than both together. Believing the "germinal" epithelium is the true source of germ cells.

## summary
The testis is covered by three coats -- tunica vaginalis, tunica albuginea and tunica vasculosa -- and the posterior border of the tunica albuginea thickens to form the mediastinum testis. The vas deferens is a thick-walled muscular tube approximately 45 cm long running from the epididymis to the ejaculatory duct.

## sections
### Definition
The testis has three coats, from outermost to innermost: the tunica vaginalis, a peritoneal remnant with parietal and visceral layers separated by a fluid-containing cavity; the tunica albuginea, a dense white fibrous coat; and the tunica vasculosa, the innermost, vascular layer. The vas deferens (ductus deferens) is a thick-walled muscular tube roughly 45 cm long running from the tail of the epididymis, through the spermatic cord and inguinal canal, over the pelvic brim, to join the seminal vesicle's duct and form the ejaculatory duct.

### Mechanism
The posterior border of the tunica albuginea is thickened to form the mediastinum testis, a fibrous wedge projecting into the gland that gives off septa dividing the testis into lobules and through which the rete testis and efferent ductules pass on their way to the epididymis. The vas deferens's thick muscular wall allows peristaltic propulsion of sperm at ejaculation, a function suited to its length and course from the scrotum to the pelvis.

### Key determinants
The number and identity of the testicular coats (three, not four) and the fact that the tunica vaginalis does have a cavity between its layers are the details most often tested against plausible-sounding wrong statements. The vas deferens's approximately 45 cm length, much longer than the epididymis or other short genital-tract segments, is what a length question is testing.

### Clinical significance
A hydrocele is an abnormal collection of fluid within the cavity of the tunica vaginalis. Vasectomy interrupts the vas deferens as a method of male sterilisation, exploiting its accessible course in the spermatic cord.

### Common misconceptions
Counting four coats of the testis instead of three. Believing the tunica vaginalis's two layers have no cavity between them. Underestimating the vas deferens's length by confusing it with the epididymis or another shorter duct.
