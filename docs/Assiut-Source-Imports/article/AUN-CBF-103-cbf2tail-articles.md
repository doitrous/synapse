## summary
De novo fatty acid synthesis (lipogenesis) builds palmitate from acetyl-CoA in the cytosol on the fatty acid synthase complex, elongating the chain two carbons at a time and consuming NADPH supplied mainly by the pentose phosphate pathway via glucose 6-phosphate dehydrogenase.

## sections
### Definition
Lipogenesis is the synthesis of a long-chain fatty acid from acetyl-CoA. It is a cytosolic process, physically and directionally the reverse of the mitochondrial beta-oxidation that degrades fatty acids.

### Mechanism
Mitochondrial acetyl-CoA is exported to the cytosol as citrate and released there by ATP-citrate lyase. Acetyl-CoA carboxylase then makes malonyl-CoA (the committed step). The fatty acid synthase complex uses malonyl-CoA as the two-carbon donor and lengthens the growing fatty acyl chain by two carbons in each cycle, losing a carbon as CO2 per addition, until palmitate (C16) is released.

### Key determinants
Each reductive cycle spends NADPH, whose main source is the pentose phosphate (hexose monophosphate) pathway -- specifically its first, rate-limiting enzyme glucose 6-phosphate dehydrogenase, with the malic enzyme contributing the remainder. The location (cytosol), the two-carbon growth increment, and the use of NADPH (not NAD+/FAD) distinguish synthesis from oxidation.

### Clinical significance
A high-carbohydrate, high-fat, sedentary lifestyle drives lipogenesis and obesity; insulin activates acetyl-CoA carboxylase and thereby the whole pathway.

### Common misconceptions
Placing synthesis in the mitochondria, expecting one-carbon growth, or naming an oxidative enzyme rather than glucose 6-phosphate dehydrogenase as the NADPH source.

## published_summary

## published_sections

## hold_these
De novo fatty acid synthesis is cytosolic, uses fatty acid synthase, and grows the chain two carbons at a time.
The NADPH it needs comes mainly from the pentose phosphate pathway (glucose 6-phosphate dehydrogenase).

## lose_the_mark
Saying fatty acid synthesis occurs in the mitochondria or that the chain grows one carbon at a time.

## summary
Most viscera receive both autonomic divisions, but the skin, sweat glands, arrector pili, most blood vessels and the adrenal medulla have sympathetic supply only, while secretomotor glands such as the lacrimal gland are parasympathetic-dominated -- and sweat glands, though sympathetic, are cholinergic.

## sections
### Definition
The autonomic nervous system usually gives an organ a dual supply (sympathetic plus parasympathetic) acting in opposite directions, but a small exam-favourite set of structures escapes this pattern.

### Mechanism
Sympathetic-only structures include the skin and its appendages -- sweat glands, arrector pili muscles, and the great majority of blood vessels -- plus the adrenal medulla (directly innervated by preganglionic sympathetic fibres). These have no parasympathetic input. On the other side, glands such as the lacrimal gland are functionally driven by parasympathetic secretomotor fibres.

### Key determinants
Sweat glands are supplied by sympathetic fibres that are cholinergic (release acetylcholine); cutting that sympathetic nerve therefore abolishes sweating rather than increasing it.

### Clinical significance
Sympathectomy of the skin produces anhidrosis (loss of sweating) and vasodilatation in the denervated area; knowing which organs have a single supply predicts the effect of cutting one nerve.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming every organ has both divisions, or forgetting that the sympathetic supply to sweat glands is cholinergic.

## published_summary

## published_sections

## hold_these

Sweat glands are sympathetic but cholinergic, so cutting the nerve stops sweating.

## lose_the_mark
Saying the skin or blood vessels receive a parasympathetic supply.

## summary
Choline is a building block of acetylcholine and of the choline-containing phospholipids -- lecithin, sphingomyelin and plasmalogens -- but not of the prostaglandins, which are made from arachidonic acid.

## sections
### Definition
Choline is a quaternary-amine nitrogenous compound (itself made by transmethylation of ethanolamine using S-adenosylmethionine) that supplies several molecules.

### Mechanism
It is acetylated to acetylcholine, the transmitter of the parasympathetic system and the neuromuscular junction, and it is incorporated into phosphatidylcholine (lecithin), sphingomyelin and the choline plasmalogens.

### Key determinants
Prostaglandins and the other eicosanoids are twenty-carbon lipids synthesised from arachidonic acid, not from choline; so on a "choline enters all of the following except" list, prostaglandins is the exclusion.

### Clinical significance
Choline/phosphatidylcholine deficiency contributes to fatty liver; lung surfactant is largely dipalmitoyl-phosphatidylcholine.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming choline, because it sits in membrane phospholipids, must also feed the eicosanoid pathway.

## summary
Antimetabolites starve dividing cells of nucleotides: methotrexate is a folate analogue that inhibits dihydrofolate reductase, and synthetic nucleotide/base analogues are used as anticancer and antiviral drugs.

## sections
### Definition
Antimetabolites resemble a normal metabolite closely enough to block the pathway that would use it; in chemotherapy the target is nucleotide (DNA/RNA precursor) synthesis, which fast-dividing cells depend on.

### Mechanism
Methotrexate competitively inhibits dihydrofolate reductase, the enzyme that regenerates tetrahydrofolate; the collapsed one-carbon folate pool halts thymidylate and purine synthesis, so DNA cannot be made. Nucleotide and base analogues (fluorouracil, mercaptopurine, cytarabine; the antiviral acyclovir) masquerade as real building blocks and derail nucleic-acid synthesis.

### Key determinants
The selectivity comes from hitting cells that divide fastest and therefore need the most nucleotides.

### Clinical significance
Methotrexate is used in cancer and autoimmune disease; folate/folinic acid rescue limits its toxicity. Distinguish its target (dihydrofolate reductase) from thymidylate synthase (fluorouracil) and ribonucleotide reductase.

### Common misconceptions
Thinking nucleotide analogues treat metabolic diseases like diabetes rather than cancer/viral infection, or confusing methotrexate's enzyme target.

Synthetic nucleotide/base analogues are anticancer (and antiviral) drugs.

## summary
Pyrimidines are built ring-first from carbamoyl phosphate and aspartate, then attached to ribose; carbamoyl phosphate is the precursor not shared with purine synthesis, and a UMP synthase defect causes hereditary orotic aciduria, treated with uridine.

## sections
### Definition
Pyrimidine nucleotides are made in the reverse order to purines: the ring is assembled first as a free base and only then joined to ribose-5-phosphate.

### Mechanism
Cytosolic carbamoyl phosphate synthetase II makes carbamoyl phosphate, which condenses with aspartate and proceeds through orotic acid to orotidine monophosphate and then UMP, the parent pyrimidine nucleotide. PRPP, aspartate, glutamine and tetrahydrofolate are shared with purine synthesis; carbamoyl phosphate is pyrimidine-specific.

### Key determinants
A defect in UMP synthase (the bifunctional enzyme of the last two steps) blocks the pathway: orotic acid accumulates and spills into the urine (orotic aciduria), and the pyrimidine shortage causes megaloblastic anaemia and failure to thrive.

### Clinical significance
Hereditary orotic aciduria is treated with oral uridine, which bypasses the block and refills the pyrimidine pool.

### Common misconceptions
Treating carbamoyl phosphate as a shared precursor, or giving a purine base instead of uridine.

## published_summary

## published_sections

## hold_these

## lose_the_mark

## summary
Mitochondrial DNA is maternally (non-Mendelianly) inherited and encodes respiratory-chain subunits, so its mutations damage oxidative phosphorylation; a nuclear-gene Mendelian disorder such as sickle cell anaemia is not caused by mtDNA mutation.

## sections
### Definition
Mitochondria carry their own small circular DNA, separate from the nuclear genome.

### Mechanism
Because the zygote's mitochondria come from the ovum, mtDNA is inherited from the mother, giving a maternal, non-Mendelian pattern: an affected mother can transmit to all children, an affected father to none. mtDNA encodes several subunits of respiratory-chain Complexes I, III, IV and V.

### Key determinants
mtDNA mutations therefore impair oxidative phosphorylation and strike high-energy tissues (nerve, muscle, retina); disorders show heteroplasmy and a threshold effect.

### Clinical significance
Examples include Leber hereditary optic neuropathy and MELAS. Sickle cell anaemia, a beta-globin nuclear-gene point mutation, is Mendelian and is NOT a mitochondrial-DNA disease.

### Common misconceptions
Attributing a nuclear-gene disorder to mtDNA, or expecting paternal transmission.

## summary
DNA replication begins by unwinding the helix, needs an RNA primer laid down before DNA synthesis (because DNA polymerase can only extend an existing 3' end), and in eukaryotes is carried out mainly by DNA polymerase delta.

## sections
### Definition
Replication is the semiconservative copying of DNA through an ordered sequence of steps at the replication fork.

### Mechanism
The first step is unwinding: helicase (with single-strand binding proteins, and topoisomerase relieving supercoiling ahead) opens the duplex. Because DNA polymerase can only add nucleotides to a free 3'-OH, primase first lays a short RNA primer, so primer formation precedes DNA synthesis. In eukaryotes DNA polymerase delta synthesises most new DNA (with polymerases alpha and epsilon), unlike bacterial DNA polymerase III.

### Key determinants
The RNA primer's purpose is to provide the 3' end that polymerase extends; primers are later removed, replaced with DNA, and the fragments sealed by ligase.

### Clinical significance
Understanding priming explains why lagging-strand synthesis is discontinuous and how antineoplastic and antiviral nucleotide analogues terminate replication.

### Common misconceptions
Thinking DNA polymerase can start a chain de novo, or naming ligase or DNA polymerase III as the eukaryotic replicative enzyme.

## published_summary

## published_sections

## hold_these
Replication starts with unwinding; an RNA primer precedes DNA synthesis because DNA polymerase only extends an existing 3' end.
DNA polymerase delta is the main eukaryotic replicative enzyme.

## lose_the_mark
Saying DNA polymerase can initiate a strand without a primer.

## summary
RNA is more susceptible than DNA to alkaline hydrolysis because ribose carries a 2'-hydroxyl group (absent from DNA's deoxyribose) that attacks the neighbouring phosphodiester bond and cleaves the chain.

## sections
### Definition
The sugar is the key difference: RNA uses ribose (with a 2'-OH), DNA uses 2'-deoxyribose (no 2'-OH).

### Mechanism
Under alkaline conditions the 2'-OH is deprotonated and its oxygen attacks the adjacent backbone phosphorus, cleaving the strand through a cyclic 2',3'-phosphate intermediate. DNA lacks the 2'-OH and so cannot undergo this internal attack.

### Key determinants
This makes RNA chemically labile and DNA the stable long-term store of genetic information; it is a distinguishing feature of most RNA molecules.

### Clinical significance
The 2'-OH lability is exploited in the laboratory (alkaline hydrolysis distinguishes RNA from DNA) and underlies the shorter functional lifespan of RNA.

### Common misconceptions
Attributing the lability to uracil or single-strandedness rather than to the ribose 2'-hydroxyl.

## published_summary

## published_sections

## hold_these
RNA's ribose 2'-OH makes it susceptible to alkaline hydrolysis; DNA's deoxyribose lacks it and resists.
This 2'-OH lability distinguishes RNA from DNA.

## lose_the_mark
Blaming RNA's alkaline lability on uracil or on being single-stranded.
