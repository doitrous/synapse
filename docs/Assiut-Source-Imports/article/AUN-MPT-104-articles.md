## summary
The first three lectures of AUN-MPT-104 lay the vocabulary and mechanisms the rest of the course builds on. Pathology is introduced by how a diagnosis is reached — cytological, histological or gross examination, each preserved by the right fixative and sometimes extended by immunohistochemistry — and by the vocabulary that describes a disease's course (pathogenesis, the mechanism that produced the lesion, versus prognosis, the prediction of what happens next). Cell injury is then followed to its irreversible endpoint through a single shared mechanism: rising intracellular calcium activating a cascade of degradative enzymes. Pharmacology opens with what the field is, how a drug is named, where drugs come from, the standard ADME sequence a drug passes through, and why the route of administration determines how much of a dose ever reaches the blood.

## sections
### Definition
Pathological diagnosis is organised by what is examined: cytopathology examines isolated cells (shed, aspirated or from a fluid sample); histopathology examines an intact tissue specimen (a biopsy) and is the definitive diagnostic method; gross pathology is the naked-eye description of a diseased organ's size, shape, colour, cut surface and consistency. Prognosis (the prediction of a disease's future course) and pathogenesis (the mechanism that produced its lesion) are distinct disease-description terms, both different again from etiology (the cause). Pharmacology is the study of drugs' actions and effects in living systems, distinct from pharmacy (drug preparation); every drug carries a chemical name, a generic/official name (regulator-approved, not company-owned) and a brand name.

### Mechanism
A specimen is placed in fixative — formalin 10% for tissue, alcohol for cytological smears — immediately after removal, to prevent autolysis and preserve tissue integrity for examination; immunohistochemistry then extends histopathology by linking an enzyme to an antibody against a specific tissue antigen (commonly a tumour marker). In cell injury, intracellular calcium — normally held at very low levels by energy-dependent membrane transport — rises when ischaemia or toxins let it leak across a damaged plasma membrane, or when it is released from injured mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum. That calcium rise activates phospholipase (degrading membrane phospholipids), proteases (breaking down structural proteins), ATPase (worsening ATP depletion) and endonucleases (fragmenting chromatin) — the shared final pathway that converts reversible injury into irreversible injury and cell death. Absorption then determines whether a drug ever reaches a therapeutic plasma concentration at all; the standard pharmacokinetic sequence is absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion (ADME), and the route chosen affects how much of the dose survives to reach systemic circulation — oral administration, subject to incomplete gut absorption and first-pass hepatic metabolism, achieves the lowest serum concentration among the routes commonly compared (IV, intrathecal, intramuscular, oral) for an equivalent dose.

### Key determinants
The distinguishing fact between cytopathology and histopathology is the specimen examined (isolated cells versus intact tissue architecture), not the use of a microscope, which both share. In cell injury, calcium is the shared downstream effector that ATP depletion and membrane damage both feed into — it is not simply one item on a list alongside them, but the mechanism through which those other insults become irreversible. In drug sourcing, insulin is the worked example spanning two source categories at once (animal-derived and human/recombinant), unlike single-source drugs such as morphine (plant) or cod liver oil (animal only).

### Clinical significance
Fine-needle aspiration cytology and core/excisional biopsy histopathology are the two everyday routes to a tissue diagnosis in surgical practice, and immunohistochemistry is the standard next step when a tumour's cell of origin or receptor status needs confirming beyond routine staining. Recognising irreversible cell injury (marked by the calcium-driven enzyme cascade) versus still-reversible injury is the pathological basis for triaging ischaemic tissue — the reason time-to-reperfusion matters clinically. Choosing a drug's route of administration for a given clinical situation (e.g. avoiding oral dosing when a rapid, reliably high plasma concentration is needed) rests directly on the absorption/route relationship taught here.

### Common misconceptions
Treating pathogenesis as a synonym for either etiology (the cause) or prognosis (the outcome), when it specifically names the mechanistic steps in between. Treating calcium's rise in cell injury as an isolated finding rather than the shared final pathway that ATP depletion and membrane damage both drive toward. Assuming "administration" itself, or steps like disintegration or expiration, are pharmacokinetic phases, when the standard sequence begins only at absorption.

## summary
A drug cannot act unless it binds a receptor, and how that receptor is built determines how fast the effect appears. This article works through the four structural/signalling superfamilies drug receptors fall into, from fastest to slowest: ligand-gated ion channels (milliseconds), G-protein coupled receptors (seconds), tyrosine-kinase-linked receptors (minutes), and intracellular/nuclear receptors (hours to days) — the last chaperoned in its resting state by heat shock protein 90 (hsp90). It closes with the well-established second messengers a G-protein coupled receptor can produce, and the reminder that GDP, despite appearing in the same list, is not one of them.

## sections
### Definition
Ligand-gated ion channels (ionotropic receptors) are directly linked to an ion channel, so ligand binding opens the channel immediately — nicotinic acetylcholine, glutamate, GABA, glycine and 5-HT3 receptors are this type. G-protein coupled (metabotropic) receptors activate a G-protein on binding, which regulates an effector enzyme and changes a second messenger's concentration — muscarinic acetylcholine and adrenergic receptors are this type. Enzyme (tyrosine-kinase)-linked receptors have an extracellular ligand-binding domain and a cytoplasmic domain with intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity — the insulin receptor is the worked example. Intracellular (nuclear) receptors bind lipid-soluble ligands that cross the cell membrane directly — steroid hormones, thyroid hormone and vitamin D — and are chaperoned by heat shock protein 90 (hsp90) in their resting, unbound state.

### Mechanism
Ligand binding to an ionotropic receptor directly opens its channel, producing a response within milliseconds — the fastest of the four, since no intermediate signalling step exists. A G-protein coupled receptor's ligand binding activates a G-protein, which regulates an effector such as adenylyl cyclase or phospholipase C, changing the concentration of a second messenger — cAMP, cGMP, IP3, DAG or calcium are the well-established second messengers; GDP is not one of them, it is the inactive-state nucleotide the G-protein's alpha subunit carries before activation, not a signal the receptor produces. This unfolds over seconds. A tyrosine-kinase-linked receptor's ligand binding at the extracellular domain stimulates the cytoplasmic tyrosine kinase, phosphorylating target proteins; the intracellular signal can outlast the ligand's own binding, and the response unfolds over minutes. An intracellular receptor's lipid-soluble ligand diffuses across the cell membrane and binds the receptor directly; hsp90, normally bound to the receptor in the ligand's absence, dissociates on binding, and the receptor-ligand complex translocates to the nucleus to bind a DNA response element and alter gene transcription — the slowest response, unfolding over hours to days because it requires new protein synthesis.

### Key determinants
The four superfamilies rank by speed in the same order they are structurally "closer" to the ion channel or gene they ultimately affect: ion-channel-linked is fastest because ligand binding and the channel are the same molecular event; nuclear-linked is slowest because the pathway must reach the genome and produce new protein before any effect appears. Nicotinic acetylcholine receptors are the teaching exception worth isolating: despite being a cholinergic receptor like the (G-protein coupled) muscarinic type, nicotinic receptors are ionotropic and act on a millisecond time scale, not the seconds-to-minutes scale most other named receptors in this teaching set use. Receptor macromolecules themselves are also drawn from several structural classes beyond dedicated signalling proteins — regulatory proteins, enzymes, transport proteins and structural proteins can all serve as drug receptors.

### Clinical significance
Nicotinic receptor blockade (neuromuscular blocking drugs) produces rapid-onset paralysis for surgical anaesthesia precisely because the receptor's ion-channel mechanism is fast; GPCR-targeted drugs (beta-blockers, muscarinic antagonists) act over a comparably fast clinical time course for the same structural reason. Insulin's own tyrosine-kinase-linked receptor explains why its clinical effect, though rapid compared to steroid therapy, still takes minutes rather than milliseconds to develop. Steroid and thyroid hormone therapies act over hours to days clinically because their intracellular-receptor mechanism requires new gene transcription — a fact directly relevant to how quickly a prescriber can expect a glucocorticoid or thyroxine dose to show effect.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming all cholinergic receptors are the same type — nicotinic receptors are ligand-gated ion channels, while muscarinic receptors are G-protein coupled, and the two act on very different time scales despite sharing the same natural ligand. Counting GDP among the "second messengers" a G-protein coupled receptor produces, when it is instead the G-protein's own inactive-state bound nucleotide. Assuming a receptor's response speed is arbitrary rather than a direct consequence of how many signalling steps sit between ligand binding and the eventual effect.

## summary
Five teaching points from AUN-MPT-104's Lectures 6, 8 and 10 that were not already covered by the module's existing concepts or by the pending Kasr 108-INT pharmacology batch reused elsewhere in this course: spare receptors (a tissue can reach its maximal response before every receptor is occupied), receptor up- and down-regulation (chronic agonist exposure desensitises a tissue, chronic antagonist exposure sensitises it), tachyphylaxis (a rapid loss of response within the first few doses, distinct from gradual tolerance), the vocabulary separating quantitative (hyper-/hyporeactive), qualitative/genetic (idiosyncratic) and immunological (hypersensitive) individual variation in drug response, and two further membrane-crossing routes -- filtration and pinocytosis -- alongside the named transporter proteins MRP and SLC.

## sections
### Definition
Spare receptors are the unoccupied receptors left over once a tissue's maximal response (Emax) has already been reached by a smaller, occupied fraction. Receptor down-regulation is desensitisation from continued agonist exposure; receptor up-regulation is supersensitivity from continued antagonist exposure. Tachyphylaxis is tolerance that develops rapidly, within the first dose or first few doses, rather than gradually. Hyperreactive/hyporeactive describe an individual's quantitative departure from the expected dose-response relationship; idiosyncrasy is a qualitatively different response from a genetic cause; hypersensitivity is an allergic/immunological drug response -- the three must not be used interchangeably. Filtration moves small water-soluble drugs through aqueous membrane pores; pinocytosis engulfs fluid and dissolved substances (iron being the worked example) by membrane invagination; MRP excretes some drugs into urine at the renal tubule, and SLC transporters take up some neurotransmitters at nerve terminals.

### Mechanism
A tissue develops spare receptors when its receptor number exceeds what its downstream signalling machinery needs to reach its own ceiling response -- once that ceiling is reached, further receptor occupancy adds nothing, so the tissue's measured EC50 for an agonist sits below that agonist's Kd for the receptor. Receptor down-regulation follows chronic agonist stimulation through decreased signal sensitivity, feedback-regulated receptor synthesis, or reduced receptor number or effectiveness; receptor up-regulation follows chronic antagonist occupancy driving compensatory synthesis of new receptors, so that when the antagonist is abruptly withdrawn, the enlarged receptor population is suddenly exposed to endogenous agonist unopposed. Tachyphylaxis and ordinary tolerance are the same underlying phenomenon -- reduced response to a repeated dose -- differing only in how quickly the reduction appears. Idiosyncratic responses trace to an inherited difference in a drug-metabolising enzyme or receptor (such as an atypical pseudocholinesterase prolonging succinylcholine's action), while hypersensitivity requires prior immune sensitisation to the drug or a related compound. Filtration and pinocytosis both move a drug across a membrane without dissolving through its lipid core -- filtration through aqueous pores along a bulk flow of water, pinocytosis by engulfing the drug together with surrounding fluid in a membrane-derived vesicle -- while MRP and SLC are single named proteins each dedicated to one transport job (efflux into urine; neurotransmitter reuptake) rather than a general class of carrier.

### Key determinants
The distinguishing fact for spare receptors is that the drug's intracellular effect outlasts its own receptor binding -- a purely kinetic signature, not something inferred from dose alone. For up-/down-regulation, the direction of exposure (agonist vs antagonist) is what determines the direction of regulation, and the two run in opposite directions from what intuition might suggest. Tachyphylaxis is separated from tolerance purely by timescale (first few doses versus a longer course), not by mechanism. The hyperreactive/idiosyncratic/hypersensitive triad is separated by mechanism (dose-response quantity, genetics, immunology respectively), not by severity or by how "unusual" the reaction looks. Filtration, pinocytosis, MRP and SLC are each defined by a specific substrate and direction, not interchangeable synonyms for "transport."

### Clinical significance
Recognising receptor up-regulation is why beta-blockers are tapered rather than stopped abruptly in patients with ischaemic heart disease -- sudden withdrawal exposes an enlarged, sensitised receptor population to endogenous catecholamines. Recognising tachyphylaxis is why calcitonin for severe hypercalcaemia is paired from the outset with a slower-acting bisphosphonate, so therapy does not lapse when calcitonin's effect fades within days. Correctly identifying an idiosyncratic reaction (as opposed to a dose-related toxic one) points toward a pharmacogenetic cause and changes both acute management and future drug avoidance for that patient (and potentially their relatives). Knowing that highly protein-bound drugs and transporter-dependent excretion (such as MRP-mediated renal clearance) can be affected by co-administered drugs is part of anticipating clinically important drug interactions.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming a tissue must have 100% receptor occupancy to reach its maximal response, when spare receptors mean many tissues reach Emax well before full occupancy. Expecting antagonist exposure to reduce receptor activity the way agonist exposure does, when chronic antagonism instead produces compensatory up-regulation. Treating tachyphylaxis and ordinary tolerance as different phenomena rather than the same phenomenon on different timescales. Using "hypersensitive" as a loose synonym for "hyperreactive," when the two name mechanistically distinct kinds of individual variation.
