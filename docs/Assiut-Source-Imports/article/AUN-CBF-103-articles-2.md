## summary
Amino acids classed as nonessential can become conditionally essential (arginine, under growth or surgical stress); the sulphur-containing pair (methionine, cysteine) and secondary-structure hydrogen bonding (the alpha-helix's backbone carbonyl-to-amide bonds) round out amino-acid and protein-structure classification. Misfolded protein sharing a beta-pleated-sheet structure defines amyloid across otherwise unrelated diseases, and denaturation's one counter-intuitive effect is a rise, not a fall, in viscosity. The ubiquitin-proteasome pathway degrades polyubiquitin-tagged proteins, the route bortezomib blocks; albumin's fall during inflammation makes it the exception among acute-phase reactants. At the organelle level, the endoplasmic reticulum synthesises proteins and lipids, and the Golgi body packages and exports what the ER has made.

## sections
### Definition
Arginine, ordinarily a nonessential amino acid, becomes conditionally essential when demand outpaces the body's own synthesis -- during rapid growth or the catabolic stress of surgery, trauma or severe illness. Methionine and cysteine are the two amino acids whose side chain carries sulphur; methionine additionally initiates every polypeptide chain and, as S-adenosylmethionine, donates methyl groups throughout the cell. Secondary structure -- the alpha-helix specifically -- is held together by hydrogen bonds between the backbone carbonyl oxygen of one peptide bond and the backbone amide hydrogen of a different peptide bond further along the same chain, not between side chains and not within one peptide bond. Amyloid, whatever unrelated disease produces it (Alzheimer's neuritic plaques, myeloma-associated amyloidosis and others), shares one misfolded secondary structure: a high content of beta-pleated sheet. Denaturation -- loss of native fold without breaking the peptide-bond sequence -- decreases a protein's solubility, diffusibility and biological activity, but increases its viscosity. The ubiquitin-proteasome pathway tags proteins for degradation with polyubiquitin chains, which the 26S proteasome recognises and destroys; bortezomib inhibits the proteasome itself, specifically sparing degradation of exactly the polyubiquitinated proteins that route normally clears. Most acute-phase reactants (CRP, haptoglobin, alpha-1 antitrypsin) rise during inflammation, but albumin is the negative exception, falling as hepatic synthesis is redirected. The endoplasmic reticulum (rough ER studded with ribosomes, smooth ER without) synthesises proteins and lipids respectively; the Golgi body then modifies, sorts and packages these products into vesicles for export out of the cell or to other organelles.

### Mechanism
The alpha-helix's regular coiling comes directly from the repetition of its backbone hydrogen-bonding pattern along the whole chain -- any residue's backbone atoms can form it, which is why the alpha-helix is largely sequence-independent (aside from proline and excess glycine, which disrupt it structurally). Amyloid's beta-pleated-sheet structure, forced by protein misfolding, is what gives amyloid deposits their Congo-red birefringence and resistance to normal proteolytic clearance, regardless of which precursor protein misfolded. Denaturation's viscosity rise, its one property that moves in the "wrong" direction relative to the others, follows from an unfolded or aggregated polypeptide occupying a larger, more irregular hydrodynamic volume than its compact native fold did. In the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, ATP-dependent unfolding and proteolysis inside the 26S proteasome only proceeds once a polyubiquitin tag has been recognised -- proteins never tagged this way, or degraded by other routes entirely, are not the class a proteasome inhibitor specifically spares from clearance, since bortezomib acts specifically at the tag-dependent proteasome step. The ER-to-Golgi sequence is directional and fixed: newly synthesised secretory and membrane proteins leave the rough ER in transport vesicles, arrive at the Golgi's cis face, are progressively modified (glycosylation, proteolytic processing) as they move through the stack, and leave the trans face already sorted for their final destination -- secretion, the plasma membrane, or another organelle such as the lysosome.

### Key determinants
Sulphur content (methionine, cysteine) is a distinct classification axis from metabolic fate (ketogenic/glucogenic) or side-chain charge (acidic/basic/neutral) -- the same amino acid can appear as the answer to several differently-framed questions for different reasons, and only the reason asked about should be matched. Whether a protein property falls or rises with denaturation is not uniform: solubility, diffusibility and biological activity fall together, while viscosity alone rises. Albumin's status as the sole common negative acute-phase reactant among the frequently listed proteins (CRP, haptoglobin, alpha-1 antitrypsin, albumin) is the recurring distractor pattern in this topic.

### Clinical significance
Recognising albumin's fall as an acute-phase response, not necessarily malnutrition or liver failure, changes how a low albumin is interpreted in an acutely unwell patient. Bortezomib's proteasome-inhibiting mechanism, exploited in multiple myeloma, follows directly from malignant plasma cells' heavy dependence on proteasome-mediated clearance of the large immunoglobulin burden they produce. Amyloid's shared beta-pleated-sheet structure across otherwise unrelated diseases (Alzheimer's, myeloma-associated amyloidosis) is what a single histochemical stain (Congo red) can detect regardless of the underlying disease.

### Common misconceptions
Placing the alpha-helix's hydrogen bonds between side chains, or within a single peptide bond, rather than between backbone atoms of separated peptide bonds. Assuming every denaturation-associated property falls, missing viscosity's rise. Assuming a proteasome inhibitor blocks all intracellular degradation rather than specifically the polyubiquitin-tagged route. Assuming all acute-phase reactants rise, missing albumin as the negative exception. Reversing the ER's and Golgi's roles (assigning packaging/export to the ER, or synthesis to the Golgi).

## summary
Vitamins are accessory food factors, organic and diet-derived, distinct from proteins/carbohydrates/fats and from hormones. Vitamin C's specific mechanistic role is as the cofactor for collagen's proline/lysine hydroxylation, the direct biochemical basis of scurvy. Pyridoxal phosphate (active vitamin B6) is the coenzyme transaminases require, and the same PLP-dependent pathway (via glutamate decarboxylase) makes GABA, so B6 deficiency causes convulsions, especially in infants. Vitamin B12's corrin ring is centred on cobalt; deficiency due to lost intrinsic factor is specifically pernicious anaemia, and raised urinary methylmalonate specifically marks B12 (not folate) deficiency, while folate itself is structurally a pteridine base joined to PABA and glutamate.

## sections
### Definition
A vitamin is an accessory food factor -- an organic compound the body cannot generally synthesise in adequate quantity, needed in small amounts for normal metabolism, and structurally unrelated to proteins, carbohydrates or fats and to endocrine hormones. Vitamin C's defining biochemical role beyond general antioxidant function is as the cofactor prolyl and lysyl hydroxylase require to hydroxylate proline and lysine residues during collagen synthesis, producing hydroxyproline and hydroxylysine. Pyridoxal phosphate, the active coenzyme form of vitamin B6, is required by transaminase enzymes for amino-group transfer, and by glutamate decarboxylase for converting glutamate into GABA. Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is built around a cobalt-centred corrin ring; its deficiency due to lost intrinsic factor is termed pernicious anaemia, and B12 deficiency specifically (not folate deficiency) raises urinary methylmalonate, since methylmalonyl-CoA mutase is one of only two human B12-dependent enzymes. Folate (folic acid) is structurally a pteridine ring joined to para-aminobenzoic acid and one or more glutamate residues.

### Mechanism
Hydroxyproline's hydrogen bonding is what stabilises collagen's triple helix, so vitamin C deficiency's failure to hydroxylate proline directly destabilises newly made collagen, producing scurvy's connective-tissue picture: bleeding gums, poor wound healing, weakened vessel walls. Pyridoxal phosphate's transamination role feeds both amino acid synthesis and their catabolic entry into central metabolic pathways, while its separate role as glutamate decarboxylase's coenzyme converts the excitatory neurotransmitter glutamate into the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA -- so a PLP shortfall lowers GABA and tips the CNS toward excitation, the mechanistic basis of B6-deficiency convulsions, described especially in infants. B12's corrin ring, related to but distinct from the iron-centred porphyrin/haem ring, coordinates cobalt at its centre in both coenzyme forms (methylcobalamin, adenosylcobalamin); adenosylcobalamin's specific role in methylmalonyl-CoA mutase means B12 deficiency, and B12 deficiency alone among the causes of megaloblastic anaemia, raises methylmalonate, a distinction folate deficiency (which produces the same blood picture via a different, methylation-based mechanism) does not share.

### Key determinants
Whether a B12-deficiency anaemia is specifically "pernicious anaemia" turns on the cause: lost intrinsic factor (autoimmune gastric parietal cell destruction, most commonly), not simple dietary B12 lack, is what earns the specific name. Distinguishing folate from B12 deficiency by blood picture alone is not possible (both produce megaloblastic anaemia); the distinguishing test is the methylmalonate level, raised in B12 deficiency only.

### Clinical significance
Vitamin C's collagen-hydroxylation mechanism explains scurvy's specific presentation (bleeding, poor wound healing) as opposed to a generic "vitamin deficiency" picture, and is the basis for supplementing vitamin C around major wound healing or surgery. B6-deficiency convulsions in infants are a recognised, specifically preventable cause of neonatal/infant seizures once the underlying nutritional cause is identified. The B12/folate distinction by methylmalonate matters clinically because treating a B12-deficient patient with folate alone corrects the blood picture but not the neurological damage B12 deficiency (subacute combined degeneration) causes, so getting the diagnosis right before treating is a genuine patient-safety point.

### Common misconceptions
Treating vitamins as if they were hormones or macronutrients rather than diet-derived accessory factors. Attributing vitamin C's role in scurvy only to generic antioxidant function rather than the specific collagen-hydroxylation mechanism. Assuming any B12 deficiency is "pernicious anaemia" regardless of cause. Naming iron rather than cobalt as B12's central metal. Assuming methylmalonate rises in either B12 or folate deficiency rather than specifically B12 deficiency.
