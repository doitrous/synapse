Eukaryotic mRNA: colinearity with protein and promoter binding

## summary
Transcription initiates when RNA polymerase and its associated factors bind specific sequences upstream of the transcription start site (the promoter); the mature mRNA that results is colinear with the protein it encodes, meaning codon order in the transcript matches amino acid order in the product even though extensive processing (capping, splicing, polyadenylation) intervenes between primary transcript and mature mRNA.

## sections
### Definition
Two related but distinct facts about eukaryotic mRNA recur on exams: where transcription starts (promoter recognition) and how the finished transcript relates to its protein product (colinearity).

### Mechanism
RNA polymerase, together with general transcription factors, recognises and binds promoter sequences upstream of the transcription start site before synthesis begins -- not downstream sequences, and not a restriction site or histone protein. Once the mature mRNA is produced (after capping, splicing and polyadenylation), its codon sequence is colinear with the amino acid sequence of the encoded protein: the reading order is preserved, even though the primary transcript itself was extensively modified to reach that mature, colinear form.

### Key determinants
Distinguishing promoter-binding proteins (RNA polymerase, transcription factors) from replication machinery (helicase, primase) is a common source of confusion, as is conflating "colinear" with "unmodified" -- the mature mRNA is colinear with protein, but it is not identical to the primary transcript.

### Clinical significance
Mutations disrupting promoter recognition or normal splicing/processing both ultimately corrupt the colinear relationship between gene and protein, underlying many inherited disorders of gene expression.

### Common misconceptions
Thinking mature mammalian mRNA is only minimally modified from the primary transcript, or that the primary transcript already equals the exported mRNA.

## hold_these
RNA polymerase (with its transcription factors) binds promoter sequences upstream of the transcription start site.
The mature mRNA sequence is colinear with the protein's amino acid sequence.

## lose_the_mark
Saying the primary transcript is virtually identical to the mature, exported mRNA.

## summary
Protein synthesis assembles the initiation complex (small subunit + initiation factors + mRNA + initiator tRNA), joins the large subunit, then repeats an elongation cycle of aminoacyl-tRNA delivery, peptide bond formation and one-codon translocation; each amino acid's activation onto its tRNA costs two high-energy phosphate bonds, and macrolides such as erythromycin block translocation by binding the 50S ribosomal subunit.

## sections
### Definition
Translation converts an mRNA sequence into a polypeptide through a fixed sequence of initiation and elongation steps, each with a defined energy cost, and each vulnerable to specific antibiotic inhibitors.

### Mechanism
Initiation loads the small ribosomal subunit with initiation factors, mRNA and the initiator aminoacyl-tRNA, then joins the large subunit to form the complete ribosome. Elongation then repeats: elongation factors deliver an aminoacyl-tRNA to the A site, a peptide bond forms, and the ribosome translocates forward by one codon (three bases) to read the next codon. Charging each amino acid onto its tRNA (activation) consumes two high-energy phosphate-bond equivalents, because ATP is converted all the way to AMP (via pyrophosphate hydrolysis), not just to ADP.

### Key determinants
Macrolide antibiotics such as erythromycin bind the large (50S) ribosomal subunit and specifically block the translocation step, halting bacterial protein synthesis -- distinct from inhibitors that block initiation, cause premature termination, or mimic mRNA.

### Clinical significance
Erythromycin is used for Legionnaires' disease, whooping cough and mycoplasma pneumonia, exploiting this translocation-blocking mechanism at the bacterial (not human cytosolic) ribosome.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming amino acid activation costs only one phosphate bond (it costs two, via PPi hydrolysis), or assuming all 50S-targeting antibiotics share erythromycin's exact mechanism.

## hold_these
Initiation complex assembly precedes the repeating elongation cycle (aminoacyl-tRNA delivery, peptide bond, one-codon translocation).
Amino acid activation costs two high-energy phosphate bonds (ATP to AMP).
Erythromycin blocks translocation by binding the 50S ribosomal subunit.

## lose_the_mark
Counting amino acid activation as costing only one phosphate bond.

## summary
DNA bases undergo spontaneous deamination at a constant background rate; cytosine deaminating to uracil is the classic example of damage that becomes a mutation (C-G to T-A) if not repaired before replication. Separately, sustained angiogenesis -- new blood vessel formation -- is one of the fundamental changes a growing tumour must acquire.

## sections
### Definition
Two foundational facts of genome instability and cancer biology: spontaneous base damage that causes point mutations, and the vascular-supply change that lets a tumour keep growing.

### Mechanism
Cytosine spontaneously deaminates to uracil, a base foreign to DNA; if base excision repair (uracil-DNA glycosylase) does not remove it before the next replication round, DNA polymerase pairs the uracil with adenine, converting the original C-G base pair into T-A -- a mutation. Separately, angiogenesis is the formation of new blood vessels from existing ones; a tumour beyond the reach of passive-diffusion oxygen and nutrient supply must acquire sustained angiogenesis (alongside evasion of apoptosis and limitless replicative potential) to keep growing.

### Key determinants
The mutagenic consequence of cytosine deamination depends specifically on the resulting base (uracil) going unrepaired before replication; angiogenesis is one of several distinct fundamental changes (not a growth factor itself, not contact inhibition, not differentiation).

### Clinical significance
Both facts underlie carcinogenesis: unrepaired spontaneous DNA damage accumulates mutations, and once a clone of damaged cells begins growing, angiogenesis is required to sustain it.

### Common misconceptions
Picking a deamination that does not itself introduce a foreign base, or confusing angiogenesis with the growth factors (e.g. VEGF) that drive it.

## hold_these
Cytosine deaminates to uracil; unrepaired, this converts C-G to T-A.
Sustained angiogenesis is a fundamental change a tumour acquires to keep growing.

## lose_the_mark
Confusing angiogenesis (the vessel-formation process) with a specific growth factor.

## summary
Tyrosine is iodinated to make thyroid hormone and is the shared precursor of the catecholamines (adrenaline, noradrenaline, dopamine); arginine is the precursor of the vasodilator nitric oxide and glutamate is decarboxylated to GABA. Blocking these and related pathways produces the classic aminoacidopathies: PKU (phenylalanine hydroxylase deficiency, which spares tryptophan-derived melatonin), homocystinuria (cystathionine beta synthase deficiency) and maple syrup urine disease (branched-chain amino acid catabolism).

## sections
### Definition
A cluster of Year 1 biochemistry facts link specific amino acids to the hormones, neurotransmitters and diseases built from them.

### Mechanism
Tyrosine is iodinated on thyroglobulin during thyroid hormone synthesis, and separately is hydroxylated and decarboxylated to give the catecholamines adrenaline, noradrenaline and dopamine. Glutamate is decarboxylated by glutamate decarboxylase to GABA. Arginine is converted by nitric oxide synthase to the vasodilator nitric oxide (releasing citrulline), used clinically as a hypotensive, anti-anginal target. Phenylalanine hydroxylase deficiency (PKU) blocks conversion of phenylalanine to tyrosine, starving the tyrosine-dependent pathways (melanin, thyroid hormone, catecholamines) -- but not melatonin, which is built from tryptophan instead. Cystathionine beta synthase deficiency blocks homocysteine's disposal, causing homocystinuria. Branched-chain alpha-ketoacid dehydrogenase deficiency blocks catabolism of leucine, isoleucine and valine, causing maple syrup urine disease.

### Key determinants
Each aminoacidopathy is defined by its specific missing enzyme and its specific accumulating substrate; matching disease to enzyme to substrate (not just "an amino acid disorder") is what exam stems test.

### Clinical significance
PKU, homocystinuria and maple syrup urine disease are all detectable on newborn screening and are dietary-manageable if caught early; untreated, each causes distinct neurological and systemic complications.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming tryptophan (not tyrosine) is the catecholamine precursor, assuming PKU blocks melatonin synthesis (it does not -- that pathway starts from tryptophan), or confusing the branched-chain (MSUD) and aromatic (PKU) amino acid disorders.

## hold_these
Tyrosine: iodinated for thyroid hormone, and the shared catecholamine precursor.
PKU = phenylalanine hydroxylase deficiency; it spares tryptophan-derived melatonin.
Homocystinuria = cystathionine beta synthase deficiency; MSUD = branched-chain amino acid (leucine/isoleucine/valine) catabolism defect.
GABA is made from glutamate by glutamate decarboxylase; nitric oxide is made from arginine.

## lose_the_mark
Naming tryptophan instead of tyrosine as the catecholamine precursor.
