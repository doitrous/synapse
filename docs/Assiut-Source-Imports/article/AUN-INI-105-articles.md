## summary
AUN-INI-105's opening microbiology chapter lays the vocabulary a student needs before any organism-specific teaching: what counts as a prokaryote, how the Gram stain turns cell-wall structure into a colour result, how flagella are classified by arrangement, what the plasma membrane actually does, and which bacterial structures count as virulence factors. A clone -- the population produced by one cell's repeated division -- is also introduced here as basic reproduction vocabulary.

## sections
### Definition
A clone is the population of genetically identical cells produced when a single cell divides repeatedly. A prokaryotic cell has no membrane-bound nucleus or organelles; its DNA is one circular chromosome held in the nucleoid, distinct from a eukaryotic cell's DNA, which sits inside a true, membrane-bound nucleus. The plasma membrane is the cell's selectively permeable barrier, controlling what enters and leaves -- distinct from the cell wall, which instead gives the cell its shape and rigidity.

### Mechanism
The Gram stain's colour result follows directly from cell-wall thickness: a thick peptidoglycan wall (Gram-positive) traps the crystal-violet/iodine complex through decolourisation, so the cell stays violet/purple; a thin wall with an outer lipid membrane (Gram-negative) lets that complex wash out, so the cell instead takes up the safranin counterstain and appears pink/red. Flagellar arrangement is classified purely by position and number: monotrichous (one flagellum, one pole), amphitrichous (one flagellum at each of two poles), lophotrichous (a tuft at one pole), or peritrichous (flagella all around the cell). Bacterial virulence factors -- the capsule, pili, extracellular enzymes and endotoxin -- let an organism establish infection; inclusion granules, by contrast, are an intracellular nutrient-storage structure with no direct role in pathogenesis.

### Key determinants
The distinguishing fact between a prokaryote and eukaryote is the absence of a membrane-bound nucleus and organelles, not merely "being smaller" or "having fewer structures." The Gram-stain colour is a readout of wall thickness, not an arbitrary label to memorise independently of structure. Flagellar-arrangement terms name position and count only (monotrichous/amphitrichous differ by how many poles carry a single flagellum each; lophotrichous differs from both by clustering several flagella at one pole).

### Clinical significance
The Gram stain remains the first laboratory step in identifying an unknown bacterial pathogen and in choosing empirical antibiotic therapy, because Gram-positive and Gram-negative organisms differ systematically in cell-wall structure, antibiotic susceptibility and toxin type. Recognising a bacterium's virulence factors is the basis for understanding how it produces disease and for targeting vaccines (e.g. capsular-polysaccharide vaccines) or therapy at a specific factor.

### Common misconceptions
Treating "monoclonal cells" as the standard term for a population derived from one cell, rather than "clone." Assuming a prokaryote has some organelles or a loosely-bound nucleus, rather than none and no true nucleus at all. Reversing the Gram-stain colours, or treating inclusion granules as a virulence factor rather than a storage structure.

## hold_these
A clone is a population of genetically identical cells from one parent cell; "monoclonal" is the adjective, not the noun for the population.
A prokaryote has no membrane-bound nucleus or organelles; its DNA is a single circular chromosome in the nucleoid.
Gram-positive bacteria stain violet (thick wall retains crystal violet); Gram-negative bacteria stain pink/red (thin wall, safranin counterstain).
Flagellar arrangement: monotrichous (one flagellum, one pole), amphitrichous (one at each pole), lophotrichous (tuft at one pole), peritrichous (all around).
The plasma membrane is the selectively permeable barrier controlling molecular traffic; the cell wall gives shape and rigidity -- two distinct roles.
Virulence factors: capsule, pili, extracellular enzymes, endotoxin. Inclusion granules are nutrient storage, not a virulence factor.

## lose_the_mark
Saying "monoclonal cells" instead of "clones" for a population derived from one cell.
Assuming a prokaryote has any membrane-bound organelle or true nucleus.
Reversing which Gram type stains violet vs. pink/red.
Confusing amphitrichous (one flagellum per pole, two poles) with lophotrichous (a tuft at one single pole).
Treating the plasma membrane as the cell's outermost or rigid structure.
Listing inclusion granules as a virulence factor.
