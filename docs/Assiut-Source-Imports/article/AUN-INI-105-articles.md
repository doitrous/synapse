## summary
AUN-INI-105's opening microbiology chapter lays the vocabulary a student needs before any organism-specific teaching: what counts as a prokaryote, how the Gram stain turns cell-wall structure into a colour result, how flagella are classified by arrangement, what the plasma membrane actually does, and which bacterial structures count as virulence factors. A clone -- the population produced by one cell's repeated division -- is also introduced here as basic reproduction vocabulary.

## sections
### Definition
A clone is the population of genetically identical cells produced when a single cell divides repeatedly. A prokaryotic cell has no membrane-bound nucleus or organelles; its DNA is one circular chromosome held in the nucleoid, distinct from a eukaryotic cell's DNA, which sits inside a true, membrane-bound nucleus. The plasma membrane is the cell's selectively permeable barrier, controlling what enters and leaves -- distinct from the cell wall, which instead gives the cell its shape and rigidity.

### Mechanism
The Gram stain's colour result follows directly from cell-wall thickness: a thick peptidoglycan wall (Gram-positive) traps the crystal-violet/iodine complex through decolourisation, so the cell stays violet/purple; a thin wall with an outer lipid membrane (Gram-negative) lets that complex wash out, so the cell instead takes up the safranin counterstain and appears pink/red. Flagellar arrangement is classified purely by position and number: monotrichous (one flagellum, one pole), amphitrichous (one flagellum at each of two poles), lophotrichous (a tuft at one pole), or peritrichous (flagella all around the cell). Bacterial virulence factors -- the capsule, pili, extracellular enzymes and endotoxin -- let an organism establish infection; inclusion granules, by contrast, are an intracellular nutrient-storage structure with no direct role in pathogenesis.

### Key determinants
The distinguishing fact between a prokaryote and eukaryote is the absence of a membrane-bound nucleus and organelles, not merely "being smaller" or "having fewer structures." The Gram-stain colour is a readout of wall thickness, not an arbitrary label to memorise independently of structure. Flagellar-arrangement terms name position and count only (monotrichous/amphitrichous differ by how many poles carry a single flagellum each; lophotrichous differs from both by clustering several flagella at one pole).

### Clinical significance
The Gram stain remains the first laboratory step in identifying an unknown bacterial pathogen and in choosing empirical antibiotic therapy, because Gram-positive and Gram-negative organisms differ systematically in cell-wall structure, antibiotic susceptibility and toxin type. Recognising a bacterium's virulence factors is the basis for understanding how it produces disease and for targeting vaccines (e.g. capsular-polysaccharide vaccines) or therapy at a specific factor.

### Common misconceptions
Treating "monoclonal cells" as the standard term for a population derived from one cell, rather than "clone." Assuming a prokaryote has some organelles or a loosely-bound nucleus, rather than none and no true nucleus at all. Reversing the Gram-stain colours, or treating inclusion granules as a virulence factor rather than a storage structure.

## summary
The second half of AUN-INI-105's Chapter 1 (Q51-Q100) adds four facts the first half's article did not cover: bacterial flagella are strongly antigenic despite having no human counterpart; lysozyme treatment converts a Gram-positive cell into a wall-less protoplast but a Gram-negative cell into a spheroplast that keeps a residual outer membrane; taxonomy is the specific science of naming and classifying organisms; and every true cell is prokaryotic or eukaryotic, while a virus is acellular altogether.

## sections
### Definition
Bacterial flagella are strongly antigenic, recognised by the host immune system as the flagellar (H) antigen and used for serotyping -- they share no structural or antigenic relationship with any human structure. Taxonomy is the scientific discipline of naming, describing and classifying organisms into a hierarchical, ordered system, distinct from unrelated fields such as bioinformatics, molecular biology or cosmology. Every true, living cell is either prokaryotic (bacteria, no membrane-bound nucleus) or eukaryotic (fungi, human and other animal cells, with a true nucleus and organelles); a virus is not a true cell at all, but an acellular, obligate intracellular particle with no independent metabolism or ribosomes of its own.

### Mechanism
Treating a bacterium with lysozyme (or another wall-degrading agent) in an osmotically protective medium strips away its peptidoglycan. A Gram-positive cell, which has only a peptidoglycan wall external to its membrane, is left as a fully wall-less protoplast; a Gram-negative cell, whose outer membrane survives lysozyme treatment, is left as a spheroplast that still carries a residual outer-membrane layer. Neither wall-deficient form can replicate, and both lyse without the protective medium's osmotic support.

### Key determinants
The distinguishing fact between a protoplast and a spheroplast is which Gram type it came from: protoplast is the Gram-positive result (nothing external to the membrane survives), spheroplast is the Gram-negative result (the outer membrane persists). Flagellar antigenicity follows directly from flagellin being a bacterial protein foreign to the human body -- there is no structural kinship with any human appendage that would make it "self" and therefore nonantigenic. A virus's acellular status is the key determinant separating it from bacteria and fungi: no independent metabolism, no ribosomes, no replication outside a host cell.

### Clinical significance
Flagellar (H) antigen typing, alongside somatic (O) antigen typing, underlies the serological identification of organisms such as Salmonella and E. coli in outbreak investigation. Recognising cell-wall-deficient forms (protoplasts, spheroplasts, and the related L-forms) matters clinically because such forms can persist despite wall-active antibiotic therapy, since the drug's target (the wall) is already gone. Correctly classifying an infectious agent as bacterial, fungal or viral is the first branch point in choosing an antimicrobial class, since antibacterial and antifungal drugs act on cellular targets a virus does not have.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming bacterial flagella are nonantigenic because some human cells (e.g. sperm) also have flagella-like structures -- the two share neither composition nor antigenicity. Reversing which Gram type yields a protoplast versus a spheroplast. Treating a virus as a very small bacterium or a type of cell, rather than as a fundamentally acellular particle. Confusing taxonomy (naming and classifying organisms) with molecular biology or bioinformatics merely because modern taxonomy uses molecular data as one of its tools.

### Related structures
Some bacteria store nutrient reserves as cytoplasmic inclusion (storage) granules; Corynebacterium diphtheriae is the classic example, accumulating polyphosphate as metachromatic (Babes-Ernst) granules that stain a different colour from the surrounding cytoplasm and aid its microscopic identification. This storage role is unrelated to the protein-based, motility- and antigenicity-conferring surface structures (flagella, pili) discussed above.

## summary
AUN-INI-105's third microbiology chapter covers bacteriophage biology: what a bacteriophage is, the two competing pathways it can take after infecting a bacterial cell (the lysogenic cycle of a temperate phage versus the lytic cycle of a virulent phage), and how phages mediate horizontal gene transfer between bacteria through transduction. What determines a phage's host range (the tail) and how a lysogenized cell differs from a naive one (it carries a prophage) round out the chapter's core vocabulary.

## sections
### Definition
A bacteriophage ("phage") is a virus that infects bacteria specifically, not human or other eukaryotic cells. On infecting a bacterial cell, a phage's DNA is first transcribed (its early genes are read by the host's RNA polymerase) before the cell commits to one of two pathways. A temperate phage's genome integrates into the bacterial chromosome as a prophage -- the lysogenic cycle -- while most normal bacterial functions continue. A virulent phage instead commits to the lytic cycle: it destroys the host's own DNA, directs synthesis of many new phage particles, and ends by lysing the cell.

### Mechanism
A lysogenized bacterial cell is one that carries a prophage -- the temperate phage's genome, integrated at a specific site in the bacterial chromosome. Lysogenic conversion is the process by which genes carried on that prophage change the host bacterium's phenotype (for example, conferring new toxin-producing ability), a change driven by the phage's own genes rather than by uptake of free environmental DNA. Bacteriophages also mediate gene transfer between bacteria -- transduction -- by accidentally or specifically packaging host DNA into a new phage particle during replication and delivering it to the next bacterium infected; the transfer of a specific portion of bacterial DNA "with the help of" a bacteriophage is the hallmark of this mechanism, distinct from transformation's naked-DNA uptake or conjugation's cell-to-cell contact. A phage's host specificity -- which bacterial species or strain it can infect -- is usually determined by its tail, whose fibers recognise a matching surface receptor.

### Key determinants
Whether a phage takes the lysogenic or lytic path is the central branch point in phage biology: chromosome integration and continued normal bacterial function mark lysogeny (temperate phage); host DNA destruction, new-virion synthesis and cell lysis mark the lytic cycle (virulent phage). Bacteriophages are not human pathogens -- they infect bacteria only -- but they are far from biologically inert: beyond gene transfer and lysogenic conversion, phages are used in bacterial typing (identifying strains by their pattern of susceptibility to different phages) and as vectors for gene cloning in the laboratory.

### Clinical significance
Lysogenic conversion is directly responsible for several classic examples of toxin-mediated disease, where a prophage's own genes supply the toxin gene a bacterium would not otherwise carry. Phage-mediated transduction is likewise a real route by which antibiotic-resistance and virulence genes spread between bacteria in a population, alongside conjugation and transformation.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming any virus discussed in a microbiology bank must infect humans -- a bacteriophage's host is bacteria, not human cells. Reversing which cycle (lytic vs lysogenic) integrates into the chromosome, or assuming the lytic cycle ends by ordinary bacterial cell replication rather than by lysis. Attributing host specificity to the phage's nucleic acid type or capsid shape rather than its tail.

## summary
AUN-INI-105's fourth microbiology chapter is the module's dense genetics chapter: the vocabulary of gene/genome/genotype, the vehicles of bacterial variation (plasmids, episomes, transposons, pathogenicity islands), the molecular mechanics of DNA replication and PCR, and applied tools (restriction endonucleases, DNA probes, gene therapy, gene cassettes). It closes with why Escherichia coli is genetic engineering's workhorse organism.

## sections
### Definition
A gene is a DNA segment carrying specific information; the genome is a cell's entire DNA; the genotype is its set of genetic determinants -- three related but distinct scales of genetic information. Bacteria are haploid, carrying their essential genetic information on a single circular chromosome (the nuclear body); ribosomes, the cell membrane and the cell wall are likewise essential structures, while fimbriae are not. A plasmid is an extrachromosomal genetic factor that replicates independently of the chromosome; an episome is a plasmid additionally able to integrate into the host chromosome. A transposon is a mobile DNA sequence that can move from one site to another (and may carry genes such as toxin genes), but unlike a plasmid it does not replicate autonomously. A pathogenicity island is a special class of mobile genetic element specifically defined by carrying virulence genes.

### Mechanism
DNA replication is semiconservative: helicase breaks the hydrogen bonds between nucleotides (2 for A-T, 3 for G-C) to unwind the duplex, after which the leading strand is synthesized continuously and the lagging strand intermittently, in short fragments. A codon is a 3-base mRNA code word for one amino acid. A mutation is a permanent, heritable change in DNA sequence; radioactivity, UV rays and alkylating chemicals are recognised mutagenic factors, while disinfectants are not. Bacteria regulate gene expression -- switching genes on or off rather than expressing everything constantly -- to adapt to environmental change, conserve resources, and avoid wastefully producing every enzyme at once. Gene transfer between bacteria is parasexual: a unilateral, donor-to-recipient process, achieved by conjugation (sex pili, an F plasmid, and helicase; the donor survives), transformation (a competent recipient takes up free DNA, requiring sequence homology), or transduction (a bacteriophage carries donor DNA to a recipient). Conjugative plasmids characteristically show a high frequency of transfer, a wide host range, and multiple genetic determinants together.

### Key determinants
Restriction endonucleases are bacterial enzymes that recognise a specific nucleotide sequence and cleave DNA at that site, the basic tool of recombinant DNA technology. A DNA/RNA probe is a short, labeled, single-stranded sequence used to detect a complementary sequence by hybridization -- distinct from a primer or a promoter. Gene cassettes exist freely or incorporated into an integron. Each PCR cycle proceeds through three mechanistically ordered steps: DNA denaturation, then primer annealing, then primer extension. R plasmids carry antibiotic-resistance genes and are the main genetic determinant of drug resistance in bacteria, though endotoxin (LPS) production remains chromosomally, not plasmid, encoded. Gene therapy replaces a mutated gene in the host cell with a healthy copy. Escherichia coli, owing to its well-characterised genetics and tool availability, is the bacterium most commonly used as a genetic-engineering host.

### Clinical significance
Antibiotic resistance genes moving on R plasmids, transposons and gene cassettes (via conjugation, transduction or transformation) are the molecular basis of the spread of drug resistance across bacterial populations -- a mechanism directly relevant to why resistance can appear in a species that was never itself directly exposed to an antibiotic. Restriction endonucleases, DNA probes and PCR are the working tools behind molecular diagnosis of infection and behind recombinant DNA/gene-therapy technology.

### Common misconceptions
Treating "gene," "genome" and "genotype" as interchangeable. Assuming a transposon replicates autonomously like a plasmid. Reversing the leading-vs-lagging-strand continuity, or the A-T/G-C hydrogen-bond counts. Assuming disinfectants are mutagenic because they are antimicrobial. Assuming conjugation needs no cell-to-cell contact, or that the donor dies during conjugation.

R plasmids carry antibiotic-resistance genes; endotoxin remains chromosomally encoded.
E. coli is genetic engineering's most commonly used host organism.

## lose_the_mark
Using "gene," "genome" and "genotype" interchangeably.
Saying a transposon replicates autonomously.
Reversing which DNA strand synthesizes continuously vs intermittently, or the A-T/G-C hydrogen-bond counts.
Calling disinfectants mutagenic.
Saying conjugation needs no cell contact or that the donor dies.
Reordering the PCR steps.
