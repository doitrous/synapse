## summary
The fourth article of AUN-INI-105's Nematoda chapter revisits the luminal intestinal nematodes and Trichinella -- Ascaris lumbricoides, Enterobius vermicularis, Trichuris trichiura, Ancylostoma duodenale, Strongyloides stercoralis and Trichinella spiralis -- covering their diagnosis, habitat and life cycle from a second pass through the bank's repeated Q111-164 window (Q111-164 heavily re-asks the same organisms already introduced in the chapter's Q1-110 window, but from new diagnostic, habitat and definitional angles).

## sections
### Definition
Nematodes are round worms: elongated, cylindrical, unsegmented worms with a complete (tubular) digestive tract and separate sexes, distinguishing them from the flat, leaf-shaped trematodes (flukes) and from the flat, segmented, hermaphroditic cestodes (tapeworms). Strongyloides stercoralis is unique among these nematodes in having a facultative free-living cycle in the soil, in addition to its parasitic cycle within a human host, so that rhabditiform larvae passed in the stool can either develop directly into infective filariform larvae (homogonic/direct cycle) or develop into free-living adult males and females that reproduce in the soil for one or more generations (heterogonic/indirect cycle) before again producing infective filariform larvae.

### Mechanism
The infective stage of Ancylostoma duodenale is the ensheathed third-stage (filariform) larva that develops in the soil from the egg passed in faeces; this larva actively penetrates intact human skin, usually of the feet, to establish infection, unlike the mature (embryonated) egg, which is the infective stage of Ascaris and Trichuris rather than of hookworm. The infective stage of Enterobius vermicularis is the fully embryonated (mature) egg, which becomes infective within a few hours of being deposited by the gravid female on the perianal skin and is then swallowed by the same host through hand-to-mouth contact (autoinfection) or by a close contact; this is unlike hookworm, whose infective stage is instead a skin-penetrating larva. The embryonated (mature) egg is the infective stage shared by both Trichuris trichiura (Trichocephalus trichuris) and Ascaris lumbricoides, both of which require a period of maturation in soil before their eggs become infective by ingestion; this differs from Enterobius vermicularis, whose egg embryonates within hours on the perianal skin rather than in soil, and from hookworms, which infect instead through a skin-penetrating filariform larva rather than through an ingested egg at all. Enterobius vermicularis has a short, direct life cycle confined entirely to the intestine and perianal skin, with no obligatory larval migration through the lungs, unlike Ascaris lumbricoides, Ancylostoma duodenale and Strongyloides stercoralis, all of which undergo an obligatory hepato-pulmonary (or equivalent) migratory phase before their larvae return to the intestine to mature.

### Key determinants
The adult Ascaris lumbricoides worm lives free in the lumen of the small intestine, feeding on semi-digested intestinal contents, rather than in the ileocaecal region, sigmoid colon or liver, which are instead sites associated with other intestinal parasites or with Ascaris's larval migratory phase. Of these four nematodes, only Enterobius vermicularis characteristically inhabits the large intestine (the caecum and adjacent colon), while Trichinella spiralis larvae encyst in skeletal muscle, and both Ascaris lumbricoides and Strongyloides stercoralis instead inhabit the small intestine.

### Clinical significance
Enterobius vermicularis is the classic cause of perianal itching (pruritus ani), produced by the gravid female's nocturnal migration onto the perianal skin to lay her eggs, an irritation that is often worse at night and can lead to scratching, autoinfection and secondary bacterial infection of the excoriated skin. Muscle biopsy, typically of the deltoid or gastrocnemius, is used to diagnose Trichinella spiralis by demonstrating the coiled, encysted larvae within striated muscle fibres, a technique that is not applicable to Enterobius vermicularis, Ascaris lumbricoides or Strongyloides stercoralis, none of which encyst in muscle. Trichinella spiralis is unique among these four parasites in infecting man through ingestion of infected food, specifically undercooked meat (classically pork) containing encysted larvae, whereas Strongyloides stercoralis and Necator americanus infect by skin penetration and Schistosoma mansoni infects by cercarial skin penetration during contact with contaminated water.

### Common misconceptions
Enterobius vermicularis is best detected by applying the sticky surface of cellophane tape to the perianal area early in the morning before washing or defecation, then examining the tape as a microscopic slide, since the gravid female migrates out at night to deposit eggs on the perianal skin rather than releasing them into the gut lumen; a standard stool wet mount, by contrast, is a poor method for Enterobius because so few eggs are actually present in the faeces. The diagnostic stages of ascariasis can be recovered from both faeces and sputum: the fertilized, unembryonated egg is the diagnostic stage found in the stool of the adult-worm-bearing patient, while migrating Ascaris lumbricoides larvae can be coughed up and recovered in the sputum during the obligatory pulmonary migratory phase of the life cycle (Loeffler's syndrome), before the larvae are swallowed back into the small intestine to mature.

## hold_these
Nematodes = round worms (unsegmented, complete gut, separate sexes) vs trematodes (leaf-shaped) and cestodes (segmented).
Strongyloides alone has a facultative free-living soil cycle; Ascaris, Enterobius and Trichinella are obligate parasites.
Infective stages: Ancylostoma = filariform larva (skin penetration); Enterobius = mature egg (ingestion); Ascaris + Trichuris = embryonated egg (soil-matured, ingestion).
Enterobius has no pulmonary migratory phase, unlike Ascaris, Ancylostoma and Strongyloides.
Habitat: Ascaris = small intestine; Enterobius = large intestine (caecum).
Clinical signatures: Enterobius = perianal itching; Trichinella = muscle biopsy diagnosis, ingestion of undercooked meat.
Enterobius diagnosis = perianal cellophane tape technique, not stool wet mount; ascariasis diagnostic stages = faeces AND sputum (migratory phase).

## lose_the_mark
Calling any of these worms a trematode or cestode.
Assuming Ascaris or Enterobius can complete a free-living generation like Strongyloides.
Mixing up which organism's infective stage is a larva vs an egg.
Assuming Enterobius shares Ascaris's lung-migration phase.
Swapping the small-intestine (Ascaris) and large-intestine (Enterobius) habitats.
Attributing perianal itching or muscle biopsy diagnosis to the wrong organism.
Relying on a routine stool wet mount to diagnose Enterobius, or on stool alone to diagnose ascariasis.

## summary
The fifth article of AUN-INI-105's Nematoda chapter covers the filarial and tissue nematodes -- Onchocerca volvulus, Loa loa, Wuchereria bancrofti and Brugia malayi -- and the freshwater-crustacean-borne Dracunculus/Diphyllobothrium group, focusing on arthropod vectors, clinical eponyms (blinding worm, eye worm, river blindness, elephantiasis) and diagnostic methods, plus the contrast between soil- and water-transmitted skin-penetrating parasites.

## sections
### Definition
Brugia malayi is a lymphatic filarial worm whose adult worms live in the lymphatics and lymph nodes, where chronic infection causes lymphatic obstruction and lymphoedema similar to that caused by Wuchereria bancrofti; this distinguishes it from Onchocerca volvulus, which lives in subcutaneous nodules, and from Loa loa, whose adults migrate through the subcutaneous and connective tissues. Cyclops (a small freshwater crustacean) serves as the intermediate host for Diphyllobothrium latum, Diphyllobothrium mansoni and Dracunculus medinensis, whose larvae must develop within it before becoming infective to man; Trichinella spiralis, by contrast, has no aquatic intermediate host at all and is instead acquired directly by ingesting infected meat.

### Mechanism
Simulium (the blackfly), which breeds in fast-flowing rivers and streams, is the arthropod vector of Onchocerca volvulus, transmitting the infective filariform larvae during a blood meal near the riverbank -- a habitat that gives onchocerciasis its common name, 'river blindness'. The arthropod vector of Loa loa is the Chrysops fly, also called the mango fly or deer fly, a day-biting tabanid fly of forested regions of West and Central Africa, distinguishing it from the mosquito and Simulium vectors of the other human filarial worms.

### Key determinants
Onchocerca volvulus is known as the 'blinding worm' because chronic infection (onchocerciasis) is a leading infectious cause of blindness worldwide, caused by microfilariae migrating into and damaging the eye; this is distinct from Loa loa, which is called the 'eye worm' because the adult worm itself is sometimes seen migrating visibly across the subconjunctiva. Loa loa is known as the African eye worm because the adult worm migrates through subcutaneous tissue and can sometimes be seen, and even felt, moving visibly across the subconjunctiva of the eye, a striking clinical sign unique among the human filarial worms. River blindness (onchocerciasis) is caused by Onchocerca volvulus, transmitted by the riverine Simulium blackfly, and is distinguished from lymphatic filariasis caused by Brugia malayi, from the subcutaneous/ocular migration caused by Loa loa, and from the generally mild, often asymptomatic infection caused by Mansonella ozzardi.

### Clinical significance
Elephantiasis, the gross lymphoedematous swelling most classically of the lower limbs and genitalia, is caused by chronic lymphatic filariasis due to Wuchereria bancrofti, whose adult worms live in the lymphatics and lymph nodes and cause progressive lymphatic obstruction and fibrosis; Onchocerca volvulus, Loa loa and Mansonella perstans do not characteristically cause this lymphatic obstructive picture. Lymphatic filariasis (elephantiasis) due to Wuchereria bancrofti is diagnosed by demonstrating microfilariae circulating in the peripheral blood, typically on a blood film taken at night to match the parasite's characteristic nocturnal periodicity, since the adult worms themselves remain sequestered within the lymphatics and are not found circulating in blood.

### Common misconceptions
Ancylostoma duodenale, Strongyloides stercoralis and the non-human hookworm larvae that cause creeping eruption (cutaneous larva migrans) all infect man by direct skin penetration while walking barefoot on contaminated soil; Schistosoma, by contrast, infects by cercarial skin penetration during contact with contaminated fresh water, not soil.

## hold_these
Lymphatic filarial worms (lymphatics as habitat): Wuchereria bancrofti and Brugia malayi.
Cyclops is the intermediate host for Diphyllobothrium (both species) and Dracunculus, NOT for Trichinella (food-borne).
Vectors: Onchocerca volvulus = Simulium (blackfly); Loa loa = Chrysops (mango fly); Wuchereria bancrofti = culicine mosquito; Brugia malayi = Mansonia.
Eponyms: Onchocerca volvulus = blinding worm / river blindness (ocular damage from microfilariae); Loa loa = African eye worm (visible adult migration across the eye).
Elephantiasis is caused by Wuchereria bancrofti and diagnosed by finding microfilaria in a nocturnal blood film.
Schistosoma is transmitted by water contact (cercarial skin penetration), not bare-footed soil contact like hookworm, Strongyloides and non-human hookworm larvae (creeping eruption).

## lose_the_mark
Assuming Onchocerca volvulus or Loa loa live in the lymphatics like Wuchereria bancrofti and Brugia malayi.
Assuming Trichinella spiralis uses Cyclops as an intermediate host.
Swapping the Simulium/Onchocerca and Chrysops/Loa loa vector pairings.
Swapping the 'blinding worm'/'river blindness' (Onchocerca) and 'eye worm' (Loa loa) eponyms.
Attributing elephantiasis to the wrong filarial worm, or forgetting the nocturnal-periodicity blood film timing.
Grouping Schistosoma with the soil-transmitted, skin-penetrating nematodes.
