## summary
AUN-INI-105's seventh chapter closes out the microbiology half of the bank with general mycology: fungi as eukaryotes with a true nucleus, chromosomes and mitochondria, reproducing by budding (yeasts) or mycelial fragmentation (molds); the fungal cell wall (chitin, glucan, mannan) and membrane (ergosterol, the target behind most antifungal selective toxicity); how mycoses are classified by depth (superficial, cutaneous, subcutaneous, systemic); and a tour of the medically important fungi tested in this chapter -- Candida albicans, Cryptococcus neoformans, Aspergillus flavus, the dermatophytes, and the motile zoospores of the lower fungi.

## sections
### Definition
Fungi are eukaryotic organisms -- each cell has a true, membrane-bound nucleus with chromosomes and organelles such as mitochondria, unlike prokaryotic bacteria. They reproduce differently by growth form: yeasts (unicellular) multiply by budding, while molds (filamentous) propagate their mycelium by fragmentation, each broken fragment able to regrow into a new mycelium. A mold's hyphae may be septate (cross-walled) or non-septate (coenocytic) depending on the species -- this variability is itself a testable fact, not a fixed property of "molds" in general.

### Mechanism
The fungal cell wall and membrane are the chemical basis of both fungal identity and antifungal drug selectivity. Chitin is the main structural component of most fungal cell walls (alongside glucan and mannan), chemically and structurally distinct from bacterial peptidoglycan. The fungal cytoplasmic membrane contains ergosterol as its characteristic sterol in place of the cholesterol used by human cell membranes -- viruses have no independently synthesised membrane of their own, and Chlamydia's atypical envelope lacks sterol, leaving fungi as the sterol-containing group among the three. Because ergosterol is fungus-specific, most major antifungal drug classes are built around it (polyenes bind it directly; azoles block its synthesis), giving these drugs their selective toxicity against fungi while sparing human cells.

### Key determinants
Mycoses are classified by the depth of tissue involved: superficial (outermost keratin only, no inflammation -- tinea versicolor), cutaneous/dermatophytic (living keratinized skin, hair, nail, with inflammation), subcutaneous (dermis and subcutaneous tissue), and systemic (deep organs). Dermatophytes are keratinophilic fungi confined to the superficial keratinized areas of the body and do not cause subcutaneous or systemic disease. Candida albicans is recognized in tissue by the co-occurrence of yeast cells and pseudohyphae, causes thrush, and is transmitted endogenously (as normal mucosal flora undergoing opportunistic overgrowth) rather than primarily by respiratory aerosol. Cryptococcus neoformans, by contrast, is an inhalation-acquired, capsulated opportunistic yeast causing pneumonia and meningitis, and unlike Aspergillus flavus (which produces the mycotoxin aflatoxin, causing mycotoxicosis), does not produce mycotoxins. Among the lower fungi, zoospores are a type of asexual fungal spore that, unlike most fungal spores, are motile, flagellated, and produced inside zoosporangia by aquatic fungi such as water molds.

### Clinical significance
Opportunistic fungal infections are initiated by defective cell-mediated immunity, prolonged antibiotic use (which disrupts protective bacterial flora) and chemotherapy/immunosuppression -- not by anaerobic wound conditions, which instead favor opportunistic anaerobic bacterial infection. Recognizing which fungal features matter clinically -- Candida's endogenous origin and cell-mediated-immunity dependence, Cryptococcus's inhalational acquisition and CNS tropism, dermatophytes' restriction to keratinized tissue -- shapes both the differential diagnosis of a given mycosis and the antifungal strategy chosen against it.

### Common misconceptions
Assuming every mold's hyphae are uniformly septate, or every fungus is dimorphic. Assuming antifungal selective toxicity works by the same logic as antibacterial selective toxicity (a structure entirely absent from human cells) -- for antifungals it hinges on a chemically different sterol (ergosterol vs. cholesterol) occupying an analogous role in both organisms. Assuming Candida is transmitted like an inhaled respiratory pathogen, rather than arising from its own endogenous mucosal flora. Assuming any tissue-damaging or immunocompromising condition is automatically a fungal-infection risk factor, when anaerobic wound conditions specifically favor anaerobic bacteria, not fungi.

## hold_these
Fungi are eukaryotes (true nucleus, chromosomes, mitochondria); yeasts reproduce by budding, molds propagate by mycelial fragmentation.
The fungal cell wall's major components are chitin, glucan and mannan; the fungal membrane's characteristic sterol is ergosterol, the basis of most antifungal drugs' selective toxicity.
Mycoses are classified by depth: superficial (tinea versicolor), cutaneous/dermatophytic, subcutaneous, systemic.
Candida albicans = yeasts + pseudohyphae in tissue, endogenous transmission, cell-mediated-immunity dependent; Cryptococcus neoformans = inhaled, capsulated, causes pneumonia + meningitis, no mycotoxins; Aspergillus flavus = aflatoxin, mycotoxicosis.
Opportunistic fungal infection risk factors: defective cell-mediated immunity, prolonged antibiotics, chemotherapy -- not anaerobic wound conditions.

## lose_the_mark
Treating all mold hyphae as uniformly septate or all fungi as dimorphic.
Explaining antifungal selective toxicity the same way as antibacterial selective toxicity, rather than via the ergosterol/cholesterol sterol difference.
Describing Candida albicans as respiratory-aerosol transmitted rather than endogenous/opportunistic.
Attributing mycotoxin production to Cryptococcus neoformans instead of Aspergillus flavus.
Listing anaerobic wound conditions as a risk factor for opportunistic fungal infection.
