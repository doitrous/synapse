## title
The nerve impulse is electrochemical in nature

## question
The nerve impulse is best described as:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Chemical in nature, involving chemical mechanisms contained in the fluid surrounding the cell

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A nerve impulse is not confined to chemical mechanisms in the surrounding fluid; it is a membrane event that also has a directly recordable electrical (voltage) component, which a purely chemical description leaves out.

## answer_b
Electrochemical in nature, involving the rapid movement of positively charged ions back and forth across cell membranes

## explanation_b
Correct. The nerve impulse is electrochemical: it is the rapid movement of positively charged ions, chiefly sodium moving in during depolarisation and potassium moving out during repolarisation, across the excitable membrane. This ionic movement (the chemical half) produces a propagating, measurable reversal of membrane potential (the electrical half), and it is exactly this combination that the word electrochemical names. The impulse also regenerates itself at full amplitude along the whole fibre once threshold is reached, rather than fading with distance the way a passive chemical or electrical signal alone would.

## answer_c
Movement of electrons along the interior of the cell membrane, much like electrical conduction through a copper wire

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Nerve conduction is not a flow of free electrons the way current runs through a copper wire; it is powered by ion movement through specific channels, not by electron flow.

## answer_d
Chemical in nature and occurring deep within the interior of the cell

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The impulse is not purely chemical, and it is not confined to the cell's interior; it is a membrane-surface event with a propagating electrical signature.

## title
Pancreatic beta cells secrete insulin

## question
Insulin is secreted by which type of cells in the pancreas?

## subject
fnd

## status

Claude

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Beta cells

## explanation_a
Correct. Within the pancreatic islets, the beta cells are the ones that secrete insulin, the main hypoglycaemic hormone, in response to a rise in blood glucose. Insulin lowers blood glucose by promoting its uptake and storage as glycogen and fat, and its absence or deficiency underlies diabetes mellitus. Beta cells are the most numerous islet cell type and typically occupy the centre of the islet.

Incorrect. The alpha cells secrete glucagon, the hyperglycaemic hormone that raises blood glucose, not insulin.

Delta cells

Incorrect. The delta cells secrete somatostatin, which inhibits both insulin and glucagon secretion locally; they do not secrete insulin themselves.

Beta and alpha cells

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Insulin secretion is specific to the beta cells; the alpha cells secrete the opposing hormone, glucagon, not insulin.

## title
The liver is the body's glucostat

## question
The "glucostat" of the body is the:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Liver

## explanation_a
Correct. The liver is often called the body's glucostat because it senses blood glucose and buffers it in both directions -- taking up and storing glucose as glycogen when levels rise, and releasing glucose via glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis when they fall -- more directly and immediately than any other organ. It can do this because it is the one organ that both stores substantial glycogen and retains glucose 6-phosphatase, letting it release free glucose back into the blood rather than only consuming glucose for its own metabolism. This dual store-and-release capacity is exactly what distinguishes a true glucostat from an organ that merely uses or absorbs glucose.

## answer_b
Intestine

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The intestine absorbs dietary glucose but does not buffer circulating blood glucose levels the way the liver does.

## answer_c
Adipose tissue

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Adipose tissue stores energy mainly as triacylglycerol and responds to glucose/insulin signals, but it is not the organ that directly senses and buffers blood glucose the way the liver does.

## answer_d
Muscle

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Muscle takes up glucose and stores glycogen for its own use, but unlike the liver it lacks glucose 6-phosphatase and so cannot release free glucose back into the blood, disqualifying it as the body's glucostat.

## title
Glycogenin is the protein primer for glycogen synthesis

## question
Glycogenin is:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
A protein primer for glycogen synthesis

## explanation_a
Correct. Glycogen cannot begin forming on a bare glucose molecule; the first several glucose units are covalently attached to the protein primer glycogenin, which auto-glucosylates itself and then serves as the core that glycogen synthase extends the growing chain from. Without this priming step, glycogen synthase would have nothing to attach new glucose residues to. Every glycogen molecule in the body therefore still carries a single glycogenin molecule buried at its centre, even after the chain has grown to its full branched size.

## answer_b
An intermediate in glycogen breakdown

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Glycogenin acts in glycogen synthesis, not breakdown; glycogenolysis is instead carried out by glycogen phosphorylase and the debranching enzyme.

## answer_c
A polymer of glycogen molecules

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Glycogenin is a single protein primer, not a polymer of glucose (glycogen) molecules; it is what the glycogen polymer is built onto, not the polymer itself.

## answer_d
An uncoupler of oxidative phosphorylation

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Glycogenin has no role in mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation or its uncoupling; it is specific to initiating glycogen synthesis.

Brown fat dominates neonatal thermoregulation

## question
Brown fat is:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
More important than shivering in neonatal thermoregulation

## explanation_a
Correct. In the neonate, whose shivering mechanism is still immature and who has a large surface-area-to-mass ratio that makes heat loss especially dangerous, sympathetically driven brown fat (which produces heat by uncoupled mitochondrial oxidation, non-shivering thermogenesis) is more important than shivering as a defence against cold. A newborn exposed to cold therefore relies on this chemical, sympathetically driven heat source well before it can meaningfully shiver its way warm.

Poorer in mitochondria than ordinary (white) fat

Incorrect. Brown fat is richer in mitochondria than ordinary white fat, not poorer -- its abundant mitochondria are what give it its brown colour and its capacity for direct heat production.

## answer_c
Relatively more abundant in adults than in infants

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Brown fat is relatively more abundant in infants than in adults; adults rely much more on shivering, since their brown fat depots are comparatively scarce.

## answer_d
Stimulated to generate more heat when its parasympathetic nerve supply is stimulated

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Brown fat is stimulated by its sympathetic, not parasympathetic, nerve supply, consistent with the sympathetic nervous system's general role in mobilising the body's response to cold.

## title
Heat acclimatization increases sweating capacity

## question
Following adaptation (acclimatization) to a hot climate, there is an increase in:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The ability to lose heat by sweating

## explanation_a
Correct. Heat acclimatization produces sweat gland hypertrophy and a greater sweat output for a given thermal stimulus, together with rising aldosterone action that lowers the salt content of the sweat -- so the single adaptation most directly tested here is an increased ability to lose heat by sweating. This higher-volume, lower-salt sweat lets an acclimatized person dissipate more heat for the same fluid and electrolyte cost. Someone newly arrived in a hot climate, by contrast, sweats less efficiently and loses proportionally more salt until this adaptation develops over one to two weeks.

## answer_b
Basal metabolic rate

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Basal metabolic rate is not what heat acclimatization changes; the adaptation is specific to the sweating mechanism, not a general rise in metabolic activity.

## answer_c
Muscle tone

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Muscle tone relates to cold defence (shivering thermogenesis), not to heat acclimatization, which instead improves evaporative heat loss via sweating.

## answer_d
Urinary output

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Urinary output is not the adaptation heat acclimatization produces; if anything, aldosterone's sodium-conserving action during acclimatization is aimed at the sweat glands, not primarily at increasing urine volume.

## topic
Thermoregulation

## subtopic
Heat acclimatization
