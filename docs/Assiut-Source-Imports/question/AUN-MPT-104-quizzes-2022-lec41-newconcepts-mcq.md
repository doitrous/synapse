## title
Type IV collagenase degrades the ECM in tumour spread

## question
Degradation of the ECM occurs by proteolytic enzymes secreted by tumour cells as:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
elastase

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Elastase degrades elastin fibres rather than the type IV collagen that forms the basement membrane's key structural scaffold, so it is not the enzyme this teaching point names for extracellular matrix degradation in tumour spread.

## answer_b
Lipase

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Lipase hydrolyses fat, an activity unrelated to breaching the collagenous basement membrane that separates a tumour from the tissue it invades.

## answer_c
Amylase

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Amylase digests starch, an activity with no role in degrading the extracellular matrix or basement membrane that a malignant tumour must cross to invade.

## answer_d
type IV collagenase

## explanation_d
Correct. Malignant tumour cells secrete type IV collagenase, a matrix metalloproteinase that specifically degrades type IV collagen, the main structural protein of the basement membrane. This enzymatic degradation of the extracellular matrix is one of the key steps that lets tumour cells breach the basement membrane and invade surrounding tissue, in contrast to elastase, lipase and amylase, none of which targets this collagen type.

## title
Malignant cells first appear in a lymph node's subcapsular sinus

## question
In examining a lymph node microscopically for suspicion of metastasis, the malignant cells are expected to appear first in:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
the medullary sinuses

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The medullary sinuses lie deeper within the node and filter lymph after it has already passed through the cortex, so they are not the first site where an afferent lymphatic carrying tumour cells deposits them.

## answer_b
the lymphoid follicles

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The lymphoid follicles are B-lymphocyte territory involved in the node's immune response, not the sinus pathway that lymph, and any tumour cells it carries, first traverses on entering the node.

## answer_c
the sub-capsular lymph sinuses

## explanation_c
Correct. Afferent lymphatics draining a tumour empty first into the subcapsular (marginal) sinus immediately beneath the capsule, so malignant cells travelling via lymph are expected to be trapped and first detectable there before spreading deeper into the cortical and medullary sinuses. This is why the subcapsular sinus is the first site a pathologist examines closely for early micrometastasis, ahead of the medullary sinuses, the lymphoid follicles, the efferent vessel or the paracortex.

## answer_d
the efferent vessel

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The efferent vessel carries lymph, and any surviving tumour cells, out of the node after passing through it, making it the last, not the first, structure involved.

## answer_e
the paracortical area

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The paracortical area is the thymus-dependent, T-lymphocyte zone, not the sinus system that carries incoming lymph and any tumour cells it contains.

## title
Cadherins glue normal cells to each other

## question
Normal cells are glued to each other and to their surroundings by a variety of adhesion molecules as:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
type IV collagenase

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Type IV collagenase is a degradative enzyme that breaks down the basement membrane; it is not itself an adhesion molecule that glues cells together.

## answer_b
fibronectin

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Fibronectin is an extracellular matrix glycoprotein that helps cells attach to the matrix, but the printed key names cadherins, not fibronectin, for this general cell-to-cell adhesion role.

## answer_c
cadherins.

## explanation_c
Correct. Cadherins are calcium-dependent transmembrane adhesion molecules that mediate cell-to-cell adhesion, holding normal cells together within a tissue and to their surroundings; loss of cadherin function is one of the changes that allows malignant cells to detach and invade. This distinguishes cadherins from a degradative enzyme (type IV collagenase, cathepsin D) or other matrix components (fibronectin, laminin) named among the options.

## answer_d
cathepsin D

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Cathepsin D is a proteolytic enzyme associated with tissue degradation and tumour invasion, not an adhesion molecule that glues normal cells together.

## answer_e
laminin

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Laminin is a basement membrane glycoprotein rather than the general cell-to-cell adhesion molecule the printed key identifies.

## title
Perineural invasion in malignant tumours causes pain

## question
Perineural invasion in malignant tumors cause:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Haemorrhage

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Haemorrhage is a feature of tumour bleeding from friable, disorganised vasculature, not the characteristic clinical consequence of a tumour tracking along a nerve sheath.

## answer_b
mass

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A mass describes the tumour's own bulk, not the specific symptom that follows when malignant cells specifically invade along perineural spaces.

## answer_c
ulcer

## explanation_c
Incorrect. An ulcer results when a tumour breaches an overlying epithelial or mucosal surface, a different process from spread along a nerve.

## answer_d
Pain

## explanation_d
Correct. Perineural invasion, malignant cells tracking along the perineural (Schwann cell sheath) space surrounding a nerve, characteristically produces pain, since it irritates and compresses nerve fibres along the route of spread. This is a distinct pattern of local extension from haemorrhage, mass formation or ulceration, and is why pain out of proportion to a tumour's visible size can raise suspicion for perineural involvement, particularly in tumours such as pancreatic or head-and-neck carcinomas.

## title
Tumour-associated angiogenic factors drive tumour angiogenesis

## question
Tumour angiogenesis occurs under the influence of:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
enzymes as collagenase and elastase

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Collagenase and elastase degrade the extracellular matrix to help a tumour invade, but they are not themselves the drivers of new vessel formation.

## answer_b
tumour associated angiogenic factors

## explanation_b
Correct. Tumour angiogenesis, the formation of new blood vessels that supply a growing tumour, occurs under the influence of tumour-associated angiogenic factors (such as VEGF) secreted by the tumour cells themselves. These factors stimulate proliferation and sprouting of nearby endothelial cells, supplying the tumour with the blood flow it needs to grow beyond a few millimetres and to gain access to the vasculature for haematogenous spread, unlike matrix-degrading enzymes, complement components or general inflammatory mediators such as TNF.

## answer_c
complement components

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Complement components participate in immune and inflammatory responses, not in driving new vessel formation around a tumour.

## answer_d
Chemical mediators as TNF

## explanation_d
Incorrect. TNF is a general inflammatory chemical mediator; the printed key specifically identifies tumour-associated angiogenic factors, not TNF, as the driver of tumour angiogenesis.
