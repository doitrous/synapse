## title
Diarrhea is not an atropine adverse effect

## question
Adverse effects which frequently occur during treatment with high doses of atropine include all of the following EXCEPT:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Diarrhea

## explanation_a
Correct. Atropine, a muscarinic antagonist, reduces rather than increases gastrointestinal motility. This is why it is combined with diphenoxylate as an antidiarrheal, not why it causes diarrhea, making diarrhea the one effect that does NOT belong on this adverse-effect list.

## answer_b
Urinary retention

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Urinary retention, from reduced detrusor muscle tone, is a genuine and frequent atropine adverse effect, part of its systemic antimuscarinic profile.

## answer_c
Dry mouth

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Dry mouth, from reduced salivary secretion, is one of the most characteristic atropine adverse effects and a genuine member of this list.

## answer_d
Sandy eyes

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Sandy or dry eyes, from reduced lacrimation and impaired accommodation, are also a real atropine adverse effect, consistent with its systemic antimuscarinic action.

Blurred vision is an atropine adverse effect

## question
Which of the following is an adverse effect of atropine?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Atropine reduces, not increases, salivation -- dry mouth, the opposite effect, is the genuine atropine adverse effect.

## answer_b
Decreased intraocular pressure

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Atropine's mydriasis tends to raise, not decrease, intraocular pressure, and can precipitate an attack in narrow-angle glaucoma rather than lowering pressure.

Blurred vision

## explanation_c
Correct. Atropine blocks muscarinic receptors controlling accommodation, impairing the ciliary muscle's ability to focus for near vision. Combined with its mydriasis and reduced lacrimation, this produces blurred vision as a genuine and characteristic atropine adverse effect.

## answer_d
Bradycardia

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Atropine blocks the vagal (muscarinic) restraint on the heart, producing tachycardia at clinically relevant doses, not bradycardia.

## title
Physostigmine reverses the anticholinergic toxidrome

## question
A patient presents at Assiut University Hospital with high fever, hallucinations, marked mydriasis, and a dry mouth after ingesting an unknown drug. To counteract the effects of the drug you prescribe:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
A cholinesterase reactivator

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A cholinesterase reactivator (like pralidoxime) treats organophosphate poisoning by reactivating an inhibited enzyme, not the antimuscarinic toxidrome described here, which involves no enzyme inhibition to reverse.

## answer_b
NSAIDs

## explanation_b
Incorrect. NSAIDs treat pain and inflammation, not an anticholinergic toxidrome's fever, hallucinations, mydriasis and dry mouth.

## answer_c
A parasympatholytic

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A parasympatholytic (an antimuscarinic) would worsen, not counteract, this presentation, since the patient's symptoms are already caused by excess antimuscarinic action.

## answer_d
A centrally-acting cholinomimetic

## explanation_d
Correct. Fever, hallucinations, marked mydriasis and dry mouth together describe the anticholinergic toxidrome. A centrally-acting cholinomimetic -- physostigmine, a cholinesterase inhibitor that crosses the blood-brain barrier -- raises acetylcholine both centrally and peripherally, directly reversing the antimuscarinic block causing this picture.

## title
Muscarinic antagonists are contraindicated in narrow-angle glaucoma

## question
Muscarinic antagonists are useful for all of the following EXCEPT:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Treatment of narrow angle glaucoma

## explanation_a
Correct. Muscarinic antagonists are contraindicated, not useful, for narrow-angle glaucoma. The pupillary dilation (mydriasis) they cause can obstruct aqueous humour outflow and precipitate an acute angle-closure attack. This worsens, rather than treats, the condition -- exactly the opposite of the other three genuine uses on this list.

## answer_b
Antidote for cholinergic agonists

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Muscarinic antagonists are genuinely useful as an antidote for cholinergic-agonist (including organophosphate) poisoning, blocking the excess muscarinic stimulation directly.

## answer_c
Eye examinations

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Muscarinic antagonists are genuinely useful for eye examinations, since the mydriasis they cause allows a wider view of the retina.

## answer_d
Prevention of motion sickness

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Muscarinic antagonists (scopolamine specifically) are genuinely useful for preventing motion sickness, a real therapeutic use.

## title
Anticholinergic plant poisoning resembles scopolamine toxicity

## question
An 11 year old boy was brought to the ER by some of his friends because he "started going crazy" after eating seeds from a plant while "trying to get high." The boy was incoherent his skin was hot and dry. His pupils were dilated and unresponsive to light. Blood pressure was 180/105 mm Hg, pulse was 150/min, and rectal temp was 40 C (104 F). The presumptive diagnosis was drug toxicity due to the ingestion of a compound similar to

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Cannabis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Cannabis intoxication does not typically produce hot, dry skin, fixed dilated pupils and severe hyperthermia together -- this specific combination points to antimuscarinic, not cannabinoid, toxicity.

## answer_b
Mescaline

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Mescaline is a hallucinogen but does not classically produce this hot-dry-skin, fixed-mydriasis anticholinergic toxidrome.

## answer_c
Scopolamine

## explanation_c
Correct. Hot, dry skin, fixed dilated pupils, tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia and incoherence together describe the anticholinergic toxidrome, classically produced by plants (such as Datura/jimsonweed) containing scopolamine-like antimuscarinic alkaloids. Eating unidentified seeds "to get high" is a recognised route of exposure to exactly this class of compound.

## answer_d
Digoxin

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Digoxin toxicity produces GI symptoms, visual disturbances and arrhythmias, not the hot-dry-skin, fixed-mydriasis anticholinergic picture described here.

## answer_e
Phencyclidine

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Phencyclidine (PCP) intoxication produces agitation and dissociation but not this specific combination of hot dry skin and fixed dilated pupils that marks antimuscarinic toxicity.
