## title
Neurilemmal sheath is important for nerve regeneration

## question
It is correct to say that:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
dendrites conduct impulses away from the soma

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Dendrites conduct impulses toward the soma, not away from it -- the axon is the process that carries the impulse away from the cell body toward the next cell or target.

## answer_b
axon conducts impulse toward the soma

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This reverses the axon's actual direction of conduction: the axon carries the impulse away from the soma, toward the terminal, not toward it.

## answer_c
neurilemmal sheath is important for nerve regeneration

## explanation_c
Correct. The neurilemmal sheath (sheath of Schwann), the Schwann cell's own cytoplasm and nucleus forming the outermost covering of a peripheral axon, is what makes peripheral nerve regeneration possible after injury. It provides a physical guide -- a tube the Schwann cells maintain -- for a regrowing axon to follow back toward its original target. Central nervous system axons, which lack this neurilemmal sheath, do not have the same regenerative capacity.

## answer_d
neurilemmal sheath is a lipoprotein in nature

## explanation_d
Incorrect. It is the myelin sheath, not the neurilemmal sheath, that is lipoprotein in composition -- the neurilemmal sheath is Schwann cell cytoplasm and nucleus, not a lipoprotein membrane structure.

## answer_e
myelin is formed of single layer of Schwann cells

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Myelin is formed of many concentric layers of compacted Schwann-cell membrane wrapped around the axon, not a single layer -- it is this multilayered wrapping that gives myelin its insulating property.

## title
Most important ion for resting membrane potential

## question
The most important ion during resting membrane potential is:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Na+

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Sodium's resting permeability is low compared with potassium's, so Na+ contributes only a small part of the resting potential -- it is the depolarizing ion once voltage-gated channels open, not the ion that sets the resting value.

## answer_b
K+

## explanation_b
Correct. The resting nerve membrane is far more permeable to potassium than to any other ion, through leak channels, which is why the resting membrane potential sits close to the potassium equilibrium potential. This K+ dominance, not the concentration gradients of the other ions alone, is what makes potassium the single most important ion in establishing the resting potential. Sodium, calcium and chloride each play smaller or later roles, but none dominates the resting state the way K+ does.

## answer_c
Ca++

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The resting membrane's permeability to calcium is very low; calcium instead plays a threshold-stabilising role at the channel level, not a leading role in setting the resting potential itself.

## answer_d
Cl–

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Chloride crosses the resting membrane far less readily than potassium does in most excitable cells, so it does not dominate the resting potential the way K+ does.

## answer_e
HCO3-

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Bicarbonate is not a significant determinant of the resting membrane potential in nerve fibres; its role is chiefly in acid-base buffering, not membrane electrophysiology.

Incorrect. This comparison is not the defining feature of the RMP mechanism, and RMP magnitude is generally described as similar in large nerve and large skeletal muscle fibres rather than one being characteristically more negative than the other.

## title
Depolarization brought about by increased sodium permeability

## question
During an action potential, depolarization of nerve cell membrane is brought about by:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
increased sodium permeability

## explanation_a
Correct. Depolarization is caused by a rise in sodium permeability: voltage-gated Na+ channels open, Na+ moves down its steep electrochemical gradient into the cell, and this influx drives the membrane potential toward the sodium equilibrium potential. This is the rising phase of the action potential, and the size of the depolarization tracks the size of this permeability rise.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Increased chloride permeability moves the membrane potential toward the (negative) chloride equilibrium potential, stabilising or hyperpolarizing the membrane rather than depolarizing it.

## answer_d
increased potassium permeability

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Increased potassium permeability drives the membrane potential toward the (negative) potassium equilibrium potential, producing repolarization or hyperpolarization, the opposite of depolarization.

## answer_e
both a and b are correct

## explanation_e
Incorrect as a whole, since option B (calcium permeability) is not correct for the nerve action potential's depolarization -- only sodium permeability drives it.

## title
Depolarization coincides with inability to respond to a further stimulus

## question
Depolarization in the nerve:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
is caused by Na+ outflow

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Depolarization is caused by Na+ inflow (influx down its electrochemical gradient), not outflow -- reversing the direction of Na+ movement here reverses the actual mechanism.

## answer_b
is caused by K+ outflow

## explanation_b
Incorrect. K+ outflow drives repolarization, the return toward the resting level, not depolarization -- the two phases are driven by opposite ionic movements.

## answer_c
is due to opening of inactivation gates of voltage gated Na+ channels

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Inactivation gates close, not open, as depolarization proceeds -- an open inactivation gate describes the resting, not the depolarizing, state of the channel.

## answer_d
is represented by descending limb of the spike

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Depolarization is represented by the ascending limb of the spike, not the descending limb -- the descending limb is repolarization.

## answer_e
is that during which the nerve can not respond to any stimulus

## explanation_e
Correct. Depolarization occupies roughly the same window as the absolute refractory period: once enough voltage-gated Na+ channels have opened and then had their inactivation gates close, no further stimulus, however strong, can trigger a second action potential until those channels recover. So depolarization is exactly the interval during which the nerve cannot respond to any stimulus. Only once repolarization is well underway and enough channels have recovered does the fibre regain the ability to fire again.

## subject

## explanation_b
Correct. As depolarization proceeds, voltage-gated Na+ channels' separate inactivation gates begin closing on a short delay, and once enough of them are closed the fibre enters the absolute refractory period. both c & d are correct

Incorrect, since option D is not correct and option C misassigns potassium channel activation to the depolarization phase rather than to repolarization.

## question
Threshold for production of an action potential in a nerve:

## subject

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

B

is a membrane potential (transmembrane voltage) at which the increase in K+ permeability exceeds the increase in Na+ permeability

Correct. Incorrect, since option A is not correct -- only B correctly describes threshold.

none of the above

Incorrect, since option B is correct.

Absolute refractory period: excitability completely lost

## question
The absolute refractory period:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
is a period during which excitability is completely lost

## explanation_a
Correct. During the absolute refractory period, enough voltage-gated Na+ channels have their inactivation gates closed that no stimulus, however strong, can trigger a second action potential. Excitability is not merely reduced but completely lost until enough channels recover. This qualitative difference is exactly what separates the absolute from the relative refractory period.

## answer_b
is a period during which stronger stimulus is needed to excite the muscle fiber

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Needing only a stronger-than-normal stimulus describes the relative refractory period, where some Na+ channels are still available; during the absolute refractory period, no stimulus strength suffices.

## answer_c
is coincided with the descending limb of the spike

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The absolute refractory period runs from roughly the start of depolarization through the early part of repolarization, not specifically coinciding with the descending limb (repolarization) alone.

## answer_d
is a period during which the excitability is less than normal

## explanation_d
Incorrect. "Less than normal" describes the relative refractory period's reduced, but nonzero, excitability -- the absolute refractory period is qualitatively different, a complete, not partial, loss.

## answer_e
none of the above

## explanation_e
Incorrect, since option A correctly describes the absolute refractory period.

## title
Absolute refractory period is associated with loss of excitability

## question
It is correct to say that:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
subthreshold stimulus leads to an action potential

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A subthreshold stimulus, by definition, is not strong enough to trigger the regenerative Na+ influx an action potential needs -- it produces only a fading local response, not a propagated action potential.

## answer_b
a decrease in Ca++ in ECF leads to a decrease in excitability in the nerve

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A decrease in extracellular Ca2+ removes some of calcium's membrane-stabilising effect on Na+ channels, lowering the threshold and increasing, not decreasing, excitability (hypocalcaemic tetany).

## answer_c
a decrease in K+ in ECF leads to an increase in excitability in the nerve

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A decrease in extracellular K+ hyperpolarizes the resting membrane, moving it further from threshold, which decreases excitability rather than increasing it.

## answer_d
absolute refractory period is associated with loss of excitability

## explanation_d
Correct. During the absolute refractory period, the Na+ channels needed to fire a second action potential have their inactivation gates closed and are unavailable, so the fibre's excitability is lost. No stimulus, however strong, can excite it until enough channels recover.

## answer_e
absolute refractory period is associated with only a decrease in excitability

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Describing the absolute refractory period as "only a decrease" understates it -- excitability during this period is not merely decreased but completely abolished, which is exactly what distinguishes it from the relative refractory period.

## title
Nerve excitability increased by alkalosis

## question
Nerve excitability is increased by:

## subject
neuro

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
cooling

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Cooling generally slows nerve conduction and reduces excitability, not increases it.

## answer_b
local anaesthetics

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Local anaesthetics block voltage-gated Na+ channels, preventing the depolarizing permeability rise a nerve needs to fire, so they decrease, not increase, excitability.

## answer_c
acidosis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Acidosis (a fall in blood pH) is associated with decreased nerve excitability, the opposite direction from alkalosis.

## answer_d
deep pressure

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Deep pressure on a nerve reduces or abolishes conduction, particularly in large A-type fibres, rather than increasing excitability.

## answer_e
alkalosis

## explanation_e
Correct. Alkalosis (a rise in blood pH) lowers the amount of ionised (free) extracellular calcium, even without changing total calcium -- and it is the ionised fraction that exerts calcium's membrane-stabilising effect on Na+ channels. With less ionised calcium available to stabilise the channels, the threshold falls and excitability rises, which is why hyperventilation-induced alkalosis can produce the same tetany seen with true hypocalcemia.

The excitability of the nerve is zero during:

Incorrect. Obesity reflects a sustained positive energy balance and is characterised by increased fat content in the body, not increased protein content.

## title

## question
A respiratory quotient above one occurs in all the following cases except:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Hyperventilation.

## explanation_a
Incorrect as the exception -- this statement is true. Hyperventilation blows off CO2 faster than the underlying metabolic rate would otherwise produce it, which can transiently push the measured (apparent) RQ above 1.

## answer_b

## explanation_b
Incorrect as the exception -- this statement is true. Heavy muscular exercise generates extra CO2 from bicarbonate buffering of lactic acid, on top of the CO2 from substrate oxidation, which can raise the measured RQ above 1.

Transformation of carbohydrate into fat.

Correct -- this is the exception (the false statement). Gluconeogenesis that uses fat as the substrate to build new glucose runs in the opposite metabolic direction from lipogenesis. It requires relatively more O2 uptake for the CO2 it produces, which lowers the RQ rather than raising it above 1. This is exactly why it is the exception among these otherwise RQ-raising conditions.

## answer_e
Is 1 in uncontrolled diabetic patients.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. An uncontrolled diabetic cannot use glucose effectively and relies instead on fat oxidation (with ketone body formation), giving a low RQ, not one near 1.

## title
Excess R.O. indicates the fuel used during exercise

## question
The excess R.O.:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
is the R.Q. determined during sleep.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The excess R.O. is measured in relation to exercise and its recovery, not during sleep -- sleep is an unrelated resting state with its own separate metabolic rate.

## answer_b
Indicates the fuel used during a certain exercise.

## explanation_b
Correct. The respiratory exchange ratio measured around a bout of exercise reflects which fuel is being drawn on. A ratio closer to 1 points to carbohydrate as the dominant fuel, while a ratio closer to 0.7 points to fat. Tracking it therefore indicates the fuel used during that particular exercise.

## answer_c
Determination requires estimation of the O2, consumption and CO, production during the recovery period only.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Characterising the fuel used during exercise draws on gas exchange measured during the exercise itself, not on the recovery period alone -- restricting the estimate to recovery-only measurements would miss the exercise-period substrate use this option itself asks about.

## answer_d
Is about 1 in a prolonged exercise and 0.7 in short exercises.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. This reverses the expected pattern: prolonged, lower-intensity exercise tends to draw more on fat oxidation (a ratio nearer 0.7), while short, intense exercise draws more on carbohydrate and anaerobic glycolysis (a ratio nearer or above 1), not the other way around.

## title
BMR, EXCEPT: not measured immediately after dinner

## question
About the BMR, all the following is true except:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
It is expressed in Kcal /square meter/hour.

## explanation_a
Incorrect as the exception -- this statement is true. BMR is conventionally expressed per unit body surface area per hour (Kcal/m²/hour), which allows comparison between people of different sizes.

## answer_b
Normally, it is about 40 Kcal in young adult males.

## explanation_b
Incorrect as the exception -- this statement is true. About 40 Kcal/m²/hour is the standard teaching figure for BMR in a young adult male.

## answer_c
It represents the energy expenditure needed for activity of the vital organs

## explanation_c
Incorrect as the exception -- this statement is true. BMR represents the minimum energy expenditure needed to keep the vital organs (heart, brain, kidneys, and the rest) running at complete rest.

## answer_d
It is slightly higher in males and is maximal in children at the age of 5 years

## explanation_d
Incorrect as the exception -- this statement is true. BMR is slightly higher in males than females, and is at its highest in early childhood, around age 5, gradually declining thereafter.

## answer_e
It is measured immediately after dinner at an atmospheric temp about 10 C.

## explanation_e
Correct -- this is the exception (the false statement). A valid BMR measurement requires an overnight fast, not immediately after a meal, since eating raises metabolic rate through specific dynamic action. It also requires a thermoneutral room temperature, not a cold environment such as 10C, which would itself drive up heat production through cold-induced thermogenesis. Measuring right after dinner in a cold room would inflate the reading well above the true basal value.

## title
Energy equivalent of oxygen: heat produced per litre O2 consumed

## question
The energy equivalent of oxygen is

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The amount of heat (Kcal) produced when one litre of O2 is consumed.

## explanation_a
Correct. The energy equivalent of oxygen is defined as the amount of heat, in Kcal, produced when one litre of O2 is consumed in oxidising a given substrate. It is the same heat-value idea as a foodstuff's caloric value, restated from the oxygen-consumption side. Its exact figure varies with which substrate is being oxidised.

## answer_b
Used for estimation of the metabolic rate by direct calorimetry.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The energy equivalent of oxygen is the basis of indirect calorimetry (estimating metabolic rate from measured O2 consumption), not direct calorimetry, which instead measures heat output directly.

## answer_c
About 5 Kcal /L in case of fats and 4.7 Kcal /L in case of carbohydrates.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This reverses the standard figures: carbohydrate's energy equivalent of O2 (about 5 Kcal/L) is higher than fat's (about 4.7 Kcal/L), not the other way around, since carbohydrate needs relatively less O2 to produce a given amount of CO2 and heat.

## answer_d
About 4.8 Kcal /L in case of proteins and 4.6 Kcal /L in the post-absorptive

## explanation_d
Incorrect. These specific protein and post-absorptive figures do not represent the standard definitional fact the question tests; the energy equivalent of oxygen is defined generally (option A), and its exact value depends on which substrate is being oxidised rather than a single fixed protein-specific figure.

## answer_e
Not equal to the heat value of O₂.

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The energy equivalent of oxygen essentially is a restatement of a substrate's heat value expressed per litre of O2 consumed, not a separate, unequal quantity.

## title
SDA, EXCEPT: not due to the work of digestion and absorption

## question
About the specific dynamic action (SDA), all the following is true except:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
It is an increase in the metabolic rate after food intake.

## explanation_a
Incorrect as the exception -- this statement is true. Specific dynamic action is, by definition, the rise in metabolic rate that follows eating.

## answer_b
The SDA of proteins is about 30% and is due mainly to its metabolism in the liver

## explanation_b
Incorrect as the exception -- this statement is true. Protein produces the largest SDA of the three foodstuffs, about 30%, driven mainly by the metabolic cost of processing absorbed amino acids (chiefly deamination) in the liver.

## answer_c
It is generally due to the work done during digestion and absorption of food.

## explanation_c
Correct -- this is the exception (the false statement). SDA is caused mainly by the metabolic processing of the absorbed nutrients afterward, not by the mechanical work of digesting and absorbing the food itself. This is exactly why protein, whose metabolic processing (deamination) is energetically costly, produces by far the largest SDA. Digestion and absorption work itself is not dramatically different across foodstuffs, so it cannot explain protein's outsized effect.

## answer_d
Normally, it is about 6 % for carbohydrates and 4 % for fats.

## explanation_d
Incorrect as the exception -- this statement is true. Carbohydrate's SDA is about 6% and fat's is about 4%, both well below protein's 30%.

## answer_e
It is used for warming the body on exposure to a cold weather.

## explanation_e
Incorrect as the exception -- this statement is true. The extra heat SDA generates can contribute to warming the body on exposure to cold, alongside other thermogenic mechanisms.
