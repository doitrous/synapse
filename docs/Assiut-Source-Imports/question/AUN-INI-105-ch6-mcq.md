## title
Antigenic drift is caused by mutation

## question
Antigenic drift of viruses is caused by?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Recombination

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Recombination is an exchange of genetic sequence between two related genomes, a different mechanism from the point mutations that drive drift.

## answer_b
Reassortment

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Reassortment (exchange of whole genome segments) is the mechanism behind antigenic shift, not drift, and it requires a segmented genome that drift does not.

## answer_c
Mutation

## explanation_c
Correct. Antigenic drift is a gradual change in a virus's surface antigens caused by the accumulation of point mutations in the genes encoding those surface proteins, most classically described for influenza's hemagglutinin and neuraminidase. It is a slow, continuous process that can occur in any virus with a mutable genome, unlike antigenic shift, which is abrupt and confined to segmented-genome viruses. This is why influenza vaccines require near-annual reformulation to keep pace with drift.

## answer_d
Mutation and reassortment

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Reassortment plays no role in drift specifically; combining it with mutation misattributes the mechanism of antigenic shift to drift.

## title
Nucleocapsid-only virus is naked

## question
Viruses that consist of only a nucleocapsid are considered?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Naked viruses

## explanation_a
Correct. A naked (non-enveloped) virus consists of the nucleocapsid only -- genome plus capsid -- with no surrounding lipid envelope, in contrast to an enveloped virus, whose nucleocapsid is additionally wrapped in a host-membrane-derived envelope. Naked and non-enveloped are synonyms for this same structural fact. This structural difference is why naked viruses are generally more resistant than enveloped viruses to lipid solvents, detergents and drying.

## answer_b
Incomplete viruses

## explanation_b
Incorrect. An incomplete (defective) virus is missing one or more genes needed for independent replication; that describes a genetic deficiency, not the presence or absence of an envelope.

## answer_c
Simple viruses

## explanation_c
Incorrect. "Simple virus" is not a standard structural category in virology and does not name the nucleocapsid-only arrangement.

## answer_d
Viroids

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A viroid is an uncoated circular RNA molecule with no capsid or envelope at all, not a nucleocapsid (genome plus capsid) as described here.

## title
DNA viruses replicate in nucleus, RNA viruses in cytoplasm

## question
In general, most DNA viruses multiply in the host cell's _____, while most RNA viruses multiply in the host cell's _____?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Nucleus, cytoplasm

## explanation_a
Correct. As a general rule, DNA viruses assemble and replicate in the host cell's nucleus, where host DNA polymerase and other nuclear machinery are available, while RNA viruses replicate in the host cell's cytoplasm. Poxvirus is the standard exception on the DNA side, replicating entirely in the cytoplasm because it encodes its own complete replication machinery. This nucleus-vs-cytoplasm rule is a foundational fact for predicting where a given virus's inclusion bodies or cytopathic changes will appear.

## answer_b
Cell membrane, cytoplasm

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The cell membrane is not the site of DNA virus replication, and this pairing does not match either the DNA-virus or RNA-virus rule.

## answer_c
Cytoplasm, nucleus

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This reverses the correct pairing; the general rule places DNA viruses in the nucleus and RNA viruses in the cytoplasm, not the other way round.

## answer_d
Cytoplasm, cell membrane

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Neither the cytoplasm-for-DNA-viruses nor the cell-membrane-for-RNA-viruses half of this pairing matches the general teaching rule.

## answer_e
Nucleus, endoplasmic reticulum

## explanation_e
Incorrect. RNA viruses generally replicate in the cytoplasm, not the endoplasmic reticulum specifically, even though the nucleus-for-DNA-viruses half is correct.

Opportunistic fungal infection risk factors

## question
Factors contributing to the initiation of opportunistic fungal infections include each of the following EXCEPT?

## subject
inf

## status

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Defective cell-mediated immunity

## explanation_a
Incorrect (it IS a risk factor). Fungi are classically controlled by T-cell-mediated immune responses, so defective cell-mediated immunity predisposes to opportunistic fungal infection.

## answer_b

## explanation_b
Incorrect (it IS a risk factor). Prolonged broad-spectrum antibiotic use disrupts the competing normal bacterial flora, allowing fungal overgrowth.

Defective cell-mediated immunity, prolonged antibiotic use and chemotherapy are all genuine predisposing factors for opportunistic fungal infection. This item tests the distinction between bacterial-infection risk factors and fungal-infection risk factors, which are frequently confused on exams.

## question
Which of the following can be used to quantitate the titer of infectious viruses?

## subject

## status

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Plaque assay

## explanation_a
Correct. The plaque assay quantitates the titer of infectious virus particles by counting the discrete zones of cell lysis ("plaques") that form in a susceptible cell monolayer, each plaque arising from a single infectious virion. This makes it a direct measure of infectivity, unlike methods that count total viral particles regardless of whether each is capable of infecting a cell. The plaque assay remains a standard reference method for viral titer determination in virology.

Electron microscopy

Incorrect. Hemagglutination measures total viral particle count (via red-cell agglutination), including non-infectious particles, not specifically infectious titer.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. PCR measures genome copy number, not infectious particle count, since it cannot distinguish an intact infectious virion from free or defective viral nucleic acid.

## answer_e
Enzyme immunoassay

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Enzyme immunoassay detects viral antigen or antibody, not infectivity, so it does not quantitate infectious titer.

## title
Reassortment requires a segmented genome

## question
Genetic reassortment occurs is?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Viroids

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A viroid has a single, uncoated circular RNA genome, not a segmented one, so it cannot undergo reassortment.

## answer_b
Viruses with segmented genome

## explanation_b
Correct. Genetic reassortment is the exchange of whole genome segments between two different strains of a segmented-genome virus co-infecting the same host cell, producing a hybrid progeny virus. It can only happen in viruses whose genome is naturally divided into separate segments, such as influenza's eight-segment RNA genome. Reassortment is the mechanism underlying antigenic shift, the abrupt, major antigenic change distinct from the gradual point-mutation-driven antigenic drift.

## answer_c
Defective viruses

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A defective virus's problem is missing genes, not genome segmentation, and reassortment specifically requires a segmented genome to exchange, which a defective virus does not define.

## answer_d
Host range mutants

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A host-range mutant is defined by an altered ability to infect a particular host or cell type, not by genome segmentation or reassortment.

## answer_e
Prions

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A prion has no nucleic acid at all, so it cannot undergo genetic reassortment, which requires exchanging genome segments.

## title
Phenotypic masking: genome packaged in another virus's capsid

## question
Viral genome of one virus that may be encapsulated in the capsid of the second virus is called?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Point mutation

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A point mutation is a single-nucleotide change in a genome, an unrelated concept to one virus's genome being packaged in another virus's capsid.

## answer_b
Exaltation

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Exaltation is one virus enhancing another's growth, a functional interaction distinct from genome/capsid mismatching.

## answer_c
Phenotypic masking

## explanation_c
Correct. Phenotypic masking (phenotypic mixing) occurs when two related viruses co-infect the same cell and the genome of one virus becomes packaged inside capsid proteins contributed by the other. The resulting particle carries the genetic information of one parent virus but the surface phenotype (and initial host-cell tropism) of the other. It changes only the outer coat borrowed for one generation, leaving the genome itself unaltered, unlike reassortment.

## answer_d
Interference

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Interference is one virus suppressing another's replication, the opposite kind of interaction from genome/capsid packaging.

## answer_e
Reassortment

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Reassortment is the exchange of whole genome segments, an actual genetic change, unlike phenotypic masking, which changes only the coat, not the genome.

## title
Complementation allows defective viruses to replicate

## question
A process that allows defective viruses to replicate and spread is called?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Recombination

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Recombination is an exchange of genetic sequence between two genomes, a different mechanism from the missing-function rescue described here.

## answer_b
Reassortment

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Reassortment is the exchange of whole genome segments between segmented-genome viruses, an unrelated mechanism from rescuing a defective virus's missing function.

## answer_c
Complementation

## explanation_c
Correct. Complementation is the process by which a helper virus's gene products supply the function a defective virus's own genome is missing, allowing the defective virus to replicate and spread despite its own incomplete genome. This rescue process is distinct from recombination (exchange of genetic sequence) or interference (one virus suppressing another's replication, the opposite effect). Hepatitis D virus's dependence on simultaneous hepatitis B virus infection is a clinically relevant real-world example of complementation.

## answer_d
Interference

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Interference is one virus suppressing another's replication, the opposite effect from rescuing a defective virus's replication.

## title
Conditional-lethal mutants: permissive vs restrictive conditions

## question
Mutations that can multiply under some conditions but not under other conditions are?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Plaque-size mutants

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A plaque-size mutant is defined by an altered diameter of the lysis zone it produces, not by permissive-vs-restrictive replication conditions.

## answer_b
Conditional-lethal mutants

## explanation_b
Correct. A conditional-lethal mutant is a virus mutant that can multiply under some (permissive) conditions but not under other (restrictive) conditions -- temperature-sensitive mutants are the classic example, replicating at a lower permissive temperature but not at a higher restrictive one. This condition-dependent replication pattern is the defining phenotype of this mutant class. Such mutants are valuable research tools because they can be propagated under permissive conditions and then studied under restrictive ones.

## answer_c
Host-range mutants

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A host-range mutant is defined by an altered ability to infect a particular host or cell type, not by permissive-vs-restrictive conditions for the same host.

## answer_d
Drug-resistant mutant

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A drug-resistant mutant is defined by continued replication in the presence of an antiviral drug that would inhibit wild-type virus, a different phenotype from condition-dependent replication.

## title
Exaltation: one virus enhances another's growth

## question
Exaltation means?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
One virus enhances the growth of the other virus

## explanation_a
Correct. Exaltation is the phenomenon in which one virus, through co-infection, enhances the growth (replication) of a second, different virus in the same cell -- the reverse of interference, in which one virus instead suppresses another's replication. It is not a mutation, and it does not itself involve genetic reassortment of the viral genome; it is a functional interaction between two co-infecting viruses, not a change to either genome. Exaltation and interference together illustrate that co-infecting viruses can either help or hinder one another's replication.

## answer_b
Nonsense mutation

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A nonsense mutation is a specific type of point mutation that creates a premature stop codon, an unrelated genome-sequence change, not exaltation.

## answer_c
Genetic reassortment of virus genome

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Genetic reassortment is the exchange of whole genome segments, an actual genetic change; exaltation instead describes a functional growth-enhancing interaction that leaves each virus's genome unchanged.

## answer_d
Point mutation in the genome

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A point mutation is a single-nucleotide genome change, an unrelated concept to one virus enhancing another's growth.
