## title
Detecting spare receptors

## question
Which of the following statements about spare receptors is most correct?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Spare receptors activate the effector machinery of the cell without the need for a drug

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Spare receptors still require a drug to bind and trigger the effector machinery; they are simply the extra receptors left unoccupied once enough others have already driven the response to its maximum, not receptors that self-activate without any drug at all.

## answer_b
Spare receptors, in the absence of drug, are sequestered in the cytoplasm

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Spare receptors are ordinary surface receptors, present and available for binding at all times, not receptors hidden away in the cytoplasm in the absence of drug.

## answer_c
Spare receptors may be detected by the finding that the EC50 is greater than the Kd for the agonist

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This reverses the true relationship: the presence of spare receptors is detected when a tissue's EC50 is lower, not greater, than the agonist's Kd for the receptor -- the tissue reaches half-maximal effect before the receptor is half-occupied.

## answer_d
Spare receptors will be detected if the intracellular effect of drug-receptor interaction lasts longer than the drug-receptor interaction itself

## explanation_d
Correct. Spare receptors exist when a tissue's maximal response is reached with less than full receptor occupancy. One reliable way this is detected experimentally is that the intracellular effect of the drug-receptor interaction outlasts the binding interaction itself -- the signal set in motion by transient receptor occupancy continues after the drug has already dissociated, which is only possible when enough receptors and downstream amplification exist that full occupancy was never required for the maximal signal.

## title
When receptor up-regulation occurs

## question
Regarding receptor up regulation, one of the following statements is true:

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
It may involve decrease in the effectiveness of receptors.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A decrease in receptor effectiveness describes down-regulation/desensitisation, not up-regulation, which instead produces hyperactivity or supersensitivity.

## answer_b
It usually occurs after chronic administration of an agonist.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Chronic agonist administration produces receptor down-regulation (desensitisation), not up-regulation -- this option reverses the true agonist/antagonist relationship.

## answer_c
It usually involves a decrease in the number of receptors

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Up-regulation involves an increase, not a decrease, in the number of receptors, since prolonged antagonist contact drives synthesis of new receptors.

## answer_d
It occurs after chronic administration of an antagonist.

## explanation_d
Correct. Receptor up-regulation follows long-term exposure of cells to an antagonist, which frequently produces hyperactivity or supersensitivity of the receptors: prolonged antagonist contact drives synthesis of new receptors, so the tissue ends up with more receptors than baseline. This is why abruptly stopping a chronically-taken antagonist -- a beta-blocker, for example -- can precipitate worsening angina or dysrhythmias, since the enlarged receptor population is suddenly exposed to endogenous agonist with nothing left to block it.

## title
Definition of tachyphylaxis

## question
Tachyphylaxis refers to which of the following?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Responsiveness decreased rapidly after administration of a drug

## explanation_a
Correct. Tachyphylaxis is a decrease in the intensity of response to a drug that develops rapidly -- after the first dose or only a few doses -- rather than gradually over a longer course. This is what separates it from ordinary tolerance, which is the same kind of falling response but developing slowly with sustained use.

## answer_b
Responsiveness increased rapidly after maintenance of a drug (hypersensitive)

## explanation_b
Incorrect. "Increased" responsiveness is the wrong direction for tachyphylaxis, which is specifically a decrease, and "after maintenance" describes a gradual rather than a rapid timescale.

## answer_c
Responsiveness increased rapidly after administration of a drug

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Tachyphylaxis is a decrease, not an increase, in responsiveness; increased responsiveness after a drug would instead describe hyperreactivity or sensitisation, not tachyphylaxis.

## answer_d
Responsiveness decreased rapidly after maintenance of a drug (desensitized)

## explanation_d
Incorrect. "After maintenance" describes a gradual process (ordinary tolerance or desensitisation over a longer course), whereas tachyphylaxis is specifically defined by its rapid onset, after the first or first few doses.

## title
Rapid loss of calcitonin's effect in hypercalcaemia

## question
A 44-year-old black male is brought to the emergency department with 6 h of worsening lethargy and confusion. Past medical history is significant for easy bruising, 3 months of bone pain, and frequent pneumococcal infections. Labs were ordered, revealing serum calcium of 17 mg/dL (normal: 9.0 to 10.5 mg/dL). To rapidly lower his serum calcium, you administer calcitonin. However, calcitonin alone is insufficient because it is known to rapidly and suddenly lose its effectiveness within 2 to 3 days of repeated dosing, for which reason a bisphosphonate, which takes 2 to 3 days to become effective, is added simultaneously. What is the term for the rapid decrease in response to calcitonin?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Tachyphylaxis

## explanation_a
Correct. Calcitonin's effectiveness fading within 2-3 days of repeated dosing is the textbook example of tachyphylaxis: a decrease in response that appears rapidly, after only the first few doses, rather than gradually over a long course. This is exactly why a bisphosphonate -- slower to start but not subject to the same rapid fade -- is begun at the same time, so it takes over calcium control once calcitonin's effect has tachyphylaxed away.

## answer_b
Tolerance

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Ordinary tolerance is also a fall in response with repeated dosing, but it develops gradually over a longer course; the stem specifically describes a rapid loss within days, which is the defining feature of tachyphylaxis rather than tolerance.

## answer_c
Anaphylaxis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Anaphylaxis is a severe, acute systemic allergic reaction, an unrelated concept to a drug's effectiveness fading with repeated dosing.

## answer_d
Prophylaxis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Prophylaxis means preventive treatment altogether, not a description of a drug's response fading with repeated administration.

Transporter for renal excretion of drugs

## question
The transporter protein which responsible for the excretion of some drugs into the urine is:

## subject
pharm

## status

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect. MDR1 (P-glycoprotein) is a well-known efflux transporter, but it is not the specific transporter this teaching point names for urinary drug excretion.

## answer_b
ABC

## explanation_b
Incorrect. ABC (ATP-binding cassette) is the broad superfamily that MRP itself belongs to, not the specific named transporter for renal drug excretion this question asks for.

## answer_c
SLC

## explanation_c
Incorrect. SLC (solute carrier) transporters are instead named for the uptake of some neurotransmitters, the opposite direction and a different substrate from renal drug excretion.

It is a specific, named efflux transporter, distinct from the general carrier-mediated transport mechanism taught alongside it. Renal elimination overall combines glomerular filtration, active tubular secretion (where transporters like MRP act) and passive tubular reabsorption, and MRP-mediated secretion is one concrete, named example of that active step.

## title
How water-soluble drugs are absorbed

## question
Water soluble drugs are absorbed mainly by:

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Active transport

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Active transport needs a specific carrier protein and energy, and is reserved for particular drugs resembling endogenous substrates, not the general route for water-soluble drugs.

## answer_b
Pinocytosis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Pinocytosis engulfs fluid and large or particulate substances by membrane invagination (iron being the worked example), not the typical route for ordinary small water-soluble drug molecules.

## answer_c
Passive diffusion

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Passive diffusion through the lipid membrane favours small, lipid-soluble, non-ionised molecules -- the opposite of a water-soluble drug, which cannot readily dissolve through the lipid bilayer.

## answer_d
Filtration

## explanation_d
Correct. Filtration moves small, water-soluble drugs through aqueous membrane pores or paracellular channels, carried along with bulk water flow rather than dissolving through the lipid membrane. This is exactly the route suited to a water-soluble, rather than a lipid-soluble, molecule, which cannot readily dissolve through the lipid bilayer that passive diffusion depends on. It requires no carrier protein and no energy, unlike active transport.
