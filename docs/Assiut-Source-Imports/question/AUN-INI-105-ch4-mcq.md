## title
Genetic information essential for bacterial life

## question
Genetic information essential for the life of bacteria is a function of?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Ribosomes

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Ribosomes translate mRNA into protein; they carry no genetic (DNA) information of their own and are not the source of the cell's essential genetic instructions.

## answer_b
Plasmid

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A plasmid carries accessory genetic information (such as antibiotic resistance or virulence genes) that is helpful but not essential -- a bacterium can survive without any plasmid at all, unlike its chromosome.

## answer_c
Mesosomes

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Mesosomes are infoldings of the plasma membrane, a structural feature rather than a repository of genetic information.

## answer_d
Chromosomes

## explanation_d
Correct. The bacterial chromosome carries the genetic information essential for the cell's life -- the core genes required for basic survival and reproduction -- and every living bacterial cell must have one, unlike accessory elements such as plasmids, which a cell can live without. This essential-versus-accessory distinction is exactly what separates the chromosome from a plasmid: both carry genetic information, but only the chromosome's is indispensable.

## title
Essential structures in a bacterial cell

## question
The following are essential structure in bacterial cell?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Nuclear body

## explanation_a
Correct. The nuclear body (the nucleoid) is the cytoplasmic region holding the bacterial chromosome, and it is an essential structure. Every living bacterial cell must have this single circular chromosome carrying its core genetic information. None of the other structures listed here is required the same way -- every bacterium needs its chromosome to survive, but not every bacterium needs fimbriae, inclusion granules, a plasmid, or a capsule.

## answer_b
Fimbriae

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Fimbriae assist adherence in some bacteria but are not present in, or required by, every bacterial cell; they are an accessory rather than essential structure.

## answer_c
Inclusion granules

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Inclusion granules are optional intracellular nutrient-storage deposits (such as glycogen or polyphosphate); a bacterium can live without accumulating them.

## answer_d
Plasmid

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A plasmid carries accessory (not essential) genetic information; many bacteria live entirely without any plasmid.

## answer_e
Capsule

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A capsule is an accessory virulence structure found in some, not all, bacterial species; it is not required for basic bacterial survival.

## title
A non-essential bacterial structure

## question
The following structure is not essential in bacterial cell?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Ribosomes

## explanation_a
Incorrect (this structure is essential, so it is not the answer). Ribosomes are required in every bacterial cell to translate mRNA into the proteins the cell needs to survive.

## answer_b
Cell membrane

## explanation_b
Incorrect (essential). The plasma membrane is required in every bacterial cell as the selectively permeable barrier controlling what enters and leaves the cell.

## answer_c
Cell wall

## explanation_c
Incorrect (essential, by standard teaching). The cell wall gives the bacterial cell its shape and rigidity and protects it from osmotic lysis; it is treated as an essential structure in standard bacterial-cell teaching (Mycoplasma, which naturally lacks one, is the recognised exception rather than the rule).

## answer_d
Fimbriae

## explanation_d
Correct. Fimbriae are accessory surface appendages that assist adherence to surfaces or host cells in some bacteria, but they are not required for a bacterial cell's basic survival -- many bacteria live and divide perfectly well without any fimbriae. This makes fimbriae the one structure in this list that is not essential, unlike ribosomes, the cell membrane, and the cell wall, all of which every standard bacterial cell needs.

DNA probe: a labeled sequence derived from the sought organism

## question
Short sequence of labeled single stranded DNA or RNA originally derived from the organism being sought?

## subject

## status

## vignette

## answer_a
Primer

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A primer is a short segment that provides a starting point for DNA polymerase to extend a new strand; it initiates DNA synthesis rather than being used to detect a target sequence.

## answer_b
Promotor

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A promoter is a regulatory DNA sequence upstream of a gene where RNA polymerase binds to begin transcription; it is not a labeled molecule derived from a sought organism and used as a detection reagent.

## answer_c
DNA probes

## explanation_c
Correct. A DNA (or RNA) probe is, by definition, a short, labeled, single-stranded sequence originally derived from the organism being sought, used to detect a complementary sequence in a test sample through hybridization. This detection role -- finding a matching sequence, not initiating synthesis (a primer's job) or regulating transcription (a promoter's job) -- is exactly what the term "probe" describes.

## title
Order of PCR steps

## question
Steps of PCR in order are?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Primer annealing-primer extension -DNA denaturation

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This places both annealing and extension before denaturation, but primers cannot anneal to a template that has not yet been denatured into single strands.

## answer_b
Primer extension-primer annealing -DNA denaturation

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This places extension before annealing and both before denaturation; extension requires an already-annealed primer, and annealing requires an already-denatured template, so this order violates both dependencies.

## answer_c
DNA denaturation – primer extension-primer annealing

## explanation_c
Incorrect. This places extension before annealing; a primer must anneal to its complementary template sequence before DNA polymerase can extend it, so extension cannot come first.

## answer_d
DNA denaturation - primer annealing-primer extension

## explanation_d
Correct. Each PCR cycle proceeds through denaturation, then annealing, then extension, in that fixed order. DNA denaturation (heating to separate the double-stranded template into single strands) must come first, because primer annealing (cooling to let short primers bind their complementary sequences) needs single-stranded template to bind. Primer extension (a heat-stable DNA polymerase extending the primers) comes last, since it needs those primers already annealed before it can begin synthesizing new complementary strands.

Plasmid is a?

## subject

## answer_a
Plasma protein

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A plasma protein is a blood-serum molecule (such as albumin or a clotting factor); a plasmid is DNA, not a protein, and has no connection to blood plasma.

## answer_b
Genetic factor

## explanation_b
Correct. A plasmid is, at the broadest classification level, a genetic factor -- a small, circular, extrachromosomal DNA element that carries accessory genetic information and can replicate independently of the bacterial chromosome. This "genetic factor" classification is what separates a plasmid from a protein, a granule, or a membrane component, none of which are genetic (DNA-carrying) elements at all.

## answer_c
Serum factor

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A serum factor is a blood-plasma component (unrelated terminology, similarly to "plasma protein"); a plasmid is a bacterial genetic element, not a component of blood serum.

## answer_d
Cytoplasmic granules

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Cytoplasmic (inclusion) granules are nutrient-storage deposits within the cell, not genetic material -- an entirely different kind of structure from a plasmid.

## answer_e
Part of plasma membrane

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The plasma membrane is the cell's lipid bilayer boundary; a plasmid is a free-floating cytoplasmic DNA molecule, not a component of the membrane.

## title
Mutation: a heritable change in the genome

## question
Heritable changes in genome are called?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Conjugation

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Conjugation is a mechanism of transferring DNA between two bacterial cells; it is not itself an alteration of the genome's sequence.

## answer_b
Transduction

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Transduction is a bacteriophage-mediated mechanism of DNA transfer between cells; it is not itself a change in the genome's sequence.

## answer_c
Transformation

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Transformation is uptake of free DNA by a competent recipient cell; it is a transfer mechanism, not itself a heritable sequence change.

## answer_d
Mutation

## explanation_d
Correct. A heritable change in the genome is, by definition, a mutation -- a permanent alteration of the DNA sequence that is copied into every subsequent generation of the cell. This heritability, arising from the change being replicated along with the rest of the genome at every division, is exactly what distinguishes mutation from the various gene-transfer mechanisms (conjugation, transduction, transformation), none of which by themselves alter a sequence.

## answer_e
Replication

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Replication, when accurate, copies the existing DNA sequence faithfully; it is the process a mutation can be perpetuated through, not the name for the heritable change itself.
