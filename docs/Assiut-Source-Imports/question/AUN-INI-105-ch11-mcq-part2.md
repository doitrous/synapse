## title
Trichostrongylus does not enter through the skin

## question
The portal of entry for the following parasites is the skin EXCEPT?

## subject
inf

## status

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Ancylostoma duodenale

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Ancylostoma duodenale's infective filariform larva penetrates human skin directly, so its portal of entry is the skin.

## answer_b
Strongyloides stercoralis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Strongyloides stercoralis's infective filariform larva penetrates human skin directly, so its portal of entry is the skin.

## answer_c
Schistosoma mansoni

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Schistosoma mansoni's infective cercaria penetrates human skin directly on contact with infested water, so its portal of entry is the skin.

## answer_d
Trichostrongylus colubriformis

## explanation_d
Correct. Trichostrongylus colubriformis infects the human host when its infective filariform larva, present on contaminated vegetation, is ingested -- not by skin penetration. This makes it the exception among Ancylostoma duodenale, Strongyloides stercoralis and Schistosoma mansoni, all three of which enter through the skin directly. Recognising Trichostrongylus's oral route is the recurring exam trap in a 'portal of entry is skin' list.

## title
Ancylostoma filariform larva is sheathed

## question
The filariform larva of Ancylostoma?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Sheathed

## explanation_a
Correct. The filariform (third-stage, infective) larva of Ancylostoma duodenale is sheathed -- it retains the cuticle of the preceding (second-stage) moult as a loose outer sheath. It also shows positive thermotropism and negative geotropism (moving toward warmth and away from gravity, upward on moist soil or vegetation), the behaviour that brings it into contact with skin for penetration. Its sheathed state contrasts directly with the unsheathed filariform larva of Strongyloides stercoralis, a distinguishing pair tested elsewhere in this chapter.

## answer_b
Non sheathed

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Unsheathed describes the Strongyloides stercoralis filariform larva, not that of Ancylostoma duodenale, which retains its sheath.

## answer_c
Has positive geotropism

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The Ancylostoma filariform larva shows negative geotropism (moving away from gravity, upward), not positive geotropism.

## answer_d
Has negative histotropism

## explanation_d
Incorrect. 'Negative histotropism' is not a standard descriptor used for this larva; its recognised directional behaviours are positive thermotropism and negative geotropism.

Incorrect. The standard route of infection is skin penetration by the infective filariform larva, not ingestion of eggs.

Incorrect. This figure overstates the standard teaching estimate for daily egg output per female worm and is not the fact the bank keys as correct here.

This immature-at-passage fact parallels the same principle already tested for Ascaris eggs elsewhere in this chapter.

## subject

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Correct. Hymenolepis nana, a cestode with a direct life cycle confined entirely to the intestine (no obligatory tissue or pulmonary migratory phase), is the exception -- its larvae are never found in sputum. ## title
Visceral larva migrans is due to Toxocara

## question
Visceral larva migrans is due to?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Ingestion of eggs of human Ascaris

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Ingesting embryonated eggs of the human-specific Ascaris lumbricoides causes ordinary human ascariasis, not visceral larva migrans.

## answer_b
Ingestion of eggs of Trichuris trichiura

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Ingesting Trichuris trichiura eggs causes trichuriasis, an intestinal infection, not visceral larva migrans.

## answer_c
Ingestion of eggs of Ascaris of dog and cat

## explanation_c
Correct. Visceral larva migrans (VLM) follows ingestion of embryonated eggs of Toxocara canis or Toxocara cati -- the ascarid roundworms of dogs and cats. Because man is an accidental, aberrant host for Toxocara, the hatched larvae cannot complete their normal cycle and instead wander through the viscera (liver, lung, eye, CNS), producing eosinophilic granulomatous inflammation wherever they lodge. This zoonotic dog/cat-ascarid transmission route distinguishes VLM from ordinary human ascariasis, which involves the human-specific Ascaris lumbricoides instead.

Ingestion of eggs of Enterobius vermicularis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Ingesting Enterobius vermicularis eggs causes enterobiasis, an intestinal infection confined to the human-specific pin-worm, not visceral larva migrans.

## title
Strongyloides differs from Ancylostoma in several ways

## question
Strongyloides stercoralis differs from Ancylostoma duodenale in?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Autoinfection

## explanation_a
Incorrect alone. It is true that Strongyloides alone is capable of autoinfection, but this is only one of three jointly true differences offered.

## answer_b
Rhabditiform larvae in stool

## explanation_b
Incorrect alone. It is true that Strongyloides sheds rhabditiform (not filariform) larvae in fresh stool, but this is only one of three jointly true differences offered.

## answer_c
Free living forms

## explanation_c
Incorrect alone. It is true that Strongyloides alone has a free-living generation, but this is only one of three jointly true differences offered.

## answer_d
All of the above

## explanation_d
Correct. Strongyloides stercoralis differs from Ancylostoma duodenale in three linked ways, combined here into one 'all of the above' item: it alone is capable of autoinfection (its own rhabditiform larvae can moult to the infective filariform stage within the host's bowel or perianal skin, re-penetrating without ever leaving the body); its diagnostic larval stage recovered in fresh stool is the rhabditiform larva; and it alone has a free-living (indirect) generation capable of completing an entire life cycle in soil, independent of a human host. None of these three features applies to Ancylostoma duodenale.

## title
Trichostrongylus infects by ingestion of filariform larva

## question
Infection with Trichostrongylus colubriformis is through?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Ingestion of egg

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Trichostrongylus infection is not caused by egg ingestion; the infective stage is the filariform larva.

## answer_b
Ingestion of filariform larva

## explanation_b
Correct. Trichostrongylus colubriformis infects the human host when the infective filariform (third-stage) larva, present on contaminated vegetation, is ingested. This oral route is unlike Ancylostoma duodenale and Strongyloides stercoralis, whose portal of entry is instead the skin, penetrated directly by their own infective larvae -- the same distinction tested from the opposite direction elsewhere in this chapter.

## answer_c
Ingestion of encysted metacercaria

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Ingestion of an encysted metacercaria is the infective route for trematodes such as Fasciola or Heterophyes, not for the nematode Trichostrongylus colubriformis.

## answer_d
Ingestion of rhabditiform larva

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The rhabditiform larva is a non-infective, developmental larval stage; it is the filariform (infective, third-stage) larva that is ingested.

## title
Trichostrongylus egg has a morula stage

## question
Egg of Trichostrongylus colubriformis differs from Ancylostoma egg in?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Having a morula stage

## explanation_a
Correct. The egg of Trichostrongylus colubriformis is larger than, but otherwise resembles, the egg of Ancylostoma duodenale (both are oval, thin-shelled and immature when passed) -- the key distinguishing feature tested here is that the Trichostrongylus egg already contains a morula (an early cluster of several dividing blastomeres) at the time it is passed, whereas the Ancylostoma egg is still at an earlier, less-developed cleavage stage. This morula-stage difference is the specific point the bank keys as the distinguisher, not the shared oval/thin-shelled/immature features.

## answer_b
Translucent

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Both the Trichostrongylus and Ancylostoma eggs are translucent, so this is a shared feature, not the distinguishing one.

## answer_c
Oval and thin shelled

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Both eggs are oval and thin-shelled, so this is a shared feature, not the distinguishing one.

## answer_d
Immature

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Both eggs are still developmentally immature relative to a fully infective larva when passed, so this shared feature is not the specific difference tested.

## title
Strongyloides filariform larva differs from Ancylostoma's

## question
Filariform larvae of Strongyloides stercoralis differ from that of Ancylostoma in?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
No sheath

## explanation_a
Incorrect alone. It is true the Strongyloides filariform larva is unsheathed, unlike Ancylostoma's sheathed larva, but this is only one of three jointly true differences offered.

## answer_b
Notched tail

## explanation_b
Incorrect alone. It is true the Strongyloides filariform larva has a notched (forked) tail, but this is only one of three jointly true differences offered.

## answer_c
About 1/2 mm in length

## explanation_c
Incorrect alone. It is true the Strongyloides filariform larva is shorter, roughly 1/2 mm (500-600 micrometres), but this is only one of three jointly true differences offered.

## answer_d
All of the above

## explanation_d
Correct. The filariform larva of Strongyloides stercoralis differs from that of Ancylostoma duodenale in three linked morphological features, combined here into one 'all of the above' item. It is unsheathed, having shed the second-stage cuticle, unlike Ancylostoma's retained sheath, and it has a distinctively notched (forked) tail tip. It is also shorter overall, at roughly 1/2 mm, compared with Ancylostoma's longer filariform larva.
