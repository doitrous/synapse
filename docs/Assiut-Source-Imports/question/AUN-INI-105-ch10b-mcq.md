## title
Platyhelminths needing one intermediate host, except

## question
The following platyhelminths require one intermediate host EXCEPT ?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Fasciola hepatica

## explanation_a
Incorrect as the EXCEPT answer. Fasciola hepatica needs only one intermediate host, a snail, before its metacercaria encysts on aquatic vegetation and infects man directly, so it belongs with the one-host group.

## answer_b
Schistosoma haematobium

## explanation_b
Incorrect as the EXCEPT answer. Schistosoma haematobium also needs only one intermediate host, a snail, whose cercariae then penetrate human skin directly with no second intermediate host, so it too belongs with the one-host group.

## answer_c
Fasciolopsis buski

## explanation_c
Incorrect as the EXCEPT answer. Fasciolopsis buski likewise needs only one intermediate host, a snail, before its metacercaria encysts on water vegetation and is swallowed by man, so it belongs with the one-host group.

## answer_d
Diphyllobothrium latum

## explanation_d
Correct. Diphyllobothrium latum is the exception: it needs two intermediate hosts in succession, Cyclops and then a fresh-water fish, before its plerocercoid larva becomes infective to man -- unlike Fasciola hepatica, Schistosoma haematobium and Fasciolopsis buski, which all complete their pre-human development in a single snail intermediate host. This one-host-versus-two-host contrast is the mirror image of this chapter's own Q1, which asks the same question about platyhelminths needing two hosts.

## title
Cestodes not transmitted by insects

## question
Which of the following parasites is not transmitted by insects ?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Hymenolepis diminuta

## explanation_a
Incorrect as the exception. Hymenolepis diminuta's cysticercoid larva develops in a true insect intermediate host, a grain beetle or flea, so it belongs with the insect-transmitted group.

## answer_b
Dipylidium caninum

## explanation_b
Incorrect as the exception. Dipylidium caninum's cysticercoid larva likewise develops within an infected flea, a true insect, so it too belongs with the insect-transmitted group.

## answer_c
Guinea worm

## explanation_c
Incorrect as the exception. Guinea worm (Dracunculus medinensis) is carried by Cyclops, a small crustacean loosely grouped with arthropod vectors in basic teaching, closer to the insect-transmitted group than to Diphyllobothrium latum's route.

## answer_d
Diphyllobothrium latum

## explanation_d
Correct. Diphyllobothrium latum's second intermediate host is a fresh-water fish, reached only after an earlier Cyclops stage -- man becomes infected by eating fish, a route with no insect or insect-like arthropod involved at the point of human infection at all, unlike Hymenolepis diminuta and Dipylidium caninum (true fleas/beetles) or Guinea worm (Cyclops, still an arthropod-adjacent vector). This distinction between fish-borne and arthropod-borne cestode transmission is what separates D. latum from the other three parasites in this list.

## title
Suckers as organs of fixation

## question
Suckers are organs of fixation in ?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Nematodes

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Nematodes (roundworms) attach or anchor by other means -- a mouth armed with plates or hooks, or a penetrating anterior end -- not by suckers.

## answer_b
Trematodes and some cestodes

## explanation_b
Correct. Suckers are the organ of fixation in trematodes, which typically bear an oral and a ventral sucker, and in some cestodes, whose scolex bears four suckers (as tested elsewhere in this chapter for Taenia saginata and Taenia solium) -- though not every cestode relies on suckers alone, since Diphyllobothrium latum's scolex instead uses bothria (shallow attachment grooves). Recognising that suckers span two of the three helminth groups, not just one, is what this question tests.

## answer_c
Cestodes only

## explanation_c
Incorrect as a complete answer. Suckers are not confined to cestodes alone -- trematodes also rely on them, and some cestodes (Diphyllobothrium latum) use bothria instead of true suckers.

## answer_d
Trematodes only

## explanation_d
Incorrect as a complete answer. Suckers are not confined to trematodes alone -- most cestode scoleces, such as those of the two Taenia species, also bear suckers.

## title
Cysticercosis, except

## question
Human infection with cysticercosis may occur in the following methods EXCEPT ?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Ingestion of pork contaminated with cysticercus cellulosae

## explanation_a
Correct. Eating pork containing cysticercus cellulosae gives man the adult worm, taeniasis, not cysticercosis -- cysticercosis (tissue larvae) instead requires man to ingest T. solium's EGGS, not its pork-borne larval cyst. This is the same distinction tested by this chapter's own Q41/Q52, and is exactly why cysticercosis, unlike taeniasis, can occur without ever eating pork at all.

## answer_b
Ingestion of food contaminated with Taenia solium egg

## explanation_b
Incorrect as the EXCEPT answer. Ingesting food contaminated with T. solium eggs is a genuine cysticercosis route -- the ingested egg's oncosphere penetrates the gut wall and disseminates to tissue, so this belongs with the true causes of cysticercosis.

## answer_c
External autoinfection

## explanation_c
Incorrect as the EXCEPT answer. External autoinfection -- perianal contamination with the patient's own eggs, then re-ingestion -- is a genuine route to cysticercosis in a person already carrying the adult worm, so it belongs with the true causes.

## answer_d
Internal autoinfection

## explanation_d
Incorrect as the EXCEPT answer. Internal autoinfection -- reverse peristalsis carrying gravid proglottids or eggs back into the stomach, where gastric acid frees the oncospheres -- is likewise a genuine route to cysticercosis in a T. solium carrier, so it belongs with the true causes.

## title
Cestodes characterized by

## question
Cestodes are characterized by ?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Having no nervous system

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Cestodes do have a primitive nervous system -- a nerve ring in the scolex giving off longitudinal nerve cords along the strobila -- so this statement is false.

## answer_b
Being hermaphrodites

## explanation_b
Correct. Cestodes are hermaphrodites: each proglottid carries both male and female reproductive organs, allowing self-fertilisation as well as cross-fertilisation between segments or worms. This is the same hermaphroditic body plan already established for cestodes generally in this chapter's own Q34, distinguishing them from the nematodes, which have separate sexes.

## answer_c
Having no excretory system

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Cestodes do have an excretory system, made up of flame cells (protonephridia) draining into longitudinal excretory canals, so this statement is false.

## answer_d
Being flattened dorsoventrally and unsegmented

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Cestodes are flattened dorsoventrally but they are segmented, not unsegmented -- their body is divided into a chain of proglottids, the defining feature tested in this chapter's own Q34, so the "unsegmented" half of this statement is false.
