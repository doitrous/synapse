## title
Why acetylcholine is unsuitable clinically

## question
Acetylcholine is not used in clinical practice because:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
It is very toxic

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Acetylcholine's clinical unsuitability is not primarily about toxicity -- it is about the impractical dosing its non-selectivity and instant hydrolysis would require.

## answer_b
It is very costly

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Cost is not the reason acetylcholine is avoided clinically -- more selective, longer-acting cholinomimetics such as carbachol or bethanechol are chosen instead for pharmacological, not economic, reasons.

## answer_c
The doses required are very high

## explanation_c
Correct. Acetylcholine acts non-selectively on both muscarinic and nicotinic receptors and is hydrolysed almost instantly by acetylcholinesterase and plasma cholinesterase. Together these mean the doses needed to sustain any therapeutic effect would be impractically high. This is why more selective, AChE-resistant agents are used instead.

## answer_d
It is very rapidly hydrolyzed

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Rapid hydrolysis is real, but the printed institutional answer names the resulting dosing impracticality as the reason acetylcholine is unsuitable, not the hydrolysis fact by itself.

Muscarinic stimulation causes spasm of accommodation

## question
Which of the following is the effect of stimulating muscarinic receptors?

## subject
fnd

## status

## owner

B

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Muscarinic stimulation of vascular endothelium releases nitric oxide, producing vasodilation, not vasoconstriction.

## answer_b
Spasm of accommodation

## explanation_b
Correct. Muscarinic stimulation contracts the ciliary muscle in the eye. This sustained contraction fixes the lens in its near-vision focus, a state called spasm of accommodation. It is one of the classic ocular effects of a muscarinic agonist.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Muscarinic stimulation increases, not decreases, glandular secretion, so it produces the opposite of xerostomia -- xerostomia is an antimuscarinic effect.

## title
Carbachol overdose but not neostigmine overdose causes hypotension

## question
An overdose of muscarinic agonist carbachol but not an overdose of acetylcholinesterase inhibitor neostigmine could cause the following:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Hypotension

## explanation_a
Correct. Carbachol, a direct-acting agonist, can act at muscarinic receptors with no cholinergic innervation, such as vascular endothelium. Stimulating these non-innervated receptors can produce pronounced hypotension. Neostigmine, an indirect-acting agent, can only potentiate acetylcholine where it is already being released, so it cannot reach this non-innervated site the same way.

## answer_b
Bronchoconstriction

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Both a direct-acting muscarinic agonist and an indirect-acting AChE inhibitor can produce bronchoconstriction, since both raise muscarinic tone at the (innervated) airway smooth muscle -- this is not the distinguishing effect asked for.

## answer_c
Cholinergic crisis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Cholinergic crisis is the distinguishing overdose effect of the indirect-acting agent (neostigmine), from its potentiation of nicotinic transmission at the neuromuscular junction -- not of the direct-acting agent, so this is backwards from what the question asks.

## answer_d
Miosis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Both agents would be expected to cause miosis, since both raise muscarinic tone at the (innervated) eye -- this is not the distinguishing effect.

## answer_e
Sweating

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Both agents would be expected to cause sweating, since sweat glands are innervated cholinergic sympathetic effectors reached by either drug -- this is not the distinguishing effect.
