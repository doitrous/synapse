## title
Temperate phage infection: chromosome integration

## question
When a temperate bacteriophage infects a bacterial cell?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Most viral genes are expressed

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Broad expression of most viral genes is characteristic of the productive, lytic pathway, not of a temperate phage settling into lysogeny, where most phage genes are instead kept repressed.

## answer_b
The bacterial cell is lysed

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Cell lysis is the outcome of the lytic cycle (a virulent phage, or a temperate phage that has switched out of lysogeny); a temperate phage establishing lysogeny does not lyse the cell it infects.

## answer_c
Many new viruses are produced

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Producing many new virus particles is the hallmark of the lytic cycle; a temperate phage entering lysogeny instead integrates quietly rather than replicating into progeny virions.

## answer_d
Most normal bacterial functions are turned off

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Lysogeny is compatible with the bacterium continuing most of its normal functions -- it is not a shutdown of the host cell, unlike the more disruptive lytic pathway.

## answer_e
The virus become integrated into bacterial chromosome

## explanation_e
Correct. A temperate bacteriophage is defined by its ability to enter the lysogenic cycle: rather than immediately directing lysis, its genome becomes integrated into the bacterial chromosome as a prophage. The integrated prophage is then replicated passively along with the host chromosome at every subsequent bacterial division, and most normal bacterial functions continue undisturbed. This chromosome-integration event is the single feature that most sharply distinguishes a temperate phage's behaviour from a virulent phage's lytic destruction of the host cell.

## title
Bacteriophage: definition

## question
Bacteriophage is?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Bacterium that infects viruses

## explanation_a
Incorrect. This reverses the relationship -- a bacteriophage is not a bacterium at all, and bacteria do not "infect" viruses; a virus is the infecting agent here, not the host.

## answer_b
Virus that infects bacteria

## explanation_b
Correct. A bacteriophage ("phage") is, by definition, a virus whose host is bacteria -- it attaches to and infects bacterial cells, hijacking their machinery to replicate, exactly as other viruses do with their own specific host cells. This bacterial host range is the single defining feature of the term "bacteriophage," distinguishing it from every other category of virus by the type of cell it can infect.

## answer_c
Viral antibody that lyses bacteria

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A bacteriophage is a virus, not an antibody; antibodies are host immune proteins that bind antigens, an entirely different class of molecule from a replicating virus particle.

## answer_d
Virus that infects human

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A bacteriophage's host range is bacteria specifically, not human cells -- a virus that infects human cells (such as influenza virus or HIV) is not a bacteriophage.

## title
Properties of the lytic cycle

## question
The lytic cycle?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The phage is said to be virulent

## explanation_a
Correct. A phage undergoing the lytic cycle is, by definition, called virulent -- it commits to destroying the host cell's DNA and producing new virions rather than integrating into the chromosome. This naming ("virulent phage" for the lytic pathway) is the source of the term used throughout this chapter to distinguish lytic-cycle phages from lysogeny-capable temperate phages.

## answer_b
The phage is known as temperate phage

## explanation_b
Incorrect. "Temperate phage" names the phage type capable of the opposite pathway, lysogeny, not the lytic cycle described here.

## answer_c
The phage DNA is inserted into the bacterial chromosome

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Insertion of phage DNA into the bacterial chromosome is the defining event of lysogeny (a temperate phage becoming a prophage), not of the lytic cycle, which instead destroys the host's own DNA.

## answer_d
Ends by bacterial cell replication

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The lytic cycle ends by lysis of the host bacterial cell (releasing new phage particles), not by the bacterial cell successfully completing its own replication.

## title
Virulent phages: destroying host DNA and causing lysis

## question
Phages that destroy host cell DNA, direct synthesis of phage particles, and cause lysis of the host cells are known as?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Temperate phages

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Temperate phages are capable of the opposite, lysogenic pathway -- integrating into the chromosome rather than destroying host DNA and lysing the cell.

## answer_b
Prophages

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A prophage is the integrated genome of a temperate phage within a lysogenized bacterial chromosome, not a phage actively destroying host DNA and lysing the cell.

## answer_c
Virulent phages

## explanation_c
Correct. Virulent phages are exactly the phage type described here: on infection they destroy the host bacterial cell's own DNA, redirect the cell's machinery to synthesize new phage particles, and end the cycle by lysing the host cell to release progeny virions. This trio of features -- host DNA destruction, particle synthesis, and lysis -- is the standard definition of the lytic cycle, and "virulent phage" is the term for the phage type committed to it.

## answer_d
Lysogenic phages

## explanation_d
Incorrect. "Lysogenic phage" is not standard terminology for this destructive behaviour; lysogeny describes the opposite, chromosome-integrating pathway of a temperate phage.

## answer_e
Macrophages

## explanation_e
Incorrect. A macrophage is a human/animal immune cell, an entirely different biological entity from a bacteriophage; it is included here only as an implausible distractor.

## title
What determines a phage's host specificity

## question
Host specificity of the phage is usually determined by?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Type of enzymes that a phage has

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The enzymes a phage carries are involved in steps such as nucleic acid injection or host-cell lysis, not in the initial receptor-recognition step that determines which host the phage can attach to.

## answer_b
Type of nucleic acid in the head

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Whether a phage's genome is DNA or RNA affects its own replication strategy inside a cell it has already infected; it is not the structure responsible for recognising and binding a specific host in the first place.

## answer_c
Shape of the capsid

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The capsid's shape protects and packages the phage genome; it is not the structure that makes first contact with, and recognises, the bacterial host surface.

## answer_d
The tail

## explanation_d
Correct. A bacteriophage's host specificity is usually determined by its tail: the tail fibers at the tip of the tail recognise and bind specific receptor molecules on the bacterial cell surface, and only a matching receptor allows attachment and subsequent infection. This tail-mediated receptor recognition is why phages are often highly specific for particular bacterial species or even strains, and it is the basis of the bacterial-typing applications that exploit differential phage susceptibility.

## answer_e
Contractile sheath

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The contractile sheath (part of the tail apparatus in some phages) helps drive genome injection into the host once attachment has already occurred; the tail's fibers, not the sheath specifically, are what determine which host can be recognised in the first place.
