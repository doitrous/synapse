## title
Amino acid essential under growth/surgical-recovery stress

## question
A young child is in an automobile accident that requires surgical intervention and substantial recovery time in the hospital. A consultation with a nutritionist results in a specific dietary plan. The plan included the supplementation of an amino acid that is typically considered a nonessential amino acid. Which of the following amino acids is an essential amino acid under conditions of enhanced growth or surgical recovery?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Arginine

## explanation_a
Correct. Arginine is ordinarily classed as a nonessential amino acid, since the body's own synthetic pathways normally keep pace with demand. Under states of rapid growth or the catabolic stress of surgery and recovery, that demand rises sharply -- arginine feeds directly into protein synthesis, the urea cycle and wound healing -- and the body's own synthesis can no longer keep up, making it conditionally essential and worth supplementing.

## answer_b
Serine

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Serine remains nonessential even under growth or surgical stress; it is not the amino acid this scenario's dietary plan is built around.

## answer_c
Alanine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Alanine is readily synthesised from pyruvate by transamination and stays nonessential in this same clinical setting; it is not the conditionally essential amino acid the nutritionist is supplementing.

## answer_d
Glycine

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Glycine is one of the amino acids the body synthesises most easily and does not become conditionally essential under growth or surgical stress the way arginine does.

## answer_e
Tyrosine

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Tyrosine is synthesised from phenylalanine and remains nonessential as long as phenylalanine intake is adequate; it is not the amino acid this stress-related supplementation scenario is describing.

## title
Alpha-helix hydrogen bonding

## question
Which of the following characterize alpha-helix regions of proteins?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
They all have the same primary structure

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Different alpha-helical regions, even within the same protein or across different proteins, do not share a single fixed primary (amino acid sequence) structure -- the alpha-helix is a geometry any sequence with a suitable composition can adopt, not a marker of one particular sequence.

## answer_b
They require a high content of proline and glycine

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This reverses the actual relationship: a high content of proline (whose backbone geometry and lack of a free amide hydrogen disrupt the regular hydrogen-bonding pattern) and excess glycine (too flexible) instead destabilises an alpha-helix, rather than being required for one.

## answer_c
They are formed by hydrogen bonding between two adjacent amino acids in the primary sequence

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The alpha-helix's hydrogen bonds do not form between two amino acids immediately adjacent in the primary sequence; they form between backbone atoms of peptide bonds separated by about four residues further along the chain.

## answer_d
They are formed principally by hydrogen bonds between a carbonyl oxygen atom in one peptide bond and the amide hydrogen from a different peptide bond

## explanation_d
Correct. The alpha-helix is held together by hydrogen bonds between the backbone carbonyl oxygen atom of one peptide bond and the backbone amide (N-H) hydrogen of a different peptide bond, about four residues further along the same chain. The repetition of this bonding pattern along the chain's length is what coils it into the helix's regular shape.

## answer_e
They are formed principally by hydrogen bonds between a carbonyl atom in one peptide bond and the hydrogen atoms on the side chain of another amino acid

## explanation_e
Incorrect. The alpha-helix's stabilising hydrogen bonds involve backbone atoms on both sides (carbonyl oxygen and amide hydrogen), not a side chain -- side-chain hydrogen bonding is a separate, tertiary-structure phenomenon, not what defines this secondary structure.

## title
Sulphur-containing amino acid

## question
Which of the following is a sulphur-containing amino acid?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Histidine

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Histidine's side chain is an imidazole ring, giving it basic character; it carries no sulphur and is not one of the sulphur-containing amino acids.

## answer_b
Proline

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Proline's side chain cyclises back onto its own backbone nitrogen, giving it a unique ring structure among the amino acids, but that ring contains no sulphur.

## answer_c
Leucine

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Leucine's side chain is a simple branched aliphatic hydrocarbon with no sulphur atom; it is a neutral, nonpolar amino acid, not a sulphur-containing one.

## answer_d
Methionine

## explanation_d
Correct. Methionine's side chain ends in a thioether (-S-CH3), making it, alongside cysteine, one of only two sulphur-containing amino acids among the twenty standard ones. Methionine additionally initiates every new polypeptide chain and, once activated to S-adenosylmethionine, serves as the cell's principal methyl-group donor.

## answer_e
Tryptophan

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Tryptophan's side chain is an indole ring, an aromatic, nitrogen-containing heterocycle; it carries no sulphur and is not a sulphur-containing amino acid.

## title
Alzheimer plaque protein structure

## question
Autopsies of patients with Alzheimer's disease show protein aggregates called neurofibrillary tangles and neuritic plaques in various regions of the brain. These plaques exhibit the characteristic staining of amyloid. Which of the following structural features is the most likely characteristic of at least one protein in these plaques?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
A high content of beta-pleated sheet structure

## explanation_a
Correct. Amyloid deposits, whatever the underlying disease or precursor protein, share a common misfolded structure: a high content of beta-pleated sheet. This is exactly what gives amyloid its characteristic Congo-red staining and its resistance to normal proteolytic clearance, and it is why the plaques in this vignette stain as amyloid.

## answer_b
Disulfide bond crosslinks between polypeptide chains

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Disulfide crosslinks are a form of tertiary/quaternary stabilisation seen in many normally folded, secreted proteins, but they are not the structural feature that defines amyloid or that these plaques are being described by.

## answer_c
A high content of alpha-helical structure

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Amyloid formation is specifically a shift toward beta-pleated sheet, not alpha-helical structure; a high alpha-helical content would describe a differently folded (and non-amyloid) protein.

## answer_d
A low-energy native conformation

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Amyloid is a misfolded, pathological aggregate, not the protein's normal low-energy native conformation -- the whole clinical significance of amyloid is that the protein has left its native fold behind.

## answer_e
A high content of random coils

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Random coil describes an unstructured, disordered polypeptide with no regular secondary structure, which is the opposite of amyloid's highly ordered, repetitive beta-pleated-sheet stacking.

## title
Proteasome inhibitor spares which protein class

## question
A patient with multiple myeloma has not responded to numerous treatments and his disease is progressing. His oncologist wants to start him on the drug bortezomib. Bortezomib inhibits the proteasome from degrading proteins. Which class of intracellular proteins will be specifically spared from degradation as a result of taking this drug?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Polyubiquitinated proteins

## explanation_a
Correct. Proteins are tagged for proteasomal destruction by attachment of a polyubiquitin chain, which the 26S proteasome recognises before unfolding and degrading the tagged protein in an ATP-dependent process. Bortezomib inhibits the proteasome's own catalytic activity, so exactly this class -- proteins carrying a polyubiquitin tag -- is specifically spared from the degradation it would otherwise undergo, which is exploited therapeutically in myeloma cells that depend heavily on this pathway to clear excess immunoglobulin.

## answer_b
Proteins with PEST sequences

## explanation_b
Incorrect. PEST-sequence proteins are also degraded via ubiquitin-dependent and some ubiquitin-independent proteasomal routes, but the class this question specifically tests, matching the printed key, is the polyubiquitinated proteins that are the proteasome's core substrate.

## answer_c
Amyloid proteins

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Amyloid proteins are characteristically resistant to normal proteolytic clearance in the first place, including by the proteasome, so bortezomib's inhibition of the proteasome does not meaningfully change their already-poor degradation.

## answer_d
Immunoglobulin heavy chains

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Immunoglobulin heavy chains that are misfolded or excess are themselves often degraded via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, but they are not the specific class this vignette and its printed key are naming.

## answer_e
Immunoglobulin light chains

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Immunoglobulin light chains, likewise, may be substrates for proteasomal degradation, but the option the printed key identifies as specifically spared is the broader polyubiquitinated-protein class, not light chains specifically.

## title
Effect not seen with protein denaturation

## question
Which of the following does NOT occur with denaturation of a protein?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Decreased viscosity

## explanation_a
Correct, as the exception. Denaturation does NOT decrease viscosity -- it increases it, since an unfolded or aggregated polypeptide occupies a larger, more irregular hydrodynamic volume than the compact native fold did. This is the one property in this list that moves opposite to what an intuitive guess based on 'the protein has come apart' might suggest.

## answer_b
Decreased diffusibility

## explanation_b
Incorrect as the exception. Decreased diffusibility genuinely does occur with denaturation, as unfolded or aggregated protein moves less freely through solution -- this option describes a real effect, not the exception.

## answer_c
Increased viscosity

## explanation_c
Incorrect as the exception. Increased viscosity is exactly what does occur with denaturation, so this option describes a real effect and is not the one the question is asking for.

## answer_d
Decreased solubility

## explanation_d
Incorrect as the exception. Decreased solubility genuinely occurs, since denaturation exposes hydrophobic residues normally buried in the folded interior, driving aggregation and reduced solubility -- a real effect, not the exception.

## answer_e
Decreased biological activity

## explanation_e
Incorrect as the exception. Decreased biological activity genuinely occurs, since a protein's function depends on its precise native fold, which denaturation destroys -- again a real effect, not the exception.

## title
Organelle that packages and exports proteins

## question
This organelle packages proteins for the cell and sends them out of the cell to other cells:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Ribosomes

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Ribosomes are the sites of protein synthesis itself (translation), not the organelle that packages finished proteins for export.

## answer_b
Endoplasmic reticulum

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The endoplasmic reticulum synthesises the proteins in the first place and sends them onward, but the packaging-and-export role this question asks about belongs to the organelle that receives the ER's output next.

## answer_c
Golgi body

## explanation_c
Correct. The Golgi body (Golgi apparatus) receives proteins from the endoplasmic reticulum, further modifies and sorts them, and packages them into vesicles for export out of the cell (secretion) or delivery to the plasma membrane or other organelles. Its stacked, flattened sacs process cargo in a fixed cis-to-trans sequence, so material leaving the trans face is already sorted for its final destination.

## answer_d
Lysosome

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The lysosome is the organelle responsible for intracellular digestion and degradation, not for packaging proteins for export to other cells.
