## title
Post-fracture DVT complicates to pulmonary thromboembolism

## question
76-year-old woman is hospitalized after falling and fracturing her left femoral trochanter. Two weeks later, the left leg is swollen, particularly below the knee. She experiences pain on movement of the leg; on palpation, there is tenderness. Which of the following complications is most likely to occur after these events?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Gangrenous necrosis of the foot

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Gangrenous necrosis of the foot would require arterial, not venous, compromise -- unrelated to the swelling and tenderness pattern described here.

## answer_b
Hematoma of the thigh

## explanation_b
Incorrect. A hematoma of the thigh would be a localized bleeding collection, not the leg swelling and calf tenderness pattern this vignette describes.

## answer_c
Pulmonary thromboembolism

## explanation_c
Correct. Leg swelling, pain on movement and tenderness two weeks after a hip fracture, with continued immobilization, is the classic picture of deep vein thrombosis in the leg veins. Such a thrombus can detach and travel via venous return to the lung, making pulmonary thromboembolism the most likely subsequent complication. This is the expected downstream risk of an immobilization-related DVT.

## answer_d
Chronic venous congestion

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Chronic venous congestion is a systemic pattern from right heart failure, not the localized limb finding described, and not the acute complication being asked about.

## answer_e
Disseminated intravascular coagulation

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Disseminated intravascular coagulation is a systemic, widespread coagulation disorder, not the localized, mechanical leg-swelling process described here.

## title
Immobilization causes leg DVT through venous stasis

## question
A 55-year-old woman has had discomfort and swelling of the left leg for the past week. On physical examination, the leg is slightly difficult to move, but on palpation, there is no pain. A venogram shows thrombosis of deep left leg veins. Which of the following mechanisms is most likely to cause this condition?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Turbulent blood flow

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Turbulent flow is more typically implicated in arterial or cardiac thrombus formation (e.g. at vessel bifurcations), not the classic mechanism for a leg deep vein thrombosis like this.

## answer_b
Immobilization

## explanation_b
Correct. Immobilization produces venous stasis -- stagnant, slow-moving blood in the leg veins -- one arm of Virchow's triad predisposing to thrombus formation. A leg that has been difficult to move for a week, with confirmed deep vein thrombosis, fits this stasis mechanism most directly. This is the standard mechanism tested for an immobility-associated DVT.

## answer_c
Hypercalcemia

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Hypercalcemia is not a standard, directly-tested risk factor for venous thrombosis in this context.

## answer_d
Ingestion of aspirin

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Aspirin is an antiplatelet agent that would reduce, not promote, thrombosis risk.

## answer_e
Nitric oxide release

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Nitric oxide is a vasodilator and platelet-inhibitor that would tend to reduce, not promote, thrombosis.

## title
A detached intravascular mass is called an embolus

## question
A detached intravascular mass carried by the blood from its point of origin to a distant site is called:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Embolus

## explanation_a
Correct. An embolus is by definition a detached intravascular mass -- solid, liquid or gaseous -- carried by the bloodstream from its point of origin to lodge at a distant site. This is precisely the definition given in the stem. The term describes the mass once it has detached and is travelling, not before.

## answer_b
Infarct

## explanation_b
Incorrect. An infarct is the area of tissue necrosis that can result once an embolus (or other cause) obstructs blood flow, not the travelling mass itself.

## answer_c
Thrombus

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A thrombus is the mass before it detaches and starts travelling -- once detached and travelling, it is termed an embolus.

## answer_d
Clot

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A clot describes coagulated blood forming after death or outside the vasculature, not a detached, travelling intravascular mass.

## title
Cerebral infarction is liquefactive necrosis

## question
Cerebral infarction is:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
liquifactive necrosis

## explanation_a
Correct. Cerebral infarction undergoes liquefactive necrosis, unlike infarction in most other organs. The brain's high lipid content and lack of firm connective-tissue stroma mean the dead tissue is digested into a soft, liquefied cavity rather than retaining firm architecture. This is a specifically-tested exception to the general solid-organ coagulative-necrosis pattern.

## answer_b
caseation necrosis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Caseation necrosis is the cheese-like necrosis characteristic of tuberculosis and some fungal infections, not the pattern seen in a cerebral infarct.

## answer_c
fibrinoid necrosis

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Fibrinoid necrosis is seen in immune-mediated vascular injury (e.g. malignant hypertension, vasculitis), not the typical pattern of a cerebral infarct.

## answer_d
coagulative necrosis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Coagulative necrosis is the pattern seen in most other solid-organ infarcts (kidney, spleen, heart), but the brain is the standard exception, undergoing liquefactive necrosis instead.
