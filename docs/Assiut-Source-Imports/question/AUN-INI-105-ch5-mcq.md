## title
Bactericidal agents kill bacteria, causing cell lysis

## question
Antimicrobial agents that kill the microbe leading to cell lysis are called?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Bacteriostatic

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A bacteriostatic agent inhibits bacterial growth and multiplication without necessarily killing the organism, relying on host defences to clear the now-static population -- it does not itself cause cell lysis.

## answer_b
Bactericidal

## explanation_b
Correct. A bactericidal agent kills bacteria outright, so that multiplication cannot resume. Killing frequently proceeds through cell lysis, for example when a cell-wall-active drug weakens the wall until osmotic pressure ruptures the cell. That destructive endpoint is exactly what this stem describes.

## answer_c
Narrow spectrum

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Narrow spectrum describes how many bacterial groups a drug acts against, an entirely different classification axis from whether the drug kills (bactericidal) or merely inhibits (bacteriostatic) the organisms it does act on.

## answer_d
Broad spectrum

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Broad spectrum, like narrow spectrum, describes the breadth of organisms a drug covers, not whether its effect on a susceptible organism is killing or merely growth-inhibiting.

## title
Tetracyclines share a mechanism class with aminoglycosides

## question
Tetracyclines are identical in their mechanism of action to?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Sulfonamides

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Sulfonamides act by an entirely different route -- competitive antagonism of PABA's conversion to folic acid -- rather than at the ribosome, so they are not the class tetracyclines are grouped with here.

## answer_b
Penicillin

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Penicillin inhibits bacterial cell wall synthesis, a mechanism unrelated to the ribosomal protein-synthesis target tetracyclines act on.

## answer_c
Rifampicin

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Rifampicin inhibits RNA polymerase, blocking transcription -- a nucleic-acid target, not the 30S ribosomal target tetracyclines share with aminoglycosides.

## answer_d
Aminoglycosides

## explanation_d
Correct. Tetracyclines and aminoglycosides are both classed as 30S-ribosomal-subunit-targeting protein synthesis inhibitors: tetracyclines reversibly block aminoacyl-tRNA from binding the A site, while aminoglycosides irreversibly distort the subunit to cause mRNA misreading. Though the downstream effects differ (tetracyclines bacteriostatic, aminoglycosides bactericidal), the shared ribosomal target is what the bank's teaching frame means by "identical mechanism," in contrast to the 50S-targeting or cell-wall-targeting classes.

## title
Cell wall synthesis inhibitors are the most selective antibacterials

## question
The most selective antibacterial agents are those that interfere with bacterial cell wall synthesis. This is because?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Bacterial cell walls have a unique structure not found in eukaryotic host cells

## explanation_a
Correct. Peptidoglycan, the target of cell-wall synthesis inhibitors, is a structural component unique to the bacterial cell wall with no eukaryotic counterpart. Human cells have no cell wall at all, so a drug that disrupts peptidoglycan synthesis has essentially nothing to disrupt in the host. This gives cell-wall-active drugs the sharpest selective toxicity of any antimicrobial class.

## answer_b
Bacterial cell wall synthesis is easily inhibited whereas eukaryotic cell wall synthesis is more resistant to the actions of the drugs

## explanation_b
Incorrect. This distractor invents a false premise: eukaryotic cells do not have cell-wall synthesis of any kind (resistant or otherwise) for a drug to act on, so the selectivity is not a matter of relative drug sensitivity between two comparable processes.

## answer_c
Eukaryotic cells inactivate the drugs before they can do any damage

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Selectivity here is not explained by eukaryotic cells actively inactivating the drug; it is explained by the absence of the drug's target (peptidoglycan) in eukaryotic cells altogether.

## answer_d
Eukaryotic cells do not take up the drugs

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Cell-wall synthesis inhibitors are not selective because human cells fail to take up the drug -- many such drugs do reach host cells and tissues; selectivity instead comes from the target structure being absent from host cells, not from an uptake barrier.

## title
Aminoglycoside mechanism of action: 30S subunit binding

## question
The mechanism of action of Aminoglycoside antibiotics is to?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Bind to the 30S ribosomal subunit

## explanation_a
Correct. Aminoglycosides irreversibly bind the bacterial 30S ribosomal subunit, distorting it so that mRNA is misread and abnormal, non-functional proteins are produced. A disruption severe enough to be bactericidal rather than merely bacteriostatic.

Inhibit cell wall synthesis

Incorrect. Inhibiting cell wall synthesis describes beta-lactams (penicillins, cephalosporins) and vancomycin, not aminoglycosides, whose target is the ribosome, not peptidoglycan.

Bind to bacterial DNA polymerase

Incorrect. No major antimicrobial class in this chapter binds DNA polymerase directly; quinolones instead target DNA gyrase (topoisomerase II), a different enzyme, and aminoglycosides act at the ribosome, not on DNA replication machinery.

## answer_d
Inhibit folic acid biosynthesis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Inhibiting folic acid biosynthesis describes sulfonamides (via PABA antagonism), an entirely different mechanism from the ribosomal binding aminoglycosides perform.

## title
Phage therapy uses virulent (lytic) phages

## question
If you are going to choose an agent that can be used as an alternative for antimicrobials, which of the following do you recommend?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Virulent phages

## explanation_a
Correct. Virulent (lytic) phages infect a bacterial cell and immediately hijack it to produce new phage particles, lysing and killing the host cell. The only phage type whose infection cycle actually kills bacteria, making it the recommended choice for phage therapy as an antimicrobial alternative.

## answer_b
Lysogenic phages

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Lysogenic phages integrate their genome into the host bacterial chromosome and can remain dormant without killing the cell, which defeats the purpose of an antimicrobial alternative meant to eliminate bacteria.

## answer_c
Temperate phages

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Temperate phages are the phages capable of the lysogenic cycle; like lysogenic phages generally, they do not reliably kill their host and can even carry and transfer virulence or resistance genes between bacteria (lysogenic conversion), a risk rather than a benefit for therapy.

## answer_d
Prophages

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A prophage is phage DNA already integrated into a bacterial chromosome in the lysogenic state -- inert with respect to killing that cell, and therefore not a therapeutic option.

## answer_e
Cosmids

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Cosmids are laboratory cloning vectors engineered from phage and plasmid elements to carry large DNA inserts; they are a molecular biology tool, not a naturally bactericidal agent suitable as an antimicrobial alternative.

## title
Influenza is a common airborne epidemic disease

## question
An example for common air borne epidemic disease?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Influenza

## explanation_a
Correct. Influenza spreads via respiratory droplets and droplet nuclei -- the classic airborne route. Making it the textbook example of a common epidemic disease transmitted through the air.

## answer_b
Typhoid

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Typhoid is transmitted by the faeco-oral route, typically via contaminated food or water, not through the air.

## answer_c
Encephalitis
