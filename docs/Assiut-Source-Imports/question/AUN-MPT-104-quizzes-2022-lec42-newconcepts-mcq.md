## title
Clindamycin carries a C difficile diarrhea risk to counsel on discharge

## question
A patient is being discharged from the hospital on a 3-week course of clindamycin. Which of the following potential adverse effects should be discussed with her?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Pseudotumor cerebri.

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Pseudotumor cerebri (idiopathic intracranial hypertension) is classically linked to agents such as tetracyclines or vitamin A excess, not clindamycin.

## answer_b
Clostridium difficile diarrhea.

## explanation_b
Correct. Clindamycin carries a well-recognised risk of Clostridium difficile diarrhea, including its severe form, pseudomembranous colitis, because it disrupts normal gut flora and allows C. difficile to overgrow -- a risk significant enough that any patient discharged on a multi-week clindamycin course should be specifically counselled about it. Pseudotumor cerebri, hyperbilirubinemia and nephrotoxicity are not clindamycin's characteristic adverse effects.

## answer_c
Hyperbilirubinemia.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Hyperbilirubinemia is not a characteristic clindamycin adverse effect.

## answer_d
Nephrotoxicity.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Nephrotoxicity is a concern with drugs such as aminoglycosides, not a characteristic clindamycin adverse effect.

## title
Aminoglycosides are mainly effective against aerobic, not anaerobic, gram-negative bacteria

## question
Concerning Aminoglycosides, the following are true except:

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
They cause concentration-dependant not time -dependent bacterial killing

## explanation_a
Incorrect (as the EXCEPT) -- this statement is true. Aminoglycosides show concentration-dependent killing, so their efficacy tracks peak drug levels rather than the duration of time above the MIC.

## answer_b
They are mainly effective in anaerobic gram negative bacteria

## explanation_b
Correct (as the EXCEPT). Aminoglycosides require oxygen-dependent active transport to cross the bacterial cell membrane and reach their ribosomal target, so they are mainly effective against aerobic, not anaerobic, gram-negative bacteria. Anaerobic organisms lack the oxidative transport process aminoglycoside uptake depends on, making this statement the false one among the options.

## answer_c
They have synergistic antimicrobial effects with beta-lactams

## explanation_c
Incorrect (as the EXCEPT) -- this statement is true. Aminoglycosides act synergistically with beta-lactams, since beta-lactam-induced cell wall damage improves aminoglycoside penetration into the bacterial cell.

## answer_d
Their doses should be adjusted according to creatinine clearance

## explanation_d
Incorrect (as the EXCEPT) -- this statement is true. Aminoglycosides are renally cleared and nephrotoxic, so doses are adjusted according to creatinine clearance.

## subject

Doxycycline (a tetracycline) causes fetal teeth discolouration and bone growth disturbance, moxifloxacin (a fluoroquinolone) carries a risk to developing cartilage, and gentamicin (an aminoglycoside) risks fetal ototoxicity/nephrotoxicity -- none of the other three is the safe choice this question asks for.

## title
Clindamycin and erythromycin antagonise each other at a shared ribosomal site

## question
Which of the following antibiotic combinations is inappropriate based on antagonism at the same site of action?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Clindamycin and erythromycin.

## explanation_a
Correct. Clindamycin and erythromycin both bind overlapping sites on the bacterial ribosome's 50S subunit, so combining them is antagonistic -- one drug can displace or block the other's binding, reducing the combination's net antibacterial effect compared with either drug given alone. Ciprofloxacin/amoxicillin, tigecycline/azithromycin and doxycycline/amoxicillin do not share this same-site 50S antagonism.

## answer_b
Ciprofloxacin and amoxicillin.

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Ciprofloxacin (a DNA gyrase inhibitor) and amoxicillin (a cell-wall inhibitor) act at entirely different sites, so this combination is not the same-site antagonism the question asks for.

## answer_c
Tigecycline and azithromycin.

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Tigecycline and azithromycin both target the ribosome but do not share clindamycin/erythromycin's specific overlapping 50S binding-site antagonism.

## answer_d
Doxycycline and amoxicillin.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Doxycycline (a 30S-subunit inhibitor) and amoxicillin (a cell-wall inhibitor) act at different sites, so this combination is not the same-site antagonism the question asks for.
