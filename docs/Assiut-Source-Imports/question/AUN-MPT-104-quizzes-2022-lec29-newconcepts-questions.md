## title
Sutures help wound healing by apposing edges

## question
A 20-year-old woman sustains an injury to her right calf in a mountain biking accident. On physical examination she has a 5 cm long laceration on the right lateral aspect of her lower leg. This wound is closed with sutures. Wound healing proceeds over the next week. Which of the following factors will be most likely to help wound healing in this patient?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Corticosteroid therapy

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Corticosteroid therapy impairs, not helps, healing -- it suppresses the inflammatory and fibroblastic phases of repair.

## answer_b
Presence of sutures

## explanation_b
Correct. The presence of sutures apposes the wound edges, minimising the gap the repair process must bridge. This favours quicker, first-intention-style healing with less granulation tissue. It is the one factor here that helps rather than impairs the wound's outcome.

## answer_c
Decreased tissue perfusion

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Decreased tissue perfusion impairs healing by limiting the oxygen and nutrient delivery the repair process depends on.

## answer_d
presence of bacteria

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The presence of bacteria impairs healing by prolonging the inflammatory phase and directly damaging newly formed tissue.

## title
A surgical wound does not heal by second intention

## question
The following lesion does NOT heal by second intention:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Surgical wound

## explanation_a
Correct. A surgical wound is typically a clean, incised defect whose edges can be closely apposed, for example with sutures. This lets it heal by first intention -- primary union with minimal granulation tissue -- rather than by second intention. It is therefore the standard example of a lesion excluded from the second-intention pathway.

## answer_b
Abscess

## explanation_b
Incorrect as the answer. An abscess cavity is a defect that cannot be apposed and heals by second intention, filling with granulation tissue.

## answer_c
Ulceration

## explanation_c
Incorrect as the answer. An area of ulceration is a tissue-loss defect that heals by second intention, not by primary apposition.

## answer_d
Infarction

## explanation_d
Incorrect as the answer. An infarct undergoing repair heals through organisation and, where there is a resulting tissue defect, by second-intention-style granulation and scarring, not by apposed-edge primary union.

## title
Second intention healing has more common complications

## question
Which of the following is a feature of healing by second intention?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Clean wounds

## explanation_a
Incorrect. A clean wound with apposed edges is the setting for first, not second, intention healing.

## answer_b
More common complications

## explanation_b
Correct. Healing by second intention fills a larger tissue defect with abundant granulation tissue. It heals by wound contraction over a longer time than first intention. Because of this larger defect and longer course, it carries a higher rate of complications.

## answer_c
Minimal loss of tissue

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Second intention healing specifically follows a larger, not minimal, tissue loss -- that is why it needs abundant granulation tissue to fill the gap.

## answer_d
No infection

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Second intention healing carries a higher, not a nil, infection risk, given the larger open defect and longer healing time.

## title
First intention healing occurs in a clean cut wound

## question
Which of the following is characteristic for healing by first intention?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Occur in clean cut wound

## explanation_a
Correct. First intention healing occurs in a clean, incised wound whose edges are closely apposed. It needs only a thin fibrin seal and minimal granulation tissue to close the gap. This is why it heals faster and with fewer complications than second intention.

## answer_b
More common complications

## explanation_b
Incorrect. More common complications is a feature of second, not first, intention healing.

## answer_c
Abundant granulation tissue

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Abundant granulation tissue is needed to fill the larger defect of second intention healing, not the minimal gap of first intention.

## answer_d
Take long time to heal

## explanation_d
Incorrect. First intention healing is comparatively quick, not slow -- taking a long time to heal is instead characteristic of second intention.
