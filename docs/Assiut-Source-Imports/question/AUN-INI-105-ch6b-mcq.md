## title
Retrovirus replication: RNA-to-DNA step is cytoplasmic, not nuclear

## question
Regarding the replication cycle of retrovirus, which sentence is wrong?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Retrovirus utilizes host cell DNA polymerase to replicate its genome

## explanation_a
This statement is true, in the sense the question intends: once integrated, the provirus is expressed using host cell polymerase activity, and only option C contains the sentence's actual factual error, so this is not the wrong statement being asked for.

## answer_b
Retrovirus proviral DNA is integrated to the host chromosome

## explanation_b
Correct in itself -- integration of the reverse-transcribed proviral DNA into the host chromosome is a defining, true feature of the retrovirus replication cycle, so this is not the wrong statement.

## answer_c
Retrovirus is +ssRNA and replicates in the nucleus

## explanation_c
Correct (this is the WRONG statement, as asked). A retrovirus does carry a +ssRNA genome, but that RNA does not itself replicate inside the nucleus. Instead, the virion's own reverse transcriptase converts the RNA genome into double-stranded DNA in the cytoplasm; only this DNA product is then carried into the nucleus, where it integrates into the host chromosome as a provirus. Saying the retrovirus "is +ssRNA and replicates in the nucleus" therefore conflates the RNA genome with a nuclear replication step that, for the RNA itself, never actually happens -- the RNA is converted to DNA in the cytoplasm first.

## answer_d
Retrovirus has reverse transcriptase

## explanation_d
True -- reverse transcriptase (an RNA-directed DNA polymerase) is the defining virion enzyme of every retrovirus, so this is not the wrong statement.

## title
Enveloped virus penetration is by membrane fusion

## question
Penetration of enveloped viruses is through?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Phagocytosis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Phagocytosis is a bulk-particle-engulfment process carried out by specialized phagocytic cells (such as macrophages), not the standard route by which a virus penetrates a susceptible host cell generally.

## answer_b
Pinocytosis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Pinocytosis is a general fluid-uptake form of endocytosis; it is not the specific, named mechanism by which enveloped viruses penetrate.

## answer_c
Fusion

## explanation_c
Correct. The standard penetration route for an enveloped virus is fusion: the lipid envelope fuses directly with the host plasma membrane (or, after endocytic uptake, with an endosomal membrane), releasing the nucleocapsid into the cytoplasm. This differs from the uptake route available to naked (non-enveloped) viruses, which lack a fusable lipid envelope and instead depend on receptor-mediated endocytosis (viropexis) alone, without any membrane-fusion step. Because fusion requires an envelope, it is unique to enveloped viruses and is a reliable discriminator between the two structural classes.

## answer_d
Veropexis

## explanation_d
Incorrect. "Veropexis" (viropexis) names the endocytic uptake route relevant to naked-virus entry, not the fusion mechanism that is the hallmark of enveloped-virus penetration.

## title
Positive-sense RNA genome can be translated directly as mRNA

## question
Positive stranded RNA viruses have which of the following characteristics?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Their genome RNA can be translated directly as mRNA

## explanation_a
Correct. A positive-sense (+) RNA virus genome carries the same base sequence and polarity as mRNA, so host ribosomes can translate it directly into viral protein as soon as it enters the cytoplasm, with no transcription step needed first. This is the defining functional property separating +ssRNA viruses from negative-sense (-) ssRNA viruses, whose genome is the mirror-image complement of mRNA and must first be transcribed into a +sense copy by a virion-packaged RNA-dependent RNA polymerase before translation can occur.

## answer_b
They have to transcribe their genome RNA to a mirror image copy as a mRNA

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Transcribing the genome into a mirror-image mRNA copy describes negative-sense RNA viruses, which need this extra step precisely because their genome is NOT already in the mRNA sense -- the opposite of a positive-sense genome.

## answer_c
This genome is circular

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A positive-sense RNA virus genome is linear, not circular, as a rule.

## answer_d
Their RNA genome is segmented

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Genome segmentation is a separate structural feature seen in some negative-sense and double-stranded RNA virus families; it is not a defining characteristic of positive-sense RNA viruses as a class.

Antibody antiviral mechanisms exclude MHC-I upregulation

## question
Antibodies can prevent virus infection by all of the following EXCEPT?

## subject
inf

## status

## owner

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a

## explanation_a
Incorrect as the EXCEPT answer -- neutralization (antibody binding a free virion and blocking its attachment or entry) IS a genuine antibody-mediated antiviral mechanism.

## answer_b
Opsonization

## explanation_b
Incorrect as the EXCEPT answer -- opsonization (antibody coating a virion to enhance its phagocytic uptake) IS a genuine antibody-mediated antiviral mechanism.

## answer_c
ADCC

## explanation_c
Incorrect as the EXCEPT answer -- antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC), in which antibody bound to an infected cell recruits an NK cell to kill it, IS a genuine antibody-mediated antiviral mechanism.

Increase expression of MHC-1 on surface of virus infected cells

Correct (this IS the exception). Neutralization, opsonization and ADCC are all genuine antibody (humoral) mechanisms, which is exactly why MHC-I upregulation, belonging to a different arm of the immune response, is the one that does not belong on this list.

## question
Retroviruses can synthesize DNA from RNA by which enzyme?

## subject
inf

## status

Correct. ## answer_d
Protease

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Protease is a separate retroviral enzyme, responsible for cleaving viral polyproteins during maturation, not for synthesizing DNA from RNA.

inf

## title
Protein content is common to all viruses

## question
All viruses?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Contain DNA

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Only DNA viruses contain DNA; RNA viruses do not, so this is not true of all viruses.

## answer_b
Contain RNA

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Only RNA viruses contain RNA; DNA viruses do not, so this is likewise not true of all viruses.

## answer_c
Contain protein

## explanation_c
Correct. Protein is the one chemical component found in every virus, since even the simplest viable virus needs a protein coat (capsid) around its genome. Nucleic acid type (DNA vs RNA) varies by virus family rather than applying to all viruses, and so does lipid content (present only if enveloped). Protein alone is guaranteed, which is why it is the correct answer here.

## answer_d
Are susceptible to lipid solvents

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Susceptibility to lipid solvents applies specifically to enveloped viruses, whose lipid envelope is disrupted by such solvents; naked (non-enveloped) viruses, lacking any lipid component, are NOT susceptible to lipid solvents, so this is not true of all viruses.

## title
Viruses need live cells and cannot replicate in non-living media

## question
In viral properties which of the following is the WRONG STATEMENT?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The viral envelope contains Lipoprotein

## explanation_a
Incorrect as the WRONG-statement answer -- the viral envelope IS lipoprotein in nature (lipid from the host membrane, protein encoded by the virus), so this statement is true, not wrong.

## answer_b
Viruses can replicate in non-living media

## explanation_b
Correct (this IS the wrong statement). Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites -- they can only replicate inside a living host cell, never independently -- so a virus can NOT replicate in non-living (cell-free) media; growth on an inert nutrient medium, unlike a bacterium on blood agar, simply does not apply to viruses. This is the one statement among the options that contradicts a defining, universal property of viruses.

## answer_c
Viruses need live cells to grow

## explanation_c
Incorrect as the WRONG-statement answer -- viruses genuinely DO need live cells to grow (they are obligate intracellular parasites), so this statement is true, not wrong.

## answer_d
Viruses have no ribosome

## explanation_d
Incorrect as the WRONG-statement answer -- viruses genuinely have no ribosomes of their own, instead using the host cell's ribosomes for translation, so this statement is true, not wrong.

## answer_e
Contain either RNA or DNA

## explanation_e
Incorrect as the WRONG-statement answer -- a virus's genome is genuinely either RNA or DNA, never both, so this statement is true, not wrong.

## title
Transcription forms mRNA, not protein (translation's job)

## question
In structure and classification of viruses, which is the WRONG STATEMENT?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Complex symmetry of capsid found in pox viruses

## explanation_a
Incorrect as the WRONG-statement answer -- poxviruses genuinely do show complex capsid symmetry (an irregular, brick-shaped structure), so this statement is true, not wrong.

## answer_b
The viruses contain protein and nucleic acid

## explanation_b
Incorrect as the WRONG-statement answer -- every virus genuinely does contain both protein (at minimum, its capsid) and nucleic acid (its genome), so this statement is true, not wrong.

## answer_c
Transcription is the formation of protein

## explanation_c
Correct (this IS the wrong statement). Transcription is the synthesis of RNA (mRNA) using a DNA or RNA template, carried out by an RNA polymerase -- its product is mRNA, not protein. Describing transcription as "the formation of protein" swaps the two processes' defining products; it is translation, not transcription, that produces protein, since translation is the separate, subsequent step in which ribosomes read mRNA to synthesize protein.

## answer_d
Translation is the formation of protein

## explanation_d
Incorrect as the WRONG-statement answer -- translation genuinely IS the formation of protein (from mRNA, by ribosomes), so this statement is true, not wrong.
