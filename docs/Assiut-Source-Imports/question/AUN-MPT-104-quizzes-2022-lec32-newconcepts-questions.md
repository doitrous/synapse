## title
Esmolol suits acute perioperative tachyarrhythmia

## question
35 years old patient was operated for inguinal hernia. Surgeon decided to operate him under general anesthesia (GA). After recovery from anesthesia, on routine investigations at ICU, tachycardia and extrasystoles were observed by ICU specialist. For this patient at this stage, which drugs are helpful?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Esmolol is special beta blocker used in this case

## explanation_a
Correct. Esmolol is a cardioselective beta blocker with an ultra-short duration of action, rapidly metabolised by plasma esterases. This makes it easily titratable and quickly reversible, ideal for controlling acute postoperative tachycardia and extrasystoles in a monitored ICU setting. Its brief action is specifically why it is the preferred beta blocker for this acute perioperative scenario, over a longer-acting alternative.

## answer_b
Ipratropium may be helpful to control tachycardia

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Ipratropium is a muscarinic antagonist used for bronchodilation, not a treatment for tachycardia or extrasystoles.

## answer_c
Bethanichol may improve extrasystoles

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Bethanechol is a muscarinic agonist used to stimulate bowel/bladder motility; it does not treat cardiac extrasystoles and could itself provoke bradyarrhythmia rather than help.

## answer_d
Pindolol is useful in such case

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Pindolol is a beta blocker with intrinsic sympathomimetic activity and a longer duration than esmolol, making it less suited to acute, easily-titratable perioperative control.

## title
Propranolol masks hypoglycemia manifestations

## question
In a hypertensive patient who is treated with insulin for diabetes mellitus, which of the following drugs is to be used with extra caution as it masks manifestations of hypoglycemia?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Propranolol

## explanation_a
Correct. Propranolol, a nonselective beta blocker, masks the adrenergic warning symptoms of hypoglycemia -- tachycardia, tremor and palpitations -- that would normally alert a patient to falling blood glucose. This masking effect, rather than a direct metabolic action, is why propranolol needs extra caution in an insulin-treated diabetic patient. Without these warning signs, a hypoglycemic episode can go unrecognised for longer, delaying treatment.

## answer_b
Prazosin

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Prazosin, an alpha1 blocker, does not act on the beta-adrenoceptor-mediated adrenergic symptoms of hypoglycemia.

## answer_c
Diazoxide

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Diazoxide actually raises blood glucose (by inhibiting insulin release), the opposite concern from masking hypoglycemia.

## answer_d
Nifedipine

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Nifedipine, a calcium channel blocker, does not mask the adrenergic warning symptoms of hypoglycemia the way a nonselective beta blocker does.

## title
Propranolol does not cause hyperglycemia -- the false statement

## question
One of the following statements is FALSE regarding Beta-blockers:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Atenolol has no effect on blood vessels.

## explanation_a
Incorrect as the answer (this statement is TRUE). Atenolol, a cardioselective beta1 blocker, has minimal direct effect on peripheral blood vessels compared with a nonselective agent.

## answer_b
Propranolol is contraindicated in diabetics as it causes hyperglycemia.

## explanation_b
Correct as the FALSE statement. Propranolol is not contraindicated in diabetics because it 'causes hyperglycemia' -- its actual diabetic concern is masking the adrenergic warning symptoms of hypoglycemia, not raising blood sugar directly. This is a common misstatement the question is testing recognition of. The real mechanism (symptom masking) is a different, and in some ways more dangerous, problem than hyperglycemia.

## answer_c
Propranolol causes vasoconstriction of some blood vessels

## explanation_c
Incorrect as the answer (this statement is TRUE). Unopposed alpha1-mediated vasoconstriction can occur with nonselective beta blockade (such as propranolol's beta2 blockade removing a vasodilatory counterbalance) in some vascular beds.

## answer_d
Carvedilol causes vasodilation of some blood vessels

## explanation_d
Incorrect as the answer (this statement is TRUE). Carvedilol has additional alpha1-blocking activity, causing vasodilation in some vascular beds, unlike a pure beta blocker.

## title
Acebutolol's ISA can worsen exertional angina

## question
Which of the following beta-blockers would be most likely to worsen exertional angina pectoris because of its intrinsic sympathomimetic activity?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Atenolol

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Atenolol lacks intrinsic sympathomimetic activity, so this specific angina-worsening mechanism does not apply to it.

## answer_b
Acebutolol

## explanation_b
Correct. Acebutolol's intrinsic sympathomimetic activity (ISA) means it acts as a partial agonist, providing some low-level beta-receptor stimulation even while blocking the receptor. This blunts the full heart-rate- and workload-lowering effect that beta blockade is meant to provide during exercise. Because that full suppression is exactly what protects against exertional angina, acebutolol's ISA can specifically worsen it, unlike a beta blocker without ISA.

## answer_c
Propranolol

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Propranolol lacks intrinsic sympathomimetic activity, so this specific mechanism does not apply to it.

## answer_d
Nadolol

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Nadolol lacks intrinsic sympathomimetic activity, so this specific mechanism does not apply to it.
