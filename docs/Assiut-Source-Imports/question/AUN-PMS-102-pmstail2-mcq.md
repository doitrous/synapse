## title
The clavicle's subcutaneous course

## question
Considering the true anatomical fact about the clavicle is

## subject
msk

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The lateral end is somewhat quadrangular in shape

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The clavicle's lateral (acromial) end is flattened, not quadrangular; a quadrangular cross-section describes its medial part instead.

## answer_b
It is subcutaneous throughout its whole extent

## explanation_b
Correct. The clavicle is the one long bone of the body that is subcutaneous throughout its whole extent, lying immediately deep to the skin with no overlying muscle anywhere along its S-shaped course from the sternal end medially to the acromial end laterally. This subcutaneous position is exactly why the clavicle can be palpated along its whole length in a living subject and is also why it is one of the most commonly fractured bones, since nothing cushions it from a fall onto the shoulder or an outstretched hand. Its superior surface is smooth, not rough, which rules out one of the alternative statements printed alongside this fact.

## answer_c
The superior surface is rough

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The clavicle's superior surface is smooth; it is the inferior surface that carries roughened areas for ligament attachment.

## answer_d
It is a short bone that lies horizontally across the root of the neck

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The clavicle is classified as a long bone, not a short bone, despite lying horizontally; its shaft is longer than it is wide, the defining feature of a long bone.

Correct. The scapula's posterior surface carries a prominent spine dividing it into a supraspinous fossa above and an infraspinous fossa below. The medial angle of the scapula forms the pear-shaped glenoid cavity

## explanation_d
Incorrect. It is the lateral angle of the scapula, not the medial angle, that carries the pear-shaped glenoid cavity receiving the humeral head.

## explanation_a

## answer_b
On the medial aspect of the shaft is a roughened elevation called the deltoid tuberosity

## explanation_b
Incorrect. The deltoid tuberosity lies on the lateral, not medial, aspect of the humeral shaft.

## answer_c

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The humerus's lower end carries more than just the two epicondyles; it also carries the capitulum and trochlea, the articular surfaces that meet the radius and ulna at the elbow.

## answer_d
Immediately below the head is the surgical neck. Below the neck are the greater and lesser tuberosities, separated from each other by the bicipital groove

## explanation_d
Correct. Immediately below the rounded head of the humerus lies the anatomical neck, and immediately below that lies the surgical neck, a common fracture site named because it is the neck a surgeon most often has to treat. Below the surgical neck sit the greater tuberosity laterally and the lesser tuberosity anteriorly, separated from each other by the intertubercular (bicipital) groove, which carries the tendon of the long head of biceps brachii. This sequence, head, surgical neck, then the two tuberosities either side of the bicipital groove, is the humerus's own defining proximal landmark arrangement.
