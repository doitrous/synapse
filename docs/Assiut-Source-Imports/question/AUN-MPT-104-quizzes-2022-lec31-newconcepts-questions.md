## title
Blushing is active hyperemia

## question
A 22-year-old second-year medical student develops a "red" face after being asked a question during lecture. Which of the following statements best describes this vascular reaction?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
oedema

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Oedema is fluid accumulation in the interstitial space, not the vascular reddening described here.

## answer_b
Petechial hemorrhage

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Petechial hemorrhage is a pinpoint bleed into the skin, not a reversible reddening from vasodilation.

## answer_c
Active hyperemia

## explanation_c
Correct. Blushing results from arteriolar dilation increasing arterial inflow into facial skin, an active physiological process. This is the definition of active hyperemia. It is triggered here by an embarrassing moment, through sympathetic withdrawal and local vasodilator mechanisms, not by any pathological process.

## answer_d
Acute congestion

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Acute congestion refers to impaired venous outflow, a passive process, not the arteriolar-dilation-driven active process of blushing.

## answer_e
Passive hyperemia

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Passive hyperemia (congestion) is caused by impaired venous outflow, not by increased arterial inflow as seen here.

## title
Post-fracture leg swelling is phlebothrombosis

## question
A 60-year-old woman sustained fractures of the right femur, pelvis, and left humerus in a motor vehicle collision. The fractures were stabilized, and the patient's recovery was uneventful. During a physical examination 3 weeks later, the physician observes swelling and warmth in the left leg, and there is local pain and tenderness in the left thigh. Which of the following processes is most likely occurring in the femoral vein?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Phlebothrombosis

## explanation_a
Correct. Swelling, warmth, pain and tenderness in a limb weeks after immobilization from a fracture is the classic picture of phlebothrombosis -- thrombus formation in a non-inflamed deep vein. Immobilization promotes venous stasis, one arm of Virchow's triad, favouring clot formation in the stagnant femoral vein. This is the expected complication of prolonged bed rest after major fracture stabilization.

## answer_b
Mural thrombosis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Mural thrombosis describes a thrombus adherent to the wall of a chamber (typically cardiac), not the deep-vein process described here.

## answer_c
Chronic passive congestion

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Chronic passive congestion is a systemic pattern from right heart failure, not a localized limb finding from immobilization.

## answer_d
Vegetations

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Vegetations are thrombotic/infective masses on heart valves, unrelated to a limb-vein finding after fracture.

## title
Right heart failure causes chronic venous congestion

## question
Chronic general venous congestion is noticed in patients with:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Right sided heart failure

## explanation_a
Correct. Right-sided heart failure prevents the right heart from pumping venous return forward, so blood backs up throughout the systemic venous system. This produces chronic general (systemic) venous congestion, with findings such as hepatic and splenic congestion, dependent oedema and raised jugular venous pressure. This systemic, generalized pattern is what distinguishes right heart failure's effect from a localized cause of obstruction.

## answer_b
Compression by a tumor

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Compression by a tumour causes localized, not general/systemic, venous obstruction confined to the drainage territory it compresses.

## answer_c
Portal hypertension

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Portal hypertension causes congestion specifically in the portal venous system (e.g. splenomegaly, oesophageal varices), not general systemic venous congestion.

## answer_d
Enlarged lymph node

## explanation_d
Incorrect. An enlarged lymph node compresses local venous or lymphatic drainage, not the systemic venous circulation as a whole.

## subject
fnd

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The brain is a destination for a systemic arterial embolus (e.g. from the left heart or a carotid plaque), not a venous one.

## answer_b
Lung

## explanation_b
Correct. A thrombus that forms in the systemic venous circulation and detaches travels with venous blood flow back to the right heart and out into the pulmonary arterial circulation. Incorrect. The kidney is a destination for a systemic arterial embolus, not a venous one, which is filtered by the lung first.

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Bone is not the typical destination of any embolus type described in this option set, venous or arterial.

Phlebothrombosis forms in non-inflamed veins

fnd

## title
Virchow's triad concerns thrombus formation

## question
Virchow's triad is concerned with the formation of:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Thrombus

## explanation_a
Correct. Virchow's triad names the three broad categories of factors that predispose to thrombus formation: endothelial injury, stasis or turbulence of blood flow, and hypercoagulability of the blood. It is specifically a framework for explaining why a thrombus forms, not for embolism or hemorrhage. This is why thrombus, and not the other options here, is the target concept of the triad.

## answer_b
Ecchymosis

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Ecchymosis is a bruise -- extravasated blood in the skin/subcutaneous tissue -- not explained by Virchow's triad's risk factors.

## answer_c
Embolus

## explanation_c
Incorrect. An embolus is a thrombus fragment (or other material) that has already detached and travelled; Virchow's triad explains the initial thrombus formation, not the subsequent embolic event.

## answer_d
Haematoma

## explanation_d
Incorrect. A haematoma is a localized collection of extravasated blood, not the intravascular thrombus formation Virchow's triad addresses.
