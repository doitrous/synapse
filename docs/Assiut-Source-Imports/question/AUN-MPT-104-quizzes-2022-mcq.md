Cytopathology: diagnosis from isolated cells

## question
The diagnosis of disease by examination of isolated cells in body fluids is called:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
molecular pathology

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Molecular pathology examines a disease at the level of DNA, RNA or protein molecules, not isolated whole cells in a fluid sample -- a different scale of examination from what this question describes.

## answer_b
microbiology

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Microbiology studies microorganisms (bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites) themselves, not the patient's own cells shed into a body fluid -- a different subject of examination entirely.

## answer_c
histpoathology

## explanation_c
Incorrect. "Histpoathology" (histopathology) examines an intact tissue specimen removed by a surgeon, not isolated cells in a fluid sample -- the distinguishing fact here is tissue architecture versus individual cells.

## answer_d
immunology

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Immunology studies the immune system and its responses, not the diagnostic examination of isolated cells in body fluids.

## answer_e
cytopathology

## explanation_e
Correct. Cytopathology (cytological diagnosis) examines isolated cells shed or aspirated from the body -- exfoliated cells in sputum, urine or CSF, or a fine-needle aspirate -- rather than an intact tissue specimen. It is used for screening and rapid sampling, complementary to but distinct from histopathology, which instead examines a removed piece of tissue with its architecture preserved.

## title
Absorption as critical for therapeutic plasma concentration

## question
Which of the following steps is most critical to achieve a therapeutic drug concentration in plasma?

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Distribution

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Distribution moves an already-absorbed drug from blood into tissues; it presupposes the drug already reached the blood, which is exactly the step absorption governs and this question is asking about.

## answer_b
Elimination

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Elimination (excretion) removes drug from the body after it has acted; it determines how long a concentration lasts, not whether a therapeutic concentration is reached in the first place.

## answer_c
Metabolism

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Metabolism biotransforms a drug, usually reducing its active concentration; it happens after the drug has entered the body, not as the step that determines whether it gets there at all.

## answer_d
Absorption

## explanation_d
Correct. Absorption -- a drug's movement from the site of administration into the bloodstream -- is the pharmacokinetic step most critical for achieving a therapeutic plasma drug concentration. A drug that never reaches the blood in adequate amount cannot act, however well it later distributes, metabolises or is excreted. That is why absorption, not any of the three later phases, is singled out here.

## title
ADME: the pharmacokinetic phase sequence

## question
The most common sequence of pharmacokinetics phases a drug may pass through is:

## subject
pharm

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
administration, inhalation, absorption and excretion

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Administration and inhalation are ways a drug is given (a route), not pharmacokinetic phases themselves; the standard sequence begins only once the drug starts moving through the body, at absorption.

## answer_b
Absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion.

## explanation_b
Correct. The standard sequence of pharmacokinetic phases is absorption (entry into the bloodstream), distribution (movement into tissues), metabolism (biotransformation) and excretion (removal from the body) -- abbreviated ADME. This is the reference order taught for how a drug's course through the body is organised.

## answer_c
disintegration, absorption, elimination and expiration

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Disintegration (a pharmaceutical/formulation step) and expiration are not recognised pharmacokinetic phases; the standard sequence is absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion.

## answer_d
formulation, absorption, metabolism and excretion

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Formulation is how a drug product is manufactured, not a pharmacokinetic phase the drug passes through inside the body; substituting it for distribution breaks the correct ADME sequence.

Introduction to Pharmacology

## subtopic

## question
With reference to drug receptors, which of the following is true?

## subject

## vignette

C

## answer_a
Receptor-mediated responses to drugs usually remain constant over time, even in the continued presence of the agonist

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Receptor-mediated responses commonly change over time with continued agonist presence (e.g. receptor desensitisation or downregulation), rather than remaining constant -- the opposite of what this option claims.

## answer_b
Pure pharmacologic antagonists bind to receptors and directly alter the receptors' function

## explanation_b
Incorrect. By definition, a pure pharmacologic antagonist binds a receptor with affinity but has no efficacy of its own and does not directly alter the receptor's function -- it acts only by occupying the site and preventing an agonist from binding.

## answer_c
They include regulatory proteins, enzymes, transport proteins and structural proteins

## explanation_c
Correct. Receptor macromolecules are drawn from several structural classes, not exclusively dedicated signalling proteins. Regulatory proteins, enzymes, transport proteins and structural proteins can all serve as drug receptors. This is why "receptor" in pharmacology names a functional role, not one fixed molecular family.

## answer_d
The total number of receptors is unrelated to the maximal effect of a drug

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The total number of receptors available is directly related to the maximal effect a drug can produce (e.g. via receptor reserve and spare receptors) -- receptor number is not unrelated to maximal effect.
