## title
Recurrent hematuria with atypical urine cells and an ulcerated bladder mass indicates carcinoma

## question
A 62 years male patient has hematuria for many times in past week. Cytological examination of the urine showed atypical cells. Cystoscopy showed 5 cm ulcerated mass. Which of the following is best describe the lesion

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette
A 62-year-old man with recurrent hematuria; urine cytology shows atypical cells and cystoscopy shows a 5 cm ulcerated bladder mass.

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Carcinoma

## explanation_a
Correct. An ulcerated, infiltrating bladder mass causing recurrent hematuria with atypical (malignant-appearing) cells on urine cytology is the classic clinical and cytological picture of a malignant epithelial tumour -- carcinoma, most commonly transitional cell carcinoma of the bladder in this age group. The ulceration itself, a feature of unrestrained invasive growth destroying the overlying epithelium, points to malignancy rather than a benign process.

## answer_b
Sarcoma

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Sarcoma is a malignant mesenchymal, not epithelial, tumour; the urothelial lining atypia on cytology points to an epithelial-cell process.

## answer_c
Adenoma

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Adenoma is a benign glandular epithelial tumour; it would not typically present as an ulcerated infiltrating mass with atypical cells on cytology, features that indicate malignant behaviour.

## answer_d
Papilloma

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Papilloma is a benign tumour of non-glandular (transitional/urothelial) epithelium; a benign papilloma does not ulcerate or shed atypical malignant cells the way this lesion does.

## title
Grade I, stage I designations mean well-differentiated and localized

## question
Review of a series of surgical pathology reports indicates that a certain type of neoplasm is diagnosed as grade I on a scale of I to IV. Clinically, some of the patients with this neoplasm are found to have stage I disease. Which of the following is the best interpretation of a neoplasm with these designations?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette
Surgical pathology reports grade a neoplasm as grade I (of IV) and, clinically, some patients have stage I disease.

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Unlikely to be malignant

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Grade and stage are both applied to already-diagnosed malignant neoplasms; they describe how malignant it is (grade) and how far it has spread (stage), not whether it is malignant at all.

## answer_b
Well-differentiated and localized

## explanation_b
Correct. Histological grade (I of IV) reflects how closely the tumour cells resemble their tissue of origin -- grade I means well-differentiated, closely resembling normal tissue and generally slower-growing. Clinical stage (I) reflects the anatomical extent of spread -- stage I means the tumour is localized, without regional or distant spread. Together, grade I and stage I describe a well-differentiated, localized neoplasm.

## answer_c
Arising from epithelium

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Grade and stage say nothing about the specific tissue of origin (epithelial versus mesenchymal); a tumour of either lineage can be graded and staged this way.

## answer_d
Has an in situ component

## explanation_d
Incorrect. An in situ component (confined within the basement membrane, not yet invasive) is a distinct histological finding, not what grade I / stage I designations specifically indicate.

## title
Signet ring carcinoma is a type of glandular carcinoma

## question
Signet ring carcinoma is a type of:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Squamous cell carcinoma

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Squamous cell carcinoma arises from squamous (non-glandular) epithelium and does not characteristically show the mucin-filled signet-ring morphology.

## answer_b
Transitional cell carcinoma

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Transitional cell carcinoma arises from urothelial (transitional) epithelium, not the mucin-secreting glandular epithelium signet ring carcinoma derives from.

## answer_c
Glandular carcinoma

## explanation_c
Correct. Signet ring carcinoma is a variant of glandular carcinoma (adenocarcinoma) in which tumour cells accumulate intracytoplasmic mucin that pushes the nucleus to the periphery, giving the cell its characteristic signet-ring appearance under the microscope. It classically occurs in the stomach and is a subtype of adenocarcinoma, a glandular malignancy.

## answer_d
Basal cell carcinoma

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Basal cell carcinoma is a skin malignancy of basal keratinocytes, unrelated to the mucin-secreting glandular origin of signet ring carcinoma.

## title
The edges of a malignant ulcer are everted

## question
The edges of the malignant ulcer are:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Inverted

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Inverted edges are not the classic description of a malignant ulcer's margin.

## answer_b
Undermined

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Undermined edges are classically associated with a tuberculous ulcer, not a malignant one.

## answer_c
Sharp

## explanation_c
Incorrect. A sharp, punched-out edge is more typical of a syphilitic (gummatous) ulcer.

## answer_d
Everted

## explanation_d
Correct. The edge of a malignant ulcer is classically raised and everted (rolled outward). This reflects the unrestrained proliferative growth of the malignant epithelium at the ulcer margin, heaping up and turning outward over the ulcer base. It is a distinguishing clinical sign used to differentiate a malignant ulcer from benign or infective ulcer types with their own characteristic edge patterns (undermined in tuberculous, sharp/punched-out in syphilitic, sloping in a healing/traumatic ulcer).

## title
Stroma separating individual cells distinguishes sarcoma from carcinoma

## question
The most important histologic feature that differentiates sarcoma from carcinoma is

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Necrosis is more marked

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The degree of necrosis is not the specific histological feature used to distinguish sarcoma from carcinoma; both can show necrosis.

## answer_b
Stroma separates individual cells

## explanation_b
Correct. Sarcoma cells, arising from mesenchymal tissue, are typically dispersed as individual cells within an abundant connective-tissue stroma that separates them from one another. Carcinoma cells, arising from epithelium, instead grow cohesively in nests, sheets, glands or masses held together by cell junctions, with the stroma forming a supporting framework around groups of cells rather than separating individual tumour cells. This stromal relationship to individual cells is the key histological feature distinguishing the two.

## answer_c
Vascularity is more marked

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Degree of vascularity varies by tumour type and grade in both sarcomas and carcinomas; it is not the defining histological distinguishing feature between the two categories.

## answer_d
Criteria of malignancy are more prominent

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Both sarcomas and carcinomas show the general cytological criteria of malignancy (pleomorphism, hyperchromasia, mitoses); this does not distinguish one category from the other.
