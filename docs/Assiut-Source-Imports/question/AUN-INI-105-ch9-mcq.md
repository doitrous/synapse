Spurious parasite equated with coprozoic in this bank

## question
Spurious parasite?

## subject
inf

## status

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Coprozoic parasite

## explanation_a
Correct. This bank treats a spurious finding as equivalent to a coprozoic one. Both describe a foreign organism, or another species' ingested parasite stage, that passes through the alimentary tract and appears in a stool sample. Crucially, neither one ever truly infected the examined host.

## answer_b
Temporary parasite

## explanation_b
Incorrect. "Temporary" describes the duration/pattern of an ectoparasite's host contact (feeding and leaving), not a non-infecting stool finding.

## answer_c
Incidental parasite

## explanation_c
Incorrect. An incidental parasite is a genuine infection of an unusual host species, unlike a spurious finding, which is not a true infection at all.

## answer_d
None of the above

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Since option A is the printed correct answer in this bank's own teaching, "none of the above" is wrong.

Sexual stages

Correct. The definitive (final) host harbours the parasite's adult, sexually reproducing stage. This is the standard, defining feature of the definitive host role in a parasite's life cycle. No other host role in the cycle carries this sexual-stage function.

Asexual stages

Incorrect. Asexual multiplication stages are more typically associated with an intermediate host, not the definitive host.

Larval stage

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Larval stages develop in the intermediate host, not the definitive host.

## answer_d
Immature stage

## explanation_d
Incorrect. An immature stage is, by definition, not the adult/sexual stage that characterises the definitive host.

## title
Eggs are the main pathology agent in schistosomiasis

## question
The main agent of Pathology in Schistosomiasis is?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Eggs

## explanation_a
Correct. Eggs are the main agent of pathology in schistosomiasis. Eggs trapped in tissue, whether intestinal, bladder, or hepatic, provoke a granulomatous inflammatory reaction around each one. This repeated reaction is what drives the disease's characteristic fibrosis and organ damage over time.

## answer_b
Living adult worms

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Living adult worms are comparatively well tolerated by the host immune system, having evolved mechanisms to evade it, so they cause relatively little direct damage while alive.

## answer_c
Dead adult worms

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Dead adult worms are not the main driver of chronic pathology either -- the ongoing, repeated egg-driven granulomatous response is what accounts for most disease.

## answer_d
All of the above

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Since living and dead adult worms are not the main pathology drivers, "all of the above" incorrectly elevates them to the same importance as the eggs.

Eating infected liver

Incorrect. Eating an animal's raw infected liver is a route associated with other, unrelated liver-fluke-type exposures, not with Fasciolopsis buski, which is an intestinal, not hepatic, fluke.

## title
Schistosomulum is a cercaria without a tail

## question
Schistosomulum of schistosoma is?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
A small cercaria

## explanation_a
Incorrect. The schistosomulum is not merely a smaller version of the cercaria -- it is a distinct developmental stage produced after tail loss, not just a size difference.

## answer_b
Cercaria without tail

## explanation_b
Correct. When a Schistosoma cercaria penetrates human skin, it sheds its forked tail. The resulting stage is called the schistosomulum, the same organism now without its swimming tail. It has begun adapting to survive inside the host rather than in water.

## answer_c
Hatching from egg

## explanation_c
Incorrect. The stage hatching from the egg is the miracidium, an earlier larval stage that infects the snail, not the schistosomulum.

## answer_d
Cercaria in the snail

## explanation_d
Incorrect. The cercaria develops and is released from the snail (the first intermediate host); the schistosomulum specifically names the post-skin-penetration, tail-less stage in the human host, not a stage inside the snail.

## title
Trematodes require a snail as intermediate host

## question
Trematodes?

## subject
inf

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Require snail as the intermediate host

## explanation_a
Correct. As a general rule, trematodes require a snail as their intermediate host. This generalisation holds across the group as a whole. The exact snail genus involved differs by species, but the snail-dependency itself does not.

## answer_b
Have operculated eggs for all species

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Not every trematode species has operculated eggs -- Schistosoma is the well-known exception, with non-operculate, spined eggs instead.

## answer_c
Always produce fork-tailed cercaria in their life-cycle

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Not every trematode produces a fork-tailed (furcocercous) cercaria -- that tail shape is specifically characteristic of Schistosoma's cercariae, not universal across the group.

## answer_d
Possess parencyhmal cells in the body cavity

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Trematodes are acoelomate flatworms with no true body cavity at all -- their internal spaces are filled with parenchymal tissue instead, so describing them as having "parenchymal cells in the body cavity" misstates their basic body plan.
