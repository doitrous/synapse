## title
What histamine does in inflammation

## question
Histamine is:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
A delayed mediator of type I hypersensitivity

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Histamine is an immediate, not a delayed, mediator of type I hypersensitivity -- it is released within seconds of mast-cell degranulation, producing the immediate-phase reaction, not a later one.

## answer_b
Released from neutrophils

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Histamine is stored in and released chiefly from mast cells and basophils, not from neutrophils.

## answer_c
One of the arachidonic acid derivatives

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Histamine is a biogenic (vasoactive) amine, not an arachidonic acid derivative -- prostaglandins and leukotrienes are the arachidonic acid derivatives, made through a different biosynthetic pathway entirely.

## answer_d
Is the cause of increased vascular permeability

## explanation_d
Correct. Histamine is the principal chemical mediator responsible for the increase in vascular permeability seen in acute inflammation. It acts on venular endothelium to open gaps that let protein-rich plasma escape into the interstitium as an exudate.

## title
Why the inflammatory exudate is protein-rich

## question
In acute inflammation the exudate has a higher protein content than normal tissue fluid chiefly because:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Capillary walls are more permeable

## explanation_a
Correct. The chief reason the inflammatory exudate is protein-rich is a chemical-mediator-driven increase in capillary and venule wall permeability. This lets plasma proteins, which normally stay confined to the vasculature, leak into the interstitial tissue.

## answer_b
Breakdown of tissue cells release protein

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Breakdown of local tissue cells is not the chief source of the exudate's protein content -- the dominant mechanism is plasma protein leaking through more permeable vessel walls, not protein released from dying cells.

## answer_c
Plasma cells secret gamma globulins

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Plasma cells secreting gamma globulins is a feature of a specific chronic immune response, not the general mechanism explaining why an acute inflammatory exudate is protein-rich.

## answer_d
Intracapillary pressure is raised

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Raised intracapillary (hydrostatic) pressure alone would produce a low-protein transudate, not a protein-rich exudate -- it is increased permeability, not increased pressure, that lets protein specifically escape.

## title
First vascular response to injury

## question
What is the first response of arterioles to injury in acute inflammation?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
E

## answer_a
Hyperemia

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Hyperaemia (increased blood flow) is a consequence of the vasodilatation that follows the very first, transient response, not the first response itself.

## answer_b
Edema

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Oedema results from increased vascular permeability, a later step in the vascular sequence, not the arterioles' first response to injury.

## answer_c
Vasodilatation

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Vasodilatation is real and important, but it follows, rather than precedes, a brief initial vasoconstriction -- it is the second step, not the first.

## answer_d
Redness

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Redness is a visible consequence of the hyperaemia that follows vasodilatation, several steps after the arterioles' actual first response.

## answer_e
Vasoconstriction

## explanation_e
Correct. The very first vascular response of arterioles to injury is a brief, transient vasoconstriction, lasting only seconds. Vasodilatation and increased permeability follow only after this initial reflex passes.

## title
The factor that forms inflammatory fluid exudate

## question
Which factor is involved in the formation of inflammatory fluid exudate:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Decreased interstitial osmotic pressure

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Interstitial osmotic pressure actually rises, not falls, as protein leaks into the tissue -- and even that rise is a consequence of the real driver, increased permeability, not an independent cause of exudate formation.

## answer_b
Increased vascular permeability

## explanation_b
Correct. Increased vascular permeability is produced by chemical mediators such as histamine. It is the key factor that lets protein-rich plasma escape from the vasculature into the interstitial tissue as an inflammatory exudate.

## answer_c
Vasoconstriction

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Vasoconstriction is the brief initial vascular response and, if anything, would reduce blood flow to the area, not drive fluid exudate formation.

## answer_d
Decreased intravascular hydrostatic pressure

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Intravascular hydrostatic pressure actually rises (from vasodilatation and increased blood flow), and even a fall in hydrostatic pressure would not explain a protein-rich exudate -- that specifically requires increased permeability.

## title
A feature of acute inflammation

## question
Which of the following is a feature of acute inflammation?

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Slowly progressive tissue necrosis and fibrosis

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Slowly progressive necrosis and fibrosis describes chronic, not acute, inflammation -- acute inflammation instead runs a rapid, short-lived course.

## answer_b
Granuloma formation

## explanation_b
Incorrect. Granuloma formation is a feature of a specific pattern of chronic inflammation, not of acute inflammation.

## answer_c
Hyperaemia and localized oedema

## explanation_c
Correct. Hyperaemia, increased blood flow from vasodilatation, together with localised oedema from increased vascular permeability, is the classic vascular picture of acute inflammation. Both together are direct, expected consequences of the vascular response already established for this lecture.

## answer_d
Inflammatory exudate is rich in lymphocytes and plasma cells

## explanation_d
Incorrect. An acute inflammatory exudate is rich in neutrophils, the first responding leukocyte, not lymphocytes and plasma cells, which instead characterise chronic inflammation.

## answer_e
Leucopenia

## explanation_e
Incorrect. Acute inflammation typically produces leucocytosis (a rise in circulating white cells), not leucopenia, as the bone marrow responds to the acute stimulus.

## title
Definition of chemotaxis

## question
In acute inflammation, the directed movement of leukocytes towards a particular organism (irritant) is called:

## subject
fnd

## status
Draft

## owner
Claude

## vignette

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Margination

## explanation_a
Incorrect. Margination is leukocytes lining up along the vessel wall, an earlier step that precedes their emigration and directed movement to the injury site -- not the directed movement itself.

## answer_b
Chemotaxis

## explanation_b
Correct. Chemotaxis is the directed, unidirectional locomotion of leukocytes along a chemical gradient toward a particular organism or irritant. It is what brings phagocytic cells from the vasculature to the actual site of injury or infection.

## answer_c
Exudation

## explanation_c
Incorrect. Exudation is the escape of protein-rich fluid from vessels into tissue, a fluid movement, not a description of directed leukocyte movement.

## answer_d
Transudation

## explanation_d
Incorrect. Transudation is the escape of low-protein fluid from vessels driven by a hydrostatic/osmotic imbalance, again a fluid movement, not directed leukocyte movement.
