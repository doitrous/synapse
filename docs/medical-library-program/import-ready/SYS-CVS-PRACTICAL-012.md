### The line at the inner edge of the media
Concept: CON-CVS-D3B69AE2526F7C
Difficulty: Easy
Q: A wavy refractile line lies immediately deep to the endothelium of a medium-sized artery. What is it, and what does it mark?
*= The internal elastic lamina, marking the boundary between intima and media
Why: Correct. It sits at the inner edge of the media, and its wavy outline in a fixed specimen comes from the vessel contracting after death.
* The external elastic lamina, marking the boundary with the adventitia
Why: The external lamina is at the outer edge of the media. Confusing the two is the classic error when orienting an arterial section.
* The endothelial basement membrane, thickened enough to be visible
Why: The basement membrane is present but far too thin to appear as a distinct refractile line at light microscopy.
* A fold of intima produced by the fixation process
Why: Fixation artefact is a real consideration, but this is a constant structure, not an artefact.
Explanation: The internal elastic lamina is the landmark that finds the media's inner edge. Everything else about the section is oriented from it.

### Naming the type from the wall
Concept: CON-CVS-C2107E5882448C
Difficulty: Easy
Q: The same vessel has a media of concentric smooth muscle between two elastic laminae. Which class of artery is it?
*= A muscular artery, which is what medium-sized arteries usually are
Why: Correct. Below the largest arteries the media is dominated by smooth muscle, and these are the distributing vessels.
* An elastic artery, whose media is filled with elastic sheets
Why: Elastic arteries are the largest, nearest the heart, and their media is elastic lamellae rather than muscle.
* An arteriole, whose wall is one or two muscle layers thick
Why: Arterioles are far smaller and have no distinct elastic laminae to bracket the media.
* A large vein, whose wall is thin relative to its lumen
Why: A vein's wall is thin and its lumen wide, and it has no prominent internal elastic lamina.
Explanation: Size predicts type, and the arrangement of elastic tissue confirms it: sheets throughout means elastic, two laminae bracketing muscle means muscular.

### The line at the outer edge
Concept: CON-CVS-3B6540CCDC9C20
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A second, fainter elastic layer lies at the outer boundary of that media. What is it, and what lies beyond?
*= The external elastic lamina, with the tunica adventitia beyond it
Why: Correct. It separates media from adventitia and is typically thinner and less conspicuous than the internal lamina.
* The internal elastic lamina, with the intima beyond it
Why: Reverses the orientation of the vessel; the intima is on the luminal side.
* The basement membrane of the outermost muscle cells
Why: Individual muscle cells have basement membranes, but they do not form a single visible lamina.
* The start of the perivascular nerve plexus
Why: Nerves do run in the adventitia, but they are not an elastic layer and do not form this boundary.
Explanation: Two laminae bracket the media of a muscular artery. Naming which is which identifies all three tunicae and orients the whole field.

### The lining continuous with every vessel
Concept: CON-CVS-FDFA40F0021FAC
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A section of ventricular wall shows a simple squamous layer lining the chamber. What is it continuous with?
*= The endothelium lining the blood vessels — endocardial endothelium is part of one continuous lining
Why: Correct. The endocardium is lined by endothelium continuous with that of the great vessels, so the whole circulation has a single unbroken endothelial surface.
* The mesothelium of the serous pericardium
Why: Mesothelium covers the outer surface of the heart, not the inside of the chamber.
* The epithelium of the respiratory tract, through the pulmonary veins
Why: Two different epithelia meeting at the lung do not make one continuous lining.
* Nothing — the endocardium is a distinct epithelium confined to the heart
Why: The continuity is the point: it is why an endocardial surface behaves like a vascular one.
Explanation: One continuous endothelial sheet from the endocardium through every vessel and back. That continuity is why damage to it matters for thrombosis wherever it occurs.

### What the wall is for
Concept: CON-CVS-182A0AD30A3730
Difficulty: Hard
Q: Why does the aorta have an elastic media while a muscular artery does not?
*= The aorta stores the ejected volume and recoils in diastole, smoothing pulsatile flow; a muscular artery distributes and regulates it
Why: Correct. Elastic recoil converts intermittent ejection towards continuous flow, which is a job only the large arteries near the heart can do. Muscular arteries distribute blood and adjust its regional supply.
* The aorta must resist a higher mean pressure, and elastic tissue is stronger
Why: Mean pressure is similar along the large arteries, and elasticity is about compliance rather than tensile strength.
* Muscular arteries need elastic tissue less because blood flows more slowly in them
Why: Flow velocity is not what determines the wall's composition, and it is highest in the aorta in any case.
* The aorta must actively contract to propel blood onwards
Why: The recoil is passive elastic return; elastic arteries have relatively little smooth muscle to contract with.
Explanation: Structure follows function along the tree: elastic near the heart to smooth the pulse, muscular further out to distribute and regulate, and a single layer of muscle at the arteriole to set resistance.

### The unbroken wall
Concept: CON-CVS-2A37D5DDEB19DB
Difficulty: Easy
Q: An electron micrograph shows a capillary whose endothelium is unbroken, with no pores. Where is this type found?
*= In muscle, nervous tissue, skin and lung, where a controlled barrier matters more than bulk exchange
Why: Correct. Continuous capillaries have an uninterrupted endothelium and are the type found in these tissues.
* In the intestinal mucosa and endocrine glands
Why: Those are sites of fenestrated capillaries, where fluid and small solutes must cross rapidly.
* In the liver, spleen and bone marrow
Why: Those have discontinuous sinusoids, with gaps large enough to pass cells.
* Only in the renal glomerulus
Why: The glomerulus is the most heavily fenestrated capillary bed in the body, not a continuous one.
Explanation: Capillary structure follows the organ's job. A continuous wall is the default where a controlled barrier matters, which is why it is the type in brain, muscle and lung.

### The perforated wall
Concept: CON-CVS-047FC0A529AFBA
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A second micrograph shows endothelial cells perforated by pores. What does that predict about the tissue?
*= It exchanges fluid and small solutes in bulk — intestinal mucosa, endocrine gland or kidney
Why: Correct. Fenestrations are found where large volumes of fluid and small molecules must cross quickly.
* It is a tissue with a very high oxygen demand, such as exercising muscle
Why: Gases cross a continuous endothelium readily by diffusion, so a high oxygen demand needs no fenestrations.
* It is a tissue requiring an especially tight barrier, such as brain
Why: The opposite: cerebral capillaries are continuous with particularly tight junctions.
* It is inflamed, and the pores are a response to injury
Why: Inflammation opens gaps between cells at the postcapillary venule. Fenestrations are a normal structural feature, not an inflammatory change.
Explanation: Fenestrations are for fluid and small solutes, not for gases. Recognising that tells you which organs will have them: those that secrete, absorb or filter in bulk.

### The most permeable of all
Concept: CON-CVS-740D8837FD8BFA
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: The glomerular capillary filters at a remarkable rate. What is the structural basis?
*= Exceptionally numerous fenestrae in its endothelium
Why: Correct. The glomerular endothelium is extensively fenestrated, which is a major part of why filtration there is so rapid.
* An absent endothelium, so plasma meets the basement membrane directly
Why: The endothelium is present as a continuous cell layer; it is perforated, not absent.
* Large intercellular gaps of the discontinuous type
Why: Discontinuous gaps would pass cells and large proteins, which the glomerulus specifically retains.
* Pericyte contraction opening transient channels through the wall
Why: Pericytes adjust calibre and so flow; they do not create the permeability pathway.
Explanation: The clearest example in the body of structure predicting function — fenestration in proportion to the filtration required. It is also where a cardiovascular slide connects straight to renal physiology.

### The cell that tunes the capillary
Concept: CON-CVS-CC810A201244F0
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A cell process lies against the endothelium, inside the same basement membrane. What does its presence allow?
*= Local regulation of capillary calibre, and so of flow through that capillary
Why: Correct. Pericytes contract and relax, giving control of perfusion at the level of the individual capillary rather than only at the arteriole upstream.
* Production of the plasma proteins that hold fluid in the vessel
Why: Plasma proteins are made in the liver, not by a local structural cell.
* Formation of the tight junctions that set permeability
Why: Junctions are between endothelial cells. Pericytes influence the barrier but do not constitute it.
* Nothing functional — they are inert supporting cells
Why: The older teaching that all capillary control is upstream. Pericytes add a second, local level of control.
Explanation: Perfusion is controlled at two levels: the arteriole feeding the bed, and the pericytes on the capillaries themselves. Both matter when a bed stops being recruitable.

### Who opens the door to the bed
Concept: CON-CVS-A724F66042649D
Difficulty: Hard
Q: Which vessel exerts the greatest control over how much blood reaches a capillary bed, and why is it suited to the job?
*= The arteriole, because its thick muscular wall relative to a narrow lumen makes small changes in calibre produce large changes in resistance
Why: Correct. Resistance varies steeply with radius, so a vessel with abundant muscle around a small lumen is the effective control point for flow into a bed.
* The capillary, because it is the vessel the blood must finally pass through
Why: Capillaries have no muscle and cannot regulate their own inflow; their perfusion is set upstream and modulated locally by pericytes.
* The muscular artery, because it carries the most blood towards the tissue
Why: It distributes blood regionally, but it is too proximal and too wide to be the fine control point.
* The postcapillary venule, because it determines the pressure downstream
Why: Downstream tone does influence capillary pressure and filtration, but inflow control is arteriolar.
Explanation: Resistance is exquisitely sensitive to radius, which is why the vessel with the most muscle around the smallest lumen is the one that decides how much blood a tissue receives.

### The vessel that skips the bed
Concept: CON-CVS-3797C808979141
Difficulty: Easy
Q: A section of fingertip skin shows a thick-walled vessel connecting an arteriole directly to a venule. What is it?
*= An arteriovenous anastomosis
Why: Correct. These are direct connections between arterioles and venules; when open, blood bypasses the capillary bed of that region.
* A capillary loop cut obliquely, giving a false impression of continuity
Why: Oblique sectioning is a genuine pitfall, but a thick muscular wall on a direct arteriole-to-venule connection is not an artefact.
* A lymphatic vessel draining the dermis
Why: Lymphatics drain the interstitium; they do not connect an arteriole to a venule.
* A vasa vasorum supplying the venule wall
Why: Vasa vasorum supply the walls of large vessels, not a bypass between two microvessels.
Explanation: These shunts are numerous in the skin of fingers, toes and ears, and they are how those regions conserve or lose heat — open, and blood bypasses the exchange bed entirely.

### Why an active tissue draws more blood
Concept: CON-CVS-CAFCFCE5767545
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: In a working muscle, what increases capillary perfusion locally?
*= Accumulating metabolites relax the supplying vessels, recruiting more capillaries and increasing flow
Why: Correct. Local metabolites act on the vessels feeding the bed, matching supply to demand exactly where the demand is.
* The brainstem selectively withdraws sympathetic tone from that muscle
Why: Central control cannot resolve which bed within a muscle is working. This is the misconception that all vascular control is neural.
* Circulating adrenaline is released into that muscle's supply specifically
Why: A circulating hormone reaches everywhere at once and cannot be targeted to one active bed.
* Blood viscosity falls locally, so flow rises passively
Why: Viscosity changes are minor and are not the mechanism of local metabolic control.
Explanation: Local metabolic control matches perfusion to demand tissue by tissue. It is also what fails in shock, when the bed stops responding to its own signals.

### Where the fluid and the cells leave
Concept: CON-CVS-08AA7F26A9BD28
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: In inflammation, across which vessel do fluid and leucocytes principally leave the circulation, and why there?
*= The postcapillary venule, whose wall is endothelium, basement membrane and pericytes with no muscular coat
Why: Correct. Its thin wall is where mediators open the endothelium and where leucocytes cross.
* The arteriole, because it carries the highest pressure into the bed
Why: Pressure is highest upstream, which is why this is chosen, but the muscular arteriolar wall does not leak.
* The muscular artery, because it delivers the greatest flow
Why: Its thick wall permits no exchange at all.
* The large vein, because flow through it is slowest
Why: Slow flow favours exchange, but a large vein's wall is far too thick for it.
Explanation: Exchange and emigration need a thin wall and an unhurried flow. The postcapillary venule is where both conditions hold, whatever the pressure upstream.

### Where the blood is waiting
Concept: CON-CVS-5692E7C9DCE9F5
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Which part of the circulation holds most of the blood volume at any moment, and what makes it suited to that?
*= The systemic veins, because their thin distensible walls give them high compliance
Why: Correct. The systemic veins hold the largest share of the circulating volume, and their compliance is what lets them accept and release it with little change in pressure.
* The systemic arteries, because they are under the highest pressure
Why: The commonest error here — equating pressure with volume. The arteries hold a small fraction of the total.
* The capillaries, because they are the most numerous vessels
Why: Numerous but individually tiny; their combined volume is small.
* The pulmonary circulation, which acts as the body's reservoir
Why: The pulmonary circulation holds a modest, fairly fixed volume, not the main reservoir.
Explanation: Most of the blood is venous, at low pressure, in a highly compliant reservoir. That single fact underlies venous return, the response to haemorrhage, and the effect of posture.

### Two ways to change what a tissue receives
Concept: CON-CVS-5D4C49C48AA325
Difficulty: Challenging
Q: A tissue can alter its perfusion at the arteriole and at the capillary. What does the pericyte contribute that the arteriole cannot, and what follows for measuring perfusion?
*= Control of individual capillaries within the bed, so total flow into a tissue can be adequate while the distribution within it is not
Why: Correct. The arteriole sets how much blood enters the tissue; pericytes influence which capillaries carry it. Because the two act at different levels, measured flow into a tissue does not guarantee that exchange is happening throughout it.
* Nothing the arteriole cannot do — pericytes simply reinforce arteriolar tone
Why: Treats two levels of control as one, which loses the distinction the question turns on.
* Control of the total flow into the tissue, which the arteriole cannot regulate
Why: Reverses the two. Total inflow is the arteriole's job.
* Control of capillary permeability rather than calibre, which changes exchange directly
Why: Permeability is a property of the endothelium and its junctions; pericytes act on calibre.
Explanation: Perfusion has two questions — how much blood enters, and where within the tissue it goes. That is why a warm, well-perfused-looking limb can still be exchanging poorly, and why flow alone is a poor measure of tissue oxygenation.

### The silence at the top
Concept: CON-CVS-BE94FBA0B205EF
Difficulty: Easy
Q: With the cuff inflated above systolic pressure, nothing is heard. Why?
*= The artery is fully occluded, so there is no flow to become turbulent
Why: Correct. Korotkoff sounds come from turbulent flow through a partly compressed artery; with no flow at all there is no sound.
* The artery is fully open, so flow is laminar and silent
Why: That is the silence *below* diastolic pressure, at the other end of the measurement.
* The cuff pressure damps the sound without stopping the flow
Why: Would predict quiet sounds rather than none, and misses that occlusion is complete at these pressures.
* The stethoscope is not over the artery
Why: A placement error gives silence throughout deflation, not silence confined to the top.
Explanation: There are two silences in a blood pressure measurement and they mean opposite things — no flow above systolic, smooth flow below diastolic. Sound exists only in between.

### The cuff that overstates
Concept: CON-CVS-85F9DE6C65F2F7
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A cuff is applied loosely. What does that do to the reading, and why?
*= It reads falsely high, because more cuff pressure is needed to reach and occlude the artery
Why: Correct. A loose cuff transmits its pressure to the limb inefficiently, so a higher cuff pressure is required and the recorded value overstates the true one.
* It reads falsely low, because less tissue is compressed
Why: Reasons from the tissue compressed rather than the pressure actually reaching the artery.
* It affects only the diastolic value, leaving systolic accurate
Why: Both readings depend on the cuff pressure at which the artery changes state, so both shift.
* It has no effect provided the cuff is the right width
Why: Width and fit are separate variables; a correctly sized cuff applied loosely still misreads.
Explanation: The measurement assumes cuff pressure reaches the artery. Anything breaking that assumption shifts the reading upwards, and it is a technique error rather than a finding.

### Which nerves carry the report
Concept: CON-CVS-61D2DE3408AC86
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: The aortic-arch baroreceptors sense the pressure being measured. By which nerves do they report to the brainstem?
*= The aortic nerve, which joins the vagus
Why: Correct. Aortic-arch afferents run in the aortic nerve, which travels with and joins the vagus.
* The glossopharyngeal nerve
Why: That carries the *carotid sinus* afferents. Students who learn one baroreceptor pathway commonly apply it to both.
* The sympathetic chain
Why: The chain carries the efferent limb of the reflex, not the afferent signal from the receptors.
* The recurrent laryngeal nerve, which loops around the arch
Why: It does loop around the arch, which is why proximity makes it tempting — but it is motor to the larynx.
Explanation: Two receptor sites, two afferent nerves, and a separate efferent limb. Keeping afferent and efferent apart is what makes the reflex reasonable rather than memorised.

### What the sensor is sensing
Concept: CON-CVS-A302CAFBD5028C
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What stimulus do the arterial baroreceptors actually respond to?
*= Stretch of the vessel wall, which varies with the pressure inside it
Why: Correct. They are mechanoreceptors: pressure is inferred from wall stretch, which is why arterial stiffness alters their behaviour.
* The concentration of oxygen in arterial blood
Why: That is the chemoreceptor stimulus, and those are different receptors in a similar location.
* The velocity of blood flowing past them
Why: Flow velocity is not the stimulus, and it can change independently of pressure.
* The heart rate, transmitted through the pulse
Why: Rate is one consequence of their output rather than the stimulus they detect.
Explanation: They report stretch, and stretch stands in for pressure. That is why a rigid, atherosclerotic artery blunts the reflex even when the pressure inside it is changing normally.

### Putting the number together
Concept: CON-CVS-AEBDDFAB03B5DF
Difficulty: Hard
Q: A patient's mean arterial pressure is unchanged after treatment, but their cardiac output has risen. What must have happened, and what does that show about the measurement?
*= Total peripheral resistance must have fallen, since mean pressure is the product of output and resistance — so an unchanged pressure can hide large changes in both
Why: Correct. With mean pressure as the product of the two, a rise in one and an unchanged product requires a fall in the other. A stable blood pressure is therefore not evidence of a stable circulation.
* Nothing must have happened; pressure and output are independent quantities
Why: They are directly related through resistance, which is what makes the inference possible.
* Stroke volume must have fallen to offset the rise in output
Why: Stroke volume is a component of output, not a separate offsetting term.
* The measurement must be in error, since output and pressure always move together
Why: They frequently move apart, and assuming otherwise is exactly the misreading this question exists to correct.
Explanation: Two terms, one product. Reading a blood pressure as a statement about the whole circulation ignores that a fall in resistance can conceal a large change in output, and the reverse.
