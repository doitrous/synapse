## summary
A cell spends most of its life growing and copying its genome, and only a short part of it dividing. The checkpoints between those stages are what stop a damaged genome being passed on.
## sections
### Definition
The cell cycle is the sequence of events in the life of the cell from the moment it is created until it divides itself. It has two unequal parts: interphase, where the cell grows and replicates its DNA, and mitosis, where it separates the two copies.
### Mechanism
Interphase runs G1 → S → G2. In G1 the cell grows and decides whether to commit. In S it replicates the entire genome, so every chromosome becomes two identical sister chromatids. In G2 it grows further and checks the copy.

Mitosis then separates the chromatids — prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase — and cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm into two cells.
### Key determinants
Progression is not automatic. Checkpoints at the end of G1, at the end of G2, and during mitosis each ask a different question: is the cell big enough and the DNA undamaged, was replication complete and correct, and is every chromosome correctly attached to the spindle.

A cell that fails a checkpoint arrests. If the damage cannot be repaired it is directed to apoptosis rather than allowed to divide.
### Clinical significance
Cancer is, mechanically, a failure of this control. Loss of checkpoint function lets a cell with a damaged genome divide, and the damage accumulates in its descendants.

The cycle is also why cytotoxic chemotherapy affects healthy tissue: bone marrow, gut lining and hair follicles divide rapidly, and a drug that targets dividing cells cannot distinguish them from a tumour.
### Common misconceptions
Interphase is not a resting phase. It is where the cell grows and replicates its entire genome, and it occupies most of the cycle.
## hold_these
The cell cycle is the sequence of events from a cell's creation until it divides.
Interphase (G1, S, G2) is most of the cycle; mitosis is the short separating step.
Checkpoints at G1, G2 and mitosis arrest a cell whose genome or spindle is not sound.
A cell that cannot repair the fault is directed to apoptosis rather than allowed to divide.
## lose_the_mark
Calling interphase a resting phase. The genome is replicated there, and it takes most of the cycle.
Assuming chemotherapy targets tumour cells specifically. It targets dividing cells, which is why marrow, gut and hair are affected.

## summary
Most signalling molecules never enter the cell. A receptor in the membrane binds one on the outside and produces a different signal on the inside — which is what makes the membrane a place where information crosses, not just solutes.
## sections
### Definition
A cell-surface receptor is a membrane protein that binds an extracellular signal molecule. When it binds a ligand it becomes activated and generates a cascade of intracellular signals that alter the behaviour of the cell.

The ligand itself stays outside. What crosses is information, not the molecule.
### Mechanism
Binding changes the receptor's shape, and that shape change is the switch. What follows depends on the receptor family: an ion-channel receptor opens a pore; an enzyme-linked receptor activates a catalytic domain on its inner face; a G-protein-coupled receptor activates an associated G protein, which in turn activates an enzyme that makes a second messenger.

Each step can act on many molecules, so the signal is amplified as it descends.
### Key determinants
Affinity decides which ligand binds, and receptor density decides how strongly a cell responds to a given concentration. A cell can therefore change its own sensitivity without the signal changing at all — which is what up-regulation and down-regulation mean.

Selectivity is a property of the receptor, not the messenger: the same signal can produce opposite effects in two tissues carrying different receptor subtypes.
### Clinical significance
Most drugs act at receptors, and the vocabulary of pharmacology is built on this: an agonist binds and activates, an antagonist binds and blocks without activating.

Receptor sensitivity also explains tolerance and rebound. A receptor that has been chronically stimulated is down-regulated, so withdrawal leaves the cell under-responsive; a receptor chronically blocked is up-regulated, so sudden withdrawal produces an exaggerated response.
### Common misconceptions
The receptor does not carry the ligand inside. Binding changes the receptor, and the receptor generates a separate internal signal.
## hold_these
A cell-surface receptor binds an extracellular signal and generates intracellular signals that change the cell's behaviour.
The ligand stays outside; what crosses the membrane is information.
Selectivity belongs to the receptor, so one signal can have opposite effects in different tissues.
Chronic stimulation down-regulates receptors and chronic blockade up-regulates them.
## lose_the_mark
Describing the receptor as carrying its ligand into the cell. It does not; it changes shape and signals internally.
Attributing a tissue-specific effect to the hormone. The hormone is the same everywhere — the receptor subtype is what differs.

## summary
A hormone that never enters the cell still changes what the cell does, because the receptor it binds makes a small molecule inside — and makes a great many of them.
## sections
### Definition
Small intracellular signalling molecules are called second messengers, the first messengers being the extracellular signals. They are generated in response to receptor activation and carry the signal onward inside the cell.
### Mechanism
An activated receptor switches on an enzyme, and that enzyme makes many messenger molecules from a plentiful precursor. Cyclic AMP is made from ATP by adenylyl cyclase; IP3 and diacylglycerol are cut from a membrane phospholipid by phospholipase C; calcium is not synthesised at all but released from stores into the cytosol.

Each messenger then activates its own targets, typically protein kinases, which phosphorylate many substrate proteins in turn.
### Key determinants
Amplification is the point. One bound receptor activates many enzyme molecules, each of which makes many messengers, each of which activates many kinases — so a few hormone molecules outside can change the activity of a whole cell.

Termination matters as much as generation. A messenger is degraded or pumped away continuously, so the signal stops when the stimulus stops rather than persisting.
### Clinical significance
Because messengers are shared between pathways, a drug acting on one affects everything that uses it — which explains both broad therapeutic effects and broad unwanted ones.

Cholera is the clearest demonstration of the principle: the toxin locks the G protein that activates adenylyl cyclase into its active state, cyclic AMP rises without limit in the intestinal epithelium, and the resulting secretion is the diarrhoea.
### Common misconceptions
Amplification is not a detail of the mechanism, it is the reason the mechanism exists. Without it, the number of hormone molecules available could not produce a cell-wide response.
## hold_these
Second messengers are small intracellular molecules generated when a receptor is activated; the extracellular signal is the first messenger.
Cyclic AMP, IP3, diacylglycerol and calcium are the common examples.
One bound receptor yields many messenger molecules — the signal is amplified at every step.
A messenger is continuously removed, so the response stops when the stimulus stops.
## lose_the_mark
Treating amplification as incidental. It is why a few hormone molecules can change a whole cell.
Forgetting that calcium is released from stores rather than synthesised, unlike the other messengers.

## summary
Cells that are no longer needed, or are too damaged to repair, dismantle themselves in an orderly way that leaves the surrounding tissue undisturbed. Too little of this causes cancer; too much causes degeneration.
## sections
### Definition
Programmed cell death is more commonly called apoptosis. It is an active, energy-requiring process in which a cell dismantles itself using machinery it already carries, and the fragments are cleared by neighbouring cells without provoking inflammation.
### Mechanism
The executioners are the caspases: proteases that have a cysteine at their active site and cleave their target proteins at specific aspartic acids. They exist as inactive precursors, and activating one activates the next, so a small trigger produces a decisive, irreversible cascade.

Two routes reach them. The intrinsic pathway responds to internal damage: the mitochondrion releases cytochrome c, which assembles the machinery that activates the caspases. The extrinsic pathway responds to an external instruction, delivered by a death ligand binding its receptor at the cell surface.
### Key determinants
Whether a damaged cell repairs or dies is decided by the balance between pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic proteins at the mitochondrial membrane, not by the damage alone.

Apoptosis is also a normal developmental process. The separation of fingers, and the removal of lymphocytes that would attack the self, both depend on cells dying exactly when they should.
### Clinical significance
Too little apoptosis and too much both cause disease. A cell that should die and does not is how a damaged genome persists and accumulates further mutations; excessive apoptosis contributes to neurodegeneration and to the tissue loss that follows ischaemia.

The distinction from necrosis is what makes it examinable. Apoptosis is regulated, energy-dependent, affects single cells and provokes no inflammation. Necrosis is unregulated, follows energy failure, affects groups of cells and spills their contents, which is precisely what inflames the surrounding tissue.
### Common misconceptions
Cell death is not the same as tissue damage. Apoptosis is normal and necessary, and its failure is as pathological as its excess.
## hold_these
Programmed cell death is more commonly called apoptosis.
Caspases are the proteases that carry it out, activating one another in an irreversible cascade.
The intrinsic pathway starts at the mitochondrion; the extrinsic pathway starts at a surface death receptor.
Apoptosis provokes no inflammation; necrosis does, because the cell contents spill.
## lose_the_mark
Treating apoptosis as a form of damage. It is a normal process, and too little of it is as harmful as too much.
Confusing it with necrosis. The distinguishing features are regulation, energy dependence, whether single cells or groups are affected, and whether inflammation follows.
