# Item

## title
What afterload is

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Ventricular performance is described in terms of preload, contractility and afterload.

## question
What does afterload refer to?

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The volume of blood in the ventricle at the end of filling

## explanation_a
That is preload — the stretch on the muscle before it contracts. Confusing the two is the commonest error in this vocabulary, and the way to keep them apart is by timing: preload acts before contraction, afterload during it.

## answer_b
The load or pressure the ventricular muscle must overcome during contraction

## explanation_b
Afterload is what the muscle works against once it starts to shorten. For the left ventricle it is dominated by aortic pressure and by anything obstructing outflow, which is why both hypertension and aortic stenosis raise it.

## answer_c
The force the ventricle generates during systole

## explanation_c
Force generated is the ventricle's output, not the load imposed on it. This confuses what the muscle does with what it does it against, and the two move in opposite directions in disease.

## answer_d
The residual volume left in the ventricle after ejection

## explanation_d
That is end-systolic volume, which is a *consequence* of afterload rather than its definition. The relationship is real, which makes this the closest distractor.

# Item

## title
What raises left ventricular afterload

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Three patients are described: one with long-standing hypertension, one with severe aortic stenosis, and one with a large volume of blood transfused rapidly.

## question
Which raise left ventricular afterload?

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
All three, since each increases the work the ventricle must do

## explanation_a
All three do increase cardiac work, which is why this is tempting. But work can be increased by moving more blood as well as by pushing harder, and only the second is afterload. The distinction is the point.

## answer_b
The transfusion alone, since it raises the volume the ventricle must eject

## explanation_b
A rapid transfusion raises preload — filling and stretch — not the load against which the muscle shortens. This is the preload–afterload confusion in its most concrete form.

## answer_c
The hypertension and the aortic stenosis

## explanation_c
Hypertension raises the arterial pressure the ventricle must exceed and continue to exceed throughout ejection. Stenosis adds a resistance at the valve, so a large gradient must be generated across it. Both are obstacles to ejection. The transfusion instead raises the volume delivered to the ventricle, which is preload.

## answer_d
The hypertension alone, since valve disease affects only the valve

## explanation_d
A narrowed valve is precisely an obstacle to ejection, and severe stenosis is one of the largest afterload burdens a ventricle can face. This treats the valve as separate from the outflow path it forms part of.

# Item

## title
The right ventricle's afterload

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A patient develops chronically raised pulmonary arterial pressure.

## question
What is the consequence for the right ventricle?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Its afterload rises, and over time the wall hypertrophies in response to the sustained load

## explanation_a
The right ventricle's afterload is set by the pulmonary circuit, just as the left ventricle's is set by the systemic one. A chronically raised pulmonary pressure is a sustained pressure load, and the wall thickens in response — which is why right ventricular hypertrophy points towards the lungs and the pulmonary circulation rather than towards systemic disease.

## answer_b
Its preload rises, because blood is held back in the pulmonary circulation

## explanation_b
Raised pulmonary pressure is upstream of the left heart and downstream of the right, so for the right ventricle it is a load to eject against. This reverses which side of the chamber the pressure lies on.

## answer_c
Its afterload falls, because the pulmonary vessels dilate to accommodate the pressure

## explanation_c
The pulmonary bed does recruit and distend at raised flow, which normally keeps pressure low. Once pressure is chronically raised that reserve has been exhausted, so invoking it here is applying the normal response to a state that has outgrown it.

## answer_d
Nothing changes, because the right ventricle is a volume pump and is insensitive to pressure

## explanation_d
The right ventricle is described as a volume pump because it normally works against low resistance, not because it is immune to pressure. The description is a summary of its usual conditions, and this answer promotes it to a property.

## title
Afterload on a pressure–volume loop

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A left ventricular pressure–volume loop is recorded, then repeated after afterload has been raised with preload and contractility held constant.

## question
How does the loop change?

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
It shifts to the right along the horizontal axis, with end-diastolic volume increased

## explanation_a
End-diastolic volume is preload, which was held constant. A right shift at the filling end would mean preload had changed, contradicting the conditions of the experiment.

## answer_b
It becomes taller and wider, with both stroke volume and pressure increased

## explanation_b
Pressure does rise, but stroke volume cannot also rise when the ventricle is working against a greater load with the same filling and the same contractility. This has the loop growing in every direction at no cost.

## answer_c
Its shape is unchanged but the whole loop moves upwards, since only pressure has altered

## explanation_c
A pure vertical translation would preserve stroke volume, which is the width of the loop. Raising afterload without changing contractility must cost stroke volume, so the width has to change.

## answer_d
The ventricle ejects against a higher pressure, ejection stops earlier, and end-systolic volume is greater — so the loop is taller and narrower

## explanation_d
Ejection continues only while the ventricle can exceed the arterial pressure. Raise that pressure and the point at which the ventricle can no longer keep up arrives sooner, leaving more blood behind. The loop is therefore taller and narrower, and its width — the stroke volume — has fallen.

## title
Which volume afterload acts on

## subject

## status
Draft

## vignette
Afterload is raised while filling and contractility are unchanged.

## question
Which volume is principally affected?

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
End-diastolic volume, which rises as the ventricle struggles to empty

## explanation_a
Over several beats a raised end-systolic volume does feed forward into a larger end-diastolic volume, so this is not absurd. But the immediate and principal effect is at the end of systole, and preload was specified as unchanged.

## answer_b
End-diastolic volume, which falls because less blood is delivered

## explanation_b
Delivery to the ventricle is preload, which was held constant. This changes the wrong variable and in the wrong direction.

End-systolic volume, which rises because ejection stops earlier

## explanation_c
Afterload mainly affects end-systolic volume. The ventricle ejects only while it can exceed the arterial pressure, so a higher pressure ends ejection sooner and leaves more blood behind. Stroke volume falls as the difference between an unchanged end-diastolic volume and a larger end-systolic one.

## answer_d
Stroke volume alone, with both end-diastolic and end-systolic volumes unchanged

## explanation_d
Stroke volume is the difference between the two, so it cannot change while both are fixed. This treats a derived quantity as though it were independent.

# Item

## title
Why a failing ventricle is afterload-sensitive

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A modest rise in arterial pressure has little effect on stroke volume in a healthy ventricle, but produces a marked fall in a severely impaired one.

## question
Why the difference?

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The failing ventricle has a higher preload, and preload and afterload oppose one another directly

## explanation_a
A failing ventricle often does operate at a higher preload, which is a real compensation. But the two are not opposing quantities that cancel, and raised preload does not explain heightened afterload sensitivity.

## answer_b
A healthy ventricle has contractile reserve to generate more pressure and preserve ejection; a failing one has little left to call on

## explanation_b
When afterload rises, ejection is preserved only if the ventricle can raise the pressure it generates. A healthy ventricle has reserve to do so and stroke volume barely changes. A failing ventricle is already close to its maximum, so the same rise in load cannot be matched and ejection falls. Afterload sensitivity is a measure of how little reserve remains.

## answer_c
The failing ventricle has a stiffer wall, so it cannot fill against the higher pressure

## explanation_c
Stiffness impairs filling, which is a preload problem, and arterial pressure is not what the ventricle fills against. This relocates the difficulty from ejection to filling.

## answer_d
Arterial pressure rises more steeply in heart failure, so the same stimulus produces a larger load

## explanation_d
The premise specifies the same modest rise in both. This changes the stimulus rather than explaining the different response to it.

## title
The determinants of mean arterial pressure

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Mean arterial pressure is treated as the product of two quantities.

## question
Which two?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Cardiac output and total peripheral resistance

## explanation_a
Mean arterial pressure is approximately cardiac output multiplied by total peripheral resistance. Pressure is what results when a flow meets a resistance, so every influence on blood pressure — volume, rate, contractility, vessel tone — must act through one of these two.

## answer_b
Stroke volume and heart rate

## explanation_b
That product gives cardiac output, which is one of the two terms rather than the whole expression. This is the most common partial answer, and it stops one step short.

## answer_c
Systolic and diastolic pressure

## explanation_c
Mean arterial pressure can be estimated from systolic and diastolic values, but that is a way of measuring it, not a statement of what determines it. Description and determination are different questions.

## answer_d
Blood volume and vascular compliance

## explanation_d
These two determine mean systemic filling pressure, which is a real and related quantity governing venous return. It is the closest distractor, and separating the two expressions is worth the effort.

## title
Two levels of the same relationship

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Mean arterial pressure is the product of cardiac output and total peripheral resistance; cardiac output is itself the product of heart rate and stroke volume.

## question
What follows from nesting the two expressions?

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Heart rate has a greater influence on pressure than resistance, since it appears in both expressions

## explanation_a
Heart rate appears once, inside cardiac output. Counting appearances in an equation is not a measure of influence, and this treats algebraic position as physiological weight.

## answer_b
Pressure can be raised only by increasing heart rate, since it is the fastest variable to change

## explanation_b
Speed of adjustment is not the same as being the only route. Resistance can be altered within seconds by arteriolar tone and is the principal short-term regulator.

## answer_c
Stroke volume and resistance are interchangeable, since both multiply into the same product

## explanation_c
Both feed the same product, but they are physiologically distinct and are regulated separately. Algebraic symmetry is not physiological equivalence.

## answer_d
Every influence on arterial pressure must act through heart rate, stroke volume or resistance

## explanation_d
Nesting the two gives pressure as heart rate times stroke volume times resistance. Any intervention or disease that changes pressure — haemorrhage, exercise, a vasodilator, an arrhythmia — must be doing it through at least one of those three. The value of the expression is that it exhausts the possibilities.

## title
Pressure held constant while everything changes

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
During moderate exercise cardiac output rises several-fold, yet mean arterial pressure rises only modestly.

## question
What must be happening to total peripheral resistance?

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
It must be rising, to keep pressure from falling as output increases

## explanation_a
Rising resistance alongside rising output would multiply the two increases together and send pressure up steeply. This has the sign backwards.

## answer_b
It must be unchanged, with the modest pressure rise reflecting the increased output

## explanation_b
If resistance were fixed, a several-fold rise in output would produce a several-fold rise in pressure. The modest rise observed is precisely the evidence that resistance cannot have stayed constant.

## answer_c
It must be falling substantially, because the two terms multiply and pressure has barely moved

## explanation_c
Since pressure is output times resistance, a large rise in one with little change in the product requires a large fall in the other. Physiologically this is vasodilatation in exercising muscle, whose beds are so large that they dominate total resistance. The equation predicts the direction, and the anatomy explains how it is achieved.

## answer_d
It cannot be determined, because mean arterial pressure also depends on blood volume

## explanation_d
Blood volume influences pressure through filling and hence output, so it is already accounted for within the output term. This treats a variable that acts through one of the two terms as though it were a third.

Reading a fall in pressure

## subject

A patient's mean arterial pressure has fallen substantially. Cardiac output is measured and found to be high.

What does that combination indicate?

Raised volume increases output but does not lower resistance; if anything the two would push pressure up together. This invents a single cause for two changes that must be separated.

# Item

## title
Venous backup in a failing heart

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A weakened ventricle cannot initially pump forward all the blood returning to it.

## question
What happens to the blood that is not ejected?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
It accumulates upstream, raising the pressure in the veins and the atrium behind the failing chamber

## explanation_a
Blood that is not moved onwards has nowhere to go but backwards. Volume and pressure rise in the chamber, then in the veins draining into it. This is why left-sided failure raises pulmonary venous pressure and right-sided failure raises systemic venous pressure — the congestion appears behind the chamber that failed.

## answer_b
It is destroyed, since stagnant blood is removed by the spleen

## explanation_b
Splenic removal of red cells is a slow process quite unrelated to a beat-to-beat volume mismatch. This invents a disposal route for a volume that is simply retained.

## answer_c
