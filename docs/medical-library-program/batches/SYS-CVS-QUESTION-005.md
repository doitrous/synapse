## title
The innermost layer of the endocardium

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A section through the wall of a cardiac chamber is examined from the lumen outwards.

## question
Which tissue lines the chamber?

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Simple squamous mesothelium

## explanation_a
Mesothelium lines body cavities — the pericardial, pleural and peritoneal sacs. It is squamous like endothelium and easily confused with it, but it faces a cavity rather than blood.

## answer_b
Stratified squamous epithelium

## explanation_b
A stratified lining would obstruct exchange and create turbulence. This is chosen by students who reason that a surface exposed to mechanical stress must be layered, as skin and oesophagus are.

## answer_c
Endothelium

## explanation_c
The endocardium's innermost layer is an endothelial lining, continuous with the endothelium of the vessels entering and leaving the heart. The whole circulation is lined by one uninterrupted sheet, which is why a thrombus can form anywhere the sheet is damaged.

## answer_d
Cardiac muscle covered by a basal lamina

## explanation_d
Myocardium lies deep to the endocardium, not at the surface. Students choose this by assuming the muscular organ is muscular all the way in.

# Item

## title
One continuous lining

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
The endothelium of the endocardium is continuous with that of the great vessels, which is continuous with that of the arteries, capillaries and veins.

## question
What follows from that continuity?

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Blood cells can leave the circulation anywhere along it, since there is no barrier between compartments

## explanation_a
Continuity of a lining is not an absence of barrier — the endothelium is the barrier. Leucocytes do cross it, but at specific sites and by a regulated process, not because the sheet is unbroken.

## answer_b
The entire circulation presents a single non-thrombogenic surface, and damage anywhere on it can start a thrombus

## explanation_b
Intact endothelium actively resists clotting along its whole length, and the corollary is that a breach anywhere — a valve, a coronary plaque, a vein wall — exposes a thrombogenic surface. Continuity is what makes thrombosis a single mechanism operating in many places rather than several unrelated diseases.

## answer_c
Pressure is equal throughout the circulation, since it is one connected space

## explanation_c
The circulation is one connected space, and pressure varies enormously across it precisely because resistance is distributed unevenly. Connectivity does not imply uniformity.

## answer_d
Endothelial cells are identical throughout, so a sample from any site represents all others

## explanation_d
Endothelium is markedly heterogeneous — glomerular, hepatic and cerebral endothelia differ profoundly. This is the most instructive error, because it mistakes anatomical continuity for functional uniformity.

## title
Which arteries are elastic

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Arteries are classified by what dominates the tunica media.

## question
Which vessels are the large elastic arteries?

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
The arterioles and the smallest arteries

## explanation_a
Arterioles have the highest proportion of smooth muscle and the least elastic tissue of any artery. Selecting them inverts the classification entirely.

## answer_b
The coronary and renal arteries

## explanation_b
These are muscular distributing arteries, typical of the medium-sized vessels that deliver blood to organs. Students choose them because they are the arteries named most often in clinical teaching.

Arteries within the walls of organs

## explanation_c
Intramural arteries are small and muscular. This is chosen by students who associate elasticity with deformability and assume vessels inside a moving organ must be the stretchiest.

The aorta and its large branches

## explanation_d
The aorta and the large vessels arising from it — brachiocephalic, common carotid, subclavian, pulmonary, common iliac — are the elastic arteries. Their media is built of concentric elastic lamellae, and their position closest to the heart is what makes that construction necessary.

# Item

## title
What elastic lamellae are for

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A section of aorta shows the tunica media filled with concentric sheets of elastin between layers of smooth muscle. Blood leaves the left ventricle in intermittent bursts, yet flow in the capillaries downstream is nearly continuous.

## question
How does the aortic wall bring that about?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
It stretches to store part of each stroke volume, then recoils during diastole to keep blood moving forwards

## explanation_a
Elastic lamellae let the aorta expand as blood is ejected, absorbing part of the stroke volume rather than passing it all on at once. In diastole the wall recoils and drives that stored volume forwards. Pulsatile ejection is converted towards steady flow, which is why a stiffened aorta raises systolic pressure and lowers diastolic.

## answer_b
It contracts actively during diastole, pumping blood onwards as a second heart

## explanation_b
The recoil is passive. Elastin returns energy stored by stretching; it does not generate force of its own. Students choose this because the effect resembles a pump, and calling the arrangement a "second heart" invites it.

## answer_c
Its elasticity damps the pressure wave so that it does not reach the capillaries

## explanation_c
Damping is part of the story, but the wall does more than absorb — it returns the stored energy. Treating the aorta as purely a shock absorber loses the diastolic contribution that keeps flow going.

## answer_d
Its smooth muscle adjusts calibre to hold flow constant against changing pressure

## explanation_d
This describes autoregulation by arterioles, and it operates far downstream. The aorta's smooth muscle is not the element doing this work, and attributing it there confuses a conducting vessel with a resistance vessel.

The commonest artery type

## status

## vignette
Most named arteries in the body — brachial, femoral, radial, splenic — carry blood towards a particular organ or region.

What type of artery are they?

B

Elastic arteries

Elastic arteries are the few largest vessels closest to the heart. Students choose this by assuming a named artery must be a large one.

Muscular arteries

Sinusoids are wide, leaky capillaries in the liver, spleen and bone marrow. They are not arteries at all, which makes this the outlier option that tests whether the student is reading the classification or matching on unfamiliar words.

# Item

## title
Why distributing arteries are muscular

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Blood flow is redirected between organs continuously — towards exercising muscle, towards the gut after a meal, towards the skin when body temperature rises.

## question
What does a muscular media contribute to that redistribution?

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
It generates a propulsive wave that pushes blood towards the active organ

## explanation_a
Arteries do not peristalse. Propulsion is the ventricle's job, and arteries alter where blood goes rather than driving it there. This attributes a gut mechanism to a vessel.

## answer_b
It absorbs the pressure pulse so that the organ receives steady flow

## explanation_b
Pulse damping is the elastic arteries' contribution, upstream. This attaches the wrong vessel class's function to the right vessel class's structure.

## answer_c
It allows the vessel's calibre to be adjusted, changing the share of cardiac output that reaches its territory

## explanation_c
Smooth muscle in the media lets each distributing artery change its own diameter, so the proportion of the output reaching that territory can be raised or lowered. Distribution is competitive — narrowing one bed sends more blood to the others — which is why organ blood flow is a matter of relative resistance rather than of local demand alone.

## answer_d
It contracts to maintain wall tension as vessel radius falls along the tree

## explanation_d
Wall tension does vary with radius and pressure, and vessel walls must withstand it, but withstanding tension is a structural requirement rather than a contribution to redistribution. The reasoning is physiological and answers a different question.

## title
Identifying a muscular artery in section

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A transverse section of a vessel shows a wavy, brightly refractile sheet immediately deep to the endothelium, separating it from a media of concentric smooth muscle.

## question
Which structure is that, and what does it identify?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The internal elastic lamina, which marks the vessel as a muscular artery

## explanation_a
A prominent internal elastic lamina is the feature that distinguishes muscular from elastic arteries in a routine section. Its wavy appearance is an artefact of fixation — the vessel contracts, and the inelastic intima is thrown into folds — which is exactly why it is so easy to spot.

## answer_b
The internal elastic lamina, which marks the vessel as an elastic artery

## explanation_b
Elastic arteries do have an internal elastic lamina, but it is inconspicuous because it blends with the many elastic lamellae filling the media behind it. The structure is right and the conclusion is wrong, which makes this the closest distractor.

## answer_c
The basal lamina of the endothelium, which is present in all vessels

## explanation_c
The endothelial basal lamina is far too thin to be resolved as a distinct wavy sheet at this magnification. Students choose it by naming the first layer deep to any epithelium without asking what is actually visible.

## answer_d
The external elastic lamina, which separates media from adventitia

## explanation_d
The external elastic lamina lies on the far side of the media. This is the right kind of structure at the wrong border, and separating the two is the point of learning both names.

# Item

## title
Why the lamina is conspicuous in one artery and not the other

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Both muscular and elastic arteries possess an internal elastic lamina, yet it is a reliable identifying feature in only one of them.

## question
Why?

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Elastic arteries lose their internal elastic lamina with age, so it is absent in most adult specimens

## explanation_a
The lamina persists; arterial ageing changes the media's composition rather than deleting the intimal boundary. This invents a developmental explanation for a matter of contrast.

## answer_b
The lamina is thicker in muscular arteries because they are exposed to higher pressure

## explanation_b
Pressure is highest in the aorta, an elastic artery, so the reasoning predicts the opposite of what is seen. This is chosen by students who assume every structural difference tracks mechanical load.

## answer_c
Muscular arteries have two laminae and elastic arteries only one, so the inner one stands out

## explanation_c
Many muscular arteries do have a recognisable external lamina as well, so the count is roughly right — but having two does not make either more visible. A true fact is being used to explain something it does not cause.

## answer_d
In an elastic artery the media is itself full of elastic lamellae, so the internal lamina does not stand out against its background

## explanation_d
Visibility is contrast, not size. Against a media of smooth muscle the single elastic sheet is unmistakable; against a media built of dozens of similar sheets it is simply one more. The same structure is diagnostic in one vessel and useless in the other because of what lies behind it.

Naming the three tunics from a section

## subject

## vignette
A muscular artery is examined from the lumen outwards. Four boundaries and three layers are identified.

## question
Which sequence is correct?

C

## answer_a
Intima, external elastic lamina, media, internal elastic lamina, adventitia

## explanation_a
The two laminae are transposed. This is the commonest error and the reason to attach each name to a position rather than to a list — internal is the inner boundary of the media, external the outer.

Intima, internal elastic lamina, adventitia, external elastic lamina, media

This puts the adventitia inside the media. It is chosen by students who have the two laminae in the right order but have not fixed which of the outer two layers is which.

# Item

## title
What arterioles do

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Arterioles lie between the small arteries and the capillary beds, and carry only one to three layers of smooth muscle.

## question
What is their principal role?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Regulating blood flow into the capillary beds beyond them

## explanation_a
Arterioles are the adjustable taps of the circulation. Their small radius means modest changes in calibre produce large changes in resistance, so they set how much blood enters each capillary bed and, collectively, the total peripheral resistance.

## answer_b
Exchanging oxygen and nutrients with the surrounding tissue

## explanation_b
Exchange happens in capillaries and postcapillary venules, whose walls are thin enough to permit it. An arteriolar wall with layers of smooth muscle is built for control, not for transfer.

## answer_c
Storing a reserve of blood that can be mobilised when needed

## explanation_c
The capacitance function belongs to the veins, which hold the majority of the blood volume. This transfers a venous role to the arterial side.

## answer_d

## explanation_d

# Item

## title
Where selectivity comes from if the pores are open

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Glomerular endothelial fenestrae are open, without diaphragms, and are large enough to pass plasma proteins freely. Yet almost no albumin appears in normal filtrate.

## question
How is that reconciled?

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Albumin is too large to enter the fenestrae, which are open but narrow

## explanation_a
This denies the premise: the fenestrae do admit molecules of albumin's size. Students choose it because a size-exclusion answer at the first layer is the simplest resolution available, and the item is built to close it off.

## answer_b
The endothelium is not the selective layer — selectivity comes from the basement membrane and the podocyte slit diaphragms beyond it

## explanation_b
The barrier works in series and each layer does a different job. The open endothelium provides the high water permeability that makes rapid filtration possible; the basement membrane and the podocyte slits provide the size and charge selectivity that keeps protein in. Building the two functions into different layers is what allows both to be maximised.

## answer_c
Albumin passes freely but is entirely reabsorbed in the proximal tubule

## explanation_c
Some filtered protein is reabsorbed, and this becomes important in disease. But it is a downstream rescue rather than the reason little appears in the filtrate, and treating it as the mechanism misplaces the barrier.

## answer_d
Plasma flow is too rapid for albumin to have time to cross

## explanation_d
Filtration is driven by pressure across the barrier, not limited by transit time in this way. This substitutes a kinetic argument for a structural one.

## title
Where exchange happens

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Oxygen, nutrients and waste move between blood and tissue at a particular part of the vascular bed.

## question
Which segments carry out that exchange?

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Arterioles and capillaries

## explanation_a
Arteriolar walls carry smooth muscle and are too thick for meaningful exchange. Including them extends the exchange zone upstream past the point where the wall becomes a barrier.

## answer_b
Capillaries alone

## explanation_b
Capillaries do most of it, and this is the answer most students give. It omits the postcapillary venules, which are thin-walled, highly permeable, and the principal site at which fluid and leucocytes leave the circulation in inflammation.

## answer_c
Capillaries and postcapillary venules

## explanation_c
Exchange occurs across both. Extending the zone past the capillary matters clinically: the postcapillary venule is where increased permeability produces oedema and where leucocytes emigrate, so inflammation is a venular event rather than a capillary one.

## answer_d
Capillaries and the small veins draining them

## explanation_d
Small veins acquire a muscular wall and are no longer exchange vessels. This is the closest distractor, and the distinction between a postcapillary venule and a small vein is exactly what it tests.

## title
The wall of a postcapillary venule

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A vessel just downstream of a capillary bed is sectioned. Its wall is more than endothelium alone but has no true muscular coat.

## question
What does its wall contain?

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
A single continuous layer of smooth muscle within a collagenous adventitia

## explanation_a
A defined smooth muscle layer marks the transition to a muscular venule and then a small vein, further downstream. This is chosen by students who expect a media as soon as the capillary ends.

## answer_b
Pericytes and reticular fibres

## explanation_b
The postcapillary venule's media contains pericytes together with reticular fibres. That composition is what keeps the wall thin enough for exchange while giving it some contractile capacity and mechanical support — a genuine intermediate between a capillary and a vein.

## answer_c
Elastic lamellae arranged concentrically

## explanation_c
Elastic lamellae belong to large arteries, where pressure is high and pulsatile. Venular pressure is low and steady, so a construction for storing and returning energy would serve no purpose here.

## answer_d
Endothelium and basal lamina only, as in a capillary

## explanation_d
This makes the postcapillary venule indistinguishable from a capillary, when the additional wall components are exactly what defines it as a separate segment.

# Item

## title
Bypassing the capillary bed

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
In some vascular beds a vessel runs directly from an arteriole to a venule, with no capillary between them.

## question
What is this called, and what does it do?

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
A portal system, carrying blood from one capillary bed to another

## explanation_a
A portal system connects two capillary beds in series, as between gut and liver. It contains capillaries rather than avoiding them, which is the opposite arrangement.

## answer_b
A collateral vessel, opening when the main route is obstructed

## explanation_b
Collaterals are alternative arterial routes that enlarge in response to obstruction. These connections are present normally and are opened and closed as part of routine regulation, not in response to disease.

## answer_c
An arteriovenous fistula, an abnormal communication between artery and vein

## explanation_c
An acquired or congenital fistula is pathological. The naming is close enough to be genuinely confusing, and the distinction is that the normal structure is a regulated part of the anatomy.

## answer_d
An arteriovenous anastomosis, shunting blood past the capillaries when exchange is not required

## explanation_d
Arteriovenous anastomoses connect arterioles directly to venules, bypassing the capillary bed. Opening one diverts flow past the exchange vessels. In the skin of the fingers, nose and ears this is a thermoregulatory device: blood can be sent through the skin to lose heat, or shunted past it to conserve heat.

# Item

## title
Why shunting warms or cools

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
On a cold day the fingers become pale and cold; in a warm room they flush and feel hot. Arteriovenous anastomoses in the skin are numerous at the fingers, nose and ears.

## question
How does opening them affect heat loss?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Blood is diverted through the shunts into the superficial venous plexus, so more warm blood reaches the surface and heat loss rises

## explanation_a
Opening the anastomoses lets a large volume pass rapidly through the skin's superficial vessels. The skin becomes warm and flushed and heat is lost to the environment; closing them keeps that blood in the core. The shunts control heat loss by controlling how much warm blood is brought to the surface, not by changing what is exchanged with the tissue.

## answer_b
Blood bypasses the skin altogether, so heat is retained in the core

## explanation_b
The shunts bypass the capillaries, not the skin. This is the central confusion the item exists to resolve: blood still travels through the skin, and in greater volume.

## answer_c
Heat loss falls, because blood spends less time in the capillary bed where exchange occurs

## explanation_c
Exchange with the tissue is not what loses heat to the air; blood volume near the surface is. The reasoning applies the logic of solute exchange to a thermal problem.

## answer_d
Heat loss is unchanged, since total skin blood flow is fixed by the arteriole upstream

## explanation_d
Total skin flow is not fixed — the arteriole and the shunts are regulated together, and skin flow varies enormously between cold and heat. This treats one control as a constant when both are variables.
