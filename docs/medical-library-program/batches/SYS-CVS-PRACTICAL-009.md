### Counting the chambers
Concept: CON-CVS-5621D16508394C
Difficulty: Easy
Q: An apical four-chamber view shows all four cardiac chambers. Which pair lies nearer the transducer at the apex, and how are the two sides distinguished?
*= The ventricles lie nearer the apex, and the right side is identified by its thinner wall and the more apically placed tricuspid valve
Why: Correct. In the apical view the ventricles are nearest the transducer, and the right ventricle is recognised by its thinner wall and by the tricuspid valve inserting slightly closer to the apex than the mitral.
* The atria lie nearer the apex, with the ventricles behind them
Why: Reverses the orientation. The apex of the heart is ventricular, which is what the transducer is placed over.
* The left-sided chambers lie nearer the apex, and the right-sided chambers behind them
Why: The view is divided into atria and ventricles by depth, and into left and right across the image, not front to back.
* The chambers cannot be distinguished without colour flow
Why: Wall thickness and the offset of the valve insertions distinguish them on the two-dimensional image alone.
Explanation: Orienting an echocardiogram is the whole skill: which chambers are near the transducer, and which side is which. Wall thickness follows from the pressure each chamber generates, so the anatomy identifies itself.

### The valve in the middle
Concept: CON-CVS-32F6DDF825E732
Difficulty: Easy
Q: A parasternal long-axis view shows the aortic valve. From which chamber does it lead, and how many cusps should it have?
*= From the left ventricle, with three semilunar cusps
Why: Correct. The aortic orifice is the outlet of the left ventricle, and its valve normally has three semilunar cusps.
* From the left ventricle, with two cusps
Why: A bicuspid aortic valve is the commonest congenital cardiac abnormality and is worth recognising — but it is an abnormality, not the normal.
* From the right ventricle, with three semilunar cusps
Why: Describes the pulmonary valve, which has the same cusp arrangement but a different ventricle.
* From the left atrium, at the mitral orifice
Why: The mitral orifice is an inlet with two cusps and chordae, not a semilunar outlet valve.
Explanation: Naming the chamber a valve leads from is what makes the view readable, and knowing the normal cusp count is what makes a bicuspid valve visible as an abnormality rather than as an unfamiliar image.

### The muscles inside the ventricle
Concept: CON-CVS-25C53C42DCA293
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A short-axis view at mid-ventricular level shows two papillary muscles projecting into the left ventricular cavity. Which is larger, and why is the distinction useful?
*= The anterior one, and its size means its failure affects the most valve tissue
Why: Correct. The anterior papillary muscle is the larger, so it supports more of the valve and its rupture causes the more severe regurgitation.
* The posterior one, which is larger because it lies against the inferior wall
Why: The posterior papillary muscle is the more vulnerable because of its blood supply, which is a genuine and important point — but it is not the larger.
* They are always equal, and any difference indicates disease
Why: Treats a normal difference in size as pathology.
* There is only one papillary muscle in the left ventricle
Why: The single-muscle arrangement is not the normal left ventricle, which has an anterior and a posterior papillary muscle.
Explanation: Two muscles, unequal in size and unequal in blood supply. Both facts have consequences after an infarct, and a short-axis view is where they are counted.

### What holds the cusps
Concept: CON-CVS-334F674BA1244E
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Fine linear echoes run from a papillary muscle tip to the mitral cusps. What are they and how do they attach?
*= Chordae tendineae, attaching to the adjacent halves of two neighbouring cusps
Why: Correct. Chordae from a single papillary muscle attach to the adjoining halves of two cusps, so one muscle steadies both sides of a commissure.
* Chordae tendineae, each attaching to a single cusp exclusively
Why: The intuitive one-muscle-one-cusp arrangement, and the reason students underestimate the effect of a single rupture.
* Trabeculae carneae, which are muscular ridges on the ventricular wall
Why: Trabeculae are ridges of the wall itself, not the fine cords running to the cusps.
* The moderator band, carrying conducting tissue to the papillary muscle
Why: The moderator band is a right ventricular structure and carries conducting tissue, so the association is understandable — but it does not attach to the mitral cusps.
Explanation: The chordae are the reason a cusp does not evert into the atrium. Their attachment across two cusps at once explains why a single papillary rupture is catastrophic rather than local.

### Why the root sits where it does
Concept: CON-CVS-CE1750C05B9C84
Difficulty: Hard
Q: A dilated aortic root is reported. Which structures are most immediately at risk, and why?
*= The aortic valve cusps and the coronary ostia, because the sinuses that carry the ostia are part of the root
Why: Correct. The aortic sinuses are dilatations of the root behind the cusps, and two of them give rise to the coronary arteries — so root dilatation distorts cusp coaptation and can involve the coronary origins.
* The pulmonary valve alone, since it lies immediately anterior
Why: The pulmonary valve is a close relation, but the structures within the root itself are affected first.
* The mitral valve alone, because it lies immediately posterior
Why: Aortic-mitral continuity is real and the mitral valve can be involved, but the cusps and coronary ostia are within the dilating segment itself.
* No structures are at risk, since the root is simply a conduit
Why: Treats the root as a passive tube, which loses both the sinuses and the coronary origins.
Explanation: The root is not just the first part of the aorta: it contains the valve cusps, the sinuses behind them and the coronary ostia. That is why dilatation there produces regurgitation and can threaten coronary flow at the same time.

### When the atrium contracts
Concept: CON-CVS-F9B09809C7715B
Difficulty: Easy
Q: A Doppler trace across the mitral valve shows two peaks of inflow per cardiac cycle. What does the second, later peak represent?
*= Atrial systole, which occurs in late ventricular diastole
Why: Correct. The second peak is flow driven by atrial contraction, which happens at the end of ventricular diastole, just before ventricular systole.
* Ventricular systole, seen as flow back through the valve
Why: Flow back through a closed mitral valve is regurgitation, which is an abnormal finding and would appear in the opposite direction on the trace.
* Isovolumetric relaxation, when the ventricle is filling passively
Why: Contradicts itself: during isovolumetric relaxation all valves are closed and no filling occurs.
* The opening snap of the mitral valve
Why: A sound, not a flow event, and not something a Doppler inflow trace displays.
Explanation: Two peaks, two mechanisms: passive filling as the ventricle relaxes, then atrial contraction at the end of diastole. That the atrial contribution comes last is why losing it matters more when diastole is short.

### Where most of the filling comes from
Concept: CON-CVS-D50119DD90A9D7
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: In a resting adult, which of the two inflow peaks is normally the larger, and what does that indicate?
*= The early passive peak, because most resting ventricular filling is passive
Why: Correct. At rest roughly four-fifths of filling occurs passively before the atrium contracts, so the early peak dominates.
* The late atrial peak, because atrial contraction drives most of filling
Why: The atrial contribution is around a fifth at rest. Overestimating it is the commonest error about ventricular filling.
* They are normally equal, and any difference indicates disease
Why: A reversal of the normal ratio is indeed a recognised abnormality, but equality is not the normal state.
* Neither, since filling is continuous and produces no discrete peaks
Why: Filling is not continuous; the two-peak pattern is precisely what the trace shows.
Explanation: Passive filling dominates at rest, which is why a patient can tolerate losing atrial contraction while resting and become symptomatic on exertion, when the atrial share rises.

### The closed-valve interval
Concept: CON-CVS-C5B68CCC8E4F3E
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Between aortic valve closure and mitral valve opening, what is happening in the left ventricle?
*= Isovolumetric relaxation — all valves closed, pressure falling, volume constant
Why: Correct. With both the aortic and mitral valves shut, nothing enters or leaves, so the ventricle relaxes at constant volume.
* Rapid filling, with the mitral valve already open
Why: Filling begins only once the mitral valve opens, which is the event that ends this interval.
* Isovolumetric contraction, with pressure rising at constant volume
Why: The other closed-valve interval, at the start of systole rather than the start of diastole.
* Ejection, with the aortic valve still open
Why: Ejection ends at aortic valve closure, which is the event that begins this interval.
Explanation: There are two intervals in the cycle with every valve closed, and they are distinguished by the direction the pressure is moving. Naming them correctly is what makes a pressure or Doppler trace readable.

### What the ventricle is pushing against
Concept: CON-CVS-AD0881F8B57C91
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A patient with severe aortic stenosis has a normal ejection fraction but a hypertrophied left ventricle. Which loading condition is chiefly increased?
*= Afterload, the load the ventricle must work against during ejection
Why: Correct. A stenotic valve raises the resistance to ejection, which is an increase in afterload, and chronic pressure loading produces hypertrophy.
* Preload, the volume in the ventricle at the end of filling
Why: Preload may change secondarily, but the primary problem in stenosis is the obstruction to outflow.
* Contractility, the intrinsic force of the muscle
Why: Contractility is a property of the muscle rather than a load, and it is preserved here — which is why the ejection fraction is normal.
* Heart rate, which determines the frequency of ejection
Why: Rate is not a loading condition at all.
Explanation: Pressure load produces hypertrophy; volume load produces dilatation. Reading which chamber has responded, and how, is what tells you which load has been chronically raised.

### What a fall in ejection would mean
Concept: CON-CVS-8E0A89302117C2
Difficulty: Hard
Q: On serial imaging his end-systolic volume has risen while end-diastolic volume is unchanged. What does that indicate?
*= The ventricle is emptying less completely against its load, so stroke volume has fallen
Why: Correct. Afterload acts on emptying: a rising end-systolic volume with unchanged filling means less blood is being ejected each beat.
* The ventricle is filling more, so stroke volume has risen
Why: End-diastolic volume is unchanged, so filling has not increased, and a larger residual volume means a smaller stroke volume.
* Contractility has improved, since a larger volume remains available for the next beat
Why: Inverts the meaning of the residual volume: a larger end-systolic volume indicates less complete emptying, not better function.
* Nothing can be concluded without the ejection fraction
Why: The two volumes are what the ejection fraction is calculated from; the conclusion is available directly.
Explanation: Reading the two volumes separately is more informative than reading their ratio. End-systolic volume reports emptying and therefore afterload and contractility; end-diastolic volume reports filling and therefore preload.

### Which compartment
Concept: CON-CVS-AE28ABD8CE2B0B
Difficulty: Easy
Q: An axial CT slice at the level of the cardiac chambers is being described. In which mediastinal compartment do the heart and pericardium lie?
*= The middle mediastinum
Why: Correct. The heart within its pericardium occupies the middle mediastinum, between the pleural cavities.
* The superior mediastinum
Why: Contains the great vessels above the level of the sternal angle, not the chambers themselves.
* The posterior mediastinum
Why: Lies behind the pericardium and contains the oesophagus and descending aorta.
* The anterior mediastinum
Why: A narrow space in front of the pericardium containing the thymic remnant and fat, not the heart.
Explanation: Naming the compartment orders every relation on the slice: pleura laterally, oesophagus and descending aorta behind, sternum and anterior mediastinum in front.

### The layer around the heart
Concept: CON-CVS-7C9D59D257AC65
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A thin line of low attenuation surrounds the heart, bounded by a fine dense line. Which structures are these?
*= Pericardial fluid within the pericardial cavity, bounded by the fibrous pericardium and the serous layers
Why: Correct. A small amount of fluid lies between the two serous layers, and the outer dense line is the fibrous pericardium with the parietal serous layer against it.
* The myocardium, seen as a low-attenuation layer
Why: The myocardium is soft tissue of higher attenuation and lies inside the pericardium, not around it.
* The pleural cavity extending around the heart
Why: The pleural cavities lie on either side and do not surround the heart; that is the difference between a pericardial and a pleural collection on imaging.
* Epicardial fat only, with no pericardium visible
Why: Epicardial fat is present and often prominent, and it can be genuinely difficult to separate from a small effusion — but the dense line bounding it is the fibrous pericardium.
Explanation: Being able to name the layers in order on a slice is what allows a pericardial collection to be distinguished from a pleural one, and a physiological amount of fluid from an effusion.

### The two great vessels
Concept: CON-CVS-930E4596CA5673
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: On one axial slice the pulmonary trunk lies to the left of the ascending aorta; a few slices higher the relationship has changed. Why?
*= The two vessels spiral around each other, so their relative positions change along their course
Why: Correct. The spiral relationship means each axial slice shows a different arrangement, which is normal rather than abnormal.
* The patient has moved between the two slices
Why: Motion artefact is a real consideration, but a consistent change in relative position along the course is anatomical.
* One of the vessels is aneurysmal and has displaced the other
Why: Reads a normal relationship as pathology, which is exactly the error that knowing the spiral prevents.
* The slices were acquired at different phases of the cardiac cycle
Why: Cycle phase alters chamber size, not the fundamental course of the great vessels around each other.
Explanation: The spiral is why no single axial slice defines the relationship between the great arteries. Expecting the arrangement to change with level is what stops normal anatomy being reported as displacement.

### The valve seen in cross-section
Concept: CON-CVS-5E6A3445314E64
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A slice through the right atrioventricular orifice shows the valve cusps. How is each cusp anchored?
*= Its base to the fibrous ring, and its free edge and ventricular surface to chordae tendineae from papillary muscles
Why: Correct. Both atrioventricular valves are anchored at the base to the fibrous ring and at the free edge by chordae.
* Its base to a papillary muscle and its free edge to the fibrous ring
Why: Reverses the two ends; the ring holds the base and the chordae hold the edge.
* Its base to the fibrous ring only, with no chordal attachment
Why: Removes the chordae, and unrestrained cusps would evert into the atrium during systole.
* Its whole surface to the ventricular wall, which pulls it closed
Why: The cusps must be free to move; only their edges are tethered.
Explanation: Both atrioventricular valves are built to the same plan. Learning it once on either side transfers, which is why an examiner may ask about the tricuspid having taught the mitral.

### Where the muscles arise
Concept: CON-CVS-4ABA568F6D69F1
Difficulty: Hard
Q: A slice through the ventricles shows muscular projections arising from the wall and running towards the valve. Distinguish these from the ridges on the wall itself, and state what each does.
*= Papillary muscles arise from the wall and give chordae to the cusps; trabeculae carneae are ridges of the wall with no chordal attachment
Why: Correct. The papillary muscles are anchored in the wall at their base and connect to the valve cusps through chordae; trabeculae carneae are muscular ridges of the ventricular wall itself.
* Both are papillary muscles, seen at different levels of the same structure
Why: Merges two distinct structures, and would leave the ridges unexplained on a slice where no valve is in view.
* Papillary muscles are ridges on the wall; trabeculae carneae connect to the cusps
Why: Swaps the two definitions exactly.
* Both are trabeculae carneae, one of which happens to reach the valve
Why: The septomarginal trabecula does carry conducting tissue to a papillary muscle, which makes this the most defensible of the errors — but a structure giving chordae to a cusp is a papillary muscle.
Explanation: Wall, muscle, chorda, cusp. Distinguishing the projections that are part of that chain from the ridges that are not is what makes a ventricular slice interpretable.

### Which ventricle feeds which vessel
Concept: CON-CVS-770A1368E72DFB
Difficulty: Easy
Q: On an echocardiogram of a cyanosed newborn, the aorta is seen arising from the right ventricle. What does this establish?
*= Transposition of the great arteries, in which the ventriculo-arterial connections are reversed
Why: Correct. The aorta arising from the right ventricle, with the pulmonary trunk from the left, defines transposition; the two circulations then run in parallel.
* A normal variant of the outflow tract seen in the newborn period
Why: The connection is fixed and normal from early development; it is never a variant.
* An overriding aorta straddling a septal defect
Why: An overriding aorta sits above a defect and receives from both ventricles, which is a different arrangement from arising wholly from the right.
* A dilated pulmonary trunk mistaken for the aorta
Why: A genuine identification pitfall, and the reason the vessel is traced to its branches — but the finding described is the connection, not the calibre.
Explanation: Congenital lesions are described by their connections. Establishing which vessel leaves which ventricle is the first question asked of any image of a cyanosed newborn.

### Following the vessel to be sure
Concept: CON-CVS-8424E2DDED2365
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: How would you confirm on the image that the vessel arising from the right ventricle is the aorta rather than the pulmonary trunk?
*= Follow it: the aorta gives the coronary arteries at its root and continues as the arch, while the pulmonary trunk divides into two pulmonary arteries
Why: Correct. The branching pattern identifies each vessel unambiguously — coronary ostia and an arch for the aorta, an early bifurcation for the pulmonary trunk.
* Measure its diameter, since the aorta is always the larger
Why: Relative calibre varies and is altered in disease, so it is not a reliable identifier.
* Assess the wall thickness, since the aorta has a thicker wall
Why: Wall thickness is not reliably distinguishable at this resolution, and both are elastic arteries.
* Use the position alone, since the aorta always lies posterior and right
Why: Position is exactly what is abnormal in transposition, which makes it the least reliable criterion in the case at hand.
Explanation: Identify a vessel by where it goes, not by where it is. In a heart with abnormal connections, position is the unreliable feature and branching is the dependable one.

### What the aorta is built to do
Concept: CON-CVS-182A0AD30A3730
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: The ascending aorta is an elastic artery. What does that structure contribute to the circulation?
*= It expands in systole and recoils in diastole, maintaining forward flow between beats
Why: Correct. Elastic recoil stores energy during ejection and releases it during diastole, converting intermittent ejection towards continuous flow.
* It contracts actively to propel blood onwards between beats
Why: The recoil is passive elastic return, not active contraction; elastic arteries have relatively little smooth muscle.
* It resists dilatation, keeping the pulse pressure as wide as possible
Why: Inverts the effect. Elasticity narrows the pulse pressure by absorbing the systolic volume.
* It regulates systemic vascular resistance
Why: Resistance is set by the arterioles, not by the great elastic arteries.
Explanation: The aorta is a reservoir as well as a conduit. This is why coronary flow can continue in diastole, and why the loss of elasticity with age widens the pulse pressure.

### Where the sound comes from
Concept: CON-CVS-625E445610F27A
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: A systolic murmur is heard in a patient whose imaging shows a narrowed aortic valve. What is the murmur?
*= The sound of turbulent flow through the narrowed orifice
Why: Correct. A murmur is generated by turbulence, which is what a narrowed orifice produces from an otherwise normal flow.
* The sound of the valve cusps striking one another
Why: Valve closure produces the discrete heart sounds, not the sustained noise of a murmur.
* The sound of the ventricle contracting against increased resistance
Why: Muscle contraction is silent; the sound comes from the blood, not the wall.
* An artefact of the stethoscope over a hypertrophied ventricle
Why: A murmur is a real acoustic finding, and hypertrophy itself makes no sound.
Explanation: Every murmur is turbulent flow. That single idea generates the whole approach: a murmur means an abnormal orifice, an abnormal flow, or both, and its timing tells you which valve and which phase.

### Putting the imaging and the stethoscope together
Concept: CON-CVS-AA14CA86F39233
Difficulty: Challenging
Q: The imaging locates the stenotic valve retrosternally at the level of the third costal cartilage, yet the murmur is loudest in the second right intercostal space and radiates to the neck. Why is there no contradiction?
*= The valve's anatomical projection and its auscultation area differ, because sound is carried downstream in the direction of flow
Why: Correct. The aortic valve projects behind the sternum, but the sound generated there is transmitted along the ejected blood into the ascending aorta and towards the neck, which is where it is best heard.
* The imaging must be misregistered, since the murmur locates the valve accurately
Why: Treats the auscultation area as an anatomical locator, which is precisely the misconception the item addresses.
* Radiation to the neck indicates a carotid rather than an aortic origin
Why: A carotid bruit is a genuine differential, and distinguishing it matters — but radiation along the direction of flow from a stenotic aortic valve is expected, not evidence against it.
* The murmur is being transmitted through the bones of the chest wall
Why: Bone conduction does not account for the specific, reproducible pattern of radiation in the direction of blood flow.
Explanation: The anatomy says where the valve is; the direction of flow says where you will hear it. Holding the two as separate, reconcilable facts is what allows an image and an examination finding to be read as one picture.
