# Item

## title
Length of one cardiac cycle

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A patient's heart rate is 75 beats per minute.

## question
How is the duration of one cardiac cycle obtained from that?

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
By multiplying the rate by 60, since each beat occupies a fraction of a minute

## explanation_a
Multiplying gives beats per hour, not the length of a beat. This is chosen by students who reach for the arithmetic that produces a familiar-looking number rather than checking the units.

## answer_b
By dividing the rate by 60, giving beats per second

## explanation_b
This does give beats per second, which is the reciprocal of what was asked. It is the closest distractor because it is a legitimate quantity — one step short of the answer.

## answer_c
By dividing 60 by the rate, giving the seconds each beat occupies

## explanation_c
Sixty seconds shared among 75 beats gives about 0.8 seconds per cycle. The relationship matters more than the figure: cycle length is the reciprocal of rate, so every statement about how long a phase lasts is a statement about rate as well.

## answer_d
It cannot be calculated, because systole and diastole vary independently of rate

## explanation_d
The two phases do not vary in step with one another, which is a real and important point, but the total cycle length is fixed by the rate. This denies an arithmetic relationship because a physiological complication sits inside it.

# Item

## title
Which phase gives way when the heart speeds up

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Heart rate rises from 75 to 150 beats per minute. The duration of one cycle halves.

## question
How is that reduction distributed between systole and diastole?

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Systole and diastole shorten equally, each by half

## explanation_a
An equal split is the intuitive assumption and the reason this error is so common. It also predicts that filling would be no more compromised at high rates than ejection, which is contradicted by what happens clinically.

## answer_b
Systole shortens and diastole is unchanged, because filling time is protected

## explanation_b
Nothing protects filling time. This inverts the actual behaviour, and it is chosen by students who reason from what the heart needs rather than from what it does.

## answer_c
Diastole is unchanged and systole absorbs the whole reduction, because ejection is faster at high rates

## explanation_c
Systole does shorten somewhat, so half of this is right. But it cannot absorb the whole reduction, and asserting that diastole is unchanged removes the very effect that makes tachycardia dangerous.

## answer_d
Diastole shortens far more than systole, so filling time and coronary perfusion time both fall disproportionately

## explanation_d
Systole is relatively fixed; almost all the lost time comes out of diastole. Two consequences follow at once: the ventricle has less time to fill, and — because left coronary flow is largely diastolic — the myocardium has less time to be perfused, precisely when it is working hardest. This is why a fast rate is a threat to an already-ischaemic heart.

# Item

## title
When atrial systole occurs

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
The cardiac cycle is divided into ventricular systole and ventricular diastole. Atrial contraction has to be placed within that scheme.

## question
Where does atrial systole fall?

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
At the beginning of ventricular diastole, immediately after the ventricles relax

## explanation_a
The start of ventricular diastole is when rapid passive filling occurs, driven by the pressure difference across the open atrioventricular valves. Atrial contraction comes at the other end of diastole. Students choose this by assuming the atria act as soon as they can.

## answer_b
In the final part of ventricular diastole, immediately before ventricular systole begins

## explanation_b
Atrial systole occupies the last portion of ventricular diastole, topping up a ventricle that has already filled largely passively. Placing it at the end is what makes it a top-up rather than the main filling event, and it is why the atrioventricular delay must be long enough for it to finish before the ventricle contracts.

## answer_c
Simultaneously with ventricular systole, so that both chambers contract together

## explanation_c
Simultaneous contraction would squeeze atrial blood against closing valves and back into the veins. This is what happens when atrioventricular synchrony is lost, and the fact that it is abnormal is the point.

## answer_d
During ventricular systole, filling the atria while the ventricles eject

## explanation_d
The atria do fill from the veins during ventricular systole, but that is passive filling of the atria, not atrial systole. This confuses what is happening to the atria with what the atria are doing.

# Item

## title
The fourth heart sound

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A low-pitched sound is heard immediately before the first heart sound, in a patient whose left ventricle is stiff.

## question
What produces it?

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Closure of the atrioventricular valves at the onset of ventricular systole

## explanation_a
That is the first heart sound, which this sound precedes. Students choose it because the two are adjacent in time, and the fourth sound is heard only just before it.

## answer_b
Rapid passive filling in early diastole striking a non-compliant ventricle

## explanation_b
That describes the third heart sound, which occurs in early diastole. This is the closest distractor: both extra sounds arise from filling meeting a ventricle that resists it, and the two are separated by which part of filling is responsible.

## answer_c
Atrial contraction forcing blood into a ventricle that resists filling

## explanation_c
Because atrial systole falls at the end of diastole, the sound it generates falls immediately before the first heart sound. It appears when the ventricle is stiff, so atrial contraction has to work against a wall that will not accommodate the volume readily. Its timing follows directly from when the atria contract.

## answer_d
Turbulent flow through a narrowed atrioventricular valve during filling

## explanation_d
That produces a diastolic murmur, which is a longer sound spanning filling rather than a brief one at its end. This confuses a murmur, which is turbulence, with a heart sound, which is a sudden event.

## title
Reading the cycle from pressure curves

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Left atrial, left ventricular and aortic pressure are plotted against time for one cardiac cycle, with the ventricular volume trace and the ECG beneath them.

## question
Which event marks the point at which the ventricular and aortic pressure curves meet and then separate again?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Opening of the aortic valve, when ventricular pressure first exceeds aortic pressure

## explanation_a
The two curves meet when ventricular pressure has risen to equal aortic pressure; the valve opens at that instant and the curves then run together during ejection. Every valve event on the diagram is a crossing of two pressure curves, which is what makes the whole cycle readable from the traces rather than memorised as a list of phases.

## answer_b
Closure of the mitral valve, when ventricular pressure first exceeds atrial pressure

## explanation_b
That crossing does occur and it does close the mitral valve, but it involves the ventricular and atrial curves, not the ventricular and aortic ones. The principle is right and the pair of curves is wrong.

## answer_c
The peak of ventricular contraction, when the ventricle has generated its maximum force

## explanation_c
Peak ventricular pressure occurs during ejection, after the curves have already met. This reads the diagram for its highest point rather than for a crossing.

## answer_d
The onset of the QRS complex, when ventricular depolarisation begins

## explanation_d
The QRS precedes the mechanical events and appears on the ECG trace below, not at a crossing of pressure curves. Students choose it by anchoring to the most familiar landmark on the figure.

## title
How much filling is passive

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A resting ventricle fills over the whole of diastole, but the two contributions to that filling are unequal.

## question
At rest, how is ventricular filling divided?

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Roughly half passively and half by atrial contraction

## explanation_a
An even split overstates the atrial share substantially. It is chosen by students who reason that a chamber with its own muscle must be doing a comparable amount of work to the passive process it supplements.

## answer_b
The large majority passively, before atrial contraction, with atrial systole contributing the remainder

## explanation_b
Most filling occurs passively as blood flows from atrium to ventricle down a pressure gradient once the atrioventricular valve opens; atrial contraction adds the last portion. This is why losing atrial contraction is well tolerated by a normal heart at rest, and why it stops being tolerated when passive filling is impaired.

## answer_c
Entirely by atrial contraction, since the ventricle cannot fill against a closed circuit

## explanation_c
The circuit is not closed during diastole — the atrioventricular valve is open and blood flows continuously. This denies passive filling altogether, and if it were true, atrial fibrillation would be immediately catastrophic.

## answer_d
Entirely passively, with atrial contraction serving only to close the atrioventricular valve

## explanation_d
Atrial contraction makes a real contribution, and the valve is closed by rising ventricular pressure rather than by the atrium. This is the opposite overstatement to option C and removes the atrial contribution entirely.

## title
What drives passive filling

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Most ventricular filling occurs before the atria contract, and it begins the moment the atrioventricular valve opens.

## question
What drives it?

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Contraction of the atrial wall, which begins as soon as the valve opens

## explanation_a
Atrial contraction comes at the end of diastole, not at its start. This collapses the distinction the whole concept rests on.

## answer_b
Suction generated by continued ventricular contraction after the aortic valve closes

## explanation_b
The ventricle is relaxing, not contracting, after the aortic valve closes. Elastic recoil of the relaxing ventricle does contribute a genuine suction effect, which is what makes this the strongest distractor — but calling it contraction is wrong, and it is not the main driver.

## answer_c
The pumping action of the pulmonary veins delivering blood under pressure

## explanation_c
Veins do not pump. Blood arrives from them continuously at low pressure, which is precisely why a pressure gradient rather than a pump is what moves it onwards.

## answer_d
The pressure difference between the filled atrium and the relaxing ventricle

## explanation_d
Atrial pressure exceeds ventricular pressure once the ventricle has relaxed, so blood flows down the gradient as soon as the valve opens. Filling is a passive consequence of pressures, and the atrium acts only as a top-up pump at the end.

# Item

## title
Naming the atria's role

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
The atria are sometimes described as primer pumps rather than as pumps in their own right.

## question
What does that description capture?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
They complete a filling process that is largely accomplished without them

## explanation_a
A primer adds the finishing contribution to something already substantially done. The ventricle has filled passively before the atrium contracts, so the atrium improves filling rather than achieving it. That is why the description is chosen deliberately over calling them pumps.

## answer_b
They generate the pressure that drives blood through the pulmonary circulation

## explanation_b
Pulmonary flow is driven by the right ventricle. The atria develop far too little pressure for this. Students choose it by assuming any chamber called a pump must be responsible for moving blood through a circuit.

## answer_c
They prime the ventricle by stretching it beyond its resting length before every beat

## explanation_c
Atrial contraction does increase end-diastolic volume and therefore fibre length, which matters through the length–tension relationship. But the description refers to completing filling rather than to stretching, and this reading substitutes one true effect for the intended meaning.

## answer_d
They store blood during ventricular systole and release it during diastole

## explanation_d
The atria do fill during ventricular systole and empty during diastole, so this describes a real reservoir role. It is a separate function from priming, and conflating the two loses what the word primer is doing.

## title
The atrial contribution during exercise

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
At rest, atrial contraction contributes a minor share of ventricular filling. During vigorous exercise its share rises.

## question
What is the effect on filling?

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
It falls, because the shortened diastole leaves less time for atrial contraction

## explanation_a
Atrial systole shortens along with everything else, but its relative importance rises rather than falls. This reasons from absolute duration when the question is about proportion.

## answer_b
It is unchanged, because the proportion is a fixed property of the chambers

## explanation_b
The proportion is not fixed; it depends on how much passive filling has been achieved before the atrium contracts. Treating it as a constant is what makes the exercise case surprising.

## answer_c
It rises, because shortened diastole leaves passive filling incomplete and the atrial contribution makes up more of the total

## explanation_c
As rate rises, diastole shortens and passive filling has less time to complete. The atrial contribution then represents a greater share of a filling process that would otherwise be inadequate. The atrium matters most exactly when the heart is working hardest — which is why losing it is tolerated at rest and not on exertion.

## answer_d
It rises, because sympathetic stimulation makes the atria contract with much greater force than the ventricles

## explanation_d
Sympathetic drive does increase atrial contractility, and that contributes. But the dominant reason the share rises is that passive filling has been curtailed, and this answer replaces a filling-time explanation with a contractility one.

## title
Atrial fibrillation at rest and on exertion

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A patient in atrial fibrillation, with no coordinated atrial contraction, is comfortable sitting still but becomes breathless on climbing stairs.

## question
Which explanation follows from the cardiac cycle?

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The irregular rhythm becomes more irregular on exertion, and irregularity itself limits output

## explanation_a
Irregularity does reduce the efficiency of individual beats, and it is a genuine contributor. But it is present at rest as well, so it cannot explain why symptoms appear only with exertion.

## answer_b
The atrial contribution is dispensable while diastole is long, and becomes necessary once exertion shortens it

## explanation_b
At rest, diastole is long enough for passive filling to be nearly complete, so the absent atrial contribution costs little. On exertion the rate rises, diastole shortens, passive filling is curtailed, and the contribution that would have compensated is not available. The same lesion is tolerated in one state and not the other because the cycle's timing has changed.

## answer_c
Atrial fibrillation reduces coronary flow at rest, and exertion exposes the ischaemia

## explanation_c
A fast ventricular rate does shorten diastole and reduce coronary filling time, which is a real concern. But the question asks about filling of the ventricle, and this answer substitutes a perfusion mechanism for a filling one.

## answer_d
The ventricles become less compliant during exercise, so filling is impaired independently of the atria

## explanation_d
Ventricular relaxation actually becomes faster with sympathetic stimulation, improving filling rather than impairing it. This invents a compliance change in the wrong direction.

## title
Isovolumetric relaxation

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Immediately after the semilunar valves close, the ventricle relaxes but its volume does not change.

## question
Why is the volume constant?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a

C

The strength of ventricular contraction — a stronger ventricle opens the valve at a lower pressure

The pulmonary circulation offers much lower resistance, so less pressure is needed to drive the same flow

Bronchial circulation exists and returns a small volume to the left atrium, but it does not divert right ventricular output. This invents a shunt to avoid the pressure question.
