## summary
One word covering too little and too much. Getting that right changes who you look for.
## sections
### Definition
Malnutrition refers to deficiencies, excesses, or imbalances in a person's intake of energy and/or nutrients. The word covers three failures, not one, and the third — imbalance — is why a person can be adequately fed by weight and still malnourished.

Undernutrition includes wasting (low weight-for-height), stunting (low height-for-age) and underweight (low weight-for-age). The other side of the definition is overweight, obesity and the diet-related noncommunicable diseases.
### Mechanism
Intake and requirement are two quantities, and malnutrition is any sustained mismatch between them. Requirement is not fixed: it rises in growth, pregnancy, lactation and illness, so a stable diet can become inadequate without changing.

Absorption sits between the two. A diet that meets requirement on paper does not meet it in a gut that cannot absorb, which is why chronic diarrhoeal and parasitic disease produce malnutrition at normal intakes.
### Key determinants
Which form appears depends on what is scarce. Energy scarcity produces the anthropometric deficits; a diet adequate in energy but poor in variety produces micronutrient deficiency with normal or high weight.

That combination is why undernutrition and obesity are found in the same countries, the same communities, and sometimes the same household — a pattern that a definition restricted to undernutrition cannot describe at all.
### Clinical significance
Reading malnutrition as undernutrition alone means looking only at thin patients, and missing the micronutrient deficiency in a patient whose weight is normal or raised.

It also determines what a service measures. A programme that weighs children and stops there will not detect the deficiencies that do not show in weight.
### Common misconceptions
Reading malnutrition as a synonym for undernutrition. The WHO definition covers excess and imbalance too.

Assuming adequate weight means adequate nutrition. Micronutrient deficiency is compatible with any weight.
## hold_these

Undernutrition includes wasting (low weight-for-height), stunting (low height-for-age) and underweight (low weight-for-age).

## lose_the_mark

Assuming adequate weight means adequate nutrition. Micronutrient deficiency is compatible with any weight.

## reading_time

## summary
Three measurements, and each answers a different question: how recent, how long-standing, and how much overall.
## sections
### Definition
Low height-for-age is known as stunting. It is the result of chronic or recurrent undernutrition, usually associated with poor socioeconomic conditions, poor maternal health and nutrition, frequent illness, and/or inappropriate infant and young child feeding and care in early life.

Low weight-for-height is known as wasting. It usually indicates recent and severe weight loss because a person has not had enough food to eat and/or they have had an infectious disease, such as diarrhoea, which has caused them to lose weight.
### Mechanism
Height accumulates and weight fluctuates, and that is the whole of the difference. A child who is short for their age has been deprived over a period long enough to slow skeletal growth. A child who is thin for their height has lost tissue recently.

Underweight combines the two, which makes it the least specific of the three: a low weight-for-age can arise from either process or both.
### Key determinants
Timing is what each index reports. Wasting responds within weeks to feeding and to treatment of the illness that caused it. Stunting reflects the first years of life and is largely not reversible afterwards, which is why the window for preventing it is so narrow.

The causes named in the definition are social as much as dietary — socioeconomic conditions, maternal health, feeding practice, and repeated infection all appear before any question of food quantity.
### Clinical significance
The index chosen decides what the finding means. Screening for wasting finds children who need feeding now; measuring stunting describes a population's history and the conditions it lived through.

Because diarrhoeal disease is named as a cause of wasting, treating infection and treating undernutrition are the same task rather than sequential ones.
### Common misconceptions
Treating the three indices as interchangeable. Wasting is recent and acute; stunting is chronic and largely irreversible after early childhood.

Reading undernutrition as a food-supply problem alone. The cited definition names infection, maternal health and care practices alongside intake.
## hold_these
Low height-for-age is known as stunting.
Low weight-for-height is known as wasting.
Wasting responds to feeding within weeks; stunting is largely not reversible after early childhood.
## lose_the_mark

Reading undernutrition as a food-supply problem alone. Infection, maternal health and care practices are named causes.

## summary
The deficiencies weight does not show. Anaemia is the commonest, and the people at risk are defined by physiology rather than poverty.
## sections
### Definition
Micronutrients are the vitamins and minerals the body needs in small amounts. Their deficiency is a form of malnutrition that a scale will not detect, because it is compatible with any body weight.

Anaemia is a condition in which the number of red blood cells or the haemoglobin concentration within them is lower than normal. It is the most visible consequence of micronutrient deficiency and the one most often measured.
### Mechanism
Iron deficiency, primarily due to inadequate dietary iron intake, is considered the most common nutritional deficiency leading to anaemia. Iron is needed to make haemoglobin, so a sustained shortfall reduces the haemoglobin in each cell before it reduces the number of cells.

Intake is only one term. Losses through menstruation or parasitic infestation, and increased demand in growth and pregnancy, move the balance as much as diet does.
### Key determinants
The population groups most vulnerable to anaemia include children under 5 years of age, particularly infants and children under 2 years of age, menstruating adolescent girls and women, and pregnant and postpartum women.

What those groups share is not income but physiology: each is either growing fast, losing blood regularly, or supporting another body. That is why the vulnerable groups are the same everywhere while the prevalence differs.
### Clinical significance
A haemoglobin result is a screening test for a nutritional problem, and in the named groups a low value should prompt a question about intake and losses rather than only a transfusion threshold.

Because deficiency precedes anaemia, a normal haemoglobin does not exclude depleted iron stores — which is why treating the cause matters more than correcting the number.
### Common misconceptions
Assuming a normal diet excludes micronutrient deficiency. Requirements rise in growth, menstruation and pregnancy.

Reading anaemia as a diagnosis. It is a finding, and the cause decides the treatment.

## reading_time

## summary
The other half of malnutrition, and the half a screening programme built around thin children will never see.
## sections
### Definition
Excess is one of the three failures the definition of malnutrition names, alongside deficiency and imbalance. In adults it is measured by body mass index.

For adults, overweight is a BMI greater than or equal to 25. For adults, obesity is a BMI greater than or equal to 30.
### Mechanism
BMI is weight divided by the square of height. It rises when energy intake exceeds expenditure over a sustained period, and the excess is stored as adipose tissue.

The index does not distinguish what the weight is made of. A muscular person and a person with excess adipose tissue can share a BMI, which is why the thresholds are described for populations rather than diagnoses.
### Key determinants
The thresholds quoted are for adults. Children are assessed against age- and sex-specific references instead, because body composition changes throughout growth and a single cut-off would misclassify at every age.

What drives the excess is rarely a single behaviour. Availability, cost, activity and the composition of the available diet all act, which is why it is treated as a population problem as much as an individual one.
### Clinical significance
Because excess is part of the definition of malnutrition, a nutrition service that measures only undernutrition is not measuring nutrition. Both forms occur in the same populations, and increasingly in the same households.

The BMI thresholds are useful as a first sort. What they cannot do is tell you about an individual's body composition, which is why they open an assessment rather than close it.
### Common misconceptions
Treating BMI as a measure of body fat in an individual. It is a population screening index and does not distinguish muscle from fat.

Applying the adult thresholds to children. Children are assessed against age- and sex-specific references.
## hold_these
For adults, overweight is a BMI greater than or equal to 25.
For adults, obesity is a BMI greater than or equal to 30.
BMI does not distinguish muscle from adipose tissue.
## lose_the_mark

## aliases
Dietary requirements
## reading_time
5
## summary
Not one number. What a person needs moves with what their body is doing, which is why deficiency can appear without the diet changing.
## sections
### Definition
A nutritional requirement is the amount of energy and of each nutrient a person needs to maintain health. Malnutrition refers to deficiencies, excesses, or imbalances in a person's intake of energy and/or nutrients. The word imbalance in that definition is doing work, because requirement is plural and specific rather than a single total.

Requirements are stated for groups. An individual's needs sit somewhere around the group figure and are not known precisely.
### Mechanism
Requirement is the sum of maintenance and of whatever else the body is doing. Basal metabolism, physical activity, growth, tissue repair, pregnancy and lactation each add a term, and illness adds another.

That is why the same diet can be adequate in one month and inadequate the next without anything about the food having changed.
### Key determinants
Age, sex, body size and activity set the baseline. Physiological state moves it: growth in childhood and adolescence, pregnancy and lactation, and the catabolic demand of infection or injury.

Micronutrient requirements do not scale with energy requirements, which is why a diet that supplies enough calories can still be deficient — the imbalance in the definition.
### Clinical significance
Knowing that requirement moves is what makes the vulnerable groups predictable rather than something to memorise. Every group at high nutritional risk is a group whose requirement is raised or whose losses are increased.

It also explains why dietary advice given once does not stay correct through pregnancy, illness or adolescence.
### Common misconceptions
Quoting one daily figure for everyone. Requirements are conditional, and pregnancy, lactation, growth and illness all move them.

Assuming enough energy means enough of everything. Micronutrient requirements do not scale with calories.
## hold_these
Malnutrition refers to deficiencies, excesses, or imbalances in a person's intake of energy and/or nutrients.
Requirement is the sum of maintenance and of whatever else the body is doing.
Micronutrient requirements do not scale with energy requirements.
## lose_the_mark
Quoting one daily figure for everyone. Requirements move with age, size, activity and physiological state.
Assuming adequate calories means adequate nutrition. That is the imbalance the definition names.

## arabic_title
التغذية المعوية
## aliases
Enteral feeding | Tube feeding
## reading_time
5
## summary
If the gut works, use it. Not because it is cheaper — because using it keeps it working.
## sections
### Definition
Enteral nutrition uses the gastrointestinal tract to supply nutrients. It covers feeding by any route that reaches the gut, from oral supplements to a tube passed into the stomach or jejunum.

The defining feature is the route, not the device: what makes feeding enteral is that the nutrients are absorbed by the gut.
### Mechanism
Utilizing the gut to provide nutrients helps maintain gut integrity, stimulation, and modulation of the immunological properties of the GI tract. The gut is not a passive tube; it is an organ that atrophies when unused and an immune barrier that depends on being fed.

That is the mechanism behind the clinical preference. An unfed gut loses barrier function, and what crosses a failed barrier is bacteria.
### Key determinants
Whether enteral feeding is possible turns on one question: does the gastrointestinal tract work. Obstruction, ischaemia and severe dysfunction rule it out; an unsafe swallow or an inadequate intake do not, because a tube bypasses both.

The level at which the tube ends matters for risk rather than for principle — post-pyloric placement is used where gastric feeding is not tolerated.
### Clinical significance

## summary
Damage accumulating unevenly. Age in years predicts it only loosely, which is the argument for assessing the patient in front of you.
## sections
### Definition
At the biological level, ageing results from the impact of the accumulation of a wide variety of molecular and cellular damage over time. Ageing is therefore a process rather than a state, and one that has been running throughout life rather than starting at a birthday.

These changes are neither linear nor consistent, and they are only loosely associated with a person's age in years.
### Mechanism
Damage accumulates in every tissue, and each tissue loses reserve at its own rate. What results is a gradual fall in the capacity to respond to stress, which is invisible at rest and appears when something is demanded.

Because the damage is various and the repair capacity differs between people, the trajectory differs too — which is what the cited claim means by neither linear nor consistent.
### Key determinants
Genetics, exposures, disease and circumstance all act on the rate. Two people of the same age can differ widely in reserve, and the difference is not noise but the accumulated result of different histories.

This is why age in years is a weak predictor of physiological reserve, and why the clinical questions are about function rather than about the date of birth.
### Clinical significance
Assessing the patient rather than their age changes decisions: an operation, a drug dose, a rehabilitation goal. Using age as the proxy denies treatment to the fit and imposes it on the frail.

It also matters for what is called normal. A change that is common with age is not thereby harmless, and treating every finding in an older patient as ageing is how treatable disease is missed.
### Common misconceptions
Treating age in years as a proxy for function. The cited evidence is that the association is loose.

Calling every abnormality in an older patient normal ageing. Common with age is not the same as untreatable.

## sections
### Definition
A fall is defined as an event which results in a person coming to rest inadvertently on the ground or floor or other lower level. The word inadvertently carries the definition: a controlled lowering is not a fall, and a fall from a stool is.

Older people have the highest risk of death or serious injury arising from a fall and the risk increases with age.
### Mechanism

Because several systems contribute, most falls have several causes acting together rather than one — which is why looking for the cause usually finds a combination.
### Key determinants
The contributors divide into the person and the environment. On the person's side: strength, balance, vision, cognition, continence, and the medicines they take. On the environment's side: lighting, floor surfaces, footwear and obstacles.

Medication is the contributor most readily changed, which is why a falls assessment is also a medication review.
### Clinical significance
Because the risk of serious injury rises with age, a fall in an older person is an event to investigate rather than to record. The injury is the presenting problem; the fall is the diagnosis to be made.

A fall also predicts further falls, so the visit is an opportunity to prevent the next one rather than only to treat this one.
### Common misconceptions
Recording a fall as an accident. It is usually the visible end of a treatable combination of medication, vision, balance and environment.

Looking for a single cause. Most falls in older people have several contributors acting together.

## summary
Five or more medicines at once — a count that predicts falls, frailty, disability and death, and one of the few risks a clinic visit can reduce directly.
## sections
### Definition
The use of 5 or more medications is an acceptable definition of polypharmacy. The threshold is a convention rather than a biological boundary, chosen because it is where the association with harm becomes consistent.

What it names is regular concurrent use, not the number of prescriptions ever written.
### Mechanism
Each additional medicine adds its own adverse effects, its own interactions with every other medicine, and its own demand on the patient's ability to take it correctly. The interactions grow faster than the count, which is why the risk is not simply proportional.

Medicines are also added more readily than they are stopped. A drug started for a reason that has passed usually stays, and a side effect treated with another drug is how a list lengthens without anyone deciding it should.
### Key determinants
This cut-off point of 5 drugs is associated with the risk of adverse outcomes such as falls, frailty, disability, and mortality in older adults.

What makes the count harmful is not the number itself but what sits inside it: interactions, medicines no longer indicated, and doses that were right for a younger kidney.
### Clinical significance
The count is a prompt to review rather than a target to reduce. A patient on eight necessary medicines is in a different position from one on five, three of which no longer have an indication.

Because polypharmacy is associated with falls and frailty, the medication list is part of a falls assessment rather than a separate exercise.
### Common misconceptions
Treating the count as the problem. The count is a marker; the harm comes from interactions, adverse effects and medicines no longer needed.

Reading the threshold as a limit. It marks where risk becomes consistent, not a number of medicines a patient may not exceed.
## hold_these
