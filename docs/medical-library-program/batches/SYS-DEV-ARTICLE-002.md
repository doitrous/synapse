## aliases
Growth | Growth charts
## reading_time
6
## summary
Size increases; capability develops. The chart matters more than any single measurement, because what it shows is a direction.
## sections
### Definition
In the context of childhood development, growth is defined as an irreversible constant increase in size, and development is defined as growth in psychomotor capacity. The two words are not interchangeable, and a child can be entirely normal in one and delayed in the other.

Irreversible is the operative word for growth: a child who has grown does not ungrow, which is why a flattening curve is read as a change in rate rather than as a loss.
### Mechanism
Growth is measured, not observed. Weight, length or height, and head circumference are plotted against age, and the plot is compared with a reference population.

The WHO 2006 child growth standards describe normal child growth from birth to 5 years of age under optimal environmental conditions including WHO recommendations for breastfeeding. That phrase — optimal conditions — is what makes them a standard rather than a description: they say how children *should* grow, not how the children measured happened to grow.
### Key determinants
A single measurement locates a child on a centile and says almost nothing. Three measurements over time give a trajectory, and the trajectory is the finding.

A child tracking steadily along a low centile is growing normally and is small. A child crossing centiles downwards is not growing normally, whatever centile they are currently on. The two look similar at one visit and completely different across three.
### Clinical significance
By using growth charts and doing the developmental screening, challenges in care can often be identified early. That is the whole purpose: the chart converts a slow problem into a visible one before it declares itself clinically.

Because growth responds to nutrition, infection and social circumstance, a faltering curve is a general alarm rather than a specific diagnosis — which is what makes it useful in a system where most children are seen infrequently.
### Common misconceptions
Using growth and development interchangeably. One is size and the other is capability.

Reading one point as normal or abnormal. Growth charts work by trajectory, and centile crossing is the signal a single plot cannot show.
## hold_these
In the context of childhood development, growth is defined as an irreversible constant increase in size, and development is defined as growth in psychomotor capacity.
The WHO 2006 child growth standards describe normal child growth from birth to 5 years of age under optimal environmental conditions including WHO recommendations for breastfeeding.
By using growth charts and doing the developmental screening, challenges in care can often be identified early.
A trajectory across several visits says what a single measurement cannot.
## lose_the_mark
Using growth and development as synonyms. One measures size, the other capability.
Judging a child from one plotted point. Steady tracking on a low centile and downward centile crossing look alike at a single visit.

## aliases
Milestones | Developmental delay
## reading_time

## summary
Five domains, one comparison group. A child outside the range needs assessing, not labelling.
## sections
### Definition
Developmental milestones are markers of a child's development from infancy into childhood. They are markers rather than deadlines: each describes the range within which most children in a population acquire a skill.

They are categorized into 5 domains: gross motor, fine motor, language, cognitive, and social-emotional and behavioral.
### Mechanism
Development proceeds in each domain somewhat independently, which is why the domains are separated. A child may be advanced in language and behind in gross motor, and the pattern across domains is more informative than any single missed marker.

Delay in development occurs when the child fails to attain developmental milestones as compared to peers from the same population. The comparison is explicit in the definition, and it is to peers from the same population rather than to an absolute age.
### Key determinants
Which population is used as the comparison decides what counts as delay. A reference drawn from a different setting will misclassify children in both directions, which is why the phrase in the definition matters practically and not just pedantically.

Within a child, the number of domains affected changes the meaning: a delay confined to one domain points somewhere different from a delay across all of them.
### Clinical significance
Milestones are the instrument that turns a parent's worry into something examinable. Because they are checked at routine visits, a delay is found by a system rather than by a specialist.

What follows a suspected delay is assessment, not a diagnosis. The screening step establishes that a child is outside the expected range; what is causing it is a separate question.
### Common misconceptions
Treating milestones as deadlines. They describe a population's range, and a child slightly outside it needs assessment rather than a diagnosis.

Judging a child against a population they do not belong to. The definition of delay is explicitly comparative.
## hold_these

## aliases

## reading_time

## summary
Milk alone until six months, then milk and food. What changes at six months is requirement, not the value of breastfeeding.
## sections
### Definition
WHO and UNICEF recommend all infants initiate breastfeeding within 1 hour of birth, breastfeed exclusively for the first 6 months of life and then continue breastfeeding up to 2 years of age or beyond while nutritionally adequate, safe complementary foods are introduced from six months.

Three separate recommendations sit in that sentence: when to start, what to give for six months, and what to add afterwards.
### Mechanism
Around the age of 6 months, an infant's need for energy and nutrients starts to exceed what is provided by breast milk, and additional complementary foods are necessary to meet those needs.

So the six-month point is set by a crossing of two curves — a rising requirement against what milk supplies — and not by a developmental milestone or a convention.
### Key determinants
Before six months, adding anything else displaces milk without adding what milk was missing, because nothing was missing. After six months, withholding complementary food leaves a gap that widens as the child grows.

Continued breastfeeding alongside complementary food matters because milk remains a substantial source of energy and protection well into the second year — which is why the recommendation runs to two years and beyond rather than stopping at six months.
### Clinical significance
Feeding practice is one of the causes named in the definition of stunting, so this is where a preventable and largely irreversible outcome is actually prevented.

The recommendation is also a practical benchmark: asking when breastfeeding started, whether anything else was given before six months, and when food was introduced covers most of what determines a young child's nutritional trajectory.
### Common misconceptions
Treating six months as the end of breastfeeding. It is the point at which milk stops being sufficient alone, not the point at which it stops being useful.

Introducing complementary foods early to help a child grow. Before six months they displace milk rather than supplement it.
## hold_these

Around the age of 6 months, an infant's need for energy and nutrients starts to exceed what is provided by breast milk, and additional complementary foods are necessary to meet those needs.
Six months is set by requirement crossing supply, not by convention.
## lose_the_mark

Introducing food before six months to help growth. It displaces milk rather than adding to it.

## reading_time

## summary
Testing people who feel well. It produces a risk, not a diagnosis — and it is worth doing only where a whole pathway exists behind it.
## sections
### Definition
A health screening test is a medical test or procedure performed on members of an asymptomatic population or population subgroup to assess their likelihood of having a particular disease. The population is asymptomatic by definition: this is testing people who have not complained of anything.

One important mathematical distinction is that a screen results in a risk value, as opposed to a diagnostic test that reports a value in comparison to a reference range.
### Mechanism
Because a screen returns a risk rather than an answer, a positive result begins a process instead of ending one. The confirmatory test that follows is a different test, applied to a much smaller and now higher-risk group.

Newborn screening is a state-run healthcare initiative that encompasses the process of parental education, infant screening, appropriate follow-up, diagnostic testing, disease management, and continued evaluation. Six components, of which the sample is one.
### Key determinants
The disease must have an asymptomatic period during which treatment reduces morbidity and mortality significantly more than waiting until symptoms develop. That is the condition that makes screening worthwhile at all, and it rules out screening for conditions where early knowledge changes nothing.

It also explains why screening is not simply testing more. A test that finds disease earlier without improving what happens next converts healthy people into patients for no gain.
### Clinical significance
A screening test without the pathway behind it does harm. A positive newborn screen with nowhere to refer produces an alarmed family and no treatment — which is why the definition describes a programme rather than an assay.

In childhood, screening also carries the developmental check: growth charts and developmental screening are how a slow problem is found in a child who is seen briefly and infrequently.
### Common misconceptions
Reading a positive screen as a diagnosis. A screen yields a risk value; the diagnostic test still has to be done.

Thinking of newborn screening as a blood spot. The blood spot is one of six components, and the others are what make it useful.
## hold_these

## aliases
Puberty | Tanner staging
## reading_time
5
## summary
The physical half of adolescence, and a five-stage scale for recording where a child has reached — because age does not tell you.
## sections
### Definition
Puberty is the process of physical maturation where an adolescent reaches sexual maturity and becomes capable of reproduction. It is a process with a beginning and an end, not an event.

Tanner Staging, also known as Sexual Maturity Rating, is an objective classification system that providers use to document and track the development and sequence of secondary sex characteristics of children during puberty.
### Mechanism
Secondary sex characteristics appear in a sequence, and the sequence is more consistent than its timing. That is what makes a stage recordable: a clinician is placing a child within a known order rather than estimating how far along they are in the abstract.

Staging is done on the characteristics themselves, which is why it is described as objective — two observers should reach the same stage for the same child.
### Key determinants
Children of the same age are at different stages, and children at the same stage are of different ages. This is the reason staging exists: age is a poor proxy for maturation, and maturation is what most of the associated physiology tracks.

Because the growth spurt is tied to pubertal stage rather than to age, stage also carries information about growth that a birth date does not.
### Clinical significance
Recording a stage turns a vague impression into something comparable across visits and between clinicians, which is what allows early or delayed puberty to be recognised as a deviation rather than an opinion.

It also makes the physical process separable from the psychosocial one. A child can be physically mature and still developing in judgement, and staging keeps those two questions apart.
### Common misconceptions
Equating puberty with adolescence. Puberty is the physical maturation; adolescence is the wider period, and they do not begin or end together.

Using stage as a proxy for age. The point of staging is precisely that the two come apart.
## hold_these

## summary

## sections
### Definition
Adolescent development is the psychosocial half of adolescence: the changes in identity, relationships and judgement that occur across the same years as the physical changes of puberty.

During adolescence, it is normal for young people to begin to separate from their parents and make their own identity. The word normal is doing work there — this is the expected process, not a problem to be managed.
### Mechanism
Separation and identity formation require trying things, and trying things requires taking risks. The behaviours that concern clinicians and the development that is supposed to happen are not two separate phenomena.

Physical and psychosocial development run on different timetables. Puberty completes the body; judgement continues developing afterwards, which is why the two must be assessed separately.
### Key determinants
Peers become the reference group that parents were, which changes what influences a young person's decisions and therefore what changes them. Advice that works for a child does not necessarily work for an adolescent.

A young person who is physically adult may still be some way from adult judgement, and treating physical maturity as evidence of the other is a common error with practical consequences for consent and for counselling.
### Clinical significance
Because separation is normal, a consultation that runs entirely through the parent misses the patient. Adolescents need some part of the encounter conducted with them directly.

And because risk-taking is part of development rather than a deviation from it, the clinical response is to reduce harm rather than to treat the behaviour as pathology.
### Common misconceptions
Assuming physical maturity implies judgement. Cognitive and psychosocial development continue well after puberty is complete.

Treating separation from parents as a problem. The cited source calls it normal.

## summary
Five steps, short enough for a routine visit. The first is to ask, which is the one most often skipped.
## sections
### Definition
Risk behaviours are actions that raise a person's probability of harm — and in adolescence they overlap with the experimentation through which identity is formed, which is why they are addressed rather than simply forbidden.

The clinical response is a structured brief intervention. The five major steps of the intervention are as follows: Ask, Advise, Assess, Assist, and Arrange.
### Mechanism
The five steps run in order and each does something distinct. Ask establishes the behaviour exists. Advise states a clear recommendation. Assess finds out whether the person is willing to change. Assist supports the attempt. Arrange sets the follow-up.

The order matters because the later steps are wasted without the earlier ones: assisting someone who has not been assessed as willing is effort spent in the wrong place.
### Key determinants
Whether the behaviour is raised at all is the largest single determinant, and it usually falls to the clinician. A patient who is not asked is generally not going to volunteer.

Willingness to change, established at the Assess step, decides what the rest of the encounter should be. The same five steps produce a different conversation with someone who is ready and someone who is not.
### Clinical significance
The intervention is brief by design, which is what makes it fit a routine consultation rather than requiring a specialist referral. Its value is in being done often, not in being done thoroughly once.

In adolescents it also has to be conducted with the young person rather than around them, which connects it directly to how the consultation is structured.
### Common misconceptions
Waiting for the patient to raise it. The first step is Ask, and a behaviour nobody asks about is not addressed.

Skipping to Assist. Without Assess, the support offered may not be the support the person is ready to use.

## summary
Acting before the disease, or before its consequences. Immunisation is the clearest case, and it protects people who were never vaccinated.
## sections
### Definition
Prevention is described in levels according to what it acts on. Primary prevention aims to reduce the risk of disease before it begins by targeting modifiable risk factors in individuals who are still healthy.

Tertiary prevention focuses on reducing the long-term impact of chronic diseases by minimizing complications, preventing disability, and improving overall quality of life. Between them sits secondary prevention, which acts on disease already present but not yet declared — the level screening belongs to.
### Mechanism
Each level acts at a different point on the same timeline: before the disease, during its silent phase, and after it has established. Naming the level therefore names what an intervention is trying to change.

Immunization is the process of becoming protected against a disease. It is primary prevention in its clearest form: the risk factor removed is susceptibility itself.
### Key determinants
Immunisation protects more people than it reaches. Herd immunity, also known as population immunity, is the indirect protection from an infectious disease that happens when a population is immune either through vaccination or immunity developed through previous infection.

That indirect protection is what covers those who cannot be vaccinated, and it is why coverage across a population matters and not only whether an individual patient is protected.
### Clinical significance
Placing an intervention at the right level clarifies what success would look like. A primary preventive measure is judged by disease that never occurs; a tertiary one by disability that does not follow.

And because herd immunity is a property of populations, an individual consultation about vaccination is also a public health act — the reason coverage targets exist at all.
### Common misconceptions
Reading prevention as advice. Immunisation, screening and environmental change are all prevention, and none of them is a conversation.

Judging vaccination only by the individual protected. The indirect protection reaches people who were never vaccinated.
