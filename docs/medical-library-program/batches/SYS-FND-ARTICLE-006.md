## summary
One cell, no nucleus, and a wall that decides almost everything clinical about it — how it stains, what it survives, and which antibiotics can reach it.
## sections
### Definition
Bacteria are organisms that have only one cell. Under a microscope they appear as balls, rods, or spirals, and that shape is the first thing a laboratory records about them.

They are prokaryotes: they carry their genetic material free in the cytoplasm rather than inside a nucleus. Almost every antibacterial drug exploits some structure that follows from this, which is why the same drugs do nothing against a virus or a fungus.
### Mechanism
The Gram stain sorts bacteria into two groups by what their wall does with a dye. Organisms that retain the primary stain and appear purple-brown under a microscope are Gram-positive. Organisms that do not take up the primary stain and appear red under a microscope are Gram-negative.

The difference is chemical. Gram-positive organisms have a higher peptidoglycan content, while Gram-negative organisms have a higher lipid content — an outer membrane that the stain cannot hold and that many antibiotics cannot cross either.
### Key determinants
What the stain reports is cell-wall chemistry, and everything that varies about it varies for a chemical reason. A wall damaged by prior antibiotic exposure may stain unreliably, and an organism whose wall is thin or unusual in composition may not hold the stain as its group would predict.

Shape is recorded alongside the stain because the two together narrow the field far more than either alone. Cocci in clusters, cocci in chains and rods are different starting points even when the stain result is identical.
### Clinical significance
A Gram stain comes back in minutes, long before a culture. It narrows the field enough to start treatment while the identification is still pending, which in a severely ill patient is the whole point of doing it.

It also predicts resistance in a general way. The outer membrane that makes an organism Gram-negative is a barrier to drug entry, so knowing the stain result already tells you something about which agents are plausible.
### Common misconceptions
A Gram stain is not an identification. It reports cell-wall chemistry and shape, and several very different organisms share both. The species comes from culture or a molecular test, and prescribing as though the stain named the organism is a common and avoidable error.

Some clinically important bacteria do not Gram-stain usefully at all, so a negative or uninformative stain is not evidence that no bacteria are present.
## hold_these
Bacteria are organisms that have only one cell.
Organisms that retain the primary stain and appear purple-brown under a microscope are Gram-positive.
Organisms that do not take up the primary stain and appear red under a microscope are Gram-negative.
The Gram stain reports cell-wall chemistry, not species.
## lose_the_mark
Treating a Gram stain result as an identification. It sorts organisms into two broad groups; the species still comes from culture or a molecular test.
Assuming a negative Gram stain excludes bacterial infection. Several clinically important organisms do not stain usefully.

## summary
A genome in a protein coat, with no metabolism of its own. Everything that makes a virus hard to treat follows from that one fact.
## sections
### Definition
Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites containing either an RNA or a DNA genome surrounded by a protein coat. The coat protects the genome and carries the structures by which the virus recognises the cell it will enter.

"Obligate intracellular" is the operative phrase. A virus outside a cell is inert particulate matter; it does nothing until it reaches a host cell it can enter.
### Mechanism
Viruses depend on host cells to supply the metabolic and biosynthetic machinery they need to propagate. A virus carries instructions, not the apparatus to execute them.

This is the reason antibacterial drugs are useless against viral infection, and it is worth being precise about why. Antibacterial drugs work by attacking structures bacteria have and human cells do not — a cell wall, a bacterial ribosome, a bacterial enzyme. A virus has none of these; it is using the patient's own machinery, and a drug that attacked that machinery would attack the patient.
### Key determinants
Whether a virus can infect a given cell is decided at entry: the coat must recognise something on that cell's surface. This is why viral infections are tissue-specific in a way bacterial infections often are not, and why a virus that cannot enter a cell is harmless to it.

Whether the genome is RNA or DNA determines how faithfully it is copied, and therefore how quickly the virus varies. That single property sits behind much of what differs between viral illnesses in practice.
### Clinical significance
Antiviral drugs therefore have to target the few steps that are genuinely viral: entry, the viral polymerase, assembly. There are far fewer such targets than in a bacterium, which is why antiviral coverage is narrow and organism-specific in a way antibacterial coverage is not.

It also explains why prevention carries more weight for viral disease than for bacterial disease, and why vaccination occupies the place it does in viral illness.
### Common misconceptions
A virus is not a very small bacterium. The difference is not scale but self-sufficiency: a bacterium is a cell that can grow on a plate of agar, and a virus cannot be grown without living cells to grow it in.

Prescribing an antibiotic "in case it is bacterial" for an illness already identified as viral does not cover the patient. It exposes them to adverse effects and selects for resistance, with nothing gained.
## hold_these
Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites containing either an RNA or a DNA genome surrounded by a protein coat.
Viruses depend on host cells to supply the metabolic and biosynthetic machinery they need to propagate.
A virus has no metabolism of its own, so antibacterial drug targets do not exist in it.
## lose_the_mark
Calling a virus a very small bacterium. The difference is self-sufficiency, not size.
Prescribing an antibiotic for a confirmed viral illness. It adds harm and resistance and covers nothing.

## summary
Eukaryotes, which is why they are harder to treat than bacteria — and why fungal disease ranges from a rash to a systemic illness.
## sections
### Definition
Fungi are eukaryotic microorganisms, some of which cause superficial, cutaneous, subcutaneous or systemic disease. The range in that sentence is the point: the same kingdom contains organisms responsible for a trivial skin complaint and for life-threatening infection.

Being eukaryotic matters for treatment. A fungal cell resembles a human cell far more closely than a bacterial cell does, so there are fewer structures a drug can attack without also attacking the patient.
### Mechanism
Yeasts are solitary cells that reproduce by budding, whereas moulds grow as long filaments called hyphae. The distinction is how the organism grows, and it is what a laboratory sees first on a slide or a plate.

Some medically important fungi appear in one form at one temperature and the other form at another, so the growth form observed depends on where and how the organism was cultured.
### Key determinants
Growth form is not fixed. Several medically important fungi appear as one form at one temperature and the other at another, so the form observed depends on where and how the specimen was cultured rather than on the organism alone.

Host factors matter more here than for most bacteria. Fungal disease concentrates in people whose immunity is impaired, whose barriers are broken, or who have had prolonged antibacterial treatment that removed the competing flora.
### Clinical significance
Fungal disease concentrates in people whose immunity is impaired, whose barriers are broken, or who have had prolonged antibacterial treatment that removed the competing flora.

That last route is a clinical consequence of prescribing rather than a property of the fungus, and it is the most avoidable of the three.
### Common misconceptions
Treating fungal infection as always superficial. The same group of organisms causes systemic disease, particularly where immunity is impaired, and the systemic forms are the dangerous ones.

Assuming the growth form seen on a slide fixes the organism's identity. Growth form varies with conditions for several medically important fungi.

Fungi are eukaryotes, so there are fewer drug targets that spare human cells.

## summary
An organism that both feeds on its host and injures it — and, in medicine, a field that also covers the arthropods carrying everything else.
## sections
### Definition
A parasite is a pathogen that simultaneously injures and derives sustenance from its host. Both halves are required: an organism that lives on the host without injuring it is a coloniser, not a parasite.

The injury may be mechanical, nutritional, or immunological, and it is often slow. Parasitic disease is characteristically chronic in a way bacterial disease usually is not.
### Mechanism
Medical parasitology covers three major groups: parasitic protozoa, parasitic helminths, and the arthropods that either cause disease directly or act as vectors.

Protozoa are single-celled and multiply within the host, so a small inoculum can become a large burden. Helminths are worms, usually do not multiply within the host, and the burden therefore reflects the size of the exposure. That difference changes how each is investigated and how reinfection is thought about.
### Key determinants
The third group is unlike the other two, because an arthropod may itself be the cause of disease or may only be carrying something else. A mosquito transmitting a protozoal infection is not the parasite; the protozoon is.

The distinction is not pedantry. If the arthropod is the agent, treatment addresses the patient. If it is the vector, the intervention that matters most is environmental and applies to everyone exposed, not only the patient in front of you.
### Clinical significance
Whether an organism is the agent or the vector changes what is done. If the arthropod is the agent, treatment addresses the patient. If it is the vector, the intervention that matters most is environmental and applies to everyone exposed, not only the patient in front of you.

Because protozoa multiply within the host and helminths generally do not, a protozoal burden can grow from a small exposure while a helminth burden reflects the size of the exposure itself. That difference shapes both how reinfection is thought about and how a finding is interpreted.
### Common misconceptions
Merging vector and parasite. It changes what you do: treat a person, or interrupt an exposure.

Assuming parasitic infection is always symptomatic. Chronic low-burden infection is common and may be found incidentally, which is why the finding has to be interpreted against the clinical picture rather than treated automatically.
## hold_these

Protozoa multiply within the host; helminths generally do not.
## lose_the_mark

Assuming every positive finding needs treating. Chronic low-burden infection is common and must be read against the clinical picture.

## summary
Not how often an organism is met, but how much harm it can do when it is — and the specific products by which it does it.
## sections
### Definition
Virulence is the measure of the pathogenicity of an organism. It is a quantity, not a category: organisms are more or less virulent, and the same species can vary between strains.

Factors produced by a microorganism that evoke disease are called virulence factors. These are the concrete mechanisms behind the abstract measure — the molecules and structures that turn contact into disease.
### Mechanism
Every virulence factor serves one of a small number of steps. Some mediate attachment, without which an organism is cleared before it establishes. Some mediate invasion past a barrier. Some evade the immune response, by resisting phagocytosis or by disguise. Some cause damage directly, through toxins.

Reading an unfamiliar factor by asking which of those four steps it serves is more useful than memorising factors by organism, because the same step recurs across very different species.
### Key determinants
A rarely encountered organism can be highly virulent, and a ubiquitous one nearly harmless. Virulence describes what happens after contact; how often contact occurs is a separate question about exposure and transmission.

The two are easily confused because the organisms that cause the most disease in a population are often those that combine moderate virulence with very common exposure — but that is an observation about burden, not a definition of virulence.
### Clinical significance
Virulence explains why two patients exposed to the same organism can have very different illnesses, and why an organism carried harmlessly by many can be lethal in a few. It is the bridge between microbiology and the severity a clinician actually sees.

It also explains why strain matters. The possession of a particular factor is often what separates a dangerous strain from a harmless one within a single species, which is why outbreak investigation looks below the level of the species name.
### Common misconceptions
Equating virulence with how common an organism is. The measure concerns harm per encounter, not number of encounters.

Treating virulence as fixed for a species. It varies between strains, and the possession of a particular factor is often what separates a dangerous strain from a harmless one.
## hold_these
Virulence is the measure of the pathogenicity of an organism.
Factors produced by a microorganism that evoke disease are called virulence factors.
Virulence factors serve attachment, invasion, immune evasion, or damage.
## lose_the_mark
Equating virulence with prevalence. A rare organism can be highly virulent and a ubiquitous one nearly harmless.
Treating virulence as a fixed species property. It varies between strains of the same species.

## summary
Five routes, and each one names the precaution that closes it. This is the part of microbiology that changes what you do on a ward today.
## sections
### Definition
Transmission is how a pathogen reaches a new host. It is the step in the chain of infection that clinical practice can most reliably interrupt, which is why the routes are classified so carefully.

Each route implies a different barrier. Naming the route is therefore not a taxonomic exercise; it is the step that selects the precaution.
### Mechanism
Direct contact transmission occurs when microorganisms pass from one person to another without a contaminated intermediate. Indirect transmission occurs when transfer happens through a contaminated intermediate person or object.

Droplet transmission occurs when respiratory droplets carry the infection from the respiratory tract to susceptible mucosal surfaces. Airborne transmission occurs when droplet nuclei or small particles carrying infectious agents remain in the air over time and distance.

Vehicles are contaminated inanimate objects, such as surfaces, equipment or medications, that passively carry a pathogen from its reservoir to a new host. Vectors are living entities, such as mosquitoes, fleas or ticks, that carry a pathogen from a reservoir to a host or from host to host.
### Key determinants
These two are the pair most often confused, and the difference is physical. Droplets are large enough to fall out of the air within a short distance of the person producing them. Droplet nuclei and small particles do not fall; they remain suspended over time and distance.

The consequence is that distance and a surgical mask address droplet spread, while airborne spread requires attention to the air itself — ventilation, and respirators rather than surgical masks. Applying droplet precautions to an airborne organism leaves the route open.
Both carry a pathogen from a reservoir to a host, and the difference is whether the carrier is alive. A vehicle is an inanimate object and carries passively. A vector is a living entity.

The distinction matters because interrupting them is entirely different work: cleaning and sterilisation for vehicles, and environmental or ecological control for vectors.
### Clinical significance
Transmission is the link in the chain of infection that clinical practice interrupts most reliably. Reservoir and susceptibility are usually harder to change; the route is something a ward can act on today.

That is why naming the route is a clinical act rather than a taxonomic one. The route selects the precaution, and a precaution matched to the wrong route leaves the patient and everyone around them exposed while appearing to be protection.
### Common misconceptions
Conflating droplet with airborne spread. The precautions differ, and the difference is not a technicality.

Treating transmission as a property of the organism alone. The same organism may spread by more than one route, and the route that matters is the one operating in the setting you are in.

## summary
The difference between an organism being present and an organism causing disease — and the reason a positive culture is not an instruction to prescribe.
## sections
### Definition
Colonization is the presence of an organism on a non-sterile body surface, such as skin or mucosa, in the absence of disease. The organism is there, it may even be one capable of causing disease, and nothing is wrong.

Infection is the presence of an organism in an ordinarily sterile body site, or of an organism other than the normal flora for that location on a body surface. Two different things make a finding an infection: the site should have been sterile, or the organism does not belong there.
### Mechanism
The laboratory reports what grew. It cannot report whether what grew is causing the patient's illness, because that depends on the site, on what normally lives there, and on whether the patient is actually unwell in a way the organism explains.

This is why the same result means different things from different sites. An organism recovered from a normally sterile site carries weight that the same organism recovered from a mucosal surface does not.
### Key determinants
Two things decide whether a finding is colonisation or infection: the site the specimen came from, and whether the organism belongs to the normal flora of that site. Neither is a property of the organism itself.

The clinical picture is the third input, and in practice the deciding one. An organism recovered from a normally sterile site carries weight that the same organism recovered from a mucosal surface does not, and a patient who is well carries the finding differently from one who is not.
### Clinical significance
Treating a colonising organism exposes the patient to the adverse effects of a drug they do not need, and selects for resistance in the flora they will carry afterwards.

The harm is not hypothetical and it is not confined to that patient: resistant flora are transmissible, so the cost of treating colonisation is paid partly by people who were never the patient.
### Common misconceptions
Treating a positive culture as an indication to prescribe. A colonising organism is a normal finding at many sites.

Assuming that an organism known to be pathogenic must be causing disease wherever it is found. The definition turns on the site and the clinical picture, not on the organism's reputation.

## summary
Everything the laboratory can tell you is limited by what arrives in the container. Most microbiology errors are made before the specimen leaves the patient.
## sections
### Definition
Specimens should be representative of the disease process and collected in sufficient quantity to allow complete microbiologic examination. Both conditions fail commonly: a swab of the surface above an abscess is not representative of the abscess, and a volume too small for the tests requested forces the laboratory to choose between them.

The specimen is chosen from the signs and symptoms — it should answer the question actually being asked, and that question has to be settled before the container is filled.
### Mechanism
Specimens should be collected before the administration of antibiotics. Once an antimicrobial has been given, the organism may no longer be recoverable, and the culture that comes back negative does not mean the patient was not infected.

The rule is not absolute, because there are patients in whom treatment cannot wait. What the rule requires in that situation is that the specimen be taken first and quickly, not that treatment be delayed — the two are usually minutes apart, not hours.
### Key determinants
Where a specimen has to pass a contaminated surface, there are two options: disinfect the surface, or bypass it altogether.

Bypassing means reaching the site directly rather than through the colonised route — for example aspirating a lesion after disinfecting the skin, rather than swabbing the surface. When neither is done, the result describes the flora of the surface, and the report will look like an answer while describing the wrong thing.
### Clinical significance
Most microbiology errors are made before the specimen reaches the laboratory, and they are invisible in the report. A result from the wrong material or the wrong moment is not flagged as unreliable; it is reported as a result.

This is why specimen collection is treated as a clinical skill rather than a clerical step. The decision about what to send, from where, and when, determines the ceiling on everything the laboratory can subsequently say.
### Common misconceptions
Treating a negative culture after antibiotics as evidence against infection. It is evidence that nothing grew, which after treatment is expected.

Sending a surface swab when the question concerns deep infection. The report will be confidently wrong, and it is the specimen, not the laboratory, that made it so.

## summary
Growing the organism is still what turns a suspicion into an identification and a susceptibility report — and what a negative result does not mean.
## sections
### Definition
The cause of an infection is often confirmed by isolating and culturing the microorganism, either in artificial media or in a living host. Culture is what converts "something is growing" into a named organism that can be tested.

The identification is only half of what culture provides. The other half is the isolate itself, which is the material on which susceptibility testing is performed.
### Mechanism
Non-selective media permit the growth of many microorganisms. Selective media contain inhibitory substances that permit the isolation of specific types of microorganisms.

The choice follows from the specimen. From a normally sterile site, a non-selective medium is appropriate, because anything that grows is significant. From a site with abundant normal flora, a non-selective medium yields a lawn of irrelevant growth, and a selective medium is what makes the organism of interest visible at all.
### Key determinants
What grows depends on what was sent, what medium it was put on, and the conditions of incubation. An organism with unusual growth requirements will not appear on routine media however carefully the specimen was taken.

Time is the other determinant. Culture reports in days, which is why it usually confirms or corrects a treatment decision rather than making the first one.
### Clinical significance
Antimicrobial susceptibility testing is a laboratory procedure that identifies which antimicrobial regimen is effective for an individual patient. It is performed on the isolate, which is why it depends on culture having succeeded.

This is the practical reason culture retains its place even where faster methods exist: a molecular test may identify an organism in hours, but the susceptibility result that decides the prescription usually still comes from the grown isolate.
### Common misconceptions
Expecting culture to recover every organism. Some pathogens do not grow on routine media at all, so a negative culture excludes far less than it appears to.

Reading growth from a colonised site as the cause of illness. Culture reports what grew; whether it is responsible is a clinical judgement made against the site and the patient.

## summary
Finding the organism's nucleic acid instead of growing the organism — faster, more sensitive, and answering a subtly different question.
## sections
### Definition
The polymerase chain reaction is a laboratory nucleic acid amplification technique that synthesises DNA following thermal denaturation and primer annealing. Repeated cycles multiply a target sequence until there is enough of it to detect.

The organism itself is never grown. What is detected is its genetic material, and that difference is the source of both the method's advantages and its limits.
### Mechanism
PCR is regarded as the gold standard for diagnosing bacterial and viral infections because of its high sensitivity. It finds organisms present in very small numbers, and it finds organisms that cannot be cultured at all — which for much of virology is the ordinary situation rather than the exception.

It is also fast. A result in hours rather than days changes management in a way that a retrospectively correct culture result cannot.
### Key determinants
Amplification detects nucleic acid. Nucleic acid can persist after the organism is dead, and it can be present because the patient is colonised rather than infected.

So a positive result establishes that the sequence is present, not that live organism is causing disease. The clinical picture decides that, exactly as it does for a culture from a colonised site.
### Clinical significance
Speed is what changes management. A result in hours rather than days can start the right treatment or stop the wrong one while it still matters, which a retrospectively correct culture result cannot do.

The trade-off is that the question answered is narrower. A molecular test finds what it was designed to look for, so it answers "is this organism present" well and "what is causing this illness" only as well as the panel that was chosen.
### Common misconceptions
Reading a positive result as proof of active infection. It is proof that the target sequence was in the specimen.

Assuming a molecular result replaces culture entirely. Where treatment depends on a susceptibility report, an isolate is usually still needed.

## summary
Two words used interchangeably in speech and never in practice. Bacterial spores are the line between them.
## sections
### Definition
Sterilization is the chemical or physical destruction or removal of all pathogenic microorganisms and their spores from a surface. The phrase "and their spores" is the whole of the definition's clinical weight.

Disinfection is a process of using chemicals or wet pasteurization to eliminate pathogenic microorganisms. It eliminates pathogens; it is not required to destroy spores.
### Mechanism
High-level disinfectants kill all organisms except bacterial spores. That single exception is what separates the highest grade of disinfection from sterilization.

It follows that an item entering sterile tissue cannot be merely disinfected, however high the grade. Spores survive disinfection, and sterile tissue has no defence prepared for what survives.
### Key determinants
The decision follows from what the item will touch. An item entering sterile tissue or the vascular system requires sterilization. An item contacting intact mucosa requires disinfection at a level matched to that contact. An item touching only intact skin requires less again.

Framing the choice around the item's destination rather than around the item itself is what makes the rule usable for equipment nobody has memorised.
### Clinical significance
The choice follows from what the item will touch. An item entering sterile tissue or the vascular system requires sterilization. An item contacting intact mucosa requires disinfection at a level matched to that contact. An item touching only intact skin requires less again.

Framing the decision around the item's destination rather than around the item itself is what makes the rule usable for equipment nobody has memorised, and it is why the distinction between the two words is practical rather than academic.
### Common misconceptions
Using the two words interchangeably. They name different endpoints, and the difference is spores.

Assuming that a longer or stronger disinfection reaches sterilization. High-level disinfectants are defined by the exception they do not cover, and more of the same process does not remove the exception.
## hold_these
Sterilization is the chemical or physical destruction or removal of all pathogenic microorganisms and their spores from a surface.
Disinfection is a process of using chemicals or wet pasteurization to eliminate pathogenic microorganisms.
High-level disinfectants kill all organisms except bacterial spores.
An item entering sterile tissue requires sterilization, not disinfection.
## lose_the_mark
Using sterilization and disinfection interchangeably. Bacterial spores are the dividing line.
Assuming stronger or longer disinfection amounts to sterilization. The spore exception is definitional, not a matter of degree.
