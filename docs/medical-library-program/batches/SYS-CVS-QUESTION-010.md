# Item

## title
Where most of the blood is

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
The blood volume is distributed unevenly between the arterial system, the capillaries, the heart, the lungs and the systemic veins.

## question
Which compartment holds most of it?

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
The systemic arteries, since they are under the highest pressure

## explanation_a
High pressure is not high volume. The arteries are relatively stiff and narrow-bored, and hold a modest share; their job is to conduct and to distribute, not to store.

## answer_b
The capillaries, since they are by far the most numerous vessels

## explanation_b
Capillaries have an enormous total cross-sectional area and surface, which is what makes them good at exchange. Individually they are tiny, so their combined volume is small.

## answer_c
The heart chambers and the pulmonary circulation together

## explanation_c
These hold a real and clinically important fraction — which is why the pulmonary circulation can act as a buffer for left ventricular filling. It is not the largest share.

## answer_d
The systemic veins, which hold the majority and act as the body's blood reservoir

## explanation_d
Most of the blood volume sits in the systemic veins. That is why they are described as capacitance vessels: constricting them shifts a large volume towards the heart without needing any new fluid, which is the fastest way the body has of raising venous return.

# Item

## title
Mobilising the reservoir

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Sympathetic activity increases and the systemic veins constrict. Blood volume is unchanged.

## question
What is the effect on the circulation?

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Venous return falls, because narrower veins offer more resistance to flow

## explanation_a
Venous resistance does rise slightly, and that would argue for reduced flow. But the volume shift dominates by a wide margin, so the net effect runs the other way. This is chosen by reasoning about resistance and ignoring capacitance.

## answer_b
Nothing changes, because the same volume is still in the circulation

## explanation_b
Total volume is unchanged, but where it sits is not. Blood held in a distensible reservoir is unavailable to the heart; shifted centrally it becomes filling. Volume alone does not determine venous return.

## answer_c
Venous return and therefore cardiac output rise, because blood is displaced from the reservoir towards the heart

## explanation_c
Constricting a compartment that holds most of the blood pushes a large volume centrally. Filling pressure rises, venous return increases, and by the length–tension relationship stroke volume follows. This is why venoconstriction is such an effective early compensation for blood loss — it works without a drop of new fluid.

## answer_d
Arterial pressure falls, because blood is diverted away from the arterial side

## explanation_d
Blood moved from veins towards the heart is then ejected into the arteries, so arterial pressure rises rather than falls. This treats the circulation as a set of separate containers rather than a circuit.

# Item

## title
What high venous compliance means

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A given volume of blood is added first to the arterial system and then, in a separate experiment, to the venous system. The rise in pressure is far smaller in the veins.

## question
What property does that demonstrate?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
High compliance — the veins accommodate a large volume change for a small pressure change

## explanation_a
Compliance is the volume a vessel accepts per unit rise in pressure. Thin, distensible venous walls take up a great deal of blood before pressure climbs appreciably, which is precisely what makes them a reservoir rather than a conduit under tension.

## answer_b
Low resistance — the veins allow blood to flow through them easily

## explanation_b
Venous resistance is genuinely low, and it matters for venous return. But resistance concerns flow for a given pressure difference, while the experiment describes volume held for a given pressure. Two different properties of the same vessels.

## answer_c
High capacitance, which is a different property from compliance

## explanation_c
Capacitance and compliance are used almost interchangeably for vessels — capacitance describes the consequence, compliance the property producing it. Asserting they are different concepts is the error, not the pairing itself.

## answer_d
Low elastance, meaning the veins contain no elastic tissue at all

## explanation_d
Elastance is the reciprocal of compliance, so low elastance is correct — but veins do contain elastic tissue, simply less of it and arranged differently. The absolute claim is what fails.

# Item

## title
Compliance and the shape of a filling curve

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Pressure is plotted against volume for the venous system. The curve is shallow at first and becomes progressively steeper as more volume is added.

## question
What does the changing slope indicate?

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Compliance is constant; the curve steepens only because the axes are logarithmic

## explanation_a
The steepening is a real property of the vessel wall, not an artefact of scaling. Attributing a physiological change to a plotting convention avoids the observation.

## answer_b
Compliance falls as the vessel fills, so the reservoir function is progressively lost at high volumes

## explanation_b
A distensible wall accepts volume readily while there is slack in it, then stiffens as its collagen is recruited. The veins therefore buffer volume well up to a point and poorly beyond it. That is why a circulation already congested tolerates further volume so much worse than a normal one — the reservoir has already been spent.

## answer_c
Resistance increases as the veins distend, opposing further filling

## explanation_c
Distending a vessel lowers its resistance, since resistance falls steeply with radius. The reasoning has the direction backwards and answers with the wrong property.

## answer_d
The veins begin to constrict actively once a threshold volume is reached

## explanation_d
The curve describes a passive mechanical property measured without any change in tone. Introducing an active response converts a materials observation into a reflex.

# Item

## title
Venous pressure above the heart

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A person stands upright. Venous pressure is measured at several heights above and below the right atrium.

## question
How does it vary with height?

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
It is the same everywhere, because the venous system is a continuous low-pressure compartment

## explanation_a
Continuity does not abolish gravity. A connected column of fluid has a pressure that varies along its height, which is the whole basis of measuring venous pressure by looking at a vein's height.

## answer_b
It rises with height above the atrium, because blood must be lifted further

## explanation_b
This inverts the gradient. Blood above the heart is being pulled downwards by gravity, which lowers rather than raises the pressure in those veins.

## answer_c
It falls with height above the right atrium, and neck veins may collapse where the pressure inside falls below atmospheric

## explanation_c
In a standing person the weight of the blood column raises pressure below the heart and lowers it above. High enough in the neck, intraluminal pressure falls below atmospheric and the thin vein wall collapses. The height at which the column stands is therefore a direct reading of right atrial pressure — which is why the jugular veins are inspected at a defined angle.

## answer_d
It falls with height, and the veins remain open because their valves hold them distended

## explanation_d
Valves prevent backflow; they do not hold a vein open against a negative transmural pressure. This gives the valves a structural role they do not have.

## title
Why posture matters when inspecting the neck veins

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
The height of the visible venous column in the neck is used to estimate right atrial pressure. The patient's position is specified whenever this is done.

## question
Why must the position be specified?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The measurement is a vertical height above the right atrium, so it is only interpretable once the atrium's position is fixed

## explanation_a
What is being read is the height of the blood column above the reference point. Change the patient's angle and the atrium moves relative to the neck, so the same venous pressure gives a different visible height. Fixing the position fixes the reference, which is what makes the observation a measurement rather than an impression.

Lying flat increases venous return, which genuinely raises right atrial pressure

## explanation_b
Posture does redistribute blood and does affect filling, so this is not wrong as physiology. But the reason for specifying position is the reference point for the measurement, not an underlying change in the quantity.

## answer_c
The veins are only visible when the patient is upright

## explanation_c
Lying flat usually makes the veins more visible, not less — often filling the neck entirely, which is why that position is unhelpful for estimating a height.

## answer_d
Sitting upright compresses the internal jugular vein against the clavicle

## explanation_d
No such compression occurs with posture alone. This invents a mechanical obstruction where the issue is geometric.

## title
Why the jugular veins pulsate

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Pressure waves generated in the right atrium can be seen in the veins of the neck.

## question
What allows them to be transmitted there?

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Valves in the internal jugular vein open during atrial systole, letting the wave pass

## explanation_a
This invents valves whose absence is the point. If valves were present and opened selectively, the waveform would be interrupted rather than faithfully transmitted.

## answer_b
The carotid artery lies adjacent and transmits its pulse to the vein

## explanation_b
Arterial pulsation is transmitted to the overlying tissues and is exactly what must be distinguished from the venous waveform at the bedside. It is not the source of the venous pulse, and confusing the two is the classic bedside error.

## answer_c
The wave travels backwards through the pulmonary circulation and reaches the neck by that route

## explanation_c
The pulmonary circulation lies on the other side of the heart entirely, downstream of the right ventricle. This routes the wave through the wrong circuit.

## answer_d
There are no valves between the right atrium and the venae cavae, so the atrial pressure wave passes freely into the neck veins

## explanation_d
The absence of valves between the right atrium and the great veins leaves an uninterrupted fluid column from the atrium to the neck. Pressure changes generated by atrial filling and contraction are therefore transmitted directly, which is what makes the jugular venous pulse a window onto right atrial events.

## title
Telling a venous pulse from an arterial one

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Two pulsations are visible in the neck. One is a single sharp outward movement per cardiac cycle; the other has more than one deflection per cycle and changes in height when the patient's position is altered.

## question
Which is venous, and on what reasoning?

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The first, because arterial pulsation cannot be seen through the skin of the neck

## explanation_a
Carotid pulsation is frequently visible, particularly in a thin or hyperdynamic patient. Denying that it can be seen removes the very difficulty the distinction exists to resolve.

## answer_b
The second, because the atrial pressure waveform has several components per cycle and a venous column's height depends on posture

## explanation_b
The right atrium produces several pressure deflections in one cycle — filling, contraction, and the movement of the valve plane — and all are transmitted up an unvalved column. An artery gives one ejection pulse. And because the venous reading is the height of a fluid column above the atrium, changing the patient's angle changes it, while an arterial pulse does not move in this way. Two independent features, both following from what the venous column is.

## answer_c
The second, because venous blood is under higher pressure and therefore moves more

## explanation_c
Venous pressure is far lower than arterial, which is why the venous pulsation is a gentle undulation rather than a forceful impulse. The conclusion is right and the reason is inverted.

## answer_d
Neither can be identified without ultrasound

## explanation_d
The two are routinely distinguished at the bedside by exactly the features described. Deferring to imaging discards a reliable clinical observation.

## title
Metabolites and capillary perfusion

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A tissue becomes metabolically active. Metabolites accumulate locally.

## question
What is the effect on the microcirculation?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
More capillaries are perfused and local vasomotion increases, raising the surface available for exchange

## explanation_a
Metabolites relax the precapillary vessels, so channels that were intermittently or barely perfused open up. Recruiting capillaries increases the exchange surface and shortens the distance from any cell to the nearest one — a better match to demand than simply pushing more blood through the same channels.

## answer_b
Flow through each perfused capillary rises, with no change in how many are open

## explanation_b
Velocity does increase, and that is part of the response. But recruitment matters more, because raising velocity in a fixed set of capillaries shortens transit time without improving access to the tissue.

## answer_c
Capillaries constrict to raise local pressure and drive exchange

## explanation_c
Capillaries have no smooth muscle of their own to constrict with, and raising local pressure would favour filtration rather than exchange generally. This gives the capillary a contractile capacity it lacks.

## answer_d
Arteriovenous anastomoses open, bypassing the capillary bed

## explanation_d
Shunting is the opposite of what an active tissue needs — it moves blood past the exchange vessels. Anastomoses serve thermoregulation, not metabolic supply.

## title
Why local control is a good design

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Blood flow to a tissue can be governed centrally by the nervous system or locally by the products of the tissue's own metabolism.

## question
What does the local mechanism achieve that central control cannot?

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
It responds faster, since no nerve conduction is involved

## explanation_a
Nerve conduction is very fast, and reflex vasoconstriction occurs within a second or two. Metabolic vasodilatation is not quicker; speed is not what it contributes.

## answer_b
It overrides the sympathetic system entirely, so central control is redundant in active tissue

## explanation_b
Local metabolic dilatation does override constrictor drive in working muscle, which is a real and important effect. But central control remains essential for distributing flow between competing beds, so calling it redundant overstates the case.

## answer_c
It matches supply to each tissue's own demand automatically, without the nervous system needing to know what any tissue requires

## explanation_c
The signal is generated by the demand itself: the more active a tissue, the more metabolite it produces and the more it dilates. No central assessment of any tissue's needs is required. Central control sets the pressure and the broad distribution; local control fine-tunes each bed to its own activity.

## answer_d
It maintains flow constant regardless of demand, protecting the tissue from over-perfusion

## explanation_d
Constancy in the face of changing pressure is autoregulation, a related mechanism. Metabolic control does the opposite of holding flow constant against demand — it varies flow with demand.

# Item

## title
Angiotensin II at the vessel wall

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Angiotensin II acts directly on vascular smooth muscle.

## question
Which vessels does it constrict?

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Arterioles only, since veins have too little smooth muscle to respond

## explanation_a
Veins do carry smooth muscle and do constrict, which is what allows venoconstriction to shift the blood reservoir at all. Underestimating the venous media is the error here.

## answer_b
Both arterioles and veins

## explanation_b
Angiotensin II constricts arterioles and veins alike. Constricting arterioles raises peripheral resistance; constricting veins shifts blood centrally and raises filling. Acting on both sides of the circulation means it raises pressure through resistance and through output at once.

## answer_c

## subject

## status

## vignette
Thirst can be triggered by a rise in plasma osmolality or by a fall in blood volume.

Why does the body need both?

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
The osmotic route is fast and the volume route slow, so together they cover all timescales

## explanation_a
Both operate over broadly similar timescales, and neither is notably faster. Timescale is not what separates them.

## answer_b
The volume route corrects the osmotic route when it is in error

## explanation_b
Neither corrects the other; they report different things. Treating one as a check on the other assumes they measure the same quantity.

## answer_c
The osmotic route responds to water loss and the volume route to salt loss

Osmoreceptors report concentration, which changes when water is lost without solute. Two independent failure modes need two independent sensors, and haemorrhage is exactly the case the osmotic route alone would miss.

A

Blood enters freely but leaves poorly, so it pools in the capillaries and effective circulating volume falls further

## vignette

That the compensations were misdirected from the start and worsened the outcome

The stem describes active changes in tone at two sites behaving differently from one another, which is the opposite of passivity. This denies the observation rather than explaining it.
