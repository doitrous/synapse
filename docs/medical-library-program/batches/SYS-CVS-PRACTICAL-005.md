### How a working cell responds
Mr Galal Mounir, 66, is on the coronary care unit after an anterior infarct. The monitor shows occasional beats that are much wider than the rest.
Concept: CON-CVS-77AA16A0BD5F70
Difficulty: Easy
Q: A working ventricular myocyte is described as having a fast response. What does that mean?
*= It depolarises rapidly through a fast inward sodium current once threshold is reached
Why: Correct. Working myocytes have a rapid upstroke carried by a fast sodium current, which is what allows the impulse to move quickly through the muscle.
* It contracts more quickly than a pacemaker cell
Why: Confuses an electrical description with a mechanical one. Fast and slow response refer to the upstroke of the action potential, not to contraction speed.
* It reaches threshold spontaneously and faster than nodal tissue
Why: Describes automaticity, which working myocytes do not normally show, and which is a property of the nodal cells the term is contrasted with.
* It repolarises rapidly, giving a very short refractory period
Why: The cardiac action potential is notable for a long plateau and a long refractory period, which is the opposite of this.
Rationale: Fast response describes the upstroke, and the upstroke sets conduction velocity. That is the link the rest of the case depends on.

### How the impulse moves through muscle
Concept: CON-CVS-7763A99040CEB7
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: The impulse reaches ventricular muscle that has been electrically isolated from the conducting system by the infarct. How does activation spread there?
*= From cell to cell through the myocardium, without needing the conducting system
Why: Correct. Working myocytes activate their neighbours directly, so an impulse can spread through muscle alone — more slowly, but it spreads.
* It cannot spread at all; muscle can only be activated by Purkinje fibres
Why: The misconception that the conducting system is the only route. It is the fast route, not the only one.
* It spreads through the interstitial fluid as a passive current with no cell involvement
Why: Current does flow in the interstitium, but activation is a cell-to-cell process, not a passive diffusion of charge through fluid.
* It spreads backwards up the conducting system and re-enters from the atria
Why: Retrograde conduction can occur, but it is not the mechanism by which muscle adjacent to muscle is activated.
Rationale: Cell-to-cell activation is why an ectopic beat still produces a coordinated, if slow, ventricular contraction, and it is the mechanism that makes the next question about velocity worth asking.

### Why that makes the complex wide
Concept: CON-CVS-2AD0E4A433C94D
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Conduction through working myocardium is considerably slower than through the specialised conducting system. What does that predict about a beat arising in ventricular muscle?
*= It takes longer to activate both ventricles, so the QRS complex is wide
Why: Correct. A beat that spreads mostly muscle-to-muscle activates the ventricles slowly, and the QRS records the whole of that activation, so the complex is broad.
* It activates the ventricles simultaneously, so the QRS is narrow but tall
Why: Simultaneous activation is what the conducting system achieves; bypassing it is precisely what loses that simultaneity.
* It slows the heart rate, so the intervals between complexes lengthen
Why: Confuses conduction velocity within a beat with the rate at which beats occur. They are independent.
* It shortens the PR interval, because the atria are activated late
Why: Reasons about the wrong interval. The width of the QRS reflects ventricular activation, not atrioventricular conduction.
Rationale: A wide QRS is a statement about the route, not about the rate: the impulse took the slow, muscle-to-muscle path. That single inference is what makes broad and narrow complexes worth distinguishing at the bedside.

### Reading the axis
Concept: CON-CVS-8BB9DF2D4A4452
Difficulty: Moderate
His underlying rhythm is sinus. On the 12-lead, leads I and II show positive waves of equal size.
Q: What does that tell you about the mean depolarisation vector?
*= It lies at about +30 degrees
Why: Correct. A vector at +30° projects equally onto the axes of leads I and II, so both record equally large positive waves.
* It lies at 0 degrees, along the axis of lead I
Why: A vector along lead I would give the largest wave in lead I specifically, not an equal wave in leads I and II.
* It lies at +60 degrees, along the axis of lead II
Why: The mirror-image error: at +60° lead II would dominate rather than match lead I.
* The axis cannot be determined without looking at the chest leads
Why: The limb leads define the frontal plane axis. The chest leads address the horizontal plane, which is a different question.
Rationale: The size of a deflection is the projection of the vector onto that lead's axis. Equal projections onto two known axes place the vector between them, which is what makes axis determination a geometric exercise rather than a memorised table.

### The other way to start a beat early
Concept: CON-CVS-C09D8C1327DFB7
Difficulty: Hard
His QT interval is long, and the premature beats appear to begin on the T wave.
Q: A depolarisation arising before repolarisation is complete is which type, and why is a long QT interval relevant?
*= An early afterdepolarisation — the prolonged repolarisation gives it time to arise and reach threshold
Why: Correct. Early afterdepolarisations interrupt repolarisation, and a longer repolarisation phase gives a longer window in which one can develop and trigger an action potential.
* A delayed afterdepolarisation, which arises once repolarisation is complete
Why: The right family, the wrong member. Delayed afterdepolarisations follow completed repolarisation and are associated with calcium loading rather than with a long QT.
* A normal pacemaker potential appearing in ventricular muscle
Why: A pacemaker potential is a spontaneous slow depolarisation from rest, not one that interrupts repolarisation of a preceding beat.
* A re-entrant circuit, which requires a fixed anatomical loop
Why: Re-entry is a real mechanism for arrhythmia, but it is a circuit rather than an abnormal depolarisation within a single cell.
Rationale: The two afterdepolarisations differ by timing, and the timing points at the cause: early ones arise during a prolonged repolarisation, delayed ones after it. Reading the QT interval is what tells you which you are looking at.
## debrief
The case runs one chain from the cell to the trace: a fast upstroke gives fast conduction, the conducting system exploits it, and a beat that bypasses that system must travel muscle to muscle and so writes a wide complex. The last two decisions add the other axis of reading an ECG — direction, from the size of the deflections, and timing, from where in repolarisation an early beat arises.

### Naming the pattern
Mrs Nagwa Fahmy, 59, has an inferior infarct. The angiography report describes a balanced coronary circulation.
Concept: CON-CVS-69303DBED9D05C
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What does a balanced coronary pattern mean?
*= The right and left coronary arteries contribute roughly equally to the posterior part of the heart
Why: Correct. In a balanced pattern neither vessel dominates the posterior supply; both contribute to the territory that a single dominant vessel would otherwise supply alone.
* The two coronary arteries are of equal diameter at their origins
Why: Dominance is defined by which vessel supplies the posterior territory, not by the calibre of the vessels at their origin.
* Blood flow is equal in systole and diastole
Why: Confuses balance between the two arteries with balance across the cardiac cycle. Coronary flow is in fact greatest in diastole.
* The heart is supplied equally by coronary arteries and by the anterior cardiac veins
Why: Veins drain; they do not supply. This mixes the arterial and venous sides entirely.
Rationale: Dominance is a statement about who supplies the back of the heart. Naming the pattern is what lets you predict which vessel an inferior territory depends on.

### The artery along the right border
Concept: CON-CVS-44B74F73B50D20
Difficulty: Easy
Q: The report also mentions the right marginal artery. What does it supply?
*= The right ventricle, running along the acute margin towards the apex
Why: Correct. The right marginal branch runs along the acute margin of the heart and supplies the right ventricular wall.
* The left ventricular free wall, along the obtuse margin
Why: Describes the position of a left marginal branch of the circumflex, on the other border of the heart.
* The right atrium and the sinoatrial node
Why: These are supplied by other branches of the right coronary artery, not by the marginal branch running along the ventricular border.
* The interventricular septum, from the right side
Why: The septum is supplied by the interventricular arteries running in the grooves, not by a marginal branch on the free border.
Rationale: The marginal branches follow the borders and supply free wall; the interventricular arteries follow the grooves and supply septum. Which groove or border a vessel runs in is what defines its territory.

### Where the venous blood collects
Concept: CON-CVS-7CD4CEA27DF356
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: On the posterior surface, which structures run together in the coronary sulcus, and how do they relate?
*= The circumflex artery and the coronary sinus, running together in the posterior part of the coronary sulcus
Why: Correct. The circumflex continues into the posterior coronary sulcus, where the coronary sinus also lies, so the two run together on the posterior surface.
* The anterior interventricular artery and the great cardiac vein
Why: A true pairing, but in the anterior interventricular groove, not the posterior coronary sulcus.
* The right coronary artery and the small cardiac vein, on the posterior surface
Why: They do accompany one another, but along the right part of the sulcus and the acute margin rather than in the posterior segment described.
* The posterior interventricular artery and the coronary sinus
Why: The posterior interventricular artery runs in the interventricular groove, which is a different groove from the sulcus the sinus occupies.
Rationale: The grooves organise the whole of the cardiac surface anatomy: arteries and their accompanying veins share them. Knowing which pair occupies which groove is what makes an angiogram or a posterior view of the heart readable.

### Where it drains
Concept: CON-CVS-480B355B4BFDA0
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: The coronary sinus returns most of the heart's venous blood. Where does it open?
*= Into the right atrium, between the opening of the inferior vena cava and the atrioventricular orifice
Why: Correct. The coronary sinus opens into the right atrium in that position, usually guarded by a rudimentary valve.
* Into the right ventricle, just below the tricuspid valve
Why: Would return coronary venous blood past the atrium entirely, and misplaces the opening by one chamber.
* Into the superior vena cava, before it reaches the atrium
Why: Confuses the systemic venous return above the heart with the heart's own drainage.
* Into the left atrium, alongside the pulmonary veins
Why: Would deliver deoxygenated blood into the systemic circulation. The mistake usually comes from placing the sinus on the left because the circumflex is a left-sided vessel.
Rationale: The coronary sinus drains into the right atrium, which is why the heart's own venous blood joins the systemic venous return before the lungs. Its position between the caval opening and the atrioventricular orifice is what makes it findable on a specimen and on imaging.

### The general rule, and its exception
Concept: CON-CVS-41752651BA015F
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What is the usual relationship between the cardiac veins and the coronary arteries, and which vein does not follow it?
*= Veins usually accompany the arteries in the grooves; the anterior cardiac veins are the exception, crossing the surface to reach the right atrium directly
Why: Correct. Most cardiac veins run alongside an artery and drain into the coronary sinus; the anterior cardiac veins cross the anterior surface of the right ventricle and open separately into the right atrium.
* Veins never accompany arteries in the heart, and all drain independently
Why: Overturns the general rule to accommodate the exception. Most cardiac veins do accompany an artery.
* Veins accompany arteries without exception, and every cardiac vein reaches the coronary sinus
Why: The rule with no exception, which misses the anterior cardiac veins and the reason a surgeon must know about them.
* Veins accompany the nerves rather than the arteries in the cardiac grooves
Why: Invents a neurovascular arrangement that does not describe the heart's surface anatomy.
Rationale: The rule makes the anatomy learnable and the exception makes it clinically real: veins that bypass the coronary sinus and open directly into the right atrium are encountered whenever the anterior surface of the right ventricle is operated on.
## debrief
The case is one surface, read twice: once for the arteries and once for the veins that accompany them. Dominance names who supplies the back; the grooves name where each pair runs; and the coronary sinus collects nearly all of it into the right atrium. The anterior cardiac veins are the exception that is worth carrying, because they are the ones that will not be where the rule says.

### What holds a valve cusp down
Mr Fawzy Haddad, 63, is four days after an inferior infarct when he becomes acutely breathless. A loud new murmur is audible at the apex.
Concept: CON-CVS-4ABA568F6D69F1
Difficulty: Easy
Q: What are the papillary muscles attached to at each end?
*= The ventricular wall at their base, and the valve cusps through the chordae tendineae at their tips
Why: Correct. Each papillary muscle arises from the ventricular wall and its tip gives chordae tendineae to the cusps of the atrioventricular valve.
* The ventricular wall at their base, and the fibrous skeleton at their tips
Why: The fibrous skeleton anchors the valve ring, not the papillary muscle tips. The tips connect to the cusps through chordae.
* The interventricular septum at both ends, spanning the cavity
Why: Describes a trabecula, and in the right ventricle the septomarginal trabecula does span the cavity — which is why this is chosen — but it is not the papillary arrangement.
* The valve cusps directly, without intervening chordae
Why: Removes the chordae, which are the structures that actually fail in the case being described.
Rationale: Wall to muscle to chorda to cusp: the chain is what keeps the cusp from being blown back into the atrium in systole. Naming each link is what makes it possible to say which one has broken.

### Which cusps a single muscle serves
Concept: CON-CVS-334F674BA1244E
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: To which cusps do the chordae of one papillary muscle attach?
*= To the adjacent halves of two neighbouring cusps, so each muscle steadies two cusps at once
Why: Correct. Chordae from one papillary muscle attach to the adjoining halves of two cusps, which is why one muscle failing affects the coaptation of two.
* To one cusp only, so each cusp has its own dedicated muscle
Why: The intuitive one-to-one arrangement, and the reason students underestimate how much a single rupture disturbs.
* To all cusps equally, so the valve is supported uniformly
Why: Overstates the redundancy in the opposite direction and would make a single rupture almost inconsequential.
* To the valve ring rather than to the cusps themselves
Why: The ring is part of the fibrous skeleton and is not what the chordae hold.
Rationale: One muscle, two cusps, adjacent halves. That arrangement is why the loss of a single papillary muscle produces immediate and severe regurgitation rather than a small local leak.

### The largest of them
Concept: CON-CVS-25C53C42DCA293
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: Which papillary muscle is the largest, and why does that matter here?
*= The anterior one, and its size means its rupture causes correspondingly severe valve failure
Why: Correct. The anterior papillary muscle is the largest, so it supports the most valve tissue and its failure is the most consequential.
* The posterior one, which is largest because it bears the greater load
Why: Reverses the anatomy. The posterior papillary muscle is the more vulnerable one because of its blood supply, which is a different point from size.
* The septal one, which is largest in both ventricles
Why: A septal papillary muscle is a feature of the right ventricle and is small; the left ventricle has anterior and posterior muscles.
* They are of equal size, and rupture of any is equally severe
Why: Removes the distinction the question is testing, and the difference in size and in supply is exactly what makes rupture predictable.
Rationale: Size and blood supply are separate risk factors and both matter: the largest muscle supports the most valve, while the one with the least redundant supply is the one most likely to infarct.

### The valve on the other side
Concept: CON-CVS-5E6A3445314E64
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: The right-sided valve is examined at post-mortem teaching. How is a tricuspid cusp attached?
*= Its base attaches to the fibrous ring, and its free edge and ventricular surface receive chordae tendineae from papillary muscles
Why: Correct. Each cusp is anchored at its base to the fibrous ring, while the chordae take hold of its free margin and ventricular surface.
* Its base attaches to the papillary muscle and its free edge to the fibrous ring
Why: Reverses the two ends. The ring holds the base; the chordae hold the free edge.
* It is attached only at its base, and closes by pressure alone
Why: Pressure does close the valve, but unrestrained cusps would evert into the atrium. The chordae are what prevent it.
* It attaches to the septomarginal trabecula along its whole length
Why: The septomarginal trabecula carries conducting tissue to the anterior papillary muscle in the right ventricle, which is why it is recognised — but it is not the cusp's attachment.
Rationale: Both atrioventricular valves are built the same way: ring at the base, chordae at the edge, papillary muscle at the far end of the chorda. Learning the pattern once means it transfers to either side of the heart.

### Naming the sound
Concept: CON-CVS-625E445610F27A
Difficulty: Hard
Q: The murmur is loud, but his blood pressure is falling and the echocardiogram shows severe regurgitation. What does the loudness of a murmur indicate?
*= Turbulent flow — its loudness reflects the turbulence, not the severity of the lesion
Why: Correct. A murmur is the sound of turbulent flow. Loudness depends on flow and pressure gradient, so a severe lesion with a low gradient or a falling output can be quiet, and a trivial one can be loud.
* The severity of the valve lesion, reliably in proportion
Why: The most damaging misconception in valve assessment, and the reason a quiet murmur in a shocked patient is misread as reassuring.
* The pressure in the left atrium alone
Why: A single pressure does not determine the sound; it is generated by flow across a gradient.
* The presence of valve calcification
Why: Calcification alters the quality and may cause a murmur, but loudness is not a measure of it.
Rationale: The murmur tells you flow is turbulent, and nothing more. Reading severity from loudness is what leads a deteriorating patient with a softening murmur to be judged to be improving.
## debrief
The case is one anatomical chain — wall, papillary muscle, chorda, cusp — followed until it breaks. The arrangement of chordae onto adjacent halves of two cusps explains why the failure of one muscle is not a small leak. The final decision is a deliberate warning: the loudness of the murmur measures turbulence, and a falling blood pressure is the more reliable statement about severity.

### Where the aorta should arise
A newborn boy is deeply cyanosed at two hours of age. He is not distressed and his chest is clear. The registrar suspects transposition of the great arteries.
Concept: CON-CVS-32F6DDF825E732
Difficulty: Easy
Q: In a normal heart, where does the aortic orifice lie and how is its valve constructed?
*= At the outlet of the left ventricle, guarded by three semilunar cusps
Why: Correct. The aortic orifice sits at the left ventricular outflow and its valve has three semilunar cusps.
* At the outlet of the right ventricle, guarded by three semilunar cusps
Why: Describes the pulmonary orifice. The cusp arrangement is right, the ventricle is not — which is exactly the swap the case is about.
* At the outlet of the left ventricle, guarded by two cusps and chordae tendineae
Why: Adds chordae, which belong to the atrioventricular valves. Semilunar valves have no chordae.
* At the inlet of the left ventricle, alongside the mitral orifice
Why: Places an outflow valve at the inflow. The two orifices are adjacent, which is why this is tempting, but only one is an outlet.
Rationale: The aorta leaves the left ventricle and the pulmonary trunk leaves the right. Establishing the normal connection is what makes the abnormality in this baby describable.

### The pockets behind the cusps
Concept: CON-CVS-CE1750C05B9C84
Difficulty: Moderate
Q: What are the aortic sinuses, and what is their significance?
*= Dilatations of the aortic wall behind the cusps, two of which give rise to the coronary arteries
Why: Correct. The sinuses sit behind the cusps; the right and left coronary arteries arise from two of them, and the sinuses help the cusps fall closed in diastole.
* The spaces between the cusps through which blood passes in systole
Why: Describes the orifice rather than the sinus. The sinuses are recesses in the wall behind the cusps.
* The recesses of the pericardial cavity behind the ascending aorta
Why: Confuses the aortic sinuses with the pericardial sinuses, a genuinely easy trap given the shared word.
* Dilatations of the pulmonary trunk that give rise to the bronchial arteries
Why: Puts the sinuses on the wrong vessel and invents an origin for the bronchial arteries, which arise from the descending aorta.
Rationale: The sinuses are why coronary filling happens in diastole: as the cusps fall back, blood in the sinuses is pressed into the coronary orifices behind them. It is one of the few places where a small anatomical detail directly determines a physiological event.

### What has gone wrong here
Concept: CON-CVS-770A1368E72DFB
Difficulty: Moderate
The echocardiogram confirms the diagnosis.
Q: In transposition of the great arteries, from which ventricle does the aorta arise, and what is the consequence?
*= From the right ventricle, so systemic venous blood is returned to the body without passing through the lungs
Why: Correct. With the aorta arising from the right ventricle and the pulmonary trunk from the left, the two circulations run in parallel rather than in series, and survival depends on mixing between them.
* From the left ventricle, but with the coronary arteries arising abnormally
Why: An anomalous coronary origin is a different lesion. In transposition the ventriculo-arterial connection itself is reversed.
* From both ventricles, overriding the interventricular septum
Why: Describes an overriding aorta, a feature of a different congenital lesion, not the reversed connection of transposition.
* From the right atrium, bypassing the ventricle entirely
Why: No great artery arises from an atrium. The error usually comes from reasoning about deoxygenated blood rather than about the connection.
Rationale: Transposition is defined by the connection, not by the vessels themselves. Two circulations in parallel is what makes the cyanosis profound while the lungs remain clear, which is what separates this baby clinically from a respiratory cause.

### Why the lungs are clear
Concept: CON-CVS-AE28ABD8CE2B0B
Difficulty: Hard
Q: He is deeply cyanosed but is not breathless and his lungs are clear. Why does that combination point towards the heart rather than the lungs?
*= Blood is bypassing the systemic circulation rather than failing to be oxygenated, so the lungs themselves are working normally
Why: Correct. In transposition the pulmonary circulation oxygenates blood perfectly well; the oxygenated blood simply returns to the lungs instead of reaching the body. Nothing is wrong with gas exchange, so there is no respiratory distress.
* The infant is too young to mount a respiratory response to hypoxia
Why: Newborns do respond to hypoxia. This attributes a diagnostic finding to immaturity and would remove the value of the observation.
* The cyanosis is peripheral rather than central, and so does not reflect arterial oxygenation
Why: Deep cyanosis at two hours is central. Distinguishing the two is important, but it does not explain this presentation.
* Pulmonary blood flow is reduced, so there is nothing in the lungs to hear
Why: Pulmonary blood flow in transposition is typically normal or increased, not reduced, which is part of why the chest is clear rather than quiet.
Rationale: Cyanosis with a clear chest and no distress is a circulatory routing problem until proved otherwise. The heart's position in the middle mediastinum, between the two lungs, is the reason a cardiac lesion can present with a completely normal respiratory examination.

### Putting the connections together
Concept: CON-CVS-930E4596CA5673
Difficulty: Challenging
Q: The consultant asks why the normal spiral relationship between the great vessels is relevant to understanding this lesion.
*= Normally the outflow tract spirals as it divides, which carries the aorta to the left ventricle; when it fails to spiral the vessels arise in parallel from the wrong ventricles
Why: Correct. The spiral relationship of the pulmonary trunk and ascending aorta reflects a spiral division of the outflow tract in development; failure of that spiralling leaves the great arteries running in parallel and connected to the wrong ventricles.
* The spiral is a post-natal change, so it has not yet occurred at two hours of age
Why: The relationship is established well before birth, not in the hours after it.
* The spiral relationship is what allows the coronary arteries to arise from the aorta
Why: The coronaries arise from the aortic sinuses regardless of the spiral, so this misattributes their origin.
* The spiral is a normal variant, present in only some hearts, and its absence is not significant
Why: Treats a constant feature as a variant. Its absence here is exactly what is significant.
Rationale: The normal spiral and its failure are the same fact seen twice: it explains why the vessels cross each other in a normal heart, and why they run in parallel in this one. Anatomy that seems like a curiosity in a healthy specimen turns out to be the explanation for a lesion.
## debrief
The case reads a congenital lesion through normal anatomy: the orifices and their cusps, the sinuses behind them, and the spiral division that carries each vessel to the right ventricle. Cyanosis with a clear chest and no distress is the clinical signature of blood taking the wrong route rather than failing to be oxygenated, and that inference is available at the bedside before any imaging.
