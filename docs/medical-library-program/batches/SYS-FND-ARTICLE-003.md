## aliases
Single-gene inheritance | Monogenic inheritance
## reading_time
7
## summary
Whether a single-gene condition appears depends on how many altered copies a person carries, and on which chromosome the gene sits. Four patterns cover almost everything examined at this level.
## sections
### Definition
A Mendelian condition is one determined by a single gene. Which pattern it follows depends on two things: whether one altered copy is enough or two are needed, and whether the gene sits on an autosome or on the X chromosome.

One altered copy of the gene in each cell is sufficient for a person to be affected by an autosomal dominant disorder. In autosomal recessive inheritance, variants occur in both copies of the gene in each cell.
### Mechanism
In a dominant condition an affected person carries one altered and one normal copy, so each child has a one-in-two chance of inheriting the altered one. Affected people appear in every generation, and the condition does not skip.

In a recessive condition both parents are usually unaffected carriers. Each child has a one-in-four chance of inheriting both altered copies, a one-in-two chance of being a carrier, and a one-in-four chance of inheriting neither. The condition appears to skip generations because carriers are silent.

X-linked conditions follow from males having only one X. A male with an altered X-linked gene has no second copy to compensate, so X-linked recessive conditions affect males far more often, and an affected father passes the altered X to all his daughters and none of his sons.
### Key determinants
Consanguinity raises the risk of recessive conditions specifically, because related parents are more likely to carry the same rare variant. This matters in Egyptian practice, where consanguineous marriage is common in some communities, and it is why the family history should ask about it directly.

Penetrance and expressivity complicate the picture: a person can carry a dominant variant and show no features at all, or show them far more mildly than a relative with the same variant.
### Clinical significance
The pattern determines the recurrence risk you give a family, which is the single most useful number a genetic consultation produces.

It also determines who else in the family should be offered testing. A dominant condition puts every first-degree relative at one-in-two risk; a recessive one puts the siblings at risk but usually not the children.
### Common misconceptions
An unaffected parent is not a non-carrier. In a recessive condition both parents are usually unaffected carriers, and the one-in-four risk applies afresh to every pregnancy — it is not reduced by having already had an unaffected child.
## hold_these
One altered copy of the gene in each cell is sufficient for a person to be affected by an autosomal dominant disorder.
In autosomal recessive inheritance, variants occur in both copies of the gene in each cell.
Dominant conditions appear in every generation; recessive ones appear to skip, because carriers are silent.
Consanguinity raises the risk of recessive conditions specifically.
## lose_the_mark
Treating an unaffected parent as a non-carrier. In a recessive condition both parents are usually carriers.
Believing the one-in-four risk falls after an unaffected child. Every pregnancy carries the same risk independently.

## reading_time

## summary
When a whole chromosome is gained or lost, hundreds of genes change dosage at once. That is why these conditions affect many systems together, and why they usually do not run in families.
## sections
### Definition
A chromosomal disorder is caused by a change in the number or the structure of chromosomes, rather than by a variant inside a single gene. A gain or loss in the number of chromosomes from the normal 46 is called aneuploidy.
### Mechanism
Aneuploidy usually arises from non-disjunction: a chromosome pair fails to separate during meiosis, so one gamete receives two copies and the other none. Because this is a new event in that gamete, the parents' chromosomes are normal and the condition does not recur in a Mendelian pattern.

Structural abnormalities are different. A translocation moves material between chromosomes; if it is balanced the carrier is healthy, but their gametes may be unbalanced, and that does carry a recurrence risk worth counselling.
### Key determinants
Maternal age is the strongest determinant of non-disjunction, and the risk rises steeply in the later reproductive years.

Dosage explains the clinical picture. A whole extra chromosome changes the dose of every gene on it, which is why these conditions affect several body systems at once rather than one organ.
### Clinical significance
Most autosomal aneuploidies are lethal before birth. The ones compatible with survival involve the smaller chromosomes or the sex chromosomes, where the dosage change the fetus must tolerate is smaller.

Distinguishing a new aneuploidy from a familial translocation matters, because the recurrence risk is very different. That distinction is made on the karyotype, not on the clinical picture.
### Common misconceptions
Expecting a family pattern. Most aneuploidies are new events and do not recur, whereas a balanced translocation in a parent does carry a real recurrence risk — so "chromosomal" says nothing about heritability on its own.
## hold_these
A gain or loss in the number of chromosomes from the normal 46 is called aneuploidy.
Non-disjunction produces a new event, so most aneuploidies do not recur in a family.
A balanced translocation in a parent does carry a recurrence risk, and is found on the karyotype.
Whole-chromosome dosage change is why these conditions affect many systems at once.
## lose_the_mark
Assuming a chromosomal condition runs in the family. Most are new non-disjunction events.
Ignoring the karyotype in a family with recurrence. A balanced parental translocation is exactly what it would show.

## aliases
Maternal inheritance
## reading_time
5
## summary
A pedigree where every child of an affected woman is at risk and no child of an affected man is, follows from a single fact about fertilisation.
## sections
### Definition
Mitochondrial inheritance is the transmission of variants in the mitochondrial genome, which sits outside the nucleus. Because only egg cells contribute mitochondria to the developing embryo, only females can pass on mitochondrial variants to their children.
### Mechanism
The sperm contributes essentially no mitochondria to the zygote, so every mitochondrion in a new individual comes from the egg. An affected mother therefore transmits the variant to all her children; an affected father transmits it to none.

A cell carries many mitochondrial genomes, and a variant may be present in some and not others — heteroplasmy. The proportion carrying the variant differs between tissues and between siblings, which is why severity varies so widely within one family.
### Key determinants
Tissues with the highest energy demand show disease first: muscle, brain, heart, and the optic nerve. That follows directly from what the mitochondrion does.

Because the proportion of affected genomes segregates unpredictably at each generation, the severity in a child cannot be predicted from the severity in the mother — which is a real limit on what a genetic consultation can offer.
### Clinical significance
Recognising the pattern changes the advice given to a whole family, and it is one of the few patterns that can be read off a pedigree with near-certainty when it is present.

It also explains why a mitochondrial diagnosis is not excluded by a normal nuclear genome test. The two genomes are tested differently.
### Common misconceptions
Applying the pattern to an affected father. He transmits the variant to none of his children, which is exactly what separates this pattern from every autosomal one.
## hold_these
Only females pass on mitochondrial variants, because only egg cells contribute mitochondria to the embryo.
An affected mother transmits to all her children; an affected father to none.
Heteroplasmy is why severity varies so widely within one family.
The tissues with the highest energy demand are affected first.
## lose_the_mark
Applying the pattern to a father. He transmits it to none of his children.
Excluding a mitochondrial cause on a normal nuclear genome test. The two genomes are tested differently.

## aliases
Family tree | Pedigree chart
## reading_time
5
## summary
A family history becomes usable when it is drawn. The chart is what turns a list of relatives into a visible inheritance pattern.
## sections
### Definition
A pedigree, as related to genetics, is a chart that diagrams the inheritance of a trait or health condition through generations of a family. It is drawn with a standard symbol set so that any clinician can read another's.
### Mechanism
Squares are male, circles female, and a filled symbol means affected. A horizontal line joins partners, a vertical line descends to their children, and each generation occupies one row, numbered from the top.

The person who brought the family to attention is marked with an arrow. Two horizontal lines between partners indicate consanguinity, which is the feature most often omitted and most often relevant.
### Key determinants
Three generations is the usual minimum, because two rarely distinguish a dominant pattern from a chance cluster.

What is asked matters as much as what is drawn. Ages, causes of death, miscarriages and stillbirths, and ethnic background all change the interpretation, and none of them appears unless the history asks.
### Clinical significance
The pedigree is what converts a family history into a recurrence risk, and it is an examined practical skill rather than a piece of knowledge.

It also identifies who else should be offered testing, which is often the most immediately useful output of a genetic consultation.
### Common misconceptions
A pattern read from too few individuals is not a pattern. A small family can look dominant, recessive or sporadic by chance alone, which is why three generations is the working minimum.
## hold_these
A pedigree is a chart that diagrams the inheritance of a trait or condition through generations of a family.
Squares are male, circles female, filled means affected, and generations are numbered from the top.
Three generations is the working minimum for reading a pattern.
Consanguinity is drawn as a double line, and is the detail most often omitted.
## lose_the_mark
Reading a pattern from one or two generations. Chance alone produces convincing-looking clusters in small families.
Omitting miscarriages, stillbirths and ages at death. Each can change the interpretation.

## aliases
Genetic test | DNA testing
## reading_time
6
## summary
A genetic test answers a specific question about a specific target. What it cannot do is exclude everything it was not designed to look for.
## sections
### Definition
Genetic testing is a type of medical test that identifies changes in genes, chromosomes, or proteins. Which of the three is examined depends on the question being asked.
### Mechanism
A karyotype examines whole chromosomes and finds changes in number or large structural rearrangements. Targeted gene testing examines one gene or a defined panel. Broader sequencing examines many genes at once, and finds more — including findings nobody was looking for.

Each method has a resolution. A karyotype cannot see a single-base change; a targeted test cannot see a chromosomal rearrangement. Choosing the test is choosing what you are able to find.
### Key determinants
The purpose determines the test. Diagnostic testing confirms a suspected condition in a symptomatic person; carrier testing identifies an unaffected person who could transmit one; predictive testing looks for a condition that has not yet appeared.

Timing matters too. Prenatal and newborn testing operate under time pressure and answer narrower questions than testing in an adult clinic.
### Clinical significance
A result changes management only when something can be done differently because of it. That is the question to ask before ordering, and it is what separates a useful test from an interesting one.

Broader sequencing raises the problem of the incidental finding — information about a condition nobody was asking about, which the family may not have wanted. Consent has to cover that before the test, not after.
### Common misconceptions
A negative result does not exclude a genetic cause. It excludes what the test was designed to look for, and nothing else.
## hold_these
Genetic testing identifies changes in genes, chromosomes, or proteins.
Each method has a resolution: a karyotype cannot see a single-base change, and a targeted test cannot see a rearrangement.
Purpose determines the test — diagnostic, carrier, predictive, prenatal or newborn.
A negative result excludes only what was tested for.
## lose_the_mark
Reading a negative result as excluding a genetic cause. It excludes only the specific target.
Ordering broad sequencing without consenting for incidental findings. Consent must precede the test.

## summary
The purpose is not to recommend a course of action. It is to leave the family able to decide for themselves, with the information they need and the support to use it.
## sections
### Definition
A genetic consultation is a health service that provides information and support to people who have, or may be at risk for, genetic conditions. It is delivered by medical geneticists and genetic counsellors, and often by nurses and other clinicians trained in genetics.
### Mechanism
The consultation gathers a family history, draws a pedigree, establishes or confirms a diagnosis where it can, explains how the condition is inherited, sets out the recurrence risk, and describes the testing and management options that follow.

What it does not do is choose. The counsellor's role is to make an informed, independent decision possible — which means presenting options without steering, including the option of not testing.
### Key determinants
Risk has to be communicated in a form the family can use. A one-in-four risk means something different to a family that has already lost a child, and the same number lands differently depending on how it is framed.

Confidentiality is more complicated here than elsewhere, because a result about one person is often information about their relatives, who have not asked for it and may not want it.
### Clinical significance
A consultation changes what a family does: whether to test, whether to test a child, what to do in a future pregnancy. Those are decisions with lasting consequences, which is why the non-directive stance is a professional standard rather than a style.

Predictive testing for an adult-onset condition raises the question most sharply. A person may reasonably choose not to know, and that choice deserves the same support as the choice to test.
### Common misconceptions
Genetic counselling is not advice-giving. Its purpose is informed, independent decision-making, and steering the decision defeats the purpose it exists for.
## hold_these
A genetic consultation provides information and support to people who have, or may be at risk for, genetic conditions.
It is non-directive: the aim is an informed, independent decision, not a recommendation.
A result about one person is often information about relatives who did not ask for it.
Choosing not to test is a legitimate choice and deserves equal support.
## lose_the_mark
Treating the consultation as advice-giving. Steering the decision defeats its purpose.
Assuming a result belongs only to the person tested. It frequently carries information about relatives.
