## summary
Four processes decide how much drug reaches its target and for how long. They do not happen one after another — they run together from the moment of administration.
## sections
### Definition
Pharmacokinetics is the study of how the body interacts with administered substances for the entire duration of exposure. It generally examines four main parameters: absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion.

The four are usually abbreviated ADME, and the order of the letters describes emphasis rather than sequence.
### Mechanism
Absorption moves drug from the site of administration into the circulation. It is bypassed entirely by the intravenous route, which is why that route is the reference against which the others are measured.

Distribution moves drug from blood into tissue, and how widely it distributes depends on lipid solubility, protein binding and tissue perfusion. Metabolism, mostly hepatic, converts drug into forms that can be eliminated. Excretion removes drug and metabolites, mostly renally.
### Key determinants
A drug taken by mouth passes through the liver before reaching the systemic circulation, and what is removed on that first pass never reaches the target. That is why the same drug may need very different oral and intravenous doses.

Half-life determines how long a drug persists and therefore how often it must be given; it also determines how long a steady state takes to reach and how long it takes to disappear after stopping.
### Clinical significance
Kidney and liver disease change pharmacokinetics directly, which is why dose adjustment in organ impairment is a routine prescribing task rather than a specialist one.

The extremes of age change it too. A neonate's metabolic and renal capacity, and an older adult's declining renal function, both alter handling of drugs given at otherwise standard doses.
### Common misconceptions
The four processes are not stages in a queue. Absorption is still occurring while metabolism and excretion are already removing drug, and the concentration at any moment is the balance of all four.
## hold_these
Pharmacokinetics is the study of how the body interacts with administered substances for the entire duration of exposure.
It examines four parameters: absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion.
The four run concurrently, not in sequence.
First-pass metabolism is why oral and intravenous doses of the same drug can differ greatly.
## lose_the_mark
Treating ADME as sequential stages. All four operate at once, and concentration is their balance.
Using the same dose regardless of renal or hepatic function. Both change handling directly.

## summary
Pharmacokinetics is what the body does to the drug. Pharmacodynamics is the other direction, and keeping them apart is most of what makes prescribing reasoning work.
## sections
### Definition
Pharmacodynamics studies a drug's molecular, biochemical, and physiologic effects or actions. Pharmacodynamics studies the action of the drug on the organism, and pharmacokinetics studies the effect the organism has on processing the drug.

The two together answer the whole question: how much drug reaches the target, and what it does when it gets there.
### Mechanism
Most drugs act at a receptor. An agonist binds and activates it, producing the same kind of effect the natural ligand would. An antagonist binds without activating, and its effect is entirely to prevent something else from acting.

A partial agonist activates the receptor incompletely, which means it behaves as an agonist against a low background of natural stimulation and as an antagonist against a high one.
### Key determinants
Affinity determines whether a drug binds; efficacy determines what happens once it has. The two are independent, and an antagonist is precisely a drug with high affinity and no efficacy.

Receptor number is not fixed. Chronic stimulation reduces it and chronic blockade increases it, so the same dose does not produce the same effect indefinitely.
### Clinical significance
Tolerance and rebound both follow from receptor regulation rather than from any change in the drug. A receptor chronically blocked is up-regulated, so abrupt withdrawal leaves the tissue over-responsive — which is why some drugs must be tapered rather than stopped.

The same reasoning explains why a partial agonist can be used to reduce a stronger agonist's effect while still preventing complete withdrawal.
### Common misconceptions
The two branches point in opposite directions, and confusing them makes the reasoning collapse. A change in renal function is pharmacokinetic; a change in receptor number is pharmacodynamic, and they call for different responses.
## hold_these
Pharmacodynamics studies a drug's molecular, biochemical and physiologic effects.
Pharmacodynamics is the drug acting on the organism; pharmacokinetics is the organism acting on the drug.
Affinity decides whether a drug binds; efficacy decides what happens once it has.
Chronic stimulation down-regulates receptors and chronic blockade up-regulates them.
## lose_the_mark
Confusing the two branches. A change in renal function is pharmacokinetic; a change in receptor number is pharmacodynamic.
Stopping a chronically blocking drug abruptly. Up-regulated receptors leave the tissue over-responsive.

## reading_time

## summary
A curve with two independent properties: where it sits, and how high it reaches. Confusing them is why "more potent" gets mistaken for "better".
## sections
### Definition
The dose–response relationship describes how the size of a drug's effect changes with the amount given. Between the minimally effective dose and the toxic dose lies the therapeutic window, where the safest and most effective treatment will occur.
### Mechanism
Plotted against the logarithm of dose, the relationship is a sigmoid curve. Effect rises slowly at low doses, steeply through the middle, and then plateaus once the target is saturated.

Two properties of that curve are independent. Potency is its position on the dose axis — how much drug is needed. Efficacy is the height of the plateau — how much effect is achievable at all.
### Key determinants
A competitive antagonist shifts the curve to the right without lowering the plateau: more agonist restores the full effect. A non-competitive antagonist lowers the plateau, and no amount of agonist restores it.

That difference is diagnostic. The shape of the change identifies the kind of blockade.
### Clinical significance
Efficacy usually matters more than potency. A drug needing ten times the dose but reaching a higher plateau is the better drug for a patient who needs that plateau; the dose is a manufacturing detail.

The steep middle of the curve is where small dose changes produce large effect changes, and it is where careful titration matters.
### Common misconceptions
More potent does not mean better. Potency is where the curve sits on the dose axis; efficacy is how high it reaches, and efficacy is what usually matters clinically.
## hold_these
Between the minimally effective dose and the toxic dose lies the therapeutic window.
Potency is the curve's position on the dose axis; efficacy is the height of its plateau.
A competitive antagonist shifts the curve right; a non-competitive one lowers the plateau.
The steep middle of the curve is where titration matters most.
## lose_the_mark
Reading more potent as better. Efficacy, not potency, is what usually matters clinically.
Confusing competitive with non-competitive blockade. One is overcome by more agonist; the other is not.

## reading_time

## summary
One number comparing the dose that helps with the dose that harms. It is useful, and it is a population average, which is the part that gets forgotten.
## sections
### Definition
The therapeutic index is a quantitative measurement of the relative safety of a drug, comparing the amount that produces a therapeutic effect with the amount that produces a toxic effect. A wide index means the two doses are far apart; a narrow one means they are close.
### Mechanism
The index is derived from the two dose–response curves — one for the wanted effect and one for toxicity — by comparing the doses at which each reaches a defined point.

Because it compares two population curves, it says how far apart the effective and toxic doses are on average. It says nothing about the distance in any individual patient.
### Key determinants
A narrow index makes everything else matter more: an interaction, a change in renal function, or an error in dose that would be inconsequential for a wide-index drug can be dangerous for a narrow one.

This is why some drugs are monitored by plasma concentration and most are not. Monitoring is worth its cost only when the window is narrow enough that the dose alone cannot guarantee the concentration.
### Clinical significance
The index is why certain prescriptions carry monitoring requirements attached, and why substituting one preparation for another is a greater risk for some drugs than others.

It is also the reason dose errors are not equally serious across drugs, and why prescribing systems put extra checks around a small subset.
### Common misconceptions
A wide index is not a guarantee of safety in a given patient. The index is a population measure and says nothing about an individual whose handling of the drug is altered.
## hold_these
The therapeutic index compares the dose producing a therapeutic effect with the dose producing toxicity.
A narrow index makes interactions, organ impairment and dose errors far more consequential.
It is a population measure and does not describe an individual patient.
Plasma concentration monitoring is worth its cost mainly for narrow-index drugs.
## lose_the_mark
Reading a wide index as safe in every patient. It is an average across a population.
Treating dose errors as equally serious across drugs. The index is what makes some of them dangerous.

## reading_time

## summary
Most unwanted effects are the drug doing exactly what it does, somewhere it was not wanted. Only a minority are immunological, and treating them all as allergy denies patients drugs they could take.
## sections
### Definition
An adverse drug reaction is a response to a drug which is noxious and unintended, and which occurs at doses normally used for prophylaxis, diagnosis, or therapy of disease or the modification of physiologic function.

The dose qualifier matters: a reaction at a normal dose is adverse, whereas harm at an excessive dose is toxicity or overdose.
### Mechanism
Most reactions are exaggerations or extensions of the drug's known pharmacology. They are dose-related, predictable from the mechanism, and usually manageable by reducing the dose.

A minority are not predictable from the pharmacology. These occur in susceptible individuals, are not dose-related in the usual sense, and include the immunological reactions — which are the ones that genuinely contraindicate re-exposure.
### Key determinants
The distinction matters because it determines what happens next. A dose-related reaction usually means adjust the dose; an idiosyncratic or immunological one usually means stop the drug and record it as a contraindication.

Number of drugs is the strongest single predictor. Each additional drug raises the chance of a reaction more than proportionately, because it raises the chance of an interaction as well.
### Clinical significance
Recording a reaction accurately is a clinical act with lasting consequences. "Allergy" written against a drug the patient merely felt nauseated by may deny them the best treatment for decades.

Reporting suspected reactions is how uncommon ones are ever detected, since pre-marketing trials are too small to find them.
### Common misconceptions
Not every unwanted effect is an allergy. Most adverse reactions are exaggerations of the drug's known action and are dose-related, not immunological, and describing them as allergy has consequences that outlast the episode.
## hold_these
An adverse drug reaction is noxious and unintended, and occurs at doses normally used in practice.
Most reactions are dose-related and predictable from the drug's known pharmacology.
A minority are idiosyncratic or immunological, and those contraindicate re-exposure.
The number of drugs a patient takes is the strongest single predictor.
## lose_the_mark
Recording every unwanted effect as an allergy. It can deny a patient the best treatment for decades.
Assuming a reaction at a normal dose is an overdose. The definition specifically covers normal doses.

## reading_time

## summary
An interaction is not only between two prescriptions. Food, supplements and the patient's own diseases interact too, and those are the ones a drug history usually misses.
## sections
### Definition
Drug interactions are reactions between a drug and other drugs, foods, beverages, supplements, or diseases. The result is a change in what one of them does — more effect, less effect, or an effect neither would produce alone.
### Mechanism
A pharmacokinetic interaction changes how much drug reaches the target. One drug may alter the absorption, metabolism or excretion of another, most often by inducing or inhibiting the enzymes that metabolise it.

A pharmacodynamic interaction changes what happens at the target instead. Two drugs with the same effect combine additively; two with opposing effects cancel; and two acting on the same system by different routes can produce far more than the sum.
### Key determinants
Narrow therapeutic index is the amplifier. An interaction changing concentration by half is inconsequential for a drug with a wide window and dangerous for one without.

Number of drugs is the other. Each addition raises the number of possible pairs faster than the number of drugs, which is why polypharmacy is a risk in itself and not merely a marker of illness.
### Clinical significance
Most clinically important interactions are predictable from mechanism, which is why a prescriber who understands the mechanism catches more of them than one who memorises pairs.

The drug history has to ask beyond prescriptions. Over-the-counter medicines, herbal preparations and dietary habits all interact, and none of them appears unless asked for directly.
### Common misconceptions
Considering only other prescribed drugs. Foods, supplements and the patient's own diseases interact too, and those are exactly the ones a drug history misses.
