# Item

## title
What arterial blood pressure measures

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Blood flows along an artery. A pressure is quoted for that artery.

## question
What does arterial blood pressure describe?

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The force with which blood is propelled forwards along the vessel

## explanation_a
Forward propulsion is driven by the pressure *difference* between two points, not by the pressure at one. This confuses the quantity with the gradient derived from it, which is the commonest conceptual slip about pressure anywhere in physiology.

## answer_b
The lateral pressure that flowing blood exerts on the arterial wall

## explanation_b
Arterial blood pressure is the pressure exerted sideways against the vessel wall by the blood within it. That is why it can be measured by opposing it with a cuff, and why it is the wall — not the flow — that bears it, which matters when the wall is diseased.

## answer_c
The resistance the arterial wall offers to distension

## explanation_c
Resistance to distension is compliance, a property of the wall itself rather than of the blood inside it. Pressure and compliance interact, but they are different quantities.

## answer_d
The volume of blood contained in the arterial system at any moment

## explanation_d
Arterial volume and pressure are related through compliance, so they move together. But a volume is not a pressure, and treating them as the same makes the effect of arterial stiffening impossible to reason about.

# Item

## title
Systolic and diastolic pressure

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Arterial pressure is quoted as two numbers rather than one.

## question
What do the two represent?

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
The pressures in the left and right sides of the circulation

## explanation_a
Systemic and pulmonary pressures are indeed very different, but both are quoted as their own pairs. This reads the two numbers as two circuits rather than as two moments.

## answer_b
The pressure at the heart and the pressure at the periphery

## explanation_b
Pressure does change along the arterial tree, and the pulse actually amplifies as it travels. But the quoted pair is not a comparison between two sites.

## answer_c
The highest pressure reached during ejection and the lowest reached before the next ejection

## explanation_c
Arterial pressure oscillates within each cardiac cycle: it peaks as the ventricle ejects and falls to its minimum just before the next ejection begins. The pair captures the extremes of that oscillation, and their difference — the pulse pressure — carries information neither number gives alone.

## answer_d
The pressure with the heart contracting and the pressure with the heart at rest

## explanation_d
This is close, and it is the phrasing students often use. The trouble is "at rest": diastolic pressure is not the pressure in a stopped circulation but the lowest point of a continuously pressurised system, sustained by elastic recoil of the arteries.

# Item

## title
What widens the pulse pressure

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
An elderly patient's systolic pressure is high while the diastolic pressure is low, giving a wide difference between them. Cardiac output is normal.

## question
What best explains this pattern?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The large arteries have stiffened, so they store less of each stroke volume and recoil less during diastole

## explanation_a
A compliant aorta absorbs part of each stroke volume, limiting the systolic rise, and returns it during diastole, supporting the diastolic pressure. Stiffen it and both effects are lost at once: systolic pressure climbs because the volume is not absorbed, and diastolic pressure falls because there is less recoil to sustain it. One change in the wall explains both numbers moving apart.

## answer_b
Cardiac output has risen, raising systolic pressure

## explanation_b
The stem specifies normal output, and a rise in output would tend to raise both numbers rather than separate them.

## answer_c
Total peripheral resistance has fallen, lowering diastolic pressure

## explanation_c
A fall in resistance does lower diastolic pressure, so half of this works. But it would lower mean pressure too and would not drive systolic pressure up, so it cannot produce both halves of the pattern.

## answer_d
The heart rate has slowed, allowing a longer diastolic run-off

## explanation_d
A slower rate does lengthen diastole and allow more run-off, lowering diastolic pressure a little. It is a real contributor and a small one, and it does not account for the raised systolic value.

# Item

## title
Silence above systolic pressure

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A cuff is inflated around the upper arm until its pressure exceeds the patient's systolic arterial pressure. A stethoscope is placed over the brachial artery below the cuff.

## question
What is heard, and why?

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
A continuous sound, because blood is being forced through a narrowed artery

## explanation_a
Turbulent sound requires flow, and at this cuff pressure there is none. The reasoning would be right at an intermediate cuff pressure, which is exactly when sounds do appear.

## answer_b
A sound with each heartbeat, because the artery pulsates against the cuff

## explanation_b
The artery does pulsate under the cuff, and that pulsation is what oscillometric devices detect. But Korotkoff sounds are generated by flow past a partial obstruction, not by the wall moving.

## answer_c
Nothing, because the stethoscope is below the cuff and out of range

## explanation_c
Placing the stethoscope distal to the cuff is correct technique — it is where sounds are heard once flow resumes. The silence has nothing to do with position.

## answer_d
Nothing, because the artery is completely occluded and no blood is flowing past the cuff

## explanation_d
When cuff pressure exceeds systolic pressure, the artery is held shut throughout the cycle. With no flow there is no turbulence and no sound. The measurement works by lowering the cuff until flow first breaks through at peak systole — the moment sounds appear is therefore the systolic pressure.

# Item

## title
Why the sounds appear and then vanish

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
As the cuff is deflated from above systolic pressure, sounds appear, continue through a range of cuff pressures, and then disappear.

## question
What accounts for the sequence?

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Sounds appear when the artery reopens and vanish when it is completely occluded again

## explanation_a
This reverses the direction of the procedure. The cuff is being deflated, so occlusion is being relieved, not re-established.

## answer_b
Sounds appear when flow becomes turbulent and vanish when the artery becomes fully occluded

## explanation_b
The first clause is right; the second is not. Full occlusion is the starting state, and deflation moves away from it.

## answer_c
Sounds appear when flow first breaks through in systole, persist while the artery is intermittently narrowed, and vanish when flow becomes continuous and smooth

## explanation_c
Three states in sequence. Above systolic pressure the artery is shut and silent. Between systolic and diastolic pressure it opens and shuts within each cycle, and blood forced through the narrowed segment is turbulent and audible. Below diastolic pressure the artery stays open throughout, flow becomes laminar, and the sound stops. The two transitions are therefore the two readings.

## answer_d
Sounds appear when the cuff pressure equals mean arterial pressure and vanish when it falls below venous pressure

## explanation_d
Mean arterial pressure is where the oscillation is greatest, which is what oscillometric devices key on — so this is not disconnected from reality. But the auscultatory transitions are at systolic and diastolic pressure, and venous pressure plays no part.

# Item

## title
A cuff applied too loosely

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A blood pressure cuff is wrapped loosely around the arm rather than snugly.

## question
What effect does this have on the reading?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
It reads falsely high, because more cuff pressure is needed to reach the artery

## explanation_a
A loose cuff bulges outwards as it inflates instead of pressing inwards on the limb, so only part of the cuff pressure is transmitted to the tissue and the artery. A higher cuff reading is therefore required before the artery is occluded, and every transition is recorded at an inflated value.

## answer_b
It reads falsely low, because the cuff cannot compress the artery at all

## explanation_b
The cuff can still compress the artery, just less efficiently. If it truly could not, no reading would be obtained rather than a low one.

## answer_c
It reads falsely low, because sound escapes around the loose cuff

## explanation_c
Sound is detected by the stethoscope below the cuff, not through it, so leakage of sound is not a mechanism. This invents an acoustic explanation for a mechanical error.

## answer_d
It has no effect, provided the cuff bladder covers the brachial artery

## explanation_d
Coverage of the artery is necessary but not sufficient — the cuff must also transmit its pressure. This treats one requirement of correct technique as the only one.

# Item

## title
Which way a measurement error runs

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Two technique errors are considered: a cuff that is too loose, and a cuff whose bladder is too narrow for the circumference of the arm.

## question
In which direction does each error push the reading?

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The loose cuff reads high and the narrow cuff reads low

## explanation_a
The two errors run in the same direction, not opposite ones. This is chosen by students who assume that two different faults must have contrasting effects.

## answer_b
Both read falsely high, because in each case more cuff pressure is required to occlude the artery

## explanation_b
Both errors have the same root: pressure applied at the cuff is not fully delivered to the artery. A loose cuff bulges outwards; a narrow cuff spreads its pressure over too little of the limb. Either way, occlusion demands a higher gauge reading, so the recorded pressure exceeds the true one. One principle explains both, which is more useful than memorising two rules.

## answer_c
Both read falsely low, because the artery is compressed less than intended

## explanation_c
The artery is indeed compressed less at any given cuff pressure — and the consequence is that a higher cuff pressure is needed, giving a higher number. The premise is right and the conclusion is inverted, which makes this the strongest distractor.

## answer_d
Neither affects the reading, provided the deflation rate is slow enough

## explanation_d
Deflation rate is a separate source of error and cannot compensate for pressure that never reaches the artery. This offers a real technique point as a remedy for an unrelated fault.

## title
Where the arterial baroreceptors are

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Stretch receptors monitor arterial pressure continuously and feed into the brainstem.

## question
Where are they mainly located?

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
In the walls of the great veins entering the right atrium

## explanation_a
Low-pressure receptors do sit in the venous side and the atria, and they sense volume rather than arterial pressure. They are a real and separate system, which is what makes this the closest distractor.

## answer_b
Within the myocardium of the left ventricle

## explanation_b
Ventricular receptors exist and contribute to reflexes, but they are not the principal arterial pressure sensors. Placing the sensor inside the pump rather than in the circuit it pressurises misreads what is being monitored.

## answer_c
In the carotid sinus and the aortic arch

## explanation_c
The carotid sinus and the aortic arch carry the main arterial baroreceptors. Both sites are positioned where the pressure being sensed is the pressure that matters — at the origin of the cerebral supply and at the exit from the heart.

## answer_d
In the renal afferent arterioles

## explanation_d
The renal afferent arteriole does sense pressure, and that sensing drives renin release and long-term pressure control. It is a different loop operating on a different timescale from the baroreflex.

# Item

## title
What the baroreceptors actually sense

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Baroreceptors are described as pressure sensors, yet they are nerve endings within the vessel wall with no way of measuring pressure directly.

## question
What do they respond to?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Stretch of the vessel wall, which varies with the pressure distending it

## explanation_a
The endings are mechanoreceptors: they fire in proportion to how much the wall around them is stretched. Because wall stretch tracks distending pressure, the firing rate reports pressure indirectly. This is why a stiffened vessel wall blunts the reflex — the same pressure produces less stretch, so the sensor under-reports.

## answer_b
The oxygen content of the blood passing them

## explanation_b
Chemical composition is monitored by the chemoreceptors in the carotid and aortic bodies, which sit close by and are easily confused with the baroreceptors. Different sensors, adjacent locations, different stimuli.

## answer_c
The velocity of blood flowing past the receptor

## explanation_c
Flow velocity and pressure are related but distinct, and the endings respond to deformation of the wall rather than to the movement of blood. A vessel can be highly stretched with very little flow.

## answer_d
The pulsatility of the pressure wave alone, not the mean level

## explanation_d
Pulsatility genuinely does affect baroreceptor firing — pulsatile pressure produces more discharge than steady pressure at the same mean. But the receptors report mean stretch as well, and confining them to pulsatility removes their main function.

# Item

## title
The reflex response to a fall in pressure

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Arterial pressure falls suddenly. Baroreceptor firing decreases.

## question
What does the reflex do?

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
Increases vagal outflow and reduces sympathetic outflow, slowing the heart

## explanation_a
This is the response to a *rise* in pressure. Applying it to a fall would deepen the hypotension, and it is the commonest error because students remember the vagal arm most readily.

## answer_b
Leaves cardiac output unchanged and corrects pressure by vasoconstriction alone

## explanation_b
Vasoconstriction is a major part of the response, but rate and contractility rise as well. Restricting the reflex to one effector halves its capacity.

## answer_c
Triggers renin release from the kidney as its principal immediate action

## explanation_c
Renin release is stimulated and matters greatly — but over minutes to hours. The baroreflex acts within seconds, and confusing the two timescales is what makes short-term and long-term pressure control blur together.

## answer_d
Reduces vagal outflow and increases sympathetic outflow, raising heart rate, contractility and vascular resistance

## explanation_d
Reduced baroreceptor firing releases the brainstem from inhibition. Sympathetic outflow rises and vagal outflow falls, so heart rate and contractility increase, arterioles constrict and veins constrict to improve filling. Every determinant of pressure is pushed in the same direction at once, within seconds.

# Item

## title
Why the baroreflex cannot fix hypertension

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
The baroreflex corrects a fall in arterial pressure within seconds. Yet in sustained hypertension it does not return pressure to normal, even though pressure is persistently above its usual level.

## question
Why not?

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The receptors are destroyed by chronically high pressure

## explanation_a
The receptors remain functional; a hypertensive patient still shows baroreflex responses to acute changes. Structural destruction would abolish those responses entirely.

## answer_b
The receptors reset over days to treat the new pressure as normal, so they defend the raised level rather than opposing it

## explanation_b
Baroreceptor firing adapts to a sustained pressure, and the reflex re-establishes its set point around the prevailing level. From then on the reflex resists deviations from the *new* pressure, defending hypertension rather than correcting it. This is why the baroreflex is a short-term stabiliser and the kidney governs pressure in the long run.

## answer_c
The reflex acts only on heart rate, which is already normal in hypertension

## explanation_c
The reflex acts on resistance and venous tone as well as on rate. Restricting it to one effector understates what it could do if it were still trying.

## answer_d
Sympathetic outflow cannot be reduced below its resting level, so there is no scope to lower pressure

## explanation_d
Sympathetic outflow can certainly be reduced further, and it is during an acute pressure rise. The limit is not the effector's range but the set point the reflex is defending.

D

Redundancy, so that pressure is maintained if one arm fails

C

Cutaneous flow falls in this response, which is why a frightened person turns pale. Thermoregulatory skin vasodilatation belongs to a different situation, and here the two demands conflict.

## title

## subject

## status
Draft

## vignette
During the response to acute threat, sympathetic activity increases throughout the body. Yet splanchnic and cutaneous vessels constrict while vessels in exercising skeletal muscle dilate.

## question
How can increased sympathetic activity produce opposite effects?

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Sympathetic fibres to skeletal muscle stop firing while those elsewhere fire more

## explanation_a
The stem specifies increased activity throughout. Selective withdrawal would be a way to achieve dilatation, but it is not what is described and it is not the main mechanism.

## answer_b
The beds differ in which receptors predominate, and local metabolic vasodilatation in working muscle overrides the constrictor signal

## explanation_b
Vascular responses depend on what the vessel carries and what else is acting on it. Beds dominated by constrictor receptors constrict. The transmitter is different at each site — noradrenaline at some, adrenaline at others

## explanation_c
Circulating adrenaline does contribute and does have a different receptor profile, so this is not baseless. But the explanation for opposite responses lies in the receptors and local conditions of each bed rather than in which catecholamine arrives.

## answer_d
Blood is passively diverted to muscle because the other beds constrict, without any local change

## explanation_d
Passive diversion does occur and contributes to the redistribution. But it cannot by itself produce active dilatation in the muscle bed, and treating muscle as a purely passive recipient misses the local control that makes the response work.

A

One number cannot separate its two factors. The second patient must have a measurement error, since perfusion and pressure track together

The premise that they track together is exactly what the comparison disproves. Rejecting the data to preserve the assumption is the reasoning failure being examined.

Venous pressure influences the gradient across a tissue and matters in congestion, but it does not explain a difference in peripheral temperature and urine output at the same arterial pressure.
