# Item

## title
What automaticity means

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A cell isolated from the sinoatrial node and placed in a dish, with no nerve supply and no hormones present, continues to produce action potentials at regular intervals.

## question
Which property does this demonstrate?

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
Excitability — the cell responds when a stimulus is applied

## explanation_a
Excitability is real and every cardiac myocyte has it, but it describes responding to something. The cell here is firing with nothing applied, which is precisely the observation excitability cannot account for.

## answer_b
Automaticity — the cell generates its own action potentials without external stimulus

## explanation_b
Automaticity is the capacity to depolarise to threshold spontaneously. Because the firing here is also regular, the cell shows rhythmicity as well, and the two together are what make a pacemaker a pacemaker.

## answer_c
Conductivity — the cell propagates an impulse to its neighbours

## explanation_c
Conductivity concerns passing an impulse on, not starting one. An isolated cell has no neighbours to conduct to, so the property cannot be what is being shown.

## answer_d
Contractility — the cell shortens in response to depolarisation

## explanation_d
Contractility is the mechanical response that follows the electrical event. Students choose it by reaching for the property they associate most strongly with heart muscle rather than the one the observation isolates.

# Item

## title
Why the sinoatrial node sets the rate

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
The sinoatrial node, the atrioventricular node and the Purkinje fibres are all capable of spontaneous firing, yet in a normal heart every beat originates in the sinoatrial node.

## question
Why does it dominate?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
It reaches threshold fastest, so it discharges the others before they arrive at their own threshold

## explanation_a
All three sites depolarise spontaneously, but at different rates. The sinoatrial node gets to threshold first every cycle, and its impulse depolarises the slower sites before they finish their own climb. Dominance is a race, not a rank, which is why a slowed sinoatrial node lets a lower site escape and take over.

## answer_b
It is the only site with true automaticity; the others conduct but cannot initiate

## explanation_b
This denies the premise. Lower sites certainly can initiate — that is what an escape rhythm is, and it is what keeps a patient in complete block alive.

## answer_c
It is the only part of the conducting system with a sympathetic supply

## explanation_c
Sympathetic innervation reaches the atrioventricular node and ventricular myocardium as well. Innervation modulates rate; it does not determine which site leads.

## answer_d
Its impulses are larger in amplitude, so they override the smaller impulses from lower sites

## explanation_d
Action potentials do not compete by amplitude — they are all-or-none events. This applies an analogue, signal-strength model to a system that works by timing.

# Item

## title
What a pacemaker cell lacks

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Two membrane potential traces are recorded: one from a working ventricular myocyte, which sits flat between beats, and one from a sinoatrial node cell, which never sits flat at all.

## question
What accounts for the difference in the node cell?

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Its resting potential is more negative, so it takes longer to reach threshold

## explanation_a
The node cell's maximum diastolic potential is in fact less negative than a working myocyte's resting potential, and the trace is the wrong shape for this explanation. Students choose it by assuming slower firing must mean a longer climb from further down.

## answer_b
Its sodium channels are permanently inactivated, so it depolarises through potassium efflux

## explanation_b
Potassium efflux hyperpolarises rather than depolarises, so this reverses the direction of the current. Fast sodium channels are largely unavailable in nodal cells, which is the grain of truth making the answer attractive.

## answer_c
It has no stable resting potential — an inward current makes it drift steadily towards threshold between beats

## explanation_c
Nodal cells have no true resting potential. After the most negative point of the cycle, a slow inward current begins immediately and carries the membrane up until threshold is reached, at which point the next action potential fires. Spontaneous firing is not something added to a resting cell; it is the absence of rest.

## answer_d
It is depolarised continuously by acetylcholine released from vagal endings

## explanation_d
Acetylcholine slows nodal firing rather than driving it, and an isolated node cell fires with no innervation at all. This inverts the autonomic effect and misplaces the source of the pacemaker current.

## title
When the sinoatrial node stops

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Sinoatrial node discharge ceases entirely. The atrioventricular node and the conducting tissue below it are healthy.

## question
What happens next, and why?

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
The heart stops, because only the sinoatrial node can initiate an impulse

## explanation_a
This treats automaticity as unique to the sinoatrial node. It is the belief the concept of an escape rhythm exists to correct, and if it were true complete heart block would be uniformly fatal within seconds.

## answer_b
The heart continues at its normal rate, driven by the atrioventricular node

## explanation_b
An escape rhythm does take over, but not at the normal rate — lower sites reach threshold more slowly and therefore fire more slowly. Getting the site right and the rate wrong is the commonest partial answer here.

## answer_c
The ventricles fibrillate, because they are no longer receiving an organised impulse

## explanation_c
Absence of a driving impulse produces asystole or an escape rhythm, not fibrillation, which requires disorganised re-entrant activity rather than the loss of a pacemaker. Students choose this by treating fibrillation as the default failure mode of the heart.

## answer_d
A slower escape rhythm emerges from the next fastest site, because it is no longer discharged before reaching threshold

## explanation_d
Lower sites were always climbing towards threshold; they simply never arrived first. Remove the sinoatrial node and the atrioventricular junction reaches its own threshold and takes over, at its own slower intrinsic rate. The hierarchy is a consequence of relative speed, so removing the top of it exposes the next level down.

## title
The upstroke of a working cardiac myocyte

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A working ventricular myocyte rests at about −90 mV. When a neighbouring cell depolarises it, its membrane potential is carried up to about −65 mV, and the cell then depolarises very rapidly on its own.

## question
What happens at that second value?

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The membrane reaches equilibrium for potassium, and further change requires an external current

## explanation_a
The potassium equilibrium potential is close to the resting value, not to the firing level, and the cell manifestly does change further without more external current. This confuses the potential the cell sits at with the potential at which it commits.

## answer_b
Threshold is crossed, fast sodium channels open, and the upstroke becomes self-sustaining

## explanation_b
Below threshold the depolarisation depends on current from the neighbour; at threshold enough fast sodium channels open that their inward current opens more, and the upstroke carries itself. This is why the response is described as fast: once committed, it does not depend on the stimulus continuing.

## answer_c
Calcium channels open and produce the plateau of the action potential

## explanation_c
Calcium entry does produce the plateau, but that comes after the upstroke, not at threshold. Students choose this by naming the ion most associated with cardiac muscle rather than the one that carries phase 0.

## answer_d
The sodium–potassium pump reverses, driving sodium inwards

## explanation_d
The pump moves sodium out and does not reverse to depolarise the cell; the upstroke is channel-mediated, not pump-mediated. This is chosen by students who reach for the pump whenever sodium moves.

# Item

## title
Resting potential against firing level

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A student is asked why a working myocyte's resting potential and its firing level are quoted as two separate values.

## question
What does the difference between them represent?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
The margin of depolarisation the cell must receive before it will fire — a measure of how excitable it is

## explanation_a
The gap between where the cell sits and where it commits is the stimulus it needs. Narrow the gap and the cell fires more readily; widen it and the cell becomes harder to excite. Every clinical disturbance of excitability acts by moving one of the two values.

## answer_b
The amplitude of the action potential the cell will produce

## explanation_b
Action potential amplitude is measured from the resting potential to the peak, which overshoots zero. Confusing the distance to threshold with the size of the response is the natural error when both are voltage differences on the same trace.

## answer_c
The voltage the sodium–potassium pump must generate each cycle

## explanation_c
The pump maintains the ionic gradients that make the resting potential possible; it does not generate a voltage step of this size each cycle. This attaches a maintenance mechanism to a moment-to-moment measurement.

## answer_d
The degree of hyperpolarisation reached after repolarisation

## explanation_d
Any after-hyperpolarisation is a separate feature below the resting potential, not the interval above it. Students choose this by reaching for the other place on the trace where two levels are compared.

## title
What triggers a working myocyte

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
A working ventricular myocyte in the middle of the free wall fires. It has no pacemaker activity of its own and no nerve ending on it.

## question
What depolarised it?

## correct_answer
D

## answer_a
A sympathetic nerve terminal releasing noradrenaline onto it

## explanation_a
Sympathetic fibres modulate contractility and rate but do not trigger individual working myocytes the way a motor nerve triggers a skeletal fibre. Assuming a nerve per cell is the single most common transfer error from skeletal muscle.

## answer_b
Circulating adrenaline binding to its beta receptors

## explanation_b
Circulating catecholamines change how the cell behaves once triggered, and they raise sinoatrial firing, but they do not initiate each beat in each myocyte. This confuses a modulator with an initiator.

## answer_c
Its own spontaneous drift to threshold, as in a nodal cell

## explanation_c
Working myocytes have a stable resting potential and do not drift. Under abnormal conditions they can acquire automaticity, which is what makes ectopic beats possible, but this is not the normal mechanism.

## answer_d
Depolarising current flowing from an already-active neighbouring myocyte

## explanation_d
Current passes directly from cell to cell through gap junctions, carrying each myocyte to threshold in turn. The myocardium behaves as a functional syncytium, so one impulse spreads across the whole chamber without any cell needing its own trigger.

## title
Why the myocardium behaves as one unit

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Stimulating a single point on the ventricular wall above threshold produces contraction of the entire ventricle, not a local twitch.

## question
What makes this possible?

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Each myocyte is innervated by a branch of the same nerve, so all are stimulated together

## explanation_a
There is no such shared innervation, and if there were, the ventricle would contract only as fast as the nerve fired. This is the skeletal-muscle motor unit model applied where it does not hold.

## answer_b
The action potential is long enough that it reaches every cell before any repolarises

## explanation_b
The long cardiac action potential does matter — it produces a refractory period that prevents tetany and re-entry — but duration explains why the tissue cannot be re-excited, not how the impulse spreads.

## answer_c
Gap junctions at the intercalated discs let current pass directly between cells, so the myocardium is a functional syncytium

## explanation_c
Intercalated discs contain gap junctions of low electrical resistance, so depolarising current flows straight from one cell into the next. The tissue is not one cell anatomically, but electrically it behaves as though it were — which is why the all-or-none response applies to the whole chamber rather than to a single fibre.

## answer_d
Cardiac myocytes are truly multinucleate single cells, like skeletal muscle fibres

## explanation_d
Skeletal fibres are genuine syncytia formed by fusion; cardiac myocytes remain separate cells joined end to end. "Functional syncytium" is the phrase precisely because the anatomy and the behaviour differ, and this distractor collapses that distinction.

## title
The electrical role of the fibrous skeleton

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Atrial and ventricular myocardium meet at the plane of the fibrous skeleton, which is composed of dense collagen.

## question
What is the electrical consequence?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
Atrial myocardium is insulated from ventricular myocardium, except where the conducting pathway crosses

## explanation_a
Collagen does not conduct, so the atrial and ventricular syncytia are electrically separate. The single exception is the atrioventricular bundle, which is what makes the whole system controllable: one gateway that can be timed, and if it fails, blocked.

## answer_b
Atrial and ventricular myocardium are electrically continuous, with the skeleton providing mechanical support only

## explanation_b
This is the skeleton's other job, stated as though it were the only one. If continuity existed everywhere, atrial depolarisation would reach the ventricles immediately and no atrioventricular delay would be possible.

## answer_c
The skeleton conducts more slowly than myocardium, producing the atrioventricular delay

## explanation_c
The delay is generated in the atrioventricular node, not by slow conduction through collagen. This answer invents a conducting property for a tissue whose value is that it does not conduct at all.

## answer_d
The skeleton insulates the two ventricles from one another

## explanation_d
The ventricles are continuous through the interventricular septum and depolarise together. Choosing this misplaces the insulating plane from horizontal to vertical.

## title
What insulation buys the heart

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Consider a heart in which atrial and ventricular myocardium were electrically continuous all round the atrioventricular junction, with no insulating plane.

## question
What would be lost?

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The ability of the ventricles to contract at all, since the impulse would dissipate

## explanation_a
The impulse would not dissipate; it would arrive everywhere, and sooner. Students choose this by assuming any departure from normal anatomy must abolish function rather than disorder its timing.

## answer_b
Controlled timing, and the protection of the ventricles from every atrial impulse

## explanation_b
Two things depend on the single gateway. The atrioventricular node can hold the impulse long enough for atrial contraction to finish, which requires all traffic to pass through it; and because the node conducts poorly at high rates, it filters an atrial tachycardia rather than passing it straight to the ventricles. Continuity everywhere would remove both the delay and the filter.

## answer_c
The atrial contribution to ventricular filling, since the atria would no longer contract

## explanation_c
The atria would still contract — they would simply contract at the same time as the ventricles rather than before them. The atrial contribution is lost because of the timing, not because of any failure to contract.

## answer_d
The ability of the ventricles to develop pressure, since they would depolarise from the base downwards

## explanation_d
Direction of spread does affect the efficiency of ejection, and the point about apex-to-base activation is a real one. But it is a consequence of losing the bundle branches rather than of losing insulation, and the ventricles would still develop pressure.

## title
How the impulse crosses the fibrous plane

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
The atrioventricular node lies in the lower interatrial septum, above the fibrous skeleton. The ventricular conducting tissue lies below it.

## question
What connects the two?

## correct_answer
C

## answer_a
Internodal tracts running through the fibrous skeleton

## explanation_a
Internodal pathways run within the atrial wall, from the sinoatrial to the atrioventricular node, and stop there. Students choose this by taking the most recently named conducting pathway and extending it further than it goes.

## answer_b
Direct gap-junction coupling between atrial and ventricular myocytes at the annulus

## explanation_b
This is exactly what the fibrous skeleton prevents. It is the strongest distractor because gap junctions are the correct mechanism everywhere else in the myocardium — just not across this plane.

Impulse formation in the sinoatrial node

## title
Reading atrioventricular block on the ECG

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
Two ECGs are compared. On the first, every P wave is followed by a QRS complex but the PR interval is longer than normal and constant. On the second, some P waves are followed by a QRS complex and others are not.

## question
What do the two traces show?

## correct_answer
B

## answer_a
The first shows atrial enlargement; the second shows atrioventricular block

## explanation_a
Atrial enlargement changes the shape and duration of the P wave itself, not the interval after it. Only one of the two traces is correctly identified, which is what makes this the partial answer students settle for.

## answer_b
Both show atrioventricular block — conduction delayed in the first, and intermittently failing in the second

## explanation_b
Both are atrioventricular block at different severities. A uniformly long PR interval means every impulse gets through, but slowly. A P wave with no QRS after it means that impulse did not get through at all. Reading the two together shows block as a spectrum rather than an all-or-nothing event.

## answer_c
The first shows atrioventricular block; the second shows atrial fibrillation

## explanation_c
Atrial fibrillation has no discernible P waves at all, and the second trace has them — that is the whole point of describing which ones conduct. This is chosen by students who treat any irregularity as fibrillation.

## answer_d
The first shows a bundle branch block; the second shows sinus arrest

## explanation_d
Bundle branch block widens the QRS without lengthening PR, and sinus arrest means no P wave is produced. Both descriptions contradict what the traces are said to contain.

# Item

## title
When the atria and ventricles stop agreeing

## subject
cvs

## status
Draft

## vignette
An ECG shows regular P waves at about 90 per minute and regular QRS complexes at about 35 per minute. There is no consistent relationship between them; the PR interval varies continuously and randomly.

## question
What is happening, and what is driving the ventricles?

## correct_answer
A

## answer_a
No atrial impulse is reaching the ventricles, which are being driven by a subsidiary pacemaker below the block

## explanation_a
Two independent regular rhythms with no fixed relationship means neither is causing the other. The atria are being driven normally by the sinoatrial node; nothing is crossing to the ventricles; and the ventricles are beating because conducting tissue below the block has reached its own threshold. The slow ventricular rate is the intrinsic rate of that subsidiary pacemaker, which is why the rate identifies roughly where it sits.

## answer_b
Every second or third atrial impulse is conducting, producing a slower ventricular rate

## explanation_b
Intermittent conduction at a fixed ratio would give a constant PR interval on the conducted beats and a ventricular rate that was a simple fraction of the atrial rate. Here the PR interval varies randomly and 35 is not a simple fraction of 90, so some impulses conducting cannot explain the trace.

## answer_c
The atria are fibrillating and the ventricles are responding irregularly

## explanation_c
Fibrillating atria produce no organised P waves and an irregularly irregular ventricular response. The trace describes regular P waves and regular QRS complexes, which contradicts both halves.

## answer_d
The sinoatrial node has failed and the atria are being driven retrogradely from the ventricles

## explanation_d
Retrograde conduction would tie the two rhythms together at a fixed interval, which is exactly what the trace lacks. The independence of the two rates is the evidence against any causal link in either direction.
