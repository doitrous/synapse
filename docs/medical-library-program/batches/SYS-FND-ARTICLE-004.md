Reversible injury

## summary
Injury does not become death at a fixed dose. There is a window in which everything the cell has done can be undone, and knowing where that window closes is what makes early treatment worth giving.
## sections
### Definition
Reversible cell injury is damage that stops short of the point of no return. Adaptation successfully maintains homeostasis by reversible responses that can alter cell function and structure, and reversible injury sits just beyond that: the cell is damaged, not merely adapted, but recovers fully once the stress is removed.
### Mechanism
Most reversible injury begins with a failure of energy supply. When ATP falls, the sodium pump slows, sodium and water enter, and the cell swells — the earliest and most universal change.

The endoplasmic reticulum swells with it and ribosomes detach, so protein synthesis falls. In cells handling lipid, fat accumulates as droplets because the machinery exporting it needs the ATP that is no longer there.
### Key determinants
What decides the outcome is duration and severity, not the nature of the insult. The same hypoxia that is fully recoverable at ten minutes is lethal at sixty.

Cell type matters too. A neurone tolerates minutes of hypoxia; a fibroblast tolerates hours. The difference is metabolic demand.
### Clinical significance
This window is the reason reperfusion is worth attempting at all. Restoring blood flow to reversibly injured tissue recovers it; restoring it to necrotic tissue does not, and can add injury of its own.

It is also why time is the organising variable in ischaemic disease. The clinical urgency is not about the diagnosis, it is about how much tissue is still inside the window.
### Common misconceptions
Swelling is not irreversible. Cellular swelling and fatty change both resolve completely if the stress is relieved in time, and treating them as terminal misreads what the microscope is showing.
## hold_these
Reversible injury recovers completely once the stress is removed.
Cellular swelling is the earliest and most universal change, and follows ATP failure.
Duration and severity decide the outcome, not the nature of the insult.
The window is why reperfusion is worth attempting, and why time drives urgency in ischaemia.
## lose_the_mark
Reading cellular swelling or fatty change as irreversible. Both resolve if the stress is relieved in time.
Assuming a fixed dose separates injury from death. The same insult is recoverable or lethal depending on how long it lasts.

## summary
A cell that dies because it was overwhelmed spills its contents into the tissue around it. That spill is what inflames, and it is what separates necrosis from every controlled death.
## sections
### Definition
Necrosis is often coined as accidental death, as it is generally seen as not controlled by the cell. The cell swells, its membrane fails, and its contents escape into the surrounding tissue.
### Mechanism
The sequence follows energy failure. Without ATP the pumps stop, the cell swells past what the membrane can contain, lysosomal enzymes are released, and the cell digests itself from within.

Because the membrane fails, intracellular contents reach the extracellular space. The immune system treats them as danger signals, and inflammation follows — which is the feature that distinguishes necrosis clinically.
### Key determinants
The pattern depends on the tissue and the insult. Coagulative necrosis preserves the outline of the dead tissue and follows ischaemia in most solid organs; liquefactive necrosis digests it away and follows ischaemia in the brain and infection with pus-forming organisms; caseous necrosis, with its cheese-like appearance, is characteristic of tuberculosis.

Fat necrosis and fibrinoid necrosis complete the set taught at this level.
### Clinical significance
The inflammatory response is what the patient and the blood tests show. Fever, a raised white count and released intracellular enzymes are all consequences of the spill, and are how necrosis is detected without a biopsy.

The pattern also localises the cause, which is why the histological description in a report is worth reading rather than skipping to the conclusion.
### Common misconceptions
Not all cell death is necrosis, and not all cell death inflames. Apoptosis is controlled, energy-requiring and silent; necrosis is uncontrolled, follows energy failure and inflames. The distinction is examined more often than any other in general pathology.
## hold_these
Necrosis is accidental death, not controlled by the cell.
Membrane failure spills intracellular contents, and that spill is what causes inflammation.
Coagulative necrosis follows ischaemia in solid organs; liquefactive follows it in brain and in pus-forming infection.
Caseous necrosis is characteristic of tuberculosis.
## lose_the_mark
Treating all cell death as necrosis. Apoptosis is controlled and does not inflame.
Ignoring the necrosis pattern in a report. The pattern localises the cause.

## aliases
Adaptive cellular response
## reading_time

## summary
Before a cell is injured it tries to cope, and the ways it copes are visible under the microscope. Recognising them is how a pathologist reads what the tissue has been living with.
## sections
### Definition
Adaptation successfully maintains homeostasis by reversible responses that can alter cell function and structure. Four changes cover almost everything examined: an increase in cell size, an increase in cell number, a decrease in size and activity, and a change of cell type.
### Mechanism
Hypertrophy increases cell size by making more structural protein, and occurs where cells cannot divide — cardiac and skeletal muscle. Hyperplasia increases cell number and occurs where they can.

Atrophy is a decrease in cell size and activity, achieved by degrading protein and organelles. Metaplasia replaces one differentiated cell type with another better suited to the new conditions, by redirecting how stem cells in the tissue differentiate — not by converting mature cells.
### Key determinants
Whether an adaptation is physiological or pathological depends only on the stimulus. The uterus in pregnancy and the ventricle in hypertension both hypertrophy; the process is the same and the implication is not.

The limit is the point at which the stimulus exceeds what the adaptation can accommodate. Beyond it the cell is injured, and beyond that it dies.
### Clinical significance
An adaptation is a record of what the tissue has been exposed to, which is why the appearance is diagnostically useful even when the cause is no longer present.

Metaplasia carries a further implication: the replacing tissue is in the wrong place, and persistent stimulus in metaplastic tissue is a recognised route toward dysplasia and malignancy.
### Common misconceptions
An adaptation is not a disease. It is a successful response, and it becomes pathological only when the stimulus persists beyond what the cell can accommodate.
## hold_these
Adaptation maintains homeostasis by reversible changes in cell function and structure.
Hypertrophy is more size, hyperplasia more number, atrophy less of both, metaplasia a different cell type.
Whether an adaptation is physiological or pathological depends only on the stimulus.
Metaplasia is a recognised step on the route toward dysplasia.
## lose_the_mark
Calling an adaptation a disease. It is a successful response until the stimulus exceeds it.
Describing metaplasia as mature cells converting. Stem cells in the tissue differentiate differently.

## summary
Five signs described in antiquity all follow from two changes in the microcirculation. Learning the signs without the changes is why the topic feels like memorisation.
## sections
### Definition
Acute inflammation initiates following a specific injury, triggering the release of soluble mediators such as cytokines, acute phase proteins, and chemokines. It is immediate, non-specific, and the same whatever the injury.

The 5 fundamental signs of inflammation include heat, redness, swelling, pain, and loss of function.
### Mechanism
Two vascular changes produce four of the five signs. Vasodilatation increases blood flow, giving heat and redness. Increased permeability lets protein-rich fluid leave the vessel, giving swelling — and the pressure of that fluid, with the mediators in it, gives pain. Loss of function follows from the pain and the swelling together.

The cellular phase follows the vascular one. Neutrophils marginate against the endothelium, roll, adhere, and migrate between the cells into the tissue, drawn along a chemotactic gradient to the injury.
### Key determinants
Which mediators dominate shapes what is seen: histamine acts within minutes on permeability, prostaglandins sustain vasodilatation and sensitise nerve endings, and the complement and coagulation cascades amplify both.

The outcome depends on whether the cause is removed. Complete resolution, abscess formation, healing by fibrosis, or progression to chronic inflammation are the four possibilities, and which occurs is decided by the agent rather than by the response.
### Clinical significance
Anti-inflammatory drugs work by interrupting specific steps in this sequence, which is why the same drug relieves pain, swelling and fever together.

The systemic response is measurable: fever, leucocytosis and acute phase proteins are the body-wide version of what is happening locally, and they are what a blood test detects when the site itself cannot be examined.
### Common misconceptions
The five signs are not a list to recite. Each follows from a specific vascular or cellular change, and examinations ask for the mechanism rather than the name.
## hold_these
Acute inflammation follows a specific injury and releases cytokines, acute phase proteins and chemokines.
The five fundamental signs are heat, redness, swelling, pain, and loss of function.
Vasodilatation gives heat and redness; increased permeability gives swelling, and with the mediators, pain.
The four outcomes are resolution, abscess, fibrosis, or progression to chronic inflammation.
## lose_the_mark
Reciting the five signs without their mechanisms. The mechanism is what is asked for.
Treating the outcome as determined by the inflammatory response. It is determined by whether the cause is removed.

## aliases
Chronic inflammatory response
## reading_time

## summary
When the cause cannot be cleared, destruction and repair run at the same time for months or years. That simultaneity is what makes chronic inflammation different, not merely longer.
## sections
### Definition
Chronic inflammation is also referred to as slow, long-term inflammation lasting for prolonged periods of several months to years. Tissue destruction and attempted repair proceed together, which is why the tissue is progressively remodelled rather than restored.
### Mechanism
The cell population changes. Neutrophils give way to macrophages, lymphocytes and plasma cells, and the macrophage becomes the central player — presenting antigen, secreting mediators, and driving fibrosis.

Where an agent resists clearance, macrophages aggregate and transform into epithelioid cells, sometimes fusing into giant cells, and a granuloma forms. A granuloma is an attempt at containment when elimination has failed.
### Key determinants
Chronic inflammation does not always follow an acute episode. It can begin as chronic from the outset — a persistent intracellular infection, a foreign body the tissue cannot remove, or an autoimmune process directed at the body's own tissue.

Fibrosis is the price of persistence. Every cycle of destruction and repair deposits collagen, and the accumulated collagen is what eventually impairs the organ.
### Clinical significance
The organ failure in many chronic diseases is fibrosis, not the original agent. That is why removing the cause late in the disease may stop progression without restoring function.

Granulomatous inflammation narrows the differential sharply, which is why its identification in a biopsy report changes the direction of the investigation.
### Common misconceptions
Chronic inflammation is not simply acute inflammation that lasted longer. It can begin as chronic, and its cell population and its consequences are different.
## hold_these
Chronic inflammation is slow, long-term inflammation lasting months to years.
Destruction and repair proceed at the same time, which is why the tissue is remodelled rather than restored.
Macrophages, lymphocytes and plasma cells replace neutrophils.
A granuloma is containment attempted after elimination has failed.
## lose_the_mark
Assuming chronic inflammation always follows acute. It can begin as chronic.
Attributing late organ failure to the original agent. Usually it is the accumulated fibrosis.

Tissue repair
## reading_time
6
## summary
A wound that has closed is not a wound that has healed. Strength returns over months, and most of what goes wrong clinically happens in that gap.
## sections
### Definition
Wound healing is the ordered replacement of damaged tissue. It passes through haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation and remodelling, and the phases overlap rather than following one another cleanly.
### Mechanism
Haemostasis stops the bleeding and lays down a fibrin scaffold. Inflammation clears debris and organisms.

The proliferative phase is characterized by the formation of granulation tissue, reepithelialization, and neovascularization. Fibroblasts lay down collagen, new vessels grow in, and epithelium migrates across the surface.

Remodelling then replaces the initial disorganised collagen with stronger, aligned collagen. This is the longest phase by far, and it is where strength is gained.
### Key determinants
Healing by first intention describes a clean, apposed wound with minimal tissue loss; by second intention, a wound with a gap that must fill with granulation tissue and contract. The mechanism is the same and the scale is not.

What impairs healing is largely predictable: poor perfusion, infection, foreign material, malnutrition, and anything that suppresses the inflammatory phase, including corticosteroids.
### Clinical significance
The gap between closure and strength is where wounds fail. A wound that looks healed at two weeks has only a fraction of its final tensile strength, which is what dictates when sutures come out and when a patient may lift.

In diabetes several impairing factors act together — perfusion, neuropathy and infection risk — which is why diabetic wounds are managed differently from the outset rather than after they fail.
### Common misconceptions
Closure is not healing. A wound that has closed is still remodelling and has not yet reached its final strength.
## hold_these
Wound healing passes through haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation and remodelling, and the phases overlap.
The proliferative phase forms granulation tissue, re-epithelialises and neovascularises.
Remodelling is the longest phase and is where strength is gained.
Poor perfusion, infection, foreign material, malnutrition and corticosteroids all impair healing.
## lose_the_mark
Equating closure with healing. Strength is gained over months, long after the wound has closed.
Treating a diabetic wound like any other. Several impairing factors act together from the start.

## reading_time

## summary
Cancer is not one mutation. It is an accumulation, which is why it takes decades and why incidence climbs so steeply with age.
## sections
### Definition
Cancer is a disease in which some of the body's cells grow uncontrollably and spread to other parts of the body. Carcinogenesis is the process by which a normal cell acquires those two properties.
### Mechanism
No single change is sufficient. A cell must escape the controls on its division, evade the death it should undergo, sustain its own blood supply, and acquire the ability to invade and travel — and each of those is a separate genetic change.

Two classes of gene dominate. Oncogenes drive division when activated, and a change in one copy is enough. Tumour suppressor genes restrain division, and both copies must be lost before the restraint is gone.

Oncogenes are abnormally expressed or mutated forms of the corresponding proto-oncogenes. Proto-oncogenes are the normal-cell genes from which the retroviral oncogenes originated — that is, the oncogene is a normal gene gone wrong rather than a foreign one acquired.

TP53 is the tumour suppressor to know. The TP53 protein acts as a tumour suppressor, which means that it regulates cell division by keeping cells from growing and dividing too fast or in an uncontrolled way. Somatic mutations in the TP53 gene are the most common genetic changes found in human cancer, occurring in about half of all cancers.
### Key determinants
Because several independent changes are needed, time matters more than exposure intensity for most cancers. That is the main reason incidence rises so steeply with age.

Carcinogens act by increasing the mutation rate rather than by causing cancer directly — chemical, physical, and viral agents all converge on the same mechanism, and none of them produces a tumour on its own.

Each class has a worked example. Chemical: exposure to benzene increases the risk of developing leukaemia and other blood disorders. Physical: radiation of certain wavelengths, called ionising radiation, has enough energy to damage DNA and cause cancer. Viral: almost all cases of cervical cancer are caused by infection with oncogenic types of human papillomavirus, and hepatitis B can cause a chronic infection and puts people at high risk of death from cirrhosis and liver cancer.
### Clinical significance
The multi-step model explains why screening works at all. A precursor lesion has acquired some changes but not all, and removing it interrupts the sequence before it completes.

It also explains inherited cancer syndromes. A person born with one tumour suppressor copy already lost needs fewer subsequent changes, so cancer appears earlier and often in more than one site.

Two of these agents matter particularly here. In urogenital schistosomiasis, long-standing inflammation may lead to fibrosis, obstructive uropathy, and an increased risk of bladder cancer. And aflatoxin B1 is associated with increased risk for hepatocellular carcinoma in patients with chronic hepatitis B virus — note the conditioning, which the source states and which matters: the risk is described in people who already carry chronic hepatitis B, not in isolation.

Both are avoidable exposures rather than inherited risks, which places them in public health as much as in pathology.
### Common misconceptions
Cancer is not a single mutation. It is a multi-step accumulation, which is why it takes decades and why it becomes far more common with age.

## aliases

## reading_time

## summary
Benign and malignant are not points on a scale of aggressiveness. They are separated by one capability, and everything clinically important follows from it.
## sections
### Definition
A neoplasm is a new growth that persists after the stimulus that provoked it has gone. The distinction that matters is whether it can spread: the process by which cancer cells spread to other parts of the body is called metastasis.
### Mechanism
A benign tumour grows by expansion, pushing surrounding tissue aside, and usually acquires a capsule. Its cells resemble the tissue they came from.

A malignant tumour grows by invasion, crossing tissue planes rather than displacing them, and its cells lose the resemblance. Invasion is what makes complete removal difficult; metastasis is what makes it insufficient.
### Key determinants
Spread follows anatomy. Lymphatic spread reaches the draining nodes first, which is why those nodes are examined and sampled. Haematogenous spread follows venous drainage, which is why the liver and lungs are the commonest sites for tumours draining into the portal and systemic circulations respectively. Transcoelomic spread seeds a body cavity directly.

Nomenclature follows tissue of origin: a malignant epithelial tumour is a carcinoma, a malignant connective tissue tumour a sarcoma.
### Clinical significance
The presence or absence of metastasis dominates prognosis and determines whether treatment is directed at cure or at control. It is the single most consequential fact about a tumour.

The pattern of spread also predicts where to look. Knowing the venous and lymphatic drainage of the primary site tells you which investigations will find the secondary disease.
### Common misconceptions
Growth rate is not the distinction. A slow-growing tumour that invades and metastasises is malignant; a fast-growing one that does neither is not.
## hold_these
The process by which cancer cells spread to other parts of the body is called metastasis.
Benign tumours expand and are usually encapsulated; malignant tumours invade across tissue planes.
Spread follows anatomy — lymphatic to draining nodes, haematogenous along venous drainage, transcoelomic within a cavity.
Carcinoma is epithelial in origin, sarcoma connective tissue.
## lose_the_mark
Distinguishing benign from malignant by growth rate. The distinction is invasion and metastasis.
Forgetting that the drainage of the primary site predicts where secondaries appear.

## aliases
Tumour grade and stage
## reading_time
5
## summary
Two words that sound interchangeable and are not. One is what the cells look like; the other is where the disease has reached.
## sections
### Definition
Tumour grade describes how normal or abnormal cancer cells look under a microscope. Stage refers to the extent of the cancer, such as how large the tumour is and if it has spread.

Grade is a microscopic judgement, stage an anatomical one, and a tumour can be high-grade and early-stage or the reverse.
### Mechanism
Grading assesses differentiation — how closely the tumour resembles the tissue it arose from — together with nuclear features and how many cells are dividing. A well-differentiated tumour still looks like its parent tissue; a poorly differentiated one barely does.

Staging assesses anatomy. The TNM system records the size or extent of the primary tumour, whether regional lymph nodes are involved, and whether distant metastases are present, and those three are combined into a numbered stage.
### Key determinants
Grade comes from the pathologist and needs tissue. Stage comes from imaging, operative findings and pathology together, so it is assembled from several sources and can change as they arrive.

That is why a stage may be revised after surgery. It is not that the disease changed; it is that more of it became visible.
### Clinical significance
Stage generally predicts outcome more strongly than grade, because the extent of spread constrains what treatment can achieve more than cell appearance does.

Both are needed for a treatment decision, and a report giving one without the other is incomplete.
### Common misconceptions
The two words are not interchangeable. Grade is a microscopic appearance and stage is an anatomical extent, and confusing them changes the meaning of a report entirely.
## hold_these
Tumour grade describes how normal or abnormal cancer cells look under a microscope.
Stage refers to the extent of the cancer, such as how large the tumour is and if it has spread.
TNM records the primary tumour, the regional nodes and distant metastases, and combines them into a stage.
Stage generally predicts outcome more strongly than grade.
## lose_the_mark
Using grade and stage interchangeably. One is microscopic appearance, the other anatomical extent.
Reading a change of stage after surgery as disease progression. More of the disease simply became visible.
