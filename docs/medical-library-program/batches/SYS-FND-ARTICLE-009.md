## aliases
Distribution | Volume of distribution
## reading_time

## summary
Where the drug goes after it enters the blood — never evenly, and the unevenness is what decides which sites can be treated.
## sections
### Definition
Drug distribution is the disbursement of an unmetabolized drug as it moves through the body’s blood and tissues. It is the step between absorption and elimination, and the one that determines how much drug is at the target rather than merely in the patient.

The volume of distribution is a pharmacokinetic parameter that describes a drug's tendency to remain in the plasma or distribute into other tissue compartments.
### Mechanism
Distribution is generally uneven because of differences in blood perfusion, tissue binding, regional pH, and permeability of cell membranes. Each of those four acts independently, and a drug can be excluded from a tissue by any one of them.

The extent of drug distribution into tissues depends on the degree of plasma protein and tissue binding. Drug bound to plasma protein is not free to leave the circulation, so binding holds drug in the plasma and reduces what reaches the tissue.
### Key determinants
Perfusion sets how quickly a tissue is reached; binding and permeability set how much stays. A well-perfused organ sees drug first, but a poorly permeable barrier can still keep the concentration inside it low.

The volume of distribution summarises the result as a single number. A small value means the drug has stayed largely in plasma; a large one means it has left, and a value far above total body water means it is concentrated in tissue rather than dissolved anywhere.
### Clinical significance
Distribution explains why some infections and tumours are hard to treat at doses that are adequate elsewhere: the drug is in the patient and not at the site.

It also explains why a drug with a large volume of distribution is difficult to remove from the body quickly. Most of it is not in the plasma, so procedures that clean the plasma reach very little of it.
### Common misconceptions
Assuming a drug reaches all tissues equally. Distribution is uneven by default, and the unevenness is what makes some sites hard to treat.

Treating the volume of distribution as a real anatomical volume. It is a calculated parameter and can exceed total body water.

## reading_time

## summary
The body changes the drug before it removes it. Two phases, one enzyme family doing most of the work, and the reason oral doses differ from intravenous ones.
## sections
### Definition
The chemical modification of drugs into their metabolites is known as drug metabolism or metabolic biotransformation. The purpose is to make the molecule easier to excrete, generally by making it more water-soluble.

Metabolism is not the same as inactivation. The metabolite may be inactive, may be active, or may be more toxic than the parent compound.
### Mechanism
Phase I is the direct modification of the target molecule, whereas phase II entails conjugation of the target to a polar molecule of low molecular weight. Phase I typically adds or exposes a reactive group; phase II attaches something bulky and polar to it.

The cytochrome P450 system, also known as microsomal mixed function oxidase, catalyses most phase I reactions. That single fact explains most clinically important metabolic drug interactions: two drugs handled by the same enzyme compete for it.
### Key determinants
The first-pass effect is a pharmacological phenomenon in which a medication undergoes metabolism at a specific location in the body. For an orally administered drug that location is chiefly the liver, which the drug passes through before reaching the systemic circulation.

The first-pass effect decreases the active drug's concentration upon reaching systemic circulation or its site of action. How much is removed on that first pass varies enormously between drugs, and it is why the same drug can need very different oral and intravenous doses.
### Clinical significance
Because most phase I reactions run through one enzyme family, a second drug that inhibits or induces those enzymes changes the first drug's concentration without anything else having changed.

The two directions are not symmetrical in time. Inhibition is quick, because it competes for enzyme that already exists. Induction is slow, because it requires new enzyme to be made — and it persists after the inducing drug is stopped.
### Common misconceptions
Assuming metabolism inactivates. It changes the molecule; the metabolite can be inactive, active, or more toxic than the parent.

Thinking of the first-pass effect as loss in the gut. The main site is the liver the drug passes through before reaching the systemic circulation.

## aliases
Elimination | Clearance | Half-life
## reading_time
6
## summary
How the drug leaves, how fast, and the two numbers — clearance and half-life — that turn that into a dose and an interval.
## sections
### Definition
Drug elimination is the sum of the processes of removing an administered drug from the body. Elimination is accomplished in two ways, either by excretion of an unmetabolized drug in its intact form or by metabolic biotransformation followed by excretion.

So elimination is the whole process and excretion is the removal step within it. The two words are not interchangeable.
### Mechanism
Renal filtration accounts for most drug excretion. The kidney filters, and what is filtered is then partly reabsorbed or actively secreted, so the net amount excreted depends on all three processes.

The route a drug takes follows from what metabolism did to it. A water-soluble drug can be excreted unchanged; a lipid-soluble one is reabsorbed from the tubule and must be metabolised into something more polar first.
### Key determinants
Two parameters describe the result. Clearance is defined as the ratio of a drug's elimination rate to the plasma drug concentration. It is the one that sets the maintenance dose: to hold a steady concentration, drug must be given at the rate it is being cleared.

The half-life is the amount of time for serum drug concentrations to decrease by 50%. It sets the dosing interval, the time to reach steady state, and the time for the drug to disappear after stopping.
### Clinical significance
Because most excretion is renal, renal function is the single most important patient variable for most drugs. A fall in filtration raises the concentration of a renally cleared drug at an unchanged dose.

The two parameters answer different questions, and using the wrong one is a common error. Clearance tells you how much to give; half-life tells you how often, and how long to wait.
### Common misconceptions
Using elimination and excretion as the same word. Elimination is the whole process including metabolism; excretion is the removal step.

Reading clearance as an amount removed. It is a rate relative to concentration, which is why it stays constant while the amount actually removed falls.
## hold_these

## lose_the_mark

Using half-life to set the dose. Clearance sets the maintenance dose; half-life sets the interval.

## aliases

## reading_time
6
## summary
The same drug behaves differently at the extremes of age, in organ impairment, and in pregnancy — and almost always because a kinetic step moved, not because the drug changed.
## sections
### Definition
Some patients handle drugs differently enough that a standard dose produces a non-standard concentration. The metabolism and excretion of many medications decrease with aging, requiring that the doses of some medications be decreased.

What has changed in these patients is pharmacokinetics, not pharmacodynamics: the drug still does what it always did, but there is more or less of it at the target.
### Mechanism
In older adults the dominant change is renal. One of the most important pharmacokinetic changes associated with aging is decreased renal elimination of medications.

In organ impairment the same step fails more directly. Renal disorders, such as chronic kidney disease, can reduce renal function, hindering drug excretion.

In children the dominant change is distribution rather than elimination. The volume of distribution of drugs changes in children as they age.

In pregnancy the change is dilution. During the last trimester of pregnancy, plasma volume expands, so there is an overall diluting effect on plasma proteins.
### Key determinants
Each population changes a different step, which is why one rule of thumb cannot cover them. Ageing and renal disease act on elimination. Childhood acts on distribution. Pregnancy acts on protein binding through plasma volume.

Identifying which step moved is what makes an adjustment rational rather than arbitrary, because the step determines whether the dose, the interval, or neither should change.
### Clinical significance
Renal function is the variable to check first in an older patient, because it is both the commonest change and the one affecting the largest number of drugs.

The general principle holds across all four groups: measure or estimate the step that changed, and adjust from that. Adjusting from age alone treats a proxy instead of the mechanism.
### Common misconceptions
Adjusting the dose for age as a rule of thumb. What changed is a measurable kinetic step, usually renal elimination, and the adjustment follows from that rather than from the birth date.

Assuming a changed response in these patients means altered drug sensitivity. Far more often the kinetics moved and the concentration is different.

## lose_the_mark

Assuming altered response means altered sensitivity. Usually the concentration moved, not the pharmacodynamics.
