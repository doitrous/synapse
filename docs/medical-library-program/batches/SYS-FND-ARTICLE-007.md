## summary
Fluid where it should not be. Four forces decide it, and which one has failed decides what you do about it.
## sections
### Definition
Edema is a swelling due to the expansion of interstitial fluid volume in tissues or an organ. The fluid is not new: it has moved out of the capillaries and stayed in the space between cells instead of returning.

Oedema is therefore a sign rather than a diagnosis. It says that the balance of forces across the capillary wall, or the drainage that clears the residue, has failed somewhere.
### Mechanism
Edema results from elevated capillary hydrostatic pressure, increased capillary permeability, higher interstitial oncotic pressure, lower plasma oncotic pressure, lymphatic obstruction, or a combination of these.

The first pushes more fluid out. The fourth holds less of it back. The second lets protein escape, which drags water with it. The last leaves the fluid with nowhere to go. Different problems, one appearance.
### Key determinants
Which mechanism dominates is usually readable from where the swelling is and what else is wrong. A raised hydrostatic pressure follows venous or cardiac obstruction and is worst where gravity acts. A low plasma oncotic pressure follows loss or failure of albumin production and is generalised. Lymphatic obstruction is localised to the territory that drained through the blocked route.

Permeability oedema accompanies inflammation and injury, and is distinguished by the protein content of the fluid rather than by its distribution.
### Clinical significance
The mechanism decides the treatment, and treating the wrong one does harm. Diuresis helps when the problem is volume and hydrostatic pressure, and worsens matters when the problem is that the plasma cannot hold water in the vessel at all.

Oedema is also the visible end of processes that are dangerous long before it appears, which is why the question is always what produced it rather than how to remove it.
### Common misconceptions
Treating oedema as one disease. It is a final common path with several mechanisms, and the mechanism decides the treatment.

Assuming swelling means excess total body water. In low-albumin oedema the interstitium is overfull while the circulation is underfilled, and the two need opposite things.
## hold_these
Edema is a swelling due to the expansion of interstitial fluid volume in tissues or an organ.
Edema results from elevated capillary hydrostatic pressure, increased capillary permeability, higher interstitial oncotic pressure, lower plasma oncotic pressure, lymphatic obstruction, or a combination of these.
Oedema is a sign, not a diagnosis.
## lose_the_mark
Treating oedema as one disease. The mechanism decides the treatment, and diuresing low-albumin oedema empties a circulation that is already underfilled.
Assuming visible swelling means the circulation is overfull. It can be underfilled while the interstitium overflows.

## summary
A clot inside a vessel that should be open. Three things bring it about, and they almost always act together.
## sections
### Definition
Thrombosis is the formation of a blood clot within arterial or venous blood vessels, limiting the natural flow of blood. The clot forms in a vessel that was open, and it forms in life — which is what separates it from clotting in a tube or after death.
### Mechanism
The three factors of Virchow's triad are intravascular vessel wall damage, stasis of flow, and a hypercoagulable state.

Wall damage exposes surfaces that platelets adhere to. Stasis allows activated factors to accumulate instead of being washed away, and keeps platelets against the wall. A hypercoagulable state lowers the threshold for the whole process.
### Key determinants
The triad is not a list of alternatives. Most real thrombosis arises where two or three act at once, and clinical risk assessment is largely the exercise of counting how many are present.

Where the thrombus forms shapes what it is made of. In fast arterial flow platelets dominate; in slow venous flow fibrin and trapped red cells do. That difference is why the drugs used against each are not the same.
### Clinical significance
A thrombus obstructs the vessel it forms in, and it can break loose and obstruct a vessel elsewhere. Those are two distinct harms from one lesion, and the second is often the more dangerous.

Because stasis is the most modifiable arm of the triad, most everyday prevention is about movement and about not leaving patients immobile longer than necessary.
### Common misconceptions
Treating the three factors of the triad as separate sufficient causes. They combine, and most real risk comes from more than one arm at once.

Assuming an arterial and a venous thrombus are the same lesion in different places. Their composition differs, and so does what works against them.
## hold_these

## reading_time

## summary
Something carried in the blood lodges where the vessel is too narrow for it. The blockage is here; the cause is somewhere else.
## sections
### Definition
Embolism is the blocking of a blood vessel by an embolus, which can be a blood clot or other undissolved material in the blood stream. The defining feature is transport: the material formed or entered somewhere else and travelled here.
### Mechanism
An embolus moves with the flow until it reaches a vessel narrower than itself. Where that happens is determined by the circulation it is travelling in, not by any property of the tissue that suffers.

Most emboli are fragments of thrombus, which is why embolism is usually a complication of thrombosis rather than an independent event. Material other than clot can embolise too.
### Key determinants
Where the embolus started determines where it can arrive. Something released into a systemic vein travels to the lungs; something released on the arterial side travels to whatever the artery supplies. This is why the site of the blockage points back at the source.

The size of the embolus determines how far it gets, and therefore how much tissue lies beyond it.
### Clinical significance
Finding the blockage is only half the problem, because the source is still there and can send another. Treatment that only addresses the lodged embolus leaves the mechanism intact.

This is the practical reason the distinction from thrombosis matters at the bedside rather than only in an examination.
### Common misconceptions
Using embolus and thrombus interchangeably. A thrombus forms where it is found; an embolus arrived from somewhere else, and that somewhere else still needs finding.

Assuming every embolus is a clot. Other undissolved material in the bloodstream can occlude a vessel the same way.
## hold_these
Embolism is the blocking of a blood vessel by an embolus, which can be a blood clot or other undissolved material in the blood stream.
The defining feature of an embolus is that it travelled there.
The site of the blockage points back at the source.
## lose_the_mark
Using embolus and thrombus interchangeably. The source of an embolus is elsewhere and still needs finding.
Assuming every embolus is a clot. Other undissolved material occludes vessels the same way.

## aliases
Infarct | Ischaemia
## reading_time
6
## summary
Tissue dies when its blood supply stops. Whether it dies, and how fast, is the whole of the clinical question.
## sections
### Definition
Infarction is the formation of an infarct, which is necrosis in tissue due to local ischemia resulting from obstruction of blood circulation, most commonly by a thrombus or embolus.

The step before it is ischaemia. Ischaemia is a hypoperfusion of the blood through an organ or tissue caused by a pathological constriction or obstruction of its blood vessels, or an absence of blood circulation.
### Mechanism
Obstruction reduces perfusion. Reduced perfusion starves the tissue of oxygen and of the means to clear its waste. If that state is severe enough and lasts long enough, the cells die and the region becomes an infarct.

The two words therefore name two points on one process, and the interval between them is where treatment acts.
### Key determinants
Three things decide whether ischaemia becomes infarction: how complete the obstruction is, how long it lasts, and whether the tissue has an alternative supply.

The last is why the same obstruction is catastrophic in one organ and survivable in another. Tissues with dual or overlapping supply tolerate obstruction that would kill tissue supplied by a single end artery.
### Clinical significance
Because ischaemia precedes infarction, the clinical objective is to reach the obstruction while tissue is still ischaemic rather than already dead. This is the reason time is treated as the critical variable in vascular emergencies.

Once infarction has occurred the tissue is not recoverable, and management shifts to limiting the consequences rather than reversing them.
### Common misconceptions
Treating infarction and ischaemia as the same word. Ischaemia is reduced perfusion; infarction is the necrosis that follows if it is severe or prolonged enough.

Assuming ischaemia always progresses to infarction. Whether it does depends on severity, duration, and collateral supply.
## hold_these
Infarction is the formation of an infarct, which is necrosis in tissue due to local ischemia resulting from obstruction of blood circulation, most commonly by a thrombus or embolus.
Ischaemia is a hypoperfusion of the blood through an organ or tissue caused by a pathological constriction or obstruction of its blood vessels, or an absence of blood circulation.
Severity, duration and collateral supply decide whether ischaemia becomes infarction.
## lose_the_mark
Treating ischaemia and infarction as the same word. One is reduced perfusion; the other is the necrosis that may follow.
Assuming ischaemia always progresses to infarction. Collateral supply and duration decide.

## aliases
Total body water | Fluid compartments | Hypovolaemia
## reading_time
6
## summary
Where the body's water sits, in what proportions, and what it means when the compartment that fills the vessels is the one that empties.
## sections
### Definition
For the average 70 kg man, 60% of total body weight is water, amounting to 42 L, separated into intracellular and extracellular fluid volume.

Hypovolaemia is a state of low extracellular fluid volume, generally secondary to combined sodium and water loss. It is a statement about one compartment, not about total body water.
### Mechanism
Two-thirds of the body's water is within the intracellular fluid space, which equates to 28 L. One-third of total body water is extracellular, about 14 L, of which 75% is interstitial and 25% is plasma.

So of 42 litres, only about 3.5 sit in the plasma. The circulation is the smallest compartment, which is why losses that look modest against total body water can be large against the volume that matters.
### Normal values
Total body water: about 60% of body weight, roughly 42 L in a 70 kg man.

Intracellular volume: two-thirds of total body water, about 28 L. Extracellular volume: one-third, about 14 L. Within the extracellular volume, interstitial fluid is 75% (about 10.5 L) and plasma 25% (about 3.5 L).
### Key determinants
Water moves freely between compartments along osmotic gradients, and sodium is the solute that holds it outside cells. This is why the distribution of a loss depends on what was lost with the water.

Losing water alone raises extracellular sodium, and water leaves the cells to follow it, so the loss is shared across all 42 litres. Losing sodium and water together leaves the osmotic gradient unchanged, so nothing is drawn out of the cells and the whole loss falls on the extracellular volume.
### Clinical significance
That difference is the reason hypovolaemia and dehydration are not the same problem. A combined salt and water loss empties the compartment that fills the vessels; a pure water loss is buffered across the whole body.

It also explains why the figures are worth remembering. Knowing that plasma is about 3.5 litres makes the significance of a given loss immediate in a way that a percentage of body weight does not.
### Common misconceptions
Reading the compartments as separate tanks. Water moves freely between them along osmotic gradients, which is why a sodium problem is usually a water problem.

Equating hypovolaemia with dehydration. Losing water alone raises sodium and draws fluid out of cells; losing salt and water together empties the extracellular space directly.
## hold_these

One-third of total body water is extracellular, about 14 L, of which 75% is interstitial and 25% is plasma.

## lose_the_mark

Equating hypovolaemia with dehydration. A combined salt and water loss falls entirely on the extracellular volume; a pure water loss is shared across all 42 litres.

## summary
The sodium result is usually telling you about water. Reading it as a salt problem is the commonest way to get the treatment backwards.
## sections
### Definition
Hyponatraemia is usually defined as a serum sodium concentration less than 135 mEq/L, although the threshold may vary slightly with the laboratory reference range. Hypernatraemia is defined as a serum sodium concentration greater than 145 mEq/L.
### Mechanism
Hyponatraemia is caused by an excess of total body water compared to total body sodium. The concentration falls because the denominator rose, not because sodium was lost in most cases.

Hypernatraemia is the mirror image, and in practice almost always reflects a deficit of water rather than a surplus of sodium.
### Normal values
Serum sodium below 135 mEq/L is hyponatraemia; above 145 mEq/L is hypernatraemia.

The cited source notes that the lower threshold may vary slightly depending on the laboratory's reference range, so the figure is a convention rather than a constant.
### Key determinants
Sodium concentration is a ratio, and either term can move it. Asking whether the abnormality lies in the numerator or the denominator is what turns a number into a diagnosis.

Because water follows sodium osmotically, a sodium disorder is also a statement about cell volume. That is why the brain is the organ that suffers first in both directions, and why the rate of correction matters as much as the direction.
### Clinical significance
Correcting the number rather than the problem causes harm. Giving sodium for a hyponatraemia caused by water excess, or correcting either disorder faster than the brain can adapt, produces injury that the original abnormality had not.

This is why the reasoning about water comes before the treatment, and why rate limits exist at all.
### Common misconceptions
Correcting hyponatraemia by giving sodium without asking about water. The excess water is usually the abnormality.

Reading a high sodium as too much salt. It is nearly always too little water, and the treatment follows the water.

## summary
A narrow range, and the heart is what enforces it. The number is acted on before symptoms appear, in both directions.
## sections
### Definition
An acceptable lower limit for normal serum potassium is 3.5 mmol/L, although variation exists. Hyperkalaemia is a serum or plasma potassium concentration exceeding the upper limit of normal, typically greater than 5.0 to 5.5 mEq/L.

The normal range is narrow because potassium sets the resting membrane potential, and excitable tissue tolerates little change in it.
### Mechanism
Almost all the body's potassium is inside cells, and the small fraction outside is what the blood test measures. A shift across the cell membrane therefore changes the result without any potassium having been gained or lost.

This is why the same value can mean two different things: a true deficit or surplus of total body potassium, or a redistribution between compartments.
### Normal values
Lower limit of normal: 3.5 mmol/L. Mild hypokalaemia is between 3.0 and 3.5 mEq/L, moderate between 2.5 and 3.0 mEq/L, and severe below 2.5 mEq/L.

Hyperkalaemia: typically above 5.0 to 5.5 mEq/L.

The lower threshold is not universally agreed. The dedicated StatPearls Hypokalemia chapter gives 3.5 mmol/L as the lower limit of normal; the StatPearls Electrolytes chapter defines hypokalaemia as under 3.6 mmol/L. This library follows the dedicated chapter.
### Key determinants
Whether the abnormality is a shift or a true change decides the treatment, and the result alone cannot distinguish them. The history — losses, drugs, acid-base state — is what separates the two.

Severity grading matters because the risk is not linear. The move from mild to severe hypokalaemia is a change in kind rather than degree, and the same is true at the upper end.
### Clinical significance
Mild elevations are often asymptomatic, whereas severe hyperkalaemia can precipitate life-threatening cardiac arrhythmias, muscle weakness, or paralysis.

That asymmetry between how the patient looks and what the number means is why potassium results are acted on promptly rather than observed. Waiting for symptoms in hyperkalaemia means waiting for the arrhythmia.
### Common misconceptions
Treating a number without asking where the potassium went. A shift into cells and a true depletion look identical on the result and need different treatment.

Waiting for symptoms in hyperkalaemia. The first manifestation of a severe one can be an arrhythmia.

## arabic_title

## aliases
Metabolic acidosis | Respiratory acidosis | Anion gap
## reading_time
7
## summary
Two organs, two timescales, and one number that tells you which of them has failed and which is compensating.
## sections
### Definition
Acidaemia is defined as a blood pH below 7.35. What produced it is either metabolic or respiratory.

Metabolic acidosis is characterised by a reduction in serum bicarbonate with a compensatory decrease in arterial pCO2. Respiratory acidosis is a state in which there is usually a failure of ventilation and an accumulation of carbon dioxide.
### Mechanism
Bicarbonate is handled by the kidney and carbon dioxide by the lung, so each disorder has a primary organ and the other organ compensates.

In metabolic acidosis bicarbonate falls first and ventilation increases to lower pCO2. In respiratory acidosis pCO2 rises first and the kidney retains bicarbonate in response. Reading which moved first is what identifies the primary disorder.
### Key determinants
The two compensations run on different timescales. Ventilation responds within minutes; renal retention of bicarbonate takes days. An acute and a chronic respiratory acidosis therefore show very different bicarbonate values at the same pCO2, and the difference dates the problem.

Within metabolic acidosis, the anion gap sorts the causes. The anion gap is the difference between measured cations, such as sodium and potassium, and measured anions, such as chloride and bicarbonate.
### Normal values
Acidaemia: blood pH below 7.35.

A normal anion gap depends on the concentration of phosphate and albumin in the serum and ranges from 4 to 12 mmol/L.
### Clinical significance
Identifying the primary disorder decides where to look. A metabolic acidosis sends you to the causes of acid gain or bicarbonate loss; a respiratory one sends you to ventilation.

The anion gap narrows the metabolic search further, which is why it is calculated routinely rather than only when the diagnosis is obscure.
### Common misconceptions
Reading a compensating value as a second disorder. Compensation is the expected response to the primary problem, not an additional one.

Expecting renal compensation immediately. It takes days, so the bicarbonate in an acute respiratory acidosis has barely moved.

Reading the anion gap without checking albumin. Albumin is a major unmeasured anion, so a low albumin lowers the gap and can hide a raised one.
## hold_these

Respiratory acidosis is a state in which there is usually a failure of ventilation and an accumulation of carbon dioxide.
The anion gap is the difference between measured cations, such as sodium and potassium, and measured anions, such as chloride and bicarbonate.
A normal anion gap depends on the concentration of phosphate and albumin in the serum and ranges from 4 to 12 mmol/L.
## lose_the_mark
