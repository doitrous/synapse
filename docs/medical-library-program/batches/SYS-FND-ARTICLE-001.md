## summary
Every cell is bounded by a lipid bilayer that separates its interior from the fluid around it. What crosses that boundary, and how, is set by the bilayer's hydrophobic core and by the proteins embedded in it.
## sections
### Definition
The plasma membrane separates the inner contents of a cell from its exterior environment. It is built from a lipid bilayer, which is the universal basis of cell-membrane structure.

Phospholipids arrange themselves with their water-loving heads facing the fluid on each side and their water-fearing tails pointing inward. That arrangement is spontaneous: it is what these molecules do in water, not something the cell has to maintain.
### Mechanism
The hydrophobic core is the barrier. A substance that dissolves in lipid passes through it; a substance that does not, cannot — however small it is.

Proteins spanning the bilayer provide the routes for everything else. Channels open a water-filled pore; carriers bind a solute and change shape around it; pumps do the same but spend ATP to move a solute against its gradient.
### Key determinants
Three properties of a solute decide how it crosses: its lipid solubility, its size, and its charge. Lipid solubility dominates. A charged particle is effectively excluded from the core no matter how small, which is why ions depend entirely on proteins.

Membrane composition matters too. Cholesterol sitting between the phospholipids stiffens the bilayer at higher temperatures and keeps it fluid at lower ones.
### Clinical significance
Most drugs must cross at least one plasma membrane to act, so lipid solubility is a central determinant of whether a drug can be given by mouth and whether it reaches the brain.

Membrane transport proteins are also drug targets in their own right — the proton pump of the gastric parietal cell and the sodium–potassium pump are both blocked therapeutically.
### Common misconceptions
Being small is not enough to cross a membrane, and being large is not disqualifying. Sodium is one of the smallest species in the body and cannot cross unaided; a steroid hormone is far larger and crosses freely.
## hold_these
The plasma membrane separates the inner contents of a cell from its exterior environment.
The lipid bilayer is the universal basis of cell-membrane structure.
Lipid solubility, not size, is the dominant determinant of passive crossing.
Ions and other charged species depend entirely on membrane proteins.
## lose_the_mark
Predicting that a small solute crosses the membrane because it is small. Sodium is tiny and charged, and cannot cross the lipid core unaided.
Describing the membrane as a passive barrier. Its selectivity comes from the proteins embedded in it, not from the lipid alone.

## summary
A cell holds its shape, positions its organelles and moves itself using a network of protein filaments that is constantly being built and taken apart.
## sections
### Definition
The cytoskeleton is a group of fibrous proteins that provide structural support for cells. It is not a rigid frame: its filaments are assembled and disassembled continuously, and that turnover is what allows a cell to divide, crawl and change shape.
### Mechanism
Three filament classes do different jobs. Microfilaments of actin sit just under the membrane and drive shape change and crawling. Intermediate filaments resist mechanical stress and are the most stable of the three. Microtubules of tubulin form the tracks for organelle transport and the spindle that separates chromosomes.

Motor proteins walk along these tracks. Kinesin and dynein move cargo in opposite directions along microtubules; myosin walks along actin.
### Key determinants
Which filament dominates depends on what the cell does. An epithelial cell under mechanical stress is rich in intermediate filaments; a dividing cell reorganises its microtubules into a spindle.

Assembly is regulated at the level of the subunit pool, so a drug that binds free tubulin can stop the spindle forming without touching anything already built.
### Clinical significance
The spindle is a drug target. Vinca alkaloids prevent microtubule assembly and taxanes prevent disassembly; both arrest dividing cells in mitosis, which is why both are used as chemotherapy and why both cause peripheral neuropathy — neurons depend on the same microtubule transport.

Intermediate filament proteins are also tissue markers: which one a tumour expresses helps say what tissue it arose from.
### Common misconceptions
The cytoskeleton is not a static scaffold. Treating it as one makes it impossible to explain how a cell divides at all.
## hold_these
The cytoskeleton is a group of fibrous proteins that provide structural support for cells.
Three classes: microfilaments for shape and movement, intermediate filaments for mechanical strength, microtubules for transport and the spindle.
Continuous assembly and disassembly is what allows division and migration.
## lose_the_mark
Describing the cytoskeleton as a fixed internal skeleton. Its turnover is the point, and without it mitosis cannot be explained.
Assuming a spindle drug acts only on tumour cells. Neurons use the same microtubule transport, which is where the neuropathy comes from.

## summary
The nucleus holds the cell's DNA behind a double membrane. Separating transcription from translation is what lets a eukaryotic cell process its messenger RNA before anything reads it.
## sections
### Definition
The nucleus is a cell's central organelle, which contains the cell's DNA. It is bounded by a nuclear envelope of two membranes, perforated by nuclear pores that control what passes in and out.
### Mechanism
DNA is transcribed to RNA inside the nucleus. That transcript is capped, spliced and tailed before it leaves, and only then does it reach a ribosome in the cytoplasm to be translated.

The envelope is what makes this possible. A bacterium, having no nucleus, translates a transcript while it is still being made and has no comparable opportunity to edit it.
### Key determinants
Nuclear pores are selective, not open holes: a protein carrying a nuclear localisation signal is imported, and one without it is not.

The nucleolus, a dense region within the nucleus, is where ribosomal RNA is made and ribosomal subunits are assembled. Its size tracks how much protein the cell is making.
### Clinical significance
Nuclear appearance is a core diagnostic tool. Pathologists read nuclear size, shape, chromatin pattern and nucleolar prominence to judge whether a cell is malignant, and the nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio is one of the first things assessed on a smear.

Antibodies directed against nuclear components are the basis of the antinuclear antibody test used in connective-tissue disease.
### Common misconceptions
The nucleus is not where protein is made. Transcription happens there; translation happens on ribosomes in the cytoplasm, and the envelope is exactly what keeps the two apart.
## hold_these
The nucleus is a cell's central organelle, which contains the cell's DNA.
Transcription happens in the nucleus; translation happens in the cytoplasm.
Separating the two allows the transcript to be processed before it is read.
Nuclear appearance is a primary criterion in judging malignancy.
## lose_the_mark
Calling the nucleus the site of protein synthesis. It is the site of transcription; ribosomes translate.
Treating nuclear pores as open holes. They are selective, and import depends on a signal the protein carries.

## summary
The mitochondrion converts energy already held in reduced carriers into ATP. Its two membranes are not decoration: the inner one holds the gradient that the whole process depends on.
## sections
### Definition
A mitochondrion is a membranous, bean-shaped organelle that is the energy transformer of the cell. It has an outer membrane, an inner membrane thrown into folds called cristae, and a matrix enclosed by that inner membrane.
### Mechanism
The citric acid cycle runs in the matrix and strips electrons from fuel molecules onto carriers. The electron transport chain, embedded in the inner membrane, passes those electrons down a series of complexes and uses the energy released to pump protons out of the matrix.

The result is a proton gradient across the inner membrane. ATP synthase lets protons flow back down that gradient and uses the flow to make ATP. Nothing here creates energy; the gradient is a way of holding energy that already existed in the fuel.
### Key determinants
Cristae multiply the inner membrane's surface area, so a cell with high energy demand carries not only more mitochondria but mitochondria with more cristae. Cardiac muscle is the standard example.

The organelle carries its own small circular genome, inherited from the mother alone, which encodes a handful of respiratory chain components.
### Clinical significance
Mitochondrial disease follows from that separate genome, and its inheritance pattern is unlike anything nuclear: an affected mother passes it to all her children, an affected father to none. Tissues with the highest energy demand — muscle, brain, heart — show it first.

Mitochondria also decide apoptosis. Release of cytochrome c from the intermembrane space commits a cell to death, which links this organelle directly to both degeneration and cancer.
### Common misconceptions
A mitochondrion does not make energy. It transfers energy already held in reduced carriers into ATP, and the distinction is exactly what oxidative phosphorylation describes.
## hold_these
A mitochondrion is a membranous, bean-shaped organelle that is the energy transformer of the cell.
The electron transport chain sits in the inner membrane; the citric acid cycle runs in the matrix.
The proton gradient across the inner membrane is what ATP synthase spends.
The mitochondrial genome is inherited from the mother alone.
## lose_the_mark
Saying a mitochondrion makes or produces energy. It transfers energy that already exists in reduced carriers.
Placing the electron transport chain in the matrix. It is embedded in the inner membrane, which is why the gradient can exist at all.
Applying nuclear inheritance patterns to mitochondrial disease. An affected father passes it to none of his children.
